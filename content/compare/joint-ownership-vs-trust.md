Joint ownership with right of survivorship means that when one owner dies, the other owns the property outright, without probate. A revocable living trust also avoids probate, but you keep full control and can name backups and conditions. Joint ownership is simpler, while a trust is more flexible and avoids several hidden risks.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Joint ownership | Revocable living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to set up | Low, often just a new title or account form | Higher, plus time to retitle assets |
| Avoids probate | Yes, at the first death | Yes, at every death, if funded |
| Who controls it | All owners share control | You, as trustee |
| Can you change it | Usually needs the other owner's consent | Yes, on your own |
| Exposure to co-owner's debts | Yes, in many cases | No, for a revocable trust |
| Handles incapacity | Partly, for shared accounts only | Yes, a successor trustee steps in |
| Backup beneficiaries | No, the survivor decides | Yes |
| Gift or tax issues | Adding a non-spouse can be a gift | Generally none while revocable |
| Privacy | Usually private | Usually private |

## How each one works

With joint ownership, two or more people own an asset together. With right of survivorship, the last owner standing takes it all. The deed or account form must say so, and the wording rules vary by state.

With a revocable trust, you retitle assets to yourself as trustee. You keep using them as before. At your death, the successor trustee distributes them as the trust directs. See [revocable living trusts explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).

## When people choose joint ownership

Joint ownership is common and can work well when:

- A married couple owns a home or a checking account together.
- Both owners truly mean to share the asset now, not just at death.
- The owners trust each other and their finances are aligned.
- The estate is simple and the survivor can make their own plan later.

Say a married couple owns their home as joint tenants. When one spouse dies, the other usually needs only a death certificate and a recorded affidavit to clear title. No probate is needed for the house.

## When people choose a trust

People often choose a trust instead of adding someone to a title when:

- They want a child to inherit but do not want to give that child rights today.
- They worry about a co-owner's creditors, lawsuit or divorce.
- They want to split assets among several children evenly.
- They have a blended family and want to protect children from a prior marriage.
- They want a plan for what happens if both spouses die together.

Say a widow adds her son to her house deed to skip probate. If her son is sued or divorces, the house may be at risk. If she later wants to sell or refinance, she may need his signature. A trust avoids those problems. Blended families face extra risk, as explained in [estate planning for blended families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families).

## Using both together

Many couples keep a joint checking account for daily bills and put the house and investments in a trust. That split can work, as long as the joint account fits the overall plan.

Be careful not to leave important assets in joint names by accident. A trust only controls what is titled in it. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- What forms of joint ownership does my state allow, and which do I have now?
- Would adding my child to a title count as a gift for tax purposes?
- How would joint ownership affect the tax basis of my home at death?
- What happens to joint assets if we both die at the same time?
- Would a trust or a transfer-on-death option work better for my goals?
- How would probate work for my estate if I do nothing? See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).
