A special needs trust holds money for a person with a disability without counting against means-tested benefits like SSI and Medicaid. A trustee controls it and there is no cap on how much it can hold. An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings account the beneficiary can often manage, with limits on who qualifies and how much goes in each year.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Special needs trust | ABLE account |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to set up | Attorney fees to draft | Low, opened online through a state program |
| Who qualifies | A person with a disability, at any age of onset | Disability must have begun before a set age |
| Who controls it | A trustee | Usually the beneficiary, or an authorized person |
| Contribution limit | No set limit | Yearly limit tied to the gift tax exclusion |
| Effect on SSI | Generally none, if properly drafted | Counted only above a set balance |
| Medicaid payback at death | Required for trusts funded with the person's own money | State may claim remaining funds |
| Allowed spending | Broad, at the trustee's discretion | Qualified disability expenses, including housing |
| Can you change it | Depends on the trust terms | Beneficiary and contributions can be adjusted |
| Tax treatment | Depends on how the trust is drafted | Earnings grow tax-free for qualified expenses |

Eligibility for an ABLE account requires disability onset before age 46, as of 2026. <!-- verify --> Yearly contributions are capped at the annual gift tax exclusion, which was $19,000 per recipient in 2025. <!-- verify --> For SSI, only the balance above $100,000 is counted, as of 2026. <!-- verify -->

## Two kinds of special needs trusts

A third-party special needs trust holds money from parents, grandparents or others. It usually has no Medicaid payback, so leftover funds can go to other family members.

A first-party special needs trust holds the person's own money, such as a lawsuit award or a direct inheritance. Federal law requires these to repay Medicaid at death. See [special needs trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts).

## When people choose a special needs trust

Families often choose a special needs trust when:

- They expect to leave a meaningful inheritance or life insurance payout.
- They want a trusted person managing money over the long term.
- The person may not be able to manage funds on their own.
- The disability began after the ABLE age limit.
- They want leftover funds to pass to siblings, not to repay Medicaid.

Say parents want to leave a share of their estate to a son with autism. Naming a third-party special needs trust in their will or living trust keeps his benefits intact. A trustee can pay for therapies, travel and extras over his lifetime.

## When people choose an ABLE account

People often choose an ABLE account when:

- The beneficiary wants to manage everyday spending on their own.
- They want a simple place to save part of a paycheck without losing SSI.
- They need funds for housing costs, which can be harder through a trust.
- The amounts are modest and an attorney-drafted trust would be more than needed.

Say a young woman with a disability works part time. She can deposit some earnings into an ABLE account and use a debit card for rent, groceries and transit. Her savings grow without putting her SSI at risk, up to the limits.

## Using both together

Many families use both. The special needs trust holds the larger, long-term funds. The trustee moves smaller amounts into the ABLE account for day-to-day needs the beneficiary can handle.

This setup pairs control with independence. Benefit rules are detailed and state programs differ, so coordination matters. See [irrevocable trusts explained](/guides/irrevocable-trusts-explained) and [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Should our plan use a third-party special needs trust, a first-party trust, or both?
- Does my child qualify for an ABLE account based on age of onset?
- Who should serve as trustee, and who should be the backup?
- How should relatives name the trust in their own wills and beneficiary forms?
- Does my state make Medicaid claims against ABLE accounts?
- How do housing payments from each option affect SSI?
