A simple will leaves your property directly to the people you name, through probate. A pour-over will is a backup for a living trust. It sends anything left outside the trust into it, so the trust's terms decide who gets what. The right one depends on whether you have a trust.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Pour-over will | Simple will |
|---|---|---|
| Works with a trust | Yes, it depends on one | No trust needed |
| Who receives assets | Your trust | The people or charities named in the will |
| Cost to set up | Usually part of a trust package | Usually lower on its own |
| Goes through probate | Yes, for any assets it covers | Yes, for any assets it covers |
| Privacy | The will is public, but the trust terms can stay private | The will and its gifts become public |
| Names a guardian for minor children | Yes | Yes |
| Names an executor | Yes | Yes |
| Handles incapacity | No | No |
| Can you change it | Yes, while you have capacity | Yes, while you have capacity |

## How each one works

A simple will says, in effect, "give my house to my daughter and split the rest between my two sons." It names an executor and, for parents, a guardian. Everything it covers passes through probate. See [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will).

A pour-over will says, in effect, "give anything I still own in my own name to my trust." The trust then follows its own instructions. The will is a safety net for assets that never got retitled. See [revocable living trusts explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).

## When people choose a pour-over will

People sign a pour-over will when they:

- Have a revocable living trust and want one set of instructions.
- Want to catch assets they forget to title in the trust.
- Expect to receive assets late, like an inheritance or a lawsuit payout.
- Want the trust's private terms to govern, not the will's public ones.
- Need a place to name a guardian for minor children.

Say you set up a trust and retitle your house and brokerage account. Later you buy a car in your own name and forget about the trust. The pour-over will sends the car into the trust after probate, or through a small estate process if your state offers one.

## When people choose a simple will

People often choose a simple will when they:

- Do not own real estate or have a large estate.
- Have most assets passing by beneficiary designation already.
- Want to leave everything outright to adults.
- Want a lower-cost plan now and may add a trust later.

Say a couple in their 30s rents an apartment and has a toddler. A simple will for each of them, naming a guardian and leaving everything to the other spouse, can cover the basics. See [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children).

## Using both together

You would not usually have both as current documents. A new will generally revokes earlier ones. If you set up a trust, the pour-over will typically replaces your simple will.

The best results come from keeping the pour-over will's job small. Retitle assets into the trust as you acquire them. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Do I have enough reason to set up a trust, or is a simple will enough?
- If I have a trust, which assets are still outside it?
- Does my state have a small estate process for assets a pour-over will covers?
- Should my will create a trust for my children if I do not have a living trust?
- Who should be my executor, and should it be the same person as my trustee?
- When should I review my will after major life changes?
