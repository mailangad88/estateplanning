A beneficiary designation is a form you fill out with a bank, insurer or retirement plan that names who gets that account when you die. It usually overrides whatever your will says, and the asset skips probate. A will covers everything without a named beneficiary, and it is where you name an executor and a guardian for minor children.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Beneficiary designation | Will |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to set up | Usually free, a form with the institution | Attorney fee or form cost |
| Goes through probate | No, if a living beneficiary is named | Yes |
| What it controls | Only that one account or policy | Assets in your name with no beneficiary |
| Who controls it | You, until death | You, until death |
| Privacy | Usually private | Becomes public in probate |
| Names a guardian for minor children | No | Yes |
| Can you change it | Yes, by filing a new form | Yes, by a new will or a codicil |
| Handles incapacity | No | No |
| Common pitfalls | Outdated names, no backup, naming a minor | Thinking it controls accounts it does not |

## How each one works

When you die, the institution pays the account to the person on its form after seeing a death certificate. Your executor does not get involved. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

A will only reaches assets that do not have their own path. Your executor gathers those assets through probate and distributes them as the will directs. See [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will).

## When people choose a beneficiary designation

Beneficiary designations are the usual choice for:

- 401(k), 403(b) and IRA accounts.
- Life insurance and annuities.
- Bank accounts with a payable-on-death option.
- Brokerage accounts with a transfer-on-death option.

They are fast, private and free. They work best when the beneficiary is an adult who can receive money outright. Many people also name a backup, often called a contingent beneficiary.

Forms are only as good as their last update. People often forget a form they filled out at a first job, or a policy bought decades ago. After a marriage, divorce, birth or death in the family, it is common to request a copy of every form and check the names.

Some accounts have extra rules. For example, many employer retirement plans require a spouse's written consent before you name someone else. Those rules come from federal law and the plan itself, so check with the plan administrator.

## When people choose a will

People rely on a will for:

- Assets that cannot take a beneficiary, like personal belongings.
- A house in states without transfer-on-death deeds, if there is no trust.
- Naming a guardian and executor.
- Setting up a trust for children who are still minors.
- Catching assets when every named beneficiary has died.

Say you name your 10-year-old daughter as beneficiary of your life insurance. Insurers usually will not pay a minor directly, so a court may need to appoint someone to manage the money. Naming a trust for her, created in your will or a living trust, can avoid that. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Using both together

Nearly everyone uses both. The key is keeping them in sync, because the form wins when they disagree.

Say your will leaves everything equally to your three children. Your IRA still names only your oldest child from years ago. That child likely gets the whole IRA. Reviewing forms when you update your will prevents that mismatch. See [updating your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Do my current beneficiary forms match the plan in my will?
- Should I name individuals, my estate or a trust on each account?
- What happens if a beneficiary dies before me and I named no backup?
- How should I name a minor or a beneficiary with a disability?
- Are there tax issues with naming a trust on a retirement account?
- How does my state treat beneficiary forms after a divorce?
