Probate assets are owned by you alone, with no beneficiary or surviving co-owner named, and they pass through a court process. Non-probate assets pass straight to someone by beneficiary form, joint ownership, a transfer-on-death designation or a trust. Your will only controls the probate assets, so how each asset is titled matters a great deal.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Probate assets | Non-probate assets |
|---|---|---|
| Common examples | House in your name alone, solo bank account, car, personal items | Life insurance, retirement accounts, joint accounts, trust assets, POD and TOD accounts |
| Controlled by | Your will, or state law if there is no will | The beneficiary form, title or trust |
| Court involvement | Yes, through probate | Usually none |
| Typical time to transfer | Months to a year or more | Days to weeks, in many cases |
| Cost to transfer | Court fees, and often attorney and executor fees | Usually low |
| Privacy | Probate filings are usually public | Usually private |
| Handles incapacity | No | Only a trust does |
| Can you change who inherits | Yes, by updating your will | Yes, by updating the form, title or trust |
| Risk of conflicts | Will contests are possible | Outdated forms can send money to the wrong person |

## How the two categories work

When you die, your executor gathers the probate assets, pays debts and distributes what is left through the court. See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works). The process and cost vary a lot by state.

Non-probate assets move on their own track. The bank, insurer or trustee pays the named person after seeing a death certificate and some forms. The will has no say.

## When assets end up in probate

Assets tend to land in probate when:

- They are titled in your name alone with no beneficiary.
- The named beneficiary died first and no backup was named.
- The beneficiary form names "my estate."
- A trust was set up but the asset was never moved into it.
- A joint owner died first and the survivor never made a plan.

Probate is not always bad. It gives court oversight, a set deadline for creditor claims and a clear process if family members disagree. Many states also offer a simpler process for small estates.

## When assets pass outside probate

Assets pass outside probate when they have a built-in path, such as:

- A beneficiary designation on a retirement account or life insurance. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).
- A payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designation on a bank or brokerage account. See [transfer on death and payable on death](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death).
- Joint ownership with right of survivorship.
- Title held in a living trust. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust).
- A transfer-on-death deed, in states that allow one.

Say your will splits everything equally between your two children, but your life insurance names only your son. Your son gets the insurance in full. Your daughter shares only in whatever goes through probate.

## Using both together

Most estates have some of each. A good plan makes sure the two columns tell the same story.

People often keep a will even if nearly everything passes outside probate. It catches assets they forgot, assets received late, or a beneficiary who died first. It is also where parents name a guardian for minor children, as covered in [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Which of my assets would go through probate today?
- Does my state have a small estate process, and would I qualify?
- Do my beneficiary forms match what my will says?
- Should I name backup beneficiaries on every account?
- Is it worth setting up a trust, or are designations enough?
- How would my debts be paid if most assets pass outside probate?
