An executor is the person named in a will to settle your estate through probate, usually under a court's supervision. A trustee is the person who manages assets held in a trust and follows its instructions, often with no court involved. Both are fiduciaries, which means they must act honestly and in the beneficiaries' best interest.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Executor | Trustee |
|---|---|---|
| Named in | A will | A trust document |
| Authority starts | When a court appoints them after death | When the trust says, often at death or incapacity |
| Court involvement | Usually yes, through probate | Usually no |
| Typical length of the job | Months to a year or more | Months to many years |
| Main tasks | Gather assets, pay debts, file returns, distribute | Manage, invest and distribute trust assets |
| Privacy | Probate filings are usually public | Usually private |
| Can act during your life | No | Yes, if you become incapacitated |
| Duty owed | To estate beneficiaries and creditors | To trust beneficiaries |
| Other names | Personal representative | Successor trustee |

## What each one does

An executor's job follows a fairly set path. They file the will with the court, get appointed, notify creditors, collect assets, pay bills and taxes, and then distribute what is left. Some states call this person a personal representative. See [settling an estate step by step](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step).

A trustee's job depends on what the trust says. If the trust pays everything out at death, the work may look much like an executor's, minus the court. If the trust holds money for a child until age 30, the trustee may manage investments and make decisions for years.

## When people choose an executor role

You do not really choose between the roles. You need an executor if you have a will, and a trustee if you have a trust. What you choose is who fills each one. People often name as executor someone who:

- Is organized and willing to handle paperwork and deadlines.
- Lives nearby, or can manage tasks from a distance.
- Gets along with the beneficiaries.
- Is comfortable working with a court, an attorney and an accountant.

Some states have rules about out-of-state executors, such as requiring a bond or a local agent. See [choosing an executor](/guides/choosing-an-executor).

## When people choose a trustee role

A trustee may hold the job far longer, so people often look for someone who:

- Is good with money and careful about records.
- Can say no to a beneficiary when the trust requires it.
- Will likely be available for as long as the trust lasts.
- Understands the purpose of the trust, such as supporting a child or protecting benefits.

For long-term trusts, some families name a professional trustee, like a bank or trust company, or a professional to serve alongside a family member. See [choosing a trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee).

## Using both together

Many plans include both a will and a trust, so both roles exist. A common setup names the same person as executor of the pour-over will and successor trustee of the trust.

Say your daughter is both. She would use the will, if needed, to move any stray assets into the trust. Then, as trustee, she would follow the trust's instructions for your beneficiaries. The [executor workload tool](/tools/executor-workload) can give a sense of what the role involves.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Should one person serve in both roles, or should I split them?
- Who should be my backup if my first choice cannot serve?
- Does my state allow an out-of-state executor, and what extra steps apply?
- Should my trustee have the power to hire professionals for investing or taxes?
- How will my executor and trustee be paid, if at all?
- What records should I leave to make their jobs easier?
