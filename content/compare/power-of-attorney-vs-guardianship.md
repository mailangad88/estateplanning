A power of attorney lets you choose, ahead of time, who will handle your money or medical decisions if you cannot. Guardianship is what often happens when there is no such document: a family member asks a court to appoint someone. Planning ahead usually costs less, takes less time and keeps your choices in your hands.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Power of attorney | Guardianship |
|---|---|---|
| Who chooses the decision-maker | You | A judge |
| Cost to set up | Usually modest | Often higher, with court and attorney fees |
| Time to put in place | Days, while you have capacity | Weeks to months after incapacity |
| Court involvement | None, in most cases | Yes, from start to finish |
| Privacy | Private | Court records, which may be public |
| Ongoing reporting | Usually none required | Often yearly reports to the court |
| Can you change it | Yes, while you have capacity | Only through the court |
| Effect on your rights | You keep your rights | A court may limit your legal rights |
| Other names | Agent, attorney-in-fact | Conservator, in some states |

Terms vary by state. Some states use "guardian" for personal care and "conservator" for money. Ask an attorney licensed in your state how the terms apply.

## How each one works

A financial power of attorney names an agent to manage your money, pay bills and deal with banks. A healthcare power of attorney names someone to make medical choices. A "durable" power of attorney keeps working if you lose capacity, which is the point. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

Guardianship starts with a petition to the court. There is usually notice to family, a medical evaluation and a hearing. If the judge appoints a guardian, that person may need to post a bond, file reports and get approval for big decisions.

## When people choose a power of attorney

People usually sign powers of attorney as part of a basic estate plan. Common reasons include:

- They want to pick the person who would help them.
- They want to avoid court costs and delays for their family.
- They want privacy about their health and finances.
- They have a parent or spouse who depends on them.

Say your mother has a stroke and cannot speak. If she signed a durable power of attorney, her named agent can pay her mortgage the next week. Without one, the family may wait for a court hearing.

A revocable trust can also help. A successor trustee can manage trust assets without court. See [revocable living trusts explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).

## When people choose guardianship

Guardianship is rarely a first choice, but it can be the right tool when:

- The person never signed documents and can no longer do so.
- An agent under a power of attorney is misusing money.
- Family members disagree sharply and need a neutral decision.
- The person needs court-supervised protection from exploitation.
- An adult child with a disability turns 18 and cannot make decisions alone.

Courts often consider less restrictive options first, like supported decision-making or a limited guardianship. The options and standards vary by state.

## Using both together

A power of attorney can include your preference for who should be guardian if one is ever needed. Many states give that nomination real weight.

This pairs well with healthcare documents. See [healthcare directives and living wills](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills). If long-term care is likely, an agent with clear powers can also help with benefits, as covered in [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Should my power of attorney be effective now or only on incapacity?
- What powers should my agent have, such as making gifts or changing beneficiaries?
- Who should be my agent, and who should be the backup?
- Can I nominate a guardian in case one is ever needed?
- Will banks in my state accept the form, or do they require their own?
- If a parent already lacks capacity, what are the options short of full guardianship?
