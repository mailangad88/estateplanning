A simple online will can be a reasonable choice if your estate is small, your wishes are clear, and your family situation is uncomplicated. An estate planning attorney costs more, but adds judgment: spotting problems, fitting documents together, and tailoring the plan to your state's law. Having some valid will is usually better than having none.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Online will | Estate planning attorney |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to set up | Lower, often a flat fee | Higher, flat fee or hourly |
| Time to finish | Can be done in an evening | Usually a few weeks, with meetings |
| Personal advice | Limited or none | Yes, tailored to your situation |
| Handles complex situations | Limited | Yes |
| Signing guidance | Written instructions | Often supervised signing |
| Coordination with other assets | Up to you | Reviewed as part of the plan |
| Trust options | Some offer basic trusts | Full range, drafted for your goals |
| Can you change it | Yes, by making a new one | Yes, through the attorney or a new document |
| Who answers questions later | Customer support, if offered | The attorney or firm |

## When people choose an online will

An online will can be a sensible fit in situations like these:

- You are single or married with no children, and want everything to go to one person.
- Your assets are modest and mostly pass by beneficiary designation already.
- You rent your home and own no real estate.
- You have no family conflict and no beneficiary with special needs.
- You want something in place now while you save for a fuller plan.

Say you are 30, renting, with a 401(k) and a car, and you want everything to go to your sister. A well-made online will, signed correctly, may do that job. The key is following your state's signing rules exactly. See [what makes a will valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid).

Doing nothing has real costs. If you die without a will, state law picks your heirs and the court picks a guardian for minor children. Read [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## When people choose an estate attorney

An attorney tends to add the most value when something about your situation is not simple. Common examples include:

- You own a home, or property in more than one state.
- You have children from a prior relationship, or a blended family.
- A beneficiary has a disability and may rely on public benefits.
- You own a business or a share of one.
- Your estate may be large enough for estate tax planning.
- You expect someone may challenge the will.
- You want a trust that holds money for children over time.

The biggest value is often what an attorney notices. For example, a will cannot control a retirement account with an outdated beneficiary. An attorney reviewing your whole picture may catch that. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

Attorneys can also draft documents that work together, like a trust, a pour-over will, powers of attorney and healthcare directives. For blended families, this coordination matters a great deal. See [estate planning for blended families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families).

## Using both together

Some people use both at different stages. They make a basic online will now, then work with an attorney after buying a home or having children.

If you do this, keep track of which document is current. A new will usually revokes earlier ones, but the new will should say so clearly.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Is my situation simple enough that a basic will would work?
- What would you do differently from a form document, and why?
- Do you charge a flat fee, and what does it include?
- Will you supervise the signing so it meets state requirements?
- Will you review my beneficiary designations and account titles too?
- What does it cost to update the plan later?
