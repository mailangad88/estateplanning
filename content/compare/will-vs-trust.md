A will tells a court who gets your property after you die, and it usually goes through probate. A revocable living trust holds your property during your life and passes it to your beneficiaries without probate, as long as the trust is funded. A trust costs more to set up, but it can save time, money and privacy later.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Will | Revocable living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to set up | Usually lower | Usually higher, plus time to retitle assets |
| Goes through probate | Yes, for assets in your name alone | No, for assets titled in the trust |
| Privacy | Becomes a public court record in probate | Usually stays private |
| Helps if you become incapacitated | No. A will only works after death | Yes. Your successor trustee can step in |
| Names a guardian for minor children | Yes | No. A will is still used for this |
| Who controls it during your life | You | You, as trustee |
| Can you change it | Yes, any time while you have capacity | Yes, any time while you have capacity |
| Ongoing upkeep | Review every few years | Review, and title new assets into the trust |
| Property in more than one state | May need probate in each state | Can avoid probate in each state |

## How each one works

A will is a set of instructions. It does nothing until you die. Then your executor files it with the probate court, which oversees paying debts and handing out what is left. Learn more in [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).

A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement you create and usually manage yourself. You move assets into it by retitling them, for example by signing a new deed for your house. When you die or cannot manage things, a successor trustee you picked takes over without asking a court. See [revocable living trusts explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).

## When people choose a will

People often choose a will alone when their situation is simple. Common examples include:

- Most assets already pass outside probate through beneficiary designations or joint ownership.
- The estate is small enough that the state offers a simplified probate process.
- They have young children and the main goal is naming a guardian.
- They want a lower upfront cost and are comfortable with probate later.

Say you rent your home, have a retirement account with a named beneficiary, and have two young kids. A will that names a guardian and an executor may cover most of what you need. Small estate thresholds differ by state, so it helps to check yours.

## When people choose a trust

People often choose a revocable living trust when probate would be slow, costly or public. Common examples include:

- They own a home or other real estate.
- They own property in more than one state.
- They want a plan that also covers incapacity, not just death.
- They want to leave money to children or grandchildren over time, not in one lump sum.
- They have a blended family and want clear, private instructions.

Say you own a house and a small cabin in another state. With only a will, your family may face two probate cases. If both properties are titled in a trust, your successor trustee can usually handle them without court.

A trust only avoids probate for assets that are actually in it. Retitling is the step people most often skip. Read [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) for how it works.

## Using both together

Many plans use both. The trust does most of the work. A short pour-over will sends any stray assets into the trust and names a guardian for minor children.

A typical package also includes a financial power of attorney and a healthcare directive. Those documents cover decisions the trust does not, like medical care. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and [healthcare directives and living wills](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- How long and how costly is probate likely to be in my state for an estate like mine?
- Does my state have a small estate process I might qualify for?
- Which of my assets would need to be retitled into a trust?
- Who should I name as successor trustee or executor, and should I name a backup?
- How would a trust handle money for a child who is still young?
- How often should I review the plan, and what changes should prompt an update?
