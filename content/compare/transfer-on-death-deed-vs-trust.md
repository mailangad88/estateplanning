A transfer-on-death deed, sometimes called a beneficiary deed, lets you name who will receive your home when you die, without probate. You keep full ownership and control during your life. A revocable trust can do the same job and more, such as covering incapacity, but it takes more work to set up.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Transfer-on-death deed | Revocable living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to set up | Low, a deed and a recording fee | Higher, a trust document plus retitling |
| Avoids probate | Yes, for that property | Yes, for all funded assets |
| Available everywhere | No, only in states that allow it | Yes |
| Who controls it | You, until death | You, as trustee |
| Can you change it | Yes, by revoking or recording a new deed | Yes, by amending the trust |
| Handles incapacity | No | Yes, a successor trustee steps in |
| Instructions for beneficiaries | Simple: who gets it, in what shares | Detailed: timing, conditions, care for minors |
| Covers other assets | Only the property on the deed | Any asset titled in the trust |
| Privacy | Deed is recorded in public records | Trust terms usually stay private |

## How each one works

With a transfer-on-death deed, you sign and record a deed naming one or more beneficiaries. Nothing changes during your life. You can sell, refinance or revoke it. At death, the beneficiary usually records a death certificate and an affidavit to take title.

With a revocable trust, you sign a new deed moving your home into the trust. You manage it as trustee. At death or incapacity, your successor trustee takes over. See [revocable living trusts explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).

## When people choose a transfer-on-death deed

A transfer-on-death deed can be a good fit when:

- The home is the main asset, and other accounts already have beneficiaries.
- The beneficiaries are adults who can handle an inheritance directly.
- The instructions are simple, like "equally to my two children."
- Keeping costs low matters more than detailed instructions.
- The person lives in a state that allows these deeds.

Say a retired widower owns a paid-off house and has two adult daughters. His bank accounts already have payable-on-death beneficiaries. A transfer-on-death deed may let the whole estate skip probate at little cost. See [transfer on death and payable on death](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death).

## When people choose a trust

A trust is often the better fit when:

- A beneficiary is a minor, has a disability, or struggles with money.
- There are several properties, or property in more than one state.
- Incapacity planning matters, since a deed does nothing until death.
- The owner wants conditions, like holding a share until a child turns 30.
- A beneficiary may have creditors or divorce risk.

Say you want your home to go to your son, but he is 17. A transfer-on-death deed would leave property to a minor, which may require a court-supervised account. A trust can name a trustee to manage it for him. See [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Using both together

Some people use a trust for most assets and a transfer-on-death deed for one property. Others name their own trust as the deed's beneficiary, which can work in some states.

Either way, every asset should have a clear path. A home left out of both will usually go through probate. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) and [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my state allow transfer-on-death deeds, and what are the signing rules?
- How would a mortgage or a Medicaid claim affect the property after my death?
- What happens if a beneficiary dies first?
- Can I name my trust as the deed beneficiary?
- Would a deed or a trust work better if a beneficiary is a minor?
- Do I need anything else to cover incapacity?
