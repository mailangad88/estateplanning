A revocable trust lets you keep full control: you can change it, cancel it, or take assets back at any time. An irrevocable trust is generally permanent, and you give up control of the assets you put in. People accept that trade when they need something a revocable trust cannot do, like removing assets from their taxable estate.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Revocable trust | Irrevocable trust |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to set up | Moderate | Often higher, with more planning involved |
| Who controls it | You, usually as your own trustee | A trustee, often someone other than you |
| Can you change it | Yes, any time while you have capacity | Generally no, with limited exceptions |
| Avoids probate | Yes, for funded assets | Yes, for funded assets |
| Privacy | Usually private | Usually private |
| Covers incapacity | Yes, a successor trustee steps in | Yes, the trustee is already in place |
| Counts in your taxable estate | Yes | Often no, if set up for that purpose |
| Protection from your creditors | Generally none | Can offer some, depending on state law and design |
| Use in Medicaid planning | Generally not helpful | Sometimes used, subject to the look-back period |
| Tax filing | Usually reported on your own return | Depends on how it is drafted |

## What "revocable" really means

With a revocable trust, the law treats the assets as still yours. That is why it does not shield you from creditors or reduce estate taxes. It is mainly a tool for avoiding probate and planning for incapacity. See [revocable living trusts explained](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).

With an irrevocable trust, you transfer assets to a trust you no longer own or control. Because the assets are not yours anymore, they may fall outside your taxable estate. They may also be harder for your creditors to reach, depending on the state.

## When people choose a revocable trust

Most people who set up a trust start with a revocable one. Common reasons include:

- They want to avoid probate on a home or other property.
- They want a successor trustee ready if they become unable to manage money.
- They want to keep the freedom to change beneficiaries as life changes.
- Their estate is well below the federal estate tax exemption, which is $15 million per person as of 2026. <!-- verify -->

If you marry, divorce, have grandchildren or sell your house, you can simply amend a revocable trust. That flexibility is the main draw.

## When people choose an irrevocable trust

People usually turn to an irrevocable trust for a specific goal. Common examples include:

- Holding a life insurance policy so the payout stays outside the taxable estate.
- Moving assets that are expected to grow out of a large estate.
- Planning for long-term care costs well before care is needed.
- Protecting an inheritance for a beneficiary with a disability.
- Setting aside assets for a beneficiary who struggles with money.

Medicaid planning with an irrevocable trust is very timing sensitive. Most states look back 60 months at transfers when you apply for long-term care Medicaid, and California differs. <!-- verify --> Read [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) before making any transfers.

Irrevocable trusts come in many types, each with its own rules. See [irrevocable trusts explained](/guides/irrevocable-trusts-explained) for an overview.

## Using both together

Some families use both. A revocable trust holds the house and everyday accounts. A separate irrevocable trust holds a life insurance policy or assets set aside for a particular purpose.

The two trusts do different jobs. Keeping them clearly separate, with the right assets titled in each, helps avoid confusion later.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- What goal would an irrevocable trust serve for me that a revocable trust cannot?
- How much control would I give up, and who would act as trustee?
- How would this trust be taxed, and who files its return?
- Does my state allow changes to irrevocable trusts, and how?
- If long-term care is a concern, how does my state's look-back rule apply?
- What happens to the trust if a beneficiary divorces or has debts?
