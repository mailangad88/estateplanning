You can name co-executors in your will, and it is legal in every state. Whether it is a good idea depends on the people involved and how well they work together.

Our guide to [choosing an executor](/guides/choosing-an-executor) covers the role in full. Here we look only at the question of one person versus two.

## Why people name co-executors

The most common reason is fairness. A parent with two adult children may not want to signal that one is more trusted than the other. Naming both feels even.

Other reasons come up too:

- One child lives nearby and the other has financial skills.
- The estate includes a business and one person knows it well.
- You want a second set of eyes on the money.

## The trade-off is speed

An executor signs a lot of paperwork. Opening an estate bank account, selling a house, filing tax returns and paying bills all need signatures. With co-executors, banks and courts may require both.

That works fine if both live in the same town and get along. It gets harder if one lives across the country, travels for work or simply disagrees. Each delay adds to [how long probate takes](/blog/how-long-does-probate-take).

Disagreement is the bigger risk. If co-executors cannot agree on selling the house, for example, someone may need to ask the court to decide. That costs money and time.

## A short example

Walter names his two children, Grace and Leo, as co-executors. Grace lives near the house. Leo lives three time zones away.

After Walter dies, every bank form needs both signatures. Leo has to print, sign and mail documents each time. The house sale slips by two months because the buyer's title company needs original signatures from both. Nothing went wrong, but everything took longer.

## Common alternatives

Many families find a middle path that keeps things fair without slowing them down.

| Approach | How it works | Good fit when |
|---|---|---|
| One executor plus a backup | One person serves. A second steps in only if needed. | You trust one person to share information. |
| Co-executors who may act alone | The will lets either sign on routine matters. | Both are capable and get along well. |
| One executor with a duty to report | The will asks the executor to share regular updates. | You want openness without shared control. |
| A professional executor | A bank, trust company or attorney serves. | Family tension is high or the estate is complex. |

Whether a will can let co-executors act alone depends on your state. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you what wording works.

## Questions worth asking yourself

Before naming two people, think through a few practical points.

1. Do they live near each other, or near your home?
2. Have they worked together well before, on anything with money?
3. Would one of them do most of the work anyway?
4. Would naming only one actually cause hurt feelings, or are you guessing?

Talking with your children can answer that last question fast. Our post on [talking to your parents about their estate plan](/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-parents-about-their-estate-plan) has tips that work in both directions.
