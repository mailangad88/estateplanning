A pour-over will is a backup will that goes with a living trust. In short, it says "anything still in my name when I die goes into my trust."

It exists because almost nobody moves every asset into their trust. Our guide to [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) covers how to move assets in. The pour-over will catches what slips through.

## What a pour-over will does

A pour-over will usually does three things:

1. **Sends leftover assets to your trust.** Your trust's terms then decide who gets them.
2. **Names an executor** to handle anything that has to go through probate.
3. **Names a guardian** for minor children, which a trust usually cannot do.

The trust stays the main plan. The will points back to it. Our [pour-over will vs simple will comparison](/compare/pour-over-will-vs-simple-will) shows how the two differ side by side.

## What a pour-over will does not do

A pour-over will does not avoid probate on what it covers. Say you leave a bank account in your own name. That account may need probate before it can "pour" into the trust.

It also does not override beneficiary designations. A retirement account that names your brother still goes to your brother. Our post on [whether retirement accounts go through probate](/blog/do-retirement-accounts-go-through-probate) explains why.

## A short example

Ben sets up a trust and moves his house and brokerage account into it. Two years later he buys a boat and opens a new savings account in his own name. He forgets to retitle either one.

When Ben dies, his pour-over will sends the boat and savings into his trust. His executor first takes them through a short probate. Then they join the trust, and his trustee distributes everything under one set of instructions.

## Why it matters even if your trust is funded

Even careful people end up with stray assets. Common ones include:

- A car or boat bought after the trust was signed.
- A tax refund or final paycheck paid after death.
- An inheritance you received late in life.
- A lawsuit settlement or insurance payout owed to you.

Without a pour-over will, those assets would pass under your state's intestacy rules, which may not match your trust at all. See [how long probate takes](/blog/how-long-does-probate-take) for what the leftover assets might face.

## Keeping the pour-over will small

The goal is for the pour-over will to catch as little as possible. People who use a trust usually:

- Retitle the house. See [should I put my house in a trust](/blog/should-i-put-my-house-in-a-trust).
- Retitle bank and brokerage accounts, or add the trust as payable-on-death beneficiary where allowed.
- Review accounts once a year with the [annual estate plan review](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review).

## How it differs from a regular will

A simple will spells out who gets each asset. A pour-over will usually says very little about that. It just points everything to the trust, and the trust holds the details.

That design keeps your plan in one place. If you change who inherits, you update the trust, not the will. The trust is also private in most states, while a will that goes through probate becomes a public record.

## It depends on your state

Signing rules for a will, small estate limits, and how probate handles pour-over assets vary by state. A pour-over will must meet the same signing rules as any other will. Our guide to [what makes a will valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid) explains those basics.
