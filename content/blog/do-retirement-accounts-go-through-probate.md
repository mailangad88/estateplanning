Usually not. Retirement accounts like 401(k)s and IRAs pass to the person named on the account's beneficiary form. They skip probate and go straight to that person.

But that only works if the form is filled out and up to date. Our guide to [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) covers the forms in detail. This post explains when retirement accounts do end up in probate, and how people avoid it.

## How the beneficiary form works

When you open a retirement account, you name a primary beneficiary and often a contingent, or backup, beneficiary. When you die, the account custodian pays whoever the form names.

The form is a contract with the custodian. It controls even if your will says something different. Our [beneficiary designation vs will comparison](/compare/beneficiary-designation-vs-will) explains why.

## When a retirement account does go through probate

A retirement account can end up in probate in a few common situations:

- **No beneficiary was ever named.** Many plans then pay the estate.
- **All named beneficiaries died before you.** See [what happens if a beneficiary dies before you](/blog/what-happens-if-a-beneficiary-dies-before-you).
- **You named your estate** as beneficiary.
- **The custodian lost the form** and has no record.

When the estate inherits, the account goes through probate and is distributed under your will or state intestacy law. It may also lose some tax advantages beneficiaries could have had.

## A short example

Marie has an IRA and a 401(k). She named her husband on the 401(k) years ago, but he has since died. She never named a backup. Her IRA lists her two daughters.

When Marie dies, the IRA goes straight to her daughters. The 401(k) has no living beneficiary, so the plan pays it to her estate. Her daughters still get it under her will, but only after probate, and with fewer tax choices.

## Spouse rights in employer plans

Many employer retirement plans give a spouse rights to the account under federal law. Naming someone other than your spouse often requires your spouse's signed consent. IRAs work differently, and some states add their own rules.

If you are married and want to name someone else, ask the plan administrator what is required.

## Retirement accounts and creditors

Retirement accounts passed to a named beneficiary are generally kept out of the probate estate. That often means they are not used to pay the deceased person's ordinary debts. See [what happens to debt when someone dies](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) for details.

## Keeping retirement accounts out of probate

Common steps include:

- Name a primary and a contingent beneficiary on every account.
- Update forms after marriage, divorce, a birth or a death.
- Get a written confirmation from the custodian.
- Consider a trust as beneficiary for minors. Our guide to [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) explains why.

## Naming minors on a retirement account

Some parents name their young children directly on a retirement account. That can cause trouble. Most custodians will not pay a large sum to a minor. A court may need to appoint a guardian of the money, which is the kind of process the beneficiary form was meant to avoid.

Common alternatives include naming a trust for the children, or naming a custodian under your state's transfers to minors law. Each has its own tax and timing rules. If you have young children, this is worth raising with an attorney and a tax advisor.

## It depends on your state

Federal law controls many employer plans, while state law affects IRAs, divorce rules and probate. An attorney licensed in your state can help you check how these rules apply to your accounts.
