Simple probate often takes several months to about a year from start to finish. Complicated or contested estates can take two years or longer.

Our guide to [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works) explains each stage. Here we focus on timing: what sets the clock and what slows it down.

## The stages and rough timing

Probate follows a similar path in most states, even though the details differ.

| Stage | What happens | Typical pace |
|---|---|---|
| Filing | Executor files the will and asks to be appointed. | A few weeks |
| Notice to creditors | Creditors get a set window to file claims. | Often a few months, set by state law |
| Inventory | Executor lists and values all assets. | Weeks to months |
| Paying debts and taxes | Bills, final tax returns, estate tax if any. | Months, depends on taxes |
| Distribution and closing | Heirs receive their shares, court closes the case. | Weeks to months |

The creditor window is the one step nobody can rush. Your state sets it. Everything else depends heavily on the executor and the court.

## What slows probate down

A few things add the most time:

- **A will contest.** A dispute over the will can add a year or more.
- **Real estate.** Selling a house, especially one in another state, takes time.
- **Missing paperwork.** Hunting for accounts, titles and passwords stalls the inventory.
- **Hard-to-find heirs.** Every heir usually needs notice.
- **Court backlogs.** Some counties are much busier than others.
- **Co-executors who must both sign.** See [should you name co-executors](/blog/should-you-name-co-executors-in-your-will).

## A short example

Carol's mother dies with a will, a house and two bank accounts. Carol files for probate in month one. Her state's creditor period runs about four months. She lists the house in month three and it sells in month six.

She files her mother's final tax return, pays the last bills and distributes the money in month nine. The court closes the case in month ten. That is a fairly typical pace for a simple estate.

## Ways people shorten or skip probate

Probate only covers assets in the person's name alone without a beneficiary. Many people plan so less goes through the court:

- **A funded revocable trust.** Assets in the trust skip probate. See our guide to the [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).
- **Beneficiary designations** on retirement accounts and life insurance.
- **Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death** registrations.
- **Joint ownership**, though it has trade-offs. Our [joint ownership vs trust comparison](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust) explains them.

If a trust is your plan, a [pour-over will](/blog/what-is-a-pour-over-will-and-why-do-trusts-have-one) catches anything left out, but those assets may still need probate. Very small estates may qualify for a faster process. See [can you skip probate for a small estate](/blog/can-you-skip-probate-for-a-small-estate).

## It depends on your state

Some states have a simpler, less supervised probate track. Others require more court steps. Creditor deadlines also vary. An attorney licensed in the state where the person lived can give a realistic timeline for that county.
