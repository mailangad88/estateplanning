The best way to start is with care and a practical question, not with money. Ask "Where would I find your papers if something happened?" That lands better than "Who gets the house?"

Our guide to [what estate planning is](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning) explains the documents your parents may already have. This post focuses on how to raise the subject and what to ask.

## Pick a natural opening

Many families find the talk easier when it starts from something that already happened:

- A friend or neighbour who went through a hard illness or death.
- A news story about a family fight over an estate.
- Your own planning. "We just did our wills and it made me wonder about yours."
- A move, retirement or new grandchild.

Choose a calm time. Holidays with the whole family present can feel like an ambush.

## Keep it about their wishes

Your parents may worry you are asking about your inheritance. Make it clear you are asking because you want to carry out their wishes. Good questions include:

1. Do you have a will or trust? Where is it?
2. Who have you named to handle money or medical decisions if you cannot?
3. Is there anything you want us to know about your care?
4. Where are your accounts, and how do we reach your advisors?
5. Have you written down any wishes about your funeral?

Our [funeral and burial wishes checklist](/checklists/funeral-and-burial-wishes) and [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list) give structure to the last two.

## Listen more than you talk

Your parents may have made choices you would not make. They may have named a sibling as executor or split things unevenly. Try to understand their reasons before reacting.

If they want help updating their plan, offer to find an attorney or drive them to a meeting. Let them meet with the attorney alone. That protects the plan from claims of undue influence.

## A short example

After a friend's father died without a will, Leah calls her mom, Beverly. She mentions how hard it was for her friend and asks if Beverly has a plan. Beverly says she has a will from twenty years ago.

They agree to meet the next weekend. Beverly finds the old will. It still names Leah's late uncle as executor. Beverly decides to update it, and books her own appointment. Leah helps her fill out an [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) beforehand.

## Practical documents to ask about

A full plan often includes a will or trust, a financial power of attorney, a healthcare directive and a HIPAA release. Our guides to [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and [healthcare directives](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) explain each one.

If you are named as agent, it helps to know what that role allows. See [can a power of attorney change a will](/blog/can-a-power-of-attorney-change-a-will). If you will be one of two co-executors, read [should you name co-executors in your will](/blog/should-you-name-co-executors-in-your-will) together.

## Things that tend to backfire

A few approaches tend to make parents close the door. Try to avoid:

- Bringing up the subject right after a funeral or diagnosis, when emotions are raw.
- Asking about specific amounts or who gets what.
- Showing up with a sibling group that feels like a team against them.
- Pushing a specific attorney or plan before they are ready.
- Treating one conversation as the end of it.

Patience usually works better. If the first talk ends early, leave it open. Many parents come back to the topic weeks later, once they have had time to think.

## It depends on your state

Signing rules, healthcare forms and who can act without documents all vary by state. If your parents live in a different state from you, their plan should follow their state's rules.
