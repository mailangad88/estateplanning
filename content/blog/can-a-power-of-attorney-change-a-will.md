No. Someone acting under your power of attorney cannot change your will. Making or changing a will is personal. Only you can do it, and only while you have the mental capacity to do so.

That said, an agent can sometimes take actions that change what your will ends up giving away. Our guide to [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) explains the document in full. This post looks at where those lines are drawn.

## What an agent cannot do

In general, an agent under a financial power of attorney cannot:

- Write, sign or revoke your will.
- Make a new will after you lose capacity.
- Act after you die. The power ends at death. See [what happens to a power of attorney when you die](/blog/what-happens-to-a-power-of-attorney-when-you-die).

If you lose capacity without a will, a court may have limited ways to step in, but your agent does not.

## What an agent may be able to do

The bigger risk is not changing the will itself. It is changing what the will controls. Depending on the document and your state, an agent may be able to:

- Sell your house to pay for care.
- Spend savings on your bills.
- Make gifts to family members.
- Change beneficiaries on accounts or create a trust.
- Move assets between accounts.

Many states treat the riskier powers, like gifts and beneficiary changes, as "hot powers." The document has to grant them clearly and specifically.

## A short example

Dorothy's will leaves her house to her son Michael and her savings to her daughter Kim. Kim is her agent. When Dorothy moves into assisted living, Kim sells the house to pay for care, as the document allows.

After Dorothy dies, the house is gone. Michael's gift fails because it no longer exists. Kim did nothing wrong. But the family had not talked about this in advance, and it caused hurt feelings.

Some wills say what should happen if a gifted asset is sold. Some states also have rules that protect the gift when an agent sells it. This is worth asking about.

## Ways people protect their plan

If you worry about an agent changing your plan, a few choices can help:

- **Limit gifting powers** to small amounts or specific people.
- **Require reports** from your agent to another family member.
- **Name co-agents** who must act together on large decisions.
- **Keep your will and power of attorney consistent.** An attorney can draft both with the same goals.
- **Talk with your family.** Our post on [talking to your parents about their estate plan](/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-parents-about-their-estate-plan) can help start the conversation.

A power of attorney also does not replace a guardianship in every case. Our [power of attorney vs guardianship comparison](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains when each applies.

## Signs an agent may be overstepping

Most agents act in good faith. But family members sometimes notice warning signs, such as:

- Large gifts to the agent or the agent's family.
- New beneficiary forms that favour the agent.
- Accounts moved into joint names with the agent.
- The agent refusing to share any records.

None of these prove wrongdoing on their own. An agent may have clear authority and good reasons. But they are worth a calm question, and sometimes a conversation with an attorney. In many states, adult protective services also takes reports about suspected financial abuse of older adults.

## It depends on your state

States differ on which powers must be spelled out, how agents must report, and what happens to a gift in a will when an agent sells the asset. An attorney licensed in your state can draft a document that matches your wishes.
