In many cases, yes. Most states have a shortcut for small estates that skips full probate or uses a much shorter version.

Our guide to [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works) covers the full court process. This post explains the shortcut, who can use it, and where people run into trouble.

## Two common small estate methods

States usually offer one or both of these:

**Small estate affidavit.** An heir signs a sworn statement saying the estate is under the limit and they are entitled to the property. They give it to the bank or other holder, along with a death certificate. No court case is opened in many states.

**Summary or simplified probate.** A short court process with fewer steps, less paperwork and usually a faster finish than regular probate.

The name and the form differ from state to state. Our [glossary](/glossary#small-estate-affidavit) has a short definition.

## What counts toward the limit

Each state sets a dollar limit for what counts as a small estate. It is usually based only on probate assets, the ones in the person's name alone with no beneficiary.

Assets that usually do **not** count include:

- Life insurance with a named beneficiary.
- Retirement accounts with a named beneficiary. See [do retirement accounts go through probate](/blog/do-retirement-accounts-go-through-probate).
- Joint accounts with right of survivorship.
- Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts.
- Property held in a trust.

So a person can leave a lot of total wealth and still have a small probate estate. Our [probate vs non-probate assets comparison](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains how to sort them.

## Common limits on the shortcut

Even when an estate is small, the shortcut may come with conditions:

- A waiting period after death, often a month or more.
- Real estate may be excluded or need a separate process.
- All heirs may need to agree or sign.
- No executor may already have been appointed.

## A short example

James dies leaving a checking account, an old car and a life insurance policy that names his niece, Tara. The insurance pays Tara directly. The checking account and car are under his state's small estate limit.

After the waiting period, Tara signs the affidavit and brings it to the bank with a death certificate. The bank releases the balance. She uses a similar form at the motor vehicle office for the car. No court case is needed.

## When a small estate still needs probate

Full probate may still be needed if:

- The person owned a house in their name alone, in some states.
- Heirs disagree about who is entitled to what.
- Creditors claim more than the estate can pay.
- Someone needs court authority to sue or settle a claim.

If you are dealing with a parent's accounts, read [what to do with a parent's bank account after death](/blog/what-to-do-with-a-parents-bank-account-after-death) for the step-by-step.

## It depends on your state

Small estate limits, waiting periods and forms vary widely. Some states update their limits from time to time. Check your state court website or ask an attorney licensed in your state before signing an affidavit.
