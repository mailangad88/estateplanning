For many homeowners, yes. Putting a house in a revocable living trust is one of the most common reasons people set up a trust in the first place.

But a trust only works for property you actually move into it. Our guide to [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) explains that step for every type of asset. This post covers the house.

## Why people put a house in a trust

A house in your name alone usually goes through probate when you die. If you own property in more than one state, your family may face probate in each state.

A trust can help in a few ways:

- **Skips probate.** The house passes to your beneficiaries under the trust's terms.
- **Plans for incapacity.** If you cannot manage your affairs, your successor trustee can handle the house without a court guardianship.
- **Keeps things private.** Trust transfers generally do not become public court records the way probate does.
- **Gives you control over timing.** A trust can hold the house for a child until a set age.

Our guide to the [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) explains these benefits in more detail.

## How the transfer works

Funding a house usually means signing and recording a new deed. The deed moves ownership from you to you as trustee of your trust.

1. Your attorney prepares a deed naming your trust as the owner.
2. You sign it in front of a notary.
3. The deed is recorded with the county.
4. You update your homeowner's insurance to list the trust.
5. You check whether your county needs a form to keep your property tax exemption.

Nothing changes day to day. You still live there, pay the bills and can sell or refinance. Our [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist) walks through each step.

## A short example

Linda owns a home in Arizona and a cabin in Colorado. She sets up a revocable trust and records new deeds for both properties. When she later has a stroke, her daughter takes over as successor trustee and rents out the cabin to pay for care.

When Linda dies, her daughter transfers both properties to the beneficiaries. There is no probate in either state.

## When people choose something else

A trust is not the only option. Some people pick a simpler tool:

- **Transfer-on-death deed.** Available in many states. It passes the house at death without probate, but does not help with incapacity. Our [transfer-on-death deed vs trust comparison](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust) lays out the trade-offs.
- **Joint ownership with right of survivorship.** Common for married couples, but it can create problems with children from other relationships. See [joint ownership vs trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust).

If you set up a trust but never record the deed, the house may end up passing through your [pour-over will](/blog/what-is-a-pour-over-will-and-why-do-trusts-have-one) and probate anyway. Once the house is in the trust, your trustee will have [ongoing yearly tasks](/blog/what-does-a-trustee-actually-do-each-year) after you die.

## Common mistakes with a house in trust

People who set up a trust sometimes trip over the same few issues:

- **Never recording the deed.** The trust exists, but the house is still in your name.
- **Refinancing out of the trust.** Some lenders ask you to move the house out to refinance. People forget to move it back.
- **Forgetting insurance.** The policy should list the trust as an insured or additional interest.
- **Buying a new home later** and titling it in your own name.

A quick check of your deed at each [annual estate plan review](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review) can catch these.

## It depends on your state

Deed forms, recording fees, transfer tax exemptions, homestead rules and whether a transfer-on-death deed is allowed all vary by state. An attorney licensed where the property sits should prepare or review the deed.
