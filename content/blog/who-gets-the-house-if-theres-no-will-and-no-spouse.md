If there is no will and no spouse, the house goes to your closest relatives under your state's intestacy law. In most states that means your children first, in equal shares.

Our guide to [what happens if you die without a will](/guides/what-happens-if-you-die-without-a-will) explains intestacy in full. This post walks through the house specifically, because it is often the biggest asset and the hardest to split.

## The usual order of inheritance

States set their own rules, but most follow a similar order when there is no surviving spouse:

1. **Children**, in equal shares. If a child has died, that child's own children often take their parent's share.
2. **Parents**, if there are no children or grandchildren.
3. **Siblings**, and the children of siblings who have died.
4. **Grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins**, in an order set by state law.
5. **The state**, if no relatives can be found. This is rare.

Stepchildren usually do not inherit under these rules unless they were legally adopted. The legal term is [intestate succession](/glossary#intestate-succession).

## How the house actually changes hands

Before anyone inherits, the house usually goes through [probate](/guides/how-probate-works). A court appoints an administrator, often a family member, to handle the estate. That person deals with the mortgage, pays debts and eventually transfers title.

Two things can skip this process. If the house was owned jointly with right of survivorship, it passes to the co-owner. If there was a [transfer-on-death deed](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death), it goes to the named beneficiary. Our [probate vs non-probate assets comparison](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the difference.

## A short example

Helen dies at 78. She was widowed and had no will. Her house is in her name alone. She has three adult children, and one son, Paul, died years ago leaving two kids of his own.

Under her state's law, the house is split into three shares. Her two living children each get one third. Paul's children share his third. Now five people co-own a house, and they need to agree on whether to sell, rent or buy each other out.

## Common problems with shared inheritance

When several heirs inherit one house, a few issues come up again and again:

- One heir lives in the house and others want it sold.
- An heir cannot be found or will not sign.
- Nobody wants to keep paying the mortgage, taxes and insurance during probate.
- Minor grandchildren inherit a share and need a court-supervised guardian for the money.

These problems are why many people with a home use a will, trust or deed to say exactly who gets it. Our post on [putting your house in a trust](/blog/should-i-put-my-house-in-a-trust) covers one option.

If a mortgage is still owed, read [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/blog/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-when-the-owner-dies).

## What the administrator does with the house

The court-appointed administrator has several jobs with the house during probate. They usually change the locks, keep insurance active, and pay the mortgage, taxes and utilities from estate funds. They may also arrange an appraisal so everyone knows its value.

If the heirs agree to sell, the administrator often handles the sale with court approval where required. If one heir wants to keep it, a buyout can sometimes be arranged. Our [executor vs trustee comparison](/compare/executor-vs-trustee) explains how this role differs from a trustee's.

## It depends on your state

Intestacy rules differ in the details: how shares pass down family lines, whether half-siblings take equally, and whether stepchildren ever qualify. An attorney licensed in the state where the house sits can confirm who the legal heirs are.
