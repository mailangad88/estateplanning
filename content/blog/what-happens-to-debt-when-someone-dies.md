Debts do not vanish when someone dies. They are paid from the estate, which is the money and property the person left. Heirs receive what remains after valid debts are paid.

Family members usually do not inherit the debt itself. Our guide to [settling an estate step by step](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step) shows where paying debts fits in the executor's work.

## Who is responsible for what

The executor handles debts using estate money. The executor does not pay from their own pocket.

You may be personally responsible only in a few situations:

- You co-signed the loan or credit card.
- You were a joint account holder, not just an authorized user.
- You are a surviving spouse in a state where spouses share some debts.
- You took estate property before debts were paid, in some states.

If none of these apply, a collector cannot make you pay. Many families feel pressure to pay out of guilt. That is a choice, not a requirement.

## The order debts are paid

States set a priority list for paying debts when money is tight. A common order looks like this:

1. Costs of running the estate and funeral expenses.
2. Certain family allowances, in some states.
3. Taxes owed to federal and state governments.
4. Medical bills from the final illness.
5. Other unsecured debts, like credit cards.

The details vary by state. An executor who pays a low-priority debt first may be personally on the hook if money runs out. That is one reason executors often wait for the creditor claim period to end.

## Secured debt stays with the property

Secured debts like mortgages and car loans are tied to the property. Whoever keeps the house or car usually takes on the loan or sells the property to pay it off. See [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/blog/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-when-the-owner-dies).

## Assets creditors usually cannot reach

Many assets pass outside the estate and are generally protected from the deceased person's ordinary creditors:

- Life insurance paid to a named beneficiary.
- Retirement accounts with a named beneficiary. Read [do retirement accounts go through probate](/blog/do-retirement-accounts-go-through-probate).
- Property held jointly with right of survivorship, in many cases.

Rules vary by state, especially for payable-on-death accounts. Our [probate vs non-probate assets comparison](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the split.

## A short example

When Ruth dies, she has a checking account, a paid-off car, and two credit cards with balances. Her son Aaron is executor. He sells the car, pays her funeral and final medical bill, and then has a little left for the cards.

He pays the cards what the estate can cover under state priority rules. The rest goes unpaid. Aaron never pays a cent of his own, and he sends the card companies a letter saying the estate is insolvent.

## It depends on your state

Creditor deadlines, priority order and spouse liability all depend on state law. Community property states treat spouses differently. If debts are larger than the estate, speak with an attorney licensed in your state before paying anyone.
