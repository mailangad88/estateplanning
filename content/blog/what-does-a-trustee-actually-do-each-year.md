A trustee's yearly work comes down to five things: invest, record, report, pay taxes and distribute. The details depend on the trust, but the rhythm is similar for most.

Our guide to [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) helps you pick the right person. This post shows what that person actually signs up for, year after year.

## A trustee's year at a glance

| Task | How often | What it involves |
|---|---|---|
| Review investments | At least yearly, often quarterly | Check the mix still fits the trust's goals. |
| Keep records | Ongoing | Track every deposit, payment and transfer. |
| Pay bills | Ongoing | Property taxes, insurance, repairs, advisor fees. |
| Make distributions | As the trust says | Regular payments, or decisions on requests. |
| File tax returns | Yearly | Trust income tax return, plus forms for beneficiaries. |
| Report to beneficiaries | Usually yearly | A statement of what the trust holds and did. |

These duties come from the trust document and from state law. Our [glossary entry on fiduciary duty](/glossary#fiduciary) explains the standard a trustee is held to.

## Investing with care

Most states expect a trustee to invest like a careful person would for someone else. That usually means spreading money across different investments and keeping both current and future beneficiaries in mind.

Many family trustees hire a financial advisor for this. The trustee still decides and stays responsible.

## Records and reports

Good records are the trustee's best protection. If a beneficiary ever questions a decision, the records show what happened and why.

Many states require a yearly report to beneficiaries. It often lists what the trust owns, income received, expenses paid and distributions made.

## Taxes

A trust that has become irrevocable usually files its own income tax return each year. The trustee also sends tax forms to beneficiaries who received income. An accountant who knows trusts can be worth the fee.

## Distributions and requests

Some trusts pay a set amount on a schedule. Others let the trustee decide. A common standard is money for health, education, support and maintenance.

When a beneficiary asks for money, the trustee looks at the trust's terms, the request, and the trust's long-term health. Saying no is sometimes part of the job.

## A short example

After her father dies, Joanne becomes trustee of a trust for her younger brother, Sam. Each January she gathers statements and sends them to the accountant. In March the trust files its tax return and Sam gets his tax form.

In June, Sam asks for money toward a car for a new job. Joanne checks the trust terms, which allow support. She approves it and notes the reason. In December, she meets the advisor and mails Sam a yearly summary.

## Trustee vs executor

An executor's job usually ends when the estate closes. A trustee's job can last for years. Our [executor vs trustee comparison](/compare/executor-vs-trustee) explains the difference. If your trustee will also be a beneficiary, see [can a beneficiary also be the trustee](/blog/can-a-beneficiary-also-be-the-trustee).

Trusts that hold a house add property duties like insurance and upkeep. See [should I put my house in a trust](/blog/should-i-put-my-house-in-a-trust).

## It depends on your state

Reporting rules, investment standards and trustee pay vary by state and by the trust document. A trustee who is unsure can ask an attorney licensed in the state that governs the trust.
