Yes. Each parent writes their own will, and each will can name a different guardian. Your husband cannot stop you from naming your sister in yours.

What matters more is what happens in practice. Our guide to [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children) covers the full picture. Here is how a disagreement between spouses actually plays out.

## The surviving parent usually comes first

A guardian named in a will does not take over just because one parent dies. If you die and your husband is alive and able, he will almost always keep custody. That is true no matter who your will names.

Your guardian choice mostly matters in one situation: both of you die, or the surviving parent cannot care for the children. The legal term for the person you name is guardian of the person. Our [glossary](/glossary#guardian) explains the different types.

## When both wills name different people

If you and your husband both die, and his will names his brother while yours names your sister, a court has to decide. The judge looks at both wills as evidence of what you wanted. The judge also looks at the children's best interests.

Factors courts commonly weigh include:

- Which person the children already know well.
- Where each person lives, and whether the children would change schools.
- Each person's health, age and home situation.
- The wishes of older children, in many states.

Two conflicting wills can mean a slower, more stressful process for your children. That is the main reason people try to reach agreement.

## A short example

Priya wants her sister Anjali as guardian. Her husband Mark prefers his parents. They talk it through. Mark's parents are in their seventies. Anjali has kids the same age and lives ten minutes away.

They agree on Anjali for the children. They name Mark's father as trustee of the money left for the kids. Both families have a role, and the court would see two wills that match.

## Ways couples settle the question

If you are stuck, these approaches often help:

- **Split the roles.** One person raises the children. Another manages the money. Our guide to [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors) explains how that works.
- **Write down your reasons.** A short letter explaining your choice can help a court if you cannot agree.
- **Pick a first choice and an alternate.** Your sister could be first and his choice second, or the other way around.
- **Use a worksheet together.** Scoring each candidate on the same points can move the talk away from loyalty.

## When the other parent cannot take over

There are a few cases where the surviving parent may not get custody. A court may look elsewhere if the other parent:

- Has had their parental rights ended by a court.
- Has a serious history of abuse or neglect.
- Is unable to care for the children because of illness or incarceration.
- Has been absent from the children's lives for a long time, in some states.

If you worry about your husband's ability to care for the children, the guardian question becomes more serious. A letter explaining your concerns, kept with your will, can help a court. This is a situation where talking to an attorney licensed in your state is worth it.

## It depends on your state

States differ on how much weight a will's guardian choice gets, at what age a child's preference counts, and how a court handles two conflicting nominations. Some states also let you sign a separate guardian designation outside your will.

If you and your spouse disagree, a short consult with an attorney licensed in your state can help you understand what a court there would likely do.
