If a beneficiary dies before you, their gift does not go to their estate automatically. What happens next depends on what your document says and on your state's law.

This is one of the most common gaps in estate plans. Our guide to [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) covers forms in general. Here we look at what happens to a share when the person dies first.

## Wills and trusts

In a will or trust, a gift to someone who dies before you is said to "lapse." There are a few common outcomes.

1. **Your document names a backup.** The backup inherits. This is the clearest result.
2. **Your document says what to do,** such as "to my children who survive me, in equal shares." The surviving children share it.
3. **Your state's anti-lapse law applies.** Many states send the share to the beneficiary's children if the beneficiary was a close relative.
4. **None of the above applies.** The share often falls into the rest of your estate.

Anti-lapse rules vary a lot by state. Our [glossary](/glossary#anti-lapse-statute) has a short definition.

## Beneficiary forms

Life insurance, retirement accounts and payable-on-death accounts follow the form, not your will. If your primary beneficiary dies first:

- The **contingent** beneficiary inherits, if you named one.
- If several primaries were named, the survivors may share it, depending on the form.
- If you chose **per stirpes**, the share may go to the deceased person's children.
- If no one qualifies, the account often pays your estate, which means probate.

See [do retirement accounts go through probate](/blog/do-retirement-accounts-go-through-probate) for what that means.

## A short example

Ray names his three children equally on his life insurance policy. His daughter Lisa dies a few years before him, leaving two young sons. Ray never updates the form.

The policy says the survivors share equally. So Ray's two living children each get half. Lisa's sons get nothing from the policy. Had Ray checked the per stirpes box, Lisa's third would have gone to her sons. Because they are minors, it would likely be held by a trust or custodian.

Our guide to [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors) explains why that last step matters.

## Simultaneous deaths

Sometimes a couple dies in the same accident. Many states have rules that treat a beneficiary as having died first unless they outlived you by a set period. Wills and trusts often add their own survival period. This avoids property passing through two estates in a row.

## Ways people plan for this

A few simple habits help:

- Name a contingent beneficiary on every account.
- Decide whether a deceased child's share should go to their children.
- Use clear wording in wills and trusts, like "to my descendants, per stirpes."
- Review forms after any death in the family.

Our [beneficiary designation vs will comparison](/compare/beneficiary-designation-vs-will) explains why both need attention.

## Check forms after every family change

Lapsed gifts often happen because a form was filled out once and forgotten. Life insurance from an old job, a small IRA or a savings bond can sit untouched for decades.

A death in the family is a natural time to check. So are marriage, divorce, a birth or a move. Make a list of every account with a beneficiary, note who is named as primary and contingent, and fix gaps. Many custodians let you update forms online in a few minutes. Ask for written confirmation, and keep a copy with your estate plan.

## It depends on your state

Anti-lapse laws, survival periods and how custodians handle lapsed gifts all vary. An attorney licensed in your state can check how your current documents would work.
