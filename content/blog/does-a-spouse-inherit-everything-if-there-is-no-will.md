Not always. Many people assume their husband or wife gets everything automatically. In a lot of states that is only true if there are no children, or if every child belongs to both spouses.

Our guide to [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains intestacy in general. Here we focus on the spouse's share.

## Common patterns across states

Each state writes its own intestacy law. Most fall into one of a few patterns:

| Family situation | What many states do |
|---|---|
| Spouse, no children, no parents | Spouse takes everything. |
| Spouse and children who are all from this marriage | Spouse takes everything in some states. Others split with the children. |
| Spouse and children from another relationship | Spouse takes a set amount or fraction. Children share the rest. |
| Spouse, no children, surviving parents | Spouse may share with the parents in some states. |

These are broad patterns, not rules you can rely on. The exact split depends on your state.

## Community property states work differently

A handful of states treat most property earned during marriage as owned half by each spouse. In those states, the surviving spouse already owns half of that community property. Intestacy only decides what happens to the other half and any separate property.

If you live in or moved from a community property state, the details matter. An attorney licensed there can explain them.

## What intestacy does not control

Intestacy rules only reach property in the deceased person's name alone. A lot of what married couples own passes another way:

- Jointly owned homes and bank accounts pass to the surviving owner.
- Life insurance and retirement accounts go to the named beneficiary. See [do retirement accounts go through probate](/blog/do-retirement-accounts-go-through-probate).
- Accounts with [payable-on-death](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) designations go to the named person.

So a spouse may receive most of the family's wealth even if the intestacy share is small. Or the reverse, if an old beneficiary form still names an ex. Our [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit) helps catch that.

## A short example

Tom and Rachel are married. Tom has a son, Eli, from his first marriage. Tom dies without a will. His car and a brokerage account are in his name alone.

In Tom's state, Rachel receives a fixed dollar amount plus half of the rest. Eli receives the other half. Rachel is surprised. She had assumed everything would come to her. A simple will would have let Tom decide the split himself.

Blended families face this most often. Our guide to [estate planning for blended families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families) covers ways to balance a spouse and children from earlier relationships.

## Why a simple will often helps married couples

Many married couples assume they do not need a will because everything is joint. That can work until the second spouse dies, or until both die together. Then the joint property passes under intestacy rules too.

A will lets you decide who inherits after both of you are gone. It also lets you name a guardian for minor children and an executor you trust. For blended families, it lets each spouse balance the needs of a partner and children from an earlier relationship. Our guide on [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will) explains the steps.

## It depends on your state

How much a spouse inherits without a will is one of the most state-specific rules in estate law. Your state may also give spouses extra rights, like a homestead right in the family home. Check with an attorney licensed in your state.
