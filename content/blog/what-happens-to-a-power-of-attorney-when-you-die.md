A power of attorney stops working the moment you die. It is a tool for helping you while you are alive, not for settling your estate.

Many families learn this at a hard moment, often at the bank. Our guide to [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) explains how the document works during your life. This post covers what happens after.

## Why it ends at death

A power of attorney lets your agent act in your place. Once you die, there is no one for the agent to act for. Your property now belongs to your estate, your trust, or the people you named as beneficiaries.

This is true of financial powers of attorney and healthcare powers of attorney alike. A healthcare agent may sometimes have a limited role after death, like authorizing an autopsy or organ donation, if state law allows.

## Who takes over

| Asset | Who handles it after death |
|---|---|
| Accounts in your name alone | Your executor, once appointed by the court |
| Property in your trust | Your successor trustee |
| Accounts with a named beneficiary | The beneficiary collects directly |
| Joint accounts with survivorship | The surviving owner |

Our [probate vs non-probate assets comparison](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains why each asset goes where it does. The [executor vs trustee comparison](/compare/executor-vs-trustee) covers who does what.

## What the agent should and should not do

After a death, a former agent should generally:

- Stop using the account, debit card and checkbook.
- Keep records of everything they did as agent.
- Hand those records and any property to the executor or trustee.
- Tell the bank and other institutions about the death.

They should not write checks for bills, even routine ones. Doing so can create problems later, even with good intentions.

## A short example

Kevin had been paying his father's bills for two years as his agent. His father dies on a Tuesday. On Wednesday, Kevin tries to pay the electric bill from his father's account. The bank tells him the power of attorney is no longer valid.

Kevin's sister is the named executor. She opens probate and an estate account. Kevin gives her two years of records, which makes her inventory much faster. The electric bill gets paid from the estate a few weeks later.

## The gap between death and appointment

There is often a gap of weeks between a death and an executor's appointment. During that time, no one may have authority over accounts in the person's name alone.

Planning can shrink that gap. Common tools include payable-on-death designations, joint accounts and a funded revocable trust. A successor trustee can act right away, without waiting for a court. Our guide to [settling an estate step by step](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step) explains what happens next. For bank accounts specifically, see [what to do with a parent's bank account after death](/blog/what-to-do-with-a-parents-bank-account-after-death).

## Records a former agent should hand over

A former agent's records help the executor work faster and protect the agent from questions later. Useful items include:

- Bank and credit card statements for the period they served.
- Receipts for large payments, such as care facility bills.
- A list of accounts they managed, with login information if available.
- Any gifts made under the power of attorney, and why.
- Copies of tax returns they prepared or filed.

If the agent and executor are the same person, these records still matter. Other beneficiaries may want to see them.

## It depends on your state

States differ on how they protect good-faith acts after death and on any limited role a healthcare agent has afterward. An attorney licensed in your state can explain the details.
