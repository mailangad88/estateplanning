The first step is to find out how the account was set up. The answer decides who can use the money and when.

Our guide to [settling an estate step by step](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step) covers the full process. This post focuses on bank accounts, which are often the first thing families need to deal with.

## Three common ways an account can be owned

| How it is titled | Who gets the money | Probate needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Joint with right of survivorship | The surviving joint owner | No |
| Payable-on-death (POD) beneficiary | The named beneficiary | No |
| Your parent's name alone | The estate, then heirs under the will or state law | Usually yes |

Our guide to [transfer-on-death and payable-on-death](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death) explains POD accounts in more detail. The [probate vs non-probate assets comparison](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) shows how the pieces fit.

## A power of attorney stops working at death

Many adult children were helping a parent with bills under a power of attorney. That authority ends the moment your parent dies. After that, only a joint owner, a POD beneficiary, or the executor can act. Our post on [what happens to a power of attorney when you die](/blog/what-happens-to-a-power-of-attorney-when-you-die) explains why.

It also helps to stop automatic payments that no longer make sense, like a gym membership. But keep the account open for now. Bills and refunds may still flow through it.

## Steps most families take

1. **Get several certified death certificates.** Banks usually want an original or certified copy.
2. **Call or visit the bank.** Ask how each account is titled and what they need.
3. **Do not withdraw money yet**, unless you are clearly the joint owner or POD beneficiary.
4. **If probate is needed,** the executor opens it and gets court papers, often called letters testamentary.
5. **The executor opens an estate account** and moves the money there to pay bills and later distribute.

Our [first 30 days after a death checklist](/checklists/first-30-days-after-a-death) puts these in order with other early tasks.

## A short example

After her father Samuel dies, Nadia finds two accounts. One is a savings account where she was joint owner. The other is his checking account, in his name alone.

The bank lets Nadia keep the savings account with a death certificate. The checking account is frozen. Nadia is named executor in Samuel's will, so she opens probate, gets her letters, and moves the checking balance into an estate account. She uses that account to pay his final bills.

## Joint accounts and fairness

Sometimes a parent added one child to an account just for convenience. Legally, that child may still own the account at death, even if the will splits everything equally.

In some states, other family members can argue the account was only for convenience. That is a hard and often costly fight. If you are in this spot, talk with an attorney licensed in your state.

## Small balances may skip probate

Many states have a simpler process for small estates. An heir may be able to collect a modest bank balance with an affidavit instead of opening full probate. Limits and forms vary. See [can you skip probate for a small estate](/blog/can-you-skip-probate-for-a-small-estate).

Bills the account cannot cover raise a different question. Read [what happens to debt when someone dies](/blog/what-happens-to-debt-when-someone-dies).
