A mortgage does not go away when the owner dies. The loan stays tied to the house, and someone needs to keep paying it or pay it off.

That someone is usually the estate at first, then whoever inherits. Our guide to [settling an estate step by step](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step) puts this in the order of everything else an executor does.

## Who pays while the estate is open

While the estate is being settled, the executor usually keeps paying the mortgage from estate funds. That protects the house from late fees and foreclosure. It also protects its value for the heirs.

If the estate has no cash, the heirs sometimes chip in. Some lenders will agree to a short pause. It helps to call the loan servicer early.

## What heirs can do with the house

Once the house passes to an heir, they generally have three paths:

1. **Keep the loan and keep paying.** Federal law generally stops a lender from calling the loan due just because a relative inherits.
2. **Refinance.** An heir can take out a new loan in their own name, often to buy out siblings.
3. **Sell.** The sale pays off the mortgage, and any money left goes to the heirs.

If the house is worth less than the loan, heirs can often walk away. The lender's remedy is the house, not the heirs' personal savings, unless they co-signed.

## A short example

Frank dies owning a house with a mortgage of about half its value. His will leaves it to his daughter, Mei. Frank's executor keeps making payments from his checking account for the four months the estate is open.

Mei then contacts the servicer, sends a death certificate and the deed, and becomes the "successor in interest." She keeps Frank's original interest rate and pays the loan herself. She did not need to qualify for a new loan.

## Steps that help the transition

Whether you are an executor or an heir, these steps usually come early:

- Find the latest mortgage statement and the servicer's contact information.
- Notify the servicer of the death in writing and ask about successor rights.
- Keep homeowner's insurance active. An empty house can lose coverage.
- Check for mortgage life insurance or a home equity line that also needs attention.
- Keep property taxes current.

## Reverse mortgages are different

A reverse mortgage usually becomes due when the last borrower dies. Heirs typically have a limited window to repay, sell or hand the house back. The rules come from the loan contract and federal program rules, so read the paperwork closely and call the servicer soon.

## Other debts and the house

A mortgage is one kind of secured debt. Credit cards and medical bills work differently. Read [what happens to debt when someone dies](/blog/what-happens-to-debt-when-someone-dies) for the full picture.

If there was no will, the question of who inherits the house comes first. See [who gets the house if there's no will and no spouse](/blog/who-gets-the-house-if-theres-no-will-and-no-spouse).

## When siblings inherit together

Many houses pass to two or more children at once. Then they need to decide together what to do with the mortgage. Common choices are to sell and split what is left, to have one sibling buy out the others with a refinance, or to rent the house and share the costs.

Writing down the agreement helps, even among close siblings. It should say who pays the mortgage each month and how the sale money will be split.

## It depends on your state

State law controls probate timelines, foreclosure steps and how quickly title passes. Some homes skip probate through joint ownership or a [transfer-on-death deed](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust). An attorney licensed in the state where the house sits can confirm the process.
