If you are not married, the law usually treats your partner as a legal stranger for inheritance and medical decisions. A few documents can change that.

Our guide to [what estate planning is](/guides/what-is-estate-planning) covers the core documents. This post explains why each one matters more for couples who are not married.

## What the default rules leave out

Without a plan, most states give an unmarried partner:

- **No inheritance** under intestacy law. Your estate goes to blood relatives. See [who gets the house if there's no will and no spouse](/blog/who-gets-the-house-if-theres-no-will-and-no-spouse).
- **No medical decision rights** in many states. See [who makes medical decisions if you have no healthcare directive](/blog/who-makes-medical-decisions-if-you-have-no-healthcare-directive).
- **No authority over money** if you become ill.
- **No right to stay in a home** owned by you alone.

Your family may honour your partner anyway. But they do not have to.

## Documents couples commonly sign

| Document | What it does for your partner |
|---|---|
| Will or revocable trust | Lets your partner inherit what you choose. |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Lets your partner make medical decisions. |
| HIPAA release | Lets doctors share information with your partner. |
| Financial power of attorney | Lets your partner handle bills and accounts. |
| Beneficiary forms | Sends life insurance and retirement accounts to your partner. |

The [HIPAA release](/blog/what-is-a-hipaa-release-and-why-does-it-belong-in-your-plan) is easy to overlook. It is often the first document a hospital asks for. Our [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit) can help you check every account.

## Owning a home together

How you hold title matters. Common options include:

- **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship.** The survivor owns the whole home automatically.
- **Tenants in common.** Each partner owns a share that passes under their own will.
- **A trust.** The home can pass under the trust's terms without probate.

Our [joint ownership vs trust comparison](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust) lays out the trade-offs. Some couples also sign a written agreement about who paid what, in case they separate.

## A short example

Sam and Jordan have lived together for twelve years. The house is in Sam's name. Sam dies suddenly without a will.

Under their state's law, the house goes to Sam's parents. Jordan has no legal right to stay. Sam's parents are kind, but they need to sell. A simple will or a deed change could have let Jordan keep the home.

## Children and guardianship

If you have children together, make sure both partners are legal parents. If only one is, the other may have no right to raise the child if that parent dies. A will can at least name the partner as guardian. Our guide to [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children) explains how that works.

## Taxes

Married couples can usually leave unlimited amounts to each other without federal estate tax. Unmarried partners cannot. For most couples, this does not matter. For larger estates, our guide to [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes) explains the rules. Some states also tax inheritances by non-relatives.

## Writing down the details

Couples often share bills and property without much paperwork. That is fine while you are together. It becomes a problem if one partner dies or becomes ill and family members ask who owned what.

Simple records help. Keep a list of who paid for major purchases, whose name is on each account, and how the home is titled. Our [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) works well for this. Some couples also sign a cohabitation agreement. It covers what happens to shared property if they separate, and it can support the estate plan if one partner dies.

## It depends on your state

Common law marriage, domestic partnership rules, inheritance taxes and surrogate consent laws all vary. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you what rights your partner has today.
