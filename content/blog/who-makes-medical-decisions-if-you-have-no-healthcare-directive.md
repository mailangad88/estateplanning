If you have no healthcare directive and cannot speak for yourself, your state's law decides who speaks for you. Most states have a default order, often called a surrogate consent law.

That list may or may not match who you would choose. Our guide to [healthcare directives and living wills](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) explains the documents that let you choose. This post covers what happens without them.

## The usual default order

States set their own lists, but many look something like this:

1. A court-appointed guardian, if one already exists.
2. Your spouse or, in some states, a registered domestic partner.
3. Your adult children.
4. Your parents.
5. Your adult siblings.
6. In some states, a close friend or other relative who knows your wishes.

When several people share the same level, like three adult children, doctors may ask them to agree. That can be slow when time matters.

## Where default rules cause problems

The default list works well when one obvious person exists and everyone gets along. It gets harder when:

- Adult children disagree with each other or with a stepparent.
- You are separated but not divorced, so your spouse still ranks first.
- You have a partner you are not married to.
- You have no close family at all.
- The person who ranks first lives far away or is not well.

In those cases, a court may need to appoint a guardian. Our [power of attorney vs guardianship comparison](/compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains why a court process is usually slower and costlier.

## A short example

Grace is 72, widowed, and has two sons. She has a stroke and cannot communicate. One son wants every treatment available. The other remembers her saying she would not want to be kept alive on machines.

Grace had no directive. The hospital's ethics team meets with both sons twice before they reach a decision. If Grace had named one agent and written down her wishes, her sons would have had a clear guide.

## What a directive changes

A healthcare directive lets you pick your decision-maker and record your wishes. Most plans include:

- **A healthcare power of attorney** naming an agent and backups.
- **A living will** stating your wishes about life-sustaining treatment.
- **A HIPAA release** so your agent can get information. See [what is a HIPAA release](/blog/what-is-a-hipaa-release-and-why-does-it-belong-in-your-plan).

Our [living will vs healthcare power of attorney comparison](/compare/living-will-vs-healthcare-power-of-attorney) explains how the two fit together.

These documents matter most for people the default list does not serve well. See [estate planning for unmarried couples](/blog/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) and [estate planning for people with no children](/blog/estate-planning-for-people-with-no-children).

## It depends on your state

Default surrogate lists vary a lot by state. Some include close friends. Some have special rules for certain treatment decisions. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you who would decide for you today.
