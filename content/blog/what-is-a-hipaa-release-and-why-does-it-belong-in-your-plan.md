A HIPAA release is a short signed form. It gives doctors, hospitals and insurers permission to share your health information with the people you name.

It belongs in your plan because your other documents depend on it. Our guide to [healthcare directives and living wills](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) covers the decision documents. This post explains the information piece that makes them work.

## Why the form exists

HIPAA is a federal privacy law. It limits what healthcare providers can share about you without your permission. That is a good thing most of the time.

But in an emergency, it can leave the people closest to you without answers. Hospitals often share some information with family, but practices vary, and some staff are cautious. A signed release removes the guesswork.

## How it fits with your other documents

| Document | What it does |
|---|---|
| HIPAA release | Lets named people receive your health information. |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Names someone to make medical decisions if you cannot. |
| Living will | States your wishes about life-sustaining treatment. |
| Financial power of attorney | Names someone to handle money and property. |

Our [living will vs healthcare power of attorney comparison](/compare/living-will-vs-healthcare-power-of-attorney) explains how those two differ. The [powers of attorney guide](/guides/powers-of-attorney) covers the financial side.

## Who people commonly name

You can name anyone. Common choices include:

- Your healthcare agent and backup agents.
- Your financial agent, especially if their power starts at incapacity.
- A spouse or partner.
- Adult children who are likely to help with care.

You can also name people who get information without any decision power. That can keep a whole family informed while one person decides.

## A short example

Ana and Luis are not married. Luis is in a car accident and taken to the emergency room. The staff are unsure whether they can talk to Ana.

Luis had signed a HIPAA release naming Ana, along with a healthcare power of attorney. Ana shows both on her phone. The doctors brief her right away and she helps make decisions as his agent. Our post on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/blog/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) explains why this matters even more without a marriage.

## A financial agent may need it too

Some financial powers of attorney start only when a doctor confirms you cannot manage your affairs. These are called springing powers. Without a HIPAA release, your agent may struggle to get the letter that proves it. Including your financial agent on the release can avoid that loop.

## Keeping it useful

A release works only if people can find it. Many people:

- Keep a copy with their healthcare directive.
- Give copies to their agents and their main doctor.
- Save a photo or PDF on their phone.
- Update it when they change agents.

The [annual estate plan review](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review) is a good time to check it.

## It depends on your state

HIPAA is federal, so the release works across state lines. But state forms for healthcare directives differ, and some include HIPAA wording already. An attorney licensed in your state can make sure your documents work together.
