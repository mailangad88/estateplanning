This page is written calmly on purpose. Nothing here is urgent beyond what the list says, and there is no deadline we are asking you to feel.

## The first 72 hours

1. Get certified copies of the death certificate. The funeral home usually orders them for you. Many families order more than they expect to need, because banks, insurers and the court each may want one.
2. Find the will and any trust. If the will is in a safe-deposit box, ask the bank what it needs before you go. In Illinois, whoever holds the original will must file it with the circuit court clerk in the county where the person lived as soon as possible after the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1).
3. Secure the house: keys, mail, locks, utilities and insurance. Call the home insurer, because many policies limit coverage once a home has been empty for a set time. Ask what the policy says.
4. Hold off on paying debts or distributing anything. Do not close accounts yet.
5. Notify Social Security (the funeral home often does) and pension payers.

## What is at stake

- **A missed court deadline.** In Illinois the original will must be filed with the court right after the death, and hiding it is a crime (755 ILCS 5/6-1). In New Jersey a will is probated with the county Surrogate, and it cannot be admitted until 10 days have passed since the death.
- **Personal liability.** An executor who pays heirs before debts and taxes are settled, or who ignores a creditor notice period, can be personally responsible for shortfalls. Waiting out the creditor period before paying heirs is the usual protection.
- **Missing assets.** Accounts and policies in the person's name often turn up late, through the mail. A systematic search saves months. The [first 30 days checklist](/checklists/first-30-days-after-a-death) and the [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) help.
- **A house with a mortgage.** Federal law limits lenders from calling a loan due when a residential property of fewer than five units passes to a relative after the borrower's death, (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)). Heirs still need to keep paying. Tell the loan servicer about the death and ask to be confirmed as a successor in interest, so the servicer will talk to you about the account. See [what happens to a mortgage](/blog/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-when-the-owner-dies).
- **Taxes.** A final income tax return, sometimes an estate income tax return, and estate tax only for estates over the federal exclusion of $15 million for deaths in 2026 <!-- verify --> or a state threshold. Illinois has its own estate tax on estates over $4 million. New Jersey has no estate tax but does have an inheritance tax on gifts to some heirs, such as siblings, nieces and nephews. Inherited property generally gets a new tax basis equal to its value at the date of death, which matters when you sell. Ask a CPA before you sell.
- **Family conflict.** Siblings, second spouses and stepchildren. The executor sits in the middle.

## Probate, trust administration, or neither?

| Situation | Usually means |
|---|---|
| Only a will, and assets in the person's name alone | Probate (court-supervised). See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works). |
| A funded living trust, and the person was the trustee | Trust administration, usually without court. See [executor vs trustee](/compare/executor-vs-trustee). |
| All assets have beneficiaries or joint owners | Often nothing to probate, but there may still be a final tax return. See [probate vs non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets). |
| A small estate | A small estate procedure may be enough. Illinois allows a small estate affidavit up to $150,000 with no real estate. New Jersey has a Surrogate's affidavit for small estates with no will. See [skipping probate for a small estate](/blog/can-you-skip-probate-for-a-small-estate). |

You can estimate time and cost with the [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator).
