This page is written calmly on purpose. Nothing here is urgent beyond what the list says, and there is no deadline we are asking you to feel.

## The first 72 hours

1. Get certified copies of the death certificate. Many people order more than they expect to need. [State check: how many and from whom]
2. Find the will and any trust. If the will is in a safe-deposit box, ask the bank how access works first. [State check]
3. Secure the house: keys, mail, locks, utilities and insurance. Call the home insurer, because vacant homes are often excluded after a set period. [VERIFY with the insurer]
4. Hold off on paying debts or distributing anything. Do not close accounts yet.
5. Notify Social Security (the funeral home often does) and pension payers.

## What is at stake

- **A missed court deadline.** In many states the will must be filed with the court within a short time of death. Failure to file can carry penalties. [State check: filing deadline]
- **Personal liability.** An executor who pays heirs before debts and taxes are settled, or who ignores a creditor notice period, can be personally responsible for shortfalls. [State check]
- **Missing assets.** Accounts and policies in the person's name often turn up late, through the mail. A systematic search saves months. The [first 30 days checklist](/checklists/first-30-days-after-a-death) and the [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) help.
- **A house with a mortgage.** Federal law limits lenders from calling a loan due when a residential property of fewer than five units passes to a relative after the borrower's death, so heirs generally have time to arrange payments. They still need to keep paying. [ATTORNEY: confirm and add practical note]. See [what happens to a mortgage](/blog/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-when-the-owner-dies).
- **Taxes.** A final income tax return, sometimes an estate income tax return, and estate tax only for estates over the federal exclusion of $15 million for deaths in 2026 <!-- verify --> or a state threshold. [State check]. Inherited property generally gets a new tax basis equal to its value at the date of death, which matters when you sell. Ask a CPA before you sell.
- **Family conflict.** Siblings, second spouses and stepchildren. The executor sits in the middle.

## Probate, trust administration, or neither?

| Situation | Usually means |
|---|---|
| Only a will, and assets in the person's name alone | Probate (court-supervised). See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works). |
| A funded living trust, and the person was the trustee | Trust administration, usually without court. See [executor vs trustee](/compare/executor-vs-trustee). |
| All assets have beneficiaries or joint owners | Often nothing to probate, but there may still be a final tax return. See [probate vs non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets). |
| A small estate | A small estate procedure may be enough. See [skipping probate for a small estate](/blog/can-you-skip-probate-for-a-small-estate). [State check: thresholds] |
