## What is at stake

- **Everything to the spouse.** A simple "all to my spouse" will or a jointly titled house can make the survivor the sole owner. Then the survivor can change their own will, remarry or spend the money, and your children from the first marriage may inherit nothing. See the [blended families guide](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families).
- **Stepchildren.** Under intestacy rules, stepchildren generally do not inherit. Illinois leaves them out entirely, and New Jersey reaches them only as a last resort.
- **The house.** A surviving spouse may need to live there for life, while the children want their share. A life estate or a trust can allow both, with rules on repairs, taxes and when it is sold.
- **The ex still on the form.** An old beneficiary designation on a 401(k) or an insurance policy can pay an ex-spouse regardless of your will. Many states automatically revoke gifts to an ex-spouse after divorce, but for employer plans covered by ERISA, such as most 401(k)s, federal law controls and the plan pays the person on the form (Egelhoff v. Egelhoff, 532 U.S. 141 (2001)). See the [beneficiary audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit) and [planning after divorce](/life-events/divorce).
- **Fights years from now.** A joint "mirror" will signed by both spouses can become binding on the survivor if the spouses agreed not to change it, which surprises people. In New Jersey, a contract not to revoke a will must be in writing, and signing mirror wills does not by itself create one (N.J.S.A. 3B:1-4). Ask the attorney how this works for you.

## Which situation is closest to yours

| Your structure | Common concern | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Second marriage, adult children from the first | Spouse first, children next | Spousal lifetime trust, separate beneficiary forms |
| Second marriage, young children from the first | Guardian and money for kids, plus the spouse | Trust for children, life insurance, guardian |
| "Yours, mine and ours" | Equal treatment of children | Equalizing with insurance, separate and joint shares |
| Unmarried couple with children | No default inheritance rights | Wills, beneficiary forms, trust, healthcare proxies |
| Stepchild you raised | No automatic inheritance | A named gift or trust, or adoption, which is family law work |

## What a plan usually includes

| Piece | What it does |
|---|---|
| Family map | Who is in the family, who has what, and who you want to provide for and in what order. |
| Trust structure | A trust that provides for the spouse for life, then passes the balance to your children. It can name a neutral or independent trustee, which can cost more but may reduce conflict. |
| Separate vs joint documents | Usually separate documents for each spouse, plus a clear understanding about the house. |
| Beneficiary audit | Every account and policy reviewed against the plan. |
| Marital agreement coordination | A prenup or postnup may waive spousal rights to your estate. The plan should match it. |
| Family meeting script | A short guide to telling the family what you decided and why. |

**One attorney or two?** Many couples use one attorney for both plans. If your interests truly conflict, each spouse may need a separate attorney. The attorney we match you with will explain the rules on joint representation before you start. A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement is usually drafted by a family law attorney, with each spouse separately advised.

**An honest limit.** If you and your spouse have no children from earlier relationships and a simple estate, you may not need any of the trust structures described here.
