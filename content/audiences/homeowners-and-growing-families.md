## What is at stake

- **No guardian named.** If both parents die, a judge picks who raises your children. Relatives may disagree. See the [guardianship guide](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children).
- **Insurance and retirement accounts that name a minor.** A policy that pays your child directly generally leads to a court-supervised account. Your plan can name a trust instead. See the [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).
- **Money that arrives all at once.** Many states release a minor's assets at 18 or 21. [State check: age of majority for property]. Most parents would rather have a trustee pay for school and housing and release the rest in stages.
- **A house titled the wrong way.** A deed in one name, or joint with a parent or sibling, can send the home somewhere you did not intend. See [should I put my house in a trust](/blog/should-i-put-my-house-in-a-trust).
- **An old plan.** Documents from before a second child, a move or a job change may name people who no longer fit. See [updating your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## Does a living trust make sense for you

A living trust is a tool, not a requirement. It helps most when the benefits match your situation.

| Your situation | A living trust may help | A will and good forms may be enough |
|---|---|---|
| Home plus retirement accounts, kids at home | If probate in your state is slow or costly | If your state has a simple process |
| Property in more than one state | Often yes, to avoid two probates | Rarely |
| Privacy matters to you | Yes, a trust is not a public record | Probate files are public |
| One spouse could be incapacitated | Yes, a successor trustee can step in | A power of attorney covers much of this |
| Modest assets, no real estate beyond the home | Often not needed yet | Often yes |

If you do create a trust, it must be funded. That means retitling the house and accounts into it. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) and the [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist).

## What a plan for you usually includes

1. **Wills for both parents,** naming a guardian, a backup and an executor.
2. **A trust for the children,** inside the will or in a living trust, with a trustee and ages for payments. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).
3. **Beneficiary forms** that match the plan, so insurance and retirement money does not go to a minor outright.
4. **Durable powers of attorney and healthcare directives** for each parent.
5. **A review of how your house and accounts are titled.**
6. **A guardian letter and a document locator,** so the people you named can find what they need.

**Life insurance.** We do not sell insurance. A licensed agent can help you decide how much to carry. We help the plan receive it correctly.

**What we do not do.** We do not choose your guardian or trustee. We help you weigh the choice. A guardian cannot be forced to accept, so ask first.

**An honest limit.** If your assets are modest and your family is simple, a trust may cost more than it saves.
