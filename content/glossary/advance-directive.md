"Advance directive" is an umbrella term. It covers any signed document that guides medical care in advance, most often a [Living will](/glossary/living-will) (your treatment preferences) and a [healthcare power of attorney](/glossary/healthcare-proxy) (the person you name to decide). Some states combine both into one form. Directives take effect only when a doctor determines you cannot make or communicate your own decisions, and they stop mattering the moment you can speak for yourself again. Each state has its own execution rules, which usually involve witnesses or a notary.

## Example

Grace, 66, has early heart failure. She signs one advance directive naming her daughter Tanya as agent and saying she does not want a feeding tube if she is permanently unconscious. Two years later a stroke leaves her unable to speak, and the hospital turns to the document and to Tanya instead of guessing or calling a family meeting.

## Why it matters

Without a directive, doctors turn to default rules that vary by state, and relatives may disagree about what you would have wanted. That disagreement can slow treatment and strain a family at the worst time. A directive also gives the person you trust legal standing at the bedside. Copies should go to your agent, your doctor, and your hospital portal. The overview in [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives) explains the pieces, and [HIPAA authorizations](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) help your agent get records.
