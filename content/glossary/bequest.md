Bequests come in types. A specific bequest gives a named item, such as a grandmother's ring. A general bequest gives a sum of money, such as $10,000, paid from the estate. A demonstrative bequest is a sum paid from a particular source, like a named account. Whatever remains after all of these goes to the [Residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate). If the estate runs short, state law sets an order in which bequests are reduced, and a bequest of an item the [Testator](/glossary/testator) no longer owns at death usually fails.

## Example

In her will, Ruth leaves her piano to her grandson, $15,000 to a church, and the rest of her estate to her two daughters. The piano is a specific bequest, the $15,000 is a general bequest, and the daughters take the residue. If Ruth sold the piano before she died, her grandson receives nothing in its place.

## Why it matters

Clear wording matters. Vague phrases like "my jewelry" or "to my children" can start disagreements, and a bequest to someone who dies before you may lapse unless the will provides a backup. A bequest in a will also passes only through probate, not through the beneficiary forms that govern retirement accounts.
