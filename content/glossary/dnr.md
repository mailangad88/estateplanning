A DNR covers one decision: chest compressions, shocks, and breathing tubes if the heart stops. It does not mean "do not treat." A person with a DNR still gets antibiotics, oxygen, surgery, or pain relief unless they decide otherwise. In a hospital, a doctor writes the order after discussing it with the patient or their [agent](/glossary/healthcare-proxy). Outside a hospital, paramedics generally honor only specific state-approved forms or bracelets. A [POLST](/glossary/polst) covers broader choices, and a [Living will](/glossary/living-will) states wishes without being an order.

## Example

Harold, 90 and frail, tells his doctor he does not want CPR. The doctor writes a DNR in his hospital chart and completes a state out-of-hospital form for home. When Harold collapses, paramedics see the form and do not start CPR.

## Why it matters

Without a DNR form that fits the setting, emergency teams usually must attempt resuscitation, even if a family member objects. A DNR should be reviewed whenever a person's health or goals change, and it can be revoked at any time.
