Three roles define every trust: the [Grantor (settlor, trustor)](/glossary/grantor) who creates it and supplies the property, the [Trustee](/glossary/trustee) who manages the property, and the [beneficiaries](/glossary/beneficiary) who benefit. The terms are written in a trust agreement. A trust can be [revocable](/glossary/revocable-trust) or [irrevocable](/glossary/irrevocable-trust), start during life ([Living trust](/glossary/living-trust)) or at death ([Testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust)), and serve goals from avoiding probate to protecting a child with a disability. A trust is not a separate asset; it only controls property actually placed in it.

## Example

Martin creates a trust holding rental property and investments, naming his sister trustee and his two children beneficiaries. The trust says rents pay for his children's education and that each child gets a share at 30. The trustee must follow those terms, not her own preferences.

## Why it matters

Trusts offer control that a will cannot: timing of gifts, protection for heirs, privacy, and continuity if you become incapacitated. They cost more than a will and require upkeep. Not everyone needs one. Compare the options in [the trusts guide](/learn/trusts) and [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust).
