Co-owners hold equal undivided interests, and each has the right to the whole. At the first owner's death the survivor owns the property outright, without probate, and the will has no effect on that asset. Joint tenancy is common for bank accounts and for homes owned by a married couple or a parent and child. Compare [Tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety), available only to married couples in some states, and tenancy in common, where shares can be left by will. Joint tenancy can usually be ended by one owner selling their share.

## Example

Linda adds her son Aaron to her house deed as joint tenant to avoid probate. She later dies, and Aaron owns the house at once. But Linda's other child, who was supposed to share equally, receives nothing, and Aaron's creditor had a claim against the house while Linda was alive.

## Why it matters

The shortcut carries risks: gift tax filings, loss of the full [Step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) on the added owner's share, exposure to the co-owner's debts, divorce, or lawsuits, and unintended disinheritance. Adding a child to a deed is often a poor substitute for a trust or a [Transfer-on-death deed](/glossary/transfer-on-death-deed).
