Available in a majority of states under various names, this deed is signed, notarized, and recorded with the county before death. It names a beneficiary, but the beneficiary has no ownership while you are alive, and you can sell, mortgage, or revoke without their consent. At your death, the beneficiary files a death certificate and affidavit to put title in their name. Rules differ: some states require recording before death, a few limit who can be named. Creditors and Medicaid estate recovery may still reach the property in some states.

## Example

Dolores records a transfer-on-death deed naming her son for her $300,000 home. She continues to live there and later refinances without asking him. When she dies, he records her death certificate and takes title without probate court.

## Why it matters

Compared with adding a child as a joint owner, a TOD deed avoids gift and creditor exposure during life and preserves the [Step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis). Weak points include a beneficiary who dies first, several beneficiaries who disagree, and mistakes in recording.
