Under the Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument Act (755 ILCS 27/75), the beneficiary signs and records an affidavit that identifies the beneficiary, the legal description and parcel number, the recording details of the TODI, and the date and place of the owner's death. A certified death certificate is typically recorded with it. The record gives title companies and lenders what they need to treat the beneficiary as the owner. Recording promptly matters, because the Act sets time limits that can affect the beneficiary's protection against competing claims.

## Example

Kayla inherits her father's Peoria house under a TODI. Within a month of his death she signs a notice of death affidavit, attaches the death certificate, and pays the recorder's fee of about $60 so the title company can insure a later sale.

## Why it matters

Think of it as the second half of a [Transfer on death instrument](/glossary/transfer-on-death-instrument): the first half is signed by the owner, the second by the beneficiary. Skipping it can leave the property stuck at closing. See [Illinois transfer on death instruments](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) for the full process.
