A pour-over will says, in effect, "whatever I own at death that is not already in my trust goes into my trust." Those assets pass through probate first, because the will is what moves them, and then are distributed under the trust's terms. The will can also name guardians for minor children and an executor, which trusts cannot do. It serves as a safety net for forgotten or newly acquired property, but it does not replace [Trust funding](/glossary/funding) the trust during life.

## Example

Priscilla funds her trust with her house and accounts but forgets a $40,000 brokerage account. Her pour-over will directs that account into the trust. It goes through a short probate and is then distributed under the same instructions as everything else.

## Why it matters

Everyone with a trust should have a pour-over will. It is not a way to avoid probate, though, since any asset that depends on it will go through the process. The more complete the funding, the less the will does. Read [what a pour-over will does](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) and [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
