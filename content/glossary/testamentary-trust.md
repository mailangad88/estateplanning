Unlike a [Living trust](/glossary/living-trust), which is signed and in operation while you are alive, a testamentary trust is just instructions inside a will. At death, the court admits the will, and the trust begins with the [Trustee](/glossary/trustee) named in it. It is often used to hold property for young children until they reach a chosen age, or for a spouse. Since it is created through the will, the assets pass through probate first, and the trust may remain under some court supervision depending on the state, which can mean ongoing filings.

## Example

Alan and Mei's wills say that if both die, everything goes into a trust for their two kids, managed by Mei's brother until each child turns 25. Nothing exists in trust now; the trust is created only if the will is admitted.

## Why it matters

A testamentary trust is inexpensive to write because it needs no funding step, and it gives young-child protection in a basic plan. The drawbacks are probate, public court records, and potential supervision. Larger or more complex estates often prefer a living trust.
