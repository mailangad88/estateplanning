An ordinary [Power of attorney](/glossary/power-of-attorney) ends automatically if the person who signed it loses capacity, which is exactly when it is needed most. The word "durable" fixes that: the document says, in so many words, that the [Agent (power of attorney)](/glossary/agent)'s authority continues despite incapacity. Most state laws now treat powers of attorney as durable unless they say otherwise, but the wording still matters. It can take effect immediately or only on a trigger (see [Springing power of attorney](/glossary/springing-power-of-attorney)). It ends at the principal's death, when the executor takes over.

## Example

Beatriz signs a durable power of attorney naming her son Luis. Two years later she has a stroke and cannot manage her accounts. Luis shows the document to her bank and brokerage, pays her bills, and files her tax return, without going to court.

## Why it matters

Without a durable document, families often must ask a judge to appoint a [conservator](/glossary/conservatorship), which is slower, public, and costly. Banks sometimes hesitate over older or unusual forms, so use a current one and keep copies. Be aware that the agent can have wide powers over your money, so choose carefully and name a backup.
