Courts, banks, and the IRS use "decedent" as a neutral label for the person whose property is being handled. It appears on case captions, such as "Estate of John Smith, Deceased," and on tax forms. The term does not depend on whether the person left a will; a decedent can be a [Testator](/glossary/testator) (left a will) or [Intestate](/glossary/intestate) (did not). The decedent's date of death matters a great deal: it fixes asset values for tax purposes, starts claim deadlines for creditors, and determines which laws apply.

## Example

After Carla dies, her son opens the probate case as "In re Estate of Carla Nguyen, Decedent." Date-of-death statements from her bank and brokerage, which show values on the day she died, are collected for the court inventory and for the [Step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) calculation.

## Why it matters

Knowing the term helps families read the paperwork that arrives after a loss. Most documents will ask for the decedent's full legal name, Social Security number, date of death, and last residence, so gather certified death certificates early. Many institutions need an original for each account.
