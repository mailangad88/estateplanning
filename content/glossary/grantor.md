Different states and lawyers use different words for the same role: grantor, settlor, trustor, or trust maker. The grantor signs the trust agreement, decides who the [beneficiaries](/glossary/beneficiary) are, and chooses the [Trustee](/glossary/trustee). In a [Revocable trust](/glossary/revocable-trust), the grantor usually serves as the first trustee and can change or cancel the trust at any time. In an [Irrevocable trust](/glossary/irrevocable-trust), the grantor generally gives up that control. For income tax, a revocable trust is usually a "grantor trust," meaning the grantor reports its income personally.

## Example

Leila creates a trust for her two sons and names herself trustee. As grantor she keeps the right to change beneficiaries and withdraw money while she is alive, and she names her sister as [Successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) to step in when Leila can no longer serve.

## Why it matters

The grantor's powers decide almost everything: whether creditors can reach the property, whether it is part of the taxable estate, and whether it can be changed later. Because retained control often keeps assets in the estate for tax purposes, grantors who want tax or protection benefits usually must give something up.
