Many Illinois wills are admitted on the attestation clause or an affidavit alone. If an interested person requests it, the court can require formal proof, in which the attesting witnesses testify that the testator signed, that they signed in the testator's presence, and that the testator appeared to be of sound mind (755 ILCS 5/6-21). This is separate from a will contest, which is a lawsuit that must be filed within six months after the will is admitted (755 ILCS 5/8-1). If witnesses cannot be found, other evidence may be allowed.

## Example

Beth's will leaves everything to her caregiver, Tom. Her nephew demands formal proof. Both witnesses, a lawyer and a secretary, testify that Beth signed willingly and understood her assets, and the court confirms the will.

## Why it matters

Formal proof can reveal weaknesses in a will early. A strong [Attestation clause](/glossary/attestation-clause) and witnesses who can be located make the hearing smoother. See the deadlines in [the Illinois six-month contest period](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) and the steps in [how to object to probate](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate).
