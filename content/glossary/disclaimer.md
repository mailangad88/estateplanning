A beneficiary who does not want property can sign a written disclaimer and deliver it to the executor or trustee. For federal tax purposes, a "qualified" disclaimer generally must be in writing and delivered within nine months of the death, and the person disclaiming must not have accepted the property or any benefit from it. The property then passes to the next person in line, such as a [Contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary), and the person who disclaimed cannot choose who that is. State law adds its own rules.

## Example

Hal inherits $100,000 from an aunt but is financially comfortable, and his own daughter is struggling. Within nine months he signs a disclaimer. Because the aunt's will names no backup for Hal, state law treats him as having died first, and his daughter inherits instead of him.

## Why it matters

A disclaimer can redirect assets to a family member who needs them more, or avoid unwanted ownership, without the disclaiming person making a taxable gift. It is permanent, though, and it can backfire for someone receiving Medicaid or facing creditors, where a refusal may be treated as a transfer. Always get legal advice before signing.
