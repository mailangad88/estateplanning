In supervised administration, the representative files petitions and gets court orders for actions that an independent representative could take alone, and the court reviews accounts along the way. A judge may order it when a will requires it, when an interested person shows cause, or when the court sees a need for closer oversight, such as conflict among heirs or concerns about the representative (Probate Act, 755 ILCS 5). Because of the added hearings and notices, it generally takes longer and costs more than the independent track.

## Example

Greg's will says nothing about administration, but his two children disagree about selling his $275,000 house. One child asks the court to supervise. The executor now needs an order before listing the house, and the judge reviews the sale price.

## Why it matters

Supervision slows a case, but it protects heirs when trust has broken down. It can also be a middle step before removing a representative.
