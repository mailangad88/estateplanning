The federal government has no inheritance tax. A small number of states do, and those taxes fall on the recipient, not the estate. Rates and exemptions depend on the heir's relationship to the deceased: spouses are usually exempt, children and grandchildren often are exempt or pay very little, while siblings, nieces, nephews, and unrelated heirs may pay much more. The tax can apply based on where the deceased lived and, for real estate, where the property sits. It is different from the [Estate tax](/glossary/estate-tax), which is imposed on the estate itself.

## Example

Karen, who lives in a state with an inheritance tax, leaves $200,000 to her daughter and $200,000 to her nephew. Her daughter owes nothing, but her nephew owes tax at his class's rate under state law, paid out of the estate or by him, depending on the will.

## Why it matters

If you leave money to non-spouse relatives or friends in a state with this tax, the amount heirs actually keep can fall. Executors need to check deadlines because returns are often due within months. Moving, or owning land elsewhere, can bring another state's tax.
