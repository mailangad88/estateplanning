When a will is submitted to probate, the court must be satisfied that it was properly signed. Without an affidavit, the witnesses may have to testify or sign statements, even many years later when they may be hard to find. With a notarized self-proving affidavit attached, usually signed at the same ceremony, the court generally accepts the will as validly executed. It does not stop anyone from challenging the will for other reasons, such as undue influence. Most states allow it, though the wording varies.

## Example

Tara signs her will before two neighbors and a notary, and all three sign the affidavit. When she dies years later, one witness has moved and the other has died, but the court admits the will on the affidavit alone.

## Why it matters

The affidavit saves time and expense and avoids a stalled probate. It is cheap to add if a notary is present at the signing, and is often skipped in do-it-yourself wills.
