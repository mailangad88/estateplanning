In a revocable living trust, the person who created it usually serves as the first [Trustee](/glossary/trustee). The trust document names one or more successors, in order, who step in on a specified event: the grantor's death, a doctor's certification of incapacity, or resignation. The successor then follows the trust's terms: collecting assets, paying bills and taxes, keeping records, and distributing property. Successor trustees are [fiduciaries](/glossary/fiduciary), owing duties to the beneficiaries. They may be allowed to hire help and be paid reasonable compensation.

## Example

Hannah's trust names her nephew as successor trustee. When she dies, he obtains copies of the death certificate, notifies the bank and retitles the accounts to the trust's name, pays final bills, files the trust's tax return, and distributes assets to her heirs over several months.

## Why it matters

This is one of the most significant decisions in a trust, because a successor with too little time or skill can cause delays and disputes, and one lacking honesty can cause loss. Name backups and consider whether a co-trustee or professional trustee makes sense.
