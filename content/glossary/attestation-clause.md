Illinois requires a will to be in writing, signed by the testator, and attested in the testator's presence by two or more credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3). The attestation clause records those facts and gives the witnesses' signatures and addresses. It is not strictly required, but a complete clause makes proof of the will much easier. An Illinois will can be admitted without witness testimony when the attestation clause states the facts that prove due execution and the court is satisfied, which is why a separate [Self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) is less necessary in Illinois than elsewhere.

## Example

Anita signs her will in a lawyer's conference room. Two office staff members watch her sign, then sign beneath a paragraph stating, 'We saw her sign, she appeared of sound mind, and we signed in her presence.' That paragraph is the attestation clause.

## Why it matters

A missing or sloppy clause can force witnesses to testify years later, and if they cannot be found, the will may be hard to admit. An interested witness, such as a beneficiary, can lose part of a gift.
