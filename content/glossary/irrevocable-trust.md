When a [Grantor (settlor, trustor)](/glossary/grantor) transfers property into an irrevocable trust, the grantor gives up ownership and most control, and an independent [Trustee](/glossary/trustee) manages the assets for the [beneficiaries](/glossary/beneficiary). Because the property is no longer legally the grantor's, it may be outside the taxable estate and, after a waiting period, beyond the reach of long-term care costs or creditors. Some trusts allow limited changes through a trust protector, a court, or agreement of the beneficiaries. Types include life insurance trusts, Medicaid asset protection trusts, and [special needs trusts](/glossary/special-needs-trust).

## Example

Dolores, 70, moves her house into an irrevocable trust for her children, keeping the right to live there for life. She cannot sell the house herself or take it back. If she needs nursing home care after the [look-back period](/glossary/look-back-period) has passed, the house is generally not counted for Medicaid.

## Why it matters

The tradeoff is control for protection. Moving the wrong asset, or doing so too late, can be costly, and gifts to the trust can use up part of your gift tax exemption. Income taxes and basis rules differ from a revocable trust. This is a decision to make with an attorney.
