Fiduciary describes a relationship of trust that the law enforces. It applies to a [Trustee](/glossary/trustee), an [Executor](/glossary/executor), an [Agent (power of attorney)](/glossary/agent) under a [Power of attorney](/glossary/power-of-attorney), a [Guardian](/glossary/guardian), and others who manage someone else's property or affairs. The core obligations are loyalty (no self-dealing), prudence (careful management), impartiality among beneficiaries, record keeping, and accounting. Those obligations are described in more depth under [Fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty). A fiduciary who breaches them can be ordered to repay losses and may be removed.

## Example

Nora is her mother's agent under a power of attorney. She may pay her mother's bills and care costs from her mother's account, but she may not buy her own car with that money, even if she intends to repay it. If she does, she may face a civil claim and removal.

## Why it matters

Fiduciary status means the role is more than an honor. Anyone you name takes on legal exposure, and your family relies on their honesty. Choose someone you trust with money, and consider co-fiduciaries or a professional where assets or family dynamics are complex. Beneficiaries who suspect misconduct have remedies; see [whether an executor can be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).
