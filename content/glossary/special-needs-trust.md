Means-tested benefits like SSI and Medicaid limit the assets a recipient may own. A special needs trust holds property that does not count, so long as the [Trustee](/glossary/trustee) uses it for extras, such as therapies, travel, or equipment, and not for food and shelter in a way that reduces benefits. A third-party trust is funded by family, usually through a will or living trust. A first-party trust is funded with the person's own money, such as a settlement, and generally must include a payback to Medicaid at the beneficiary's death. An [ABLE account](/glossary/able-account) can complement either.

## Example

Rosa leaves $250,000 to a third-party special needs trust for her son Mateo, who has autism and receives SSI. The trustee pays for his tutoring, a laptop, and vacations. If Rosa had left the money to Mateo directly, he would likely lose benefits until he spent it down.

## Why it matters

Leaving an inheritance directly to a disabled person is a well-meant mistake. A trust can protect benefits for life, but requires careful drafting and a trustee who understands the rules.
