A person is intestate when they die with no will, or with a will that a court finds invalid. A will can also fail in part, leaving some property "partially intestate." Assets that pass by beneficiary form, joint ownership, or trust are not affected, because they never depended on the will. Only the [Probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate) falls under the state's default rules. A court appoints an [Administrator](/glossary/administrator) to manage the process, often starting with the spouse or an adult child.

## Example

Owen, a widower with three children, dies with no will. His house and bank account, in his name alone, are divided among his children in equal shares under state law. His life insurance, naming one child, goes only to that child, which makes the overall split unequal.

## Why it matters

Dying intestate often creates results families never intended: unmarried partners and stepchildren may get nothing, a spouse may share with the deceased's children, and guardians for minors are left to a judge. It can also be slower and costlier than following a will. A short will or a trust fixes these issues.
