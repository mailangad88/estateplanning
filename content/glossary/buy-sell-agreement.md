The agreement names the triggering events (death, disability, divorce, retirement, or a desire to sell), who may buy (the company or the other owners), the price or formula, and how it will be paid. Funding is often through life or disability insurance on each owner, so cash is available at the moment of need. Structures include a cross-purchase, where owners buy each other's shares, and a redemption, where the company buys them. Valuation clauses and the tax consequences vary by structure.

## Example

Two partners, Ivan and Chloe, own a design studio 50/50. Their agreement says that if either dies, the company buys the share at a set value, funded by a policy on each partner. When Ivan dies, his widow receives the payment and Chloe owns the firm outright.

## Why it matters

Without an agreement, a deceased owner's share may pass to an heir who knows nothing about the business, or the surviving owner may be unable to afford a buyout. A stale price can also cause disputes, so reviewing it every few years is wise.
