"Living" simply means the trust starts during your lifetime, as opposed to a [Testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust) created by a will at death. Most living trusts are revocable, so you can change or cancel them, and you typically serve as your own [Trustee](/glossary/trustee). You write instructions for incapacity and for after death, and name a [Successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee). A living trust works only for assets properly retitled in its name; see [Trust funding](/glossary/funding). It does not by itself reduce income or estate taxes.

## Example

Carlos and Ana sign a living trust, retitle their house and savings accounts into it, and name their daughter as successor trustee. When Carlos later has a stroke, Ana continues to manage everything. After both die, their daughter distributes assets under the trust without going to probate court.

## Why it matters

A living trust can make incapacity and death simpler and keep your affairs private, since probate files are public. It costs more to set up than a basic will and takes upkeep. It is less helpful for small estates in simple families.
