The word has several uses. Under a will, a beneficiary receives a [Bequest](/glossary/bequest). Under a trust, a beneficiary receives distributions or benefits from the [Trustee](/glossary/trustee). On an IRA, 401(k), or life insurance policy, a beneficiary is the person named on the account form, and that form usually overrides what your will says. A beneficiary is different from an [Heir](/glossary/heir), who is someone entitled to inherit under state law if there is no will.

## Example

Marcus names his sister as primary beneficiary of his 401(k) and her daughter as the [Contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary). His will leaves his house to his brother. When Marcus dies, the plan pays his sister directly, the house goes to his brother through probate, and neither document changes the other's result.

## Why it matters

Beneficiary forms are among the most common sources of family disputes, because people forget to update them after a divorce, birth, or death. An outdated form can send money to an ex-spouse or a person who has died. Review every account every few years and after major life changes.
