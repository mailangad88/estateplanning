A trust document by itself holds nothing. It controls only the assets that have been transferred into it. Funding a trust means signing a new deed for real estate, retitling bank and brokerage accounts into the trustee's name, assigning business interests, and sometimes naming the trust as a beneficiary of an account. Retirement accounts are usually not retitled, because that could trigger tax; their beneficiary forms are coordinated instead. A [Pour-over will](/glossary/pour-over-will) acts as a safety net for anything missed.

## Example

Tom signs a living trust but never changes the deed to his house. When he dies, the house is still in his own name, so his family must open probate to sell it, which was the very thing the trust was meant to avoid.

## Why it matters

An unfunded trust is among the most common estate planning failures and the easiest to prevent. After signing, work through a funding checklist and revisit it whenever you open a new account or buy property. Make sure the deed, the insurance, and mortgage lender are all addressed.
