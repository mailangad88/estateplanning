With a spendthrift clause, a beneficiary cannot sell or borrow against their interest in the trust, and most creditors cannot force the [Trustee](/glossary/trustee) to pay them directly. Once money is distributed to the beneficiary, though, creditors can usually reach it. Exceptions exist in many states for child support, alimony, and certain government claims. The clause works best with a trustee who has discretion over distributions. It does not protect assets the beneficiary created in their own name, and protection for self-settled trusts varies a lot by state.

## Example

Vince leaves his son Dustin's share in trust with a spendthrift clause. Dustin later owes a large judgment after a business failure. The creditor cannot take Dustin's trust interest, but may pursue money the trustee has already paid him.

## Why it matters

Parents often use these clauses to protect an inheritance from divorce, lawsuits, and poor spending habits without cutting a child out. The protection is not absolute, and a heavy-handed trust can frustrate a beneficiary.
