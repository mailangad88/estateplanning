Because the [Grantor (settlor, trustor)](/glossary/grantor) keeps full control, the assets are still treated as the grantor's for most legal purposes. Creditors can generally reach them and they stay in the taxable estate. Income is reported on the grantor's personal return. What the trust adds is a plan: a [Successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) takes over smoothly if you become incapacitated, and after death assets go to beneficiaries without [Probate](/glossary/probate). At the grantor's death, the trust becomes irrevocable, and a [Step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) usually applies to the assets.

## Example

Marcy transfers her home and accounts to her revocable trust and acts as trustee. When she sells the house, she changes her mind about a gift and amends the trust. When she dies, her sister, the successor trustee, distributes everything privately.

## Why it matters

Revocable trusts offer flexibility and privacy but do not shelter assets from creditors, Medicaid, or the estate tax. Myths about "saving taxes" cause disappointment. They also need funding to work.
