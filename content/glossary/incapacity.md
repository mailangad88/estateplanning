Incapacity is a legal concept with a practical definition: you cannot understand your situation or the consequences of decisions well enough to manage your affairs. It can be sudden, as after a stroke or accident, or gradual, as with dementia. It can also be partial or come and go. Documents define it differently. A [Springing power of attorney](/glossary/springing-power-of-attorney) may need one or two physicians' written statements; a court uses a hearing. Capacity to sign a will or contract is judged by separate tests (see [Testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity)).

## Example

Joan, 79, signs her documents while she still has good days and bad ones. Two years later her doctor writes a letter finding that she can no longer manage her money. Her daughter, named as agent, begins paying bills under the [Durable power of attorney](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney) Joan signed.

## Why it matters

If you lose capacity with no documents in place, your family may have to go to court, a public and expensive process, to gain authority. Signing a durable power of attorney and healthcare proxy while you still have capacity is the best protection, and doing so earlier is easier than later.
