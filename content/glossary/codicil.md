A codicil must be signed and witnessed with the same formalities as a will, because a court treats it as part of the will. It should refer to the original will by date and say exactly what it changes. Codicils suit small edits, like swapping an executor. They are risky for larger changes: two documents can contradict each other, a codicil can be lost while the will survives, and handwritten additions can raise questions about validity. For significant changes, most attorneys redraft the will and revoke the earlier one.

## Example

Elena's will leaves $5,000 to her friend Joan. Joan moves away and Elena wants to give the money to a local library instead. She signs a short codicil, dated and witnessed by two neighbors, that revokes the Joan gift and names the library. The rest of her will stays in force.

## Why it matters

A failed or confusing amendment can cause the very dispute you meant to avoid. Scribbling edits on the original will can even invalidate parts of it. Keep the will and any codicil together, and tell your executor where both are.
