Courts look for a person who had a confidential relationship with the [Testator](/glossary/testator) (a caregiver, a child, a lawyer), who was involved in making the document, and who benefits unusually from it. Other signs include isolating the testator from family, a sudden change from earlier plans, and a testator who was ill, dependent, or confused. Ordinary persuasion or affection is not undue influence; the question is whether the document reflects the testator's own wishes. A successful challenge can void the document or the affected parts, but the burden of proof is typically on the challenger.

## Example

Ellen, 88 and recovering from a stroke, signs a new will leaving her house to a neighbor who drove her to the lawyer, sat in on the meeting, and had kept her sons from visiting. The sons contest the will, citing the changes and the isolation.

## Why it matters

Undue influence is one of the two most common grounds for contests, along with [Testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity). To reduce the risk, a person can meet privately with their attorney, explain unusual choices, and have independent witnesses and medical notes.
