The person who signs is the [Principal (power of attorney)](/glossary/principal); the person authorized is the [Agent (power of attorney)](/glossary/agent). Documents come in kinds: a financial power of attorney covers banking, bills, property, and taxes; a healthcare version covers medical decisions (see [Healthcare proxy](/glossary/healthcare-proxy)). A power of attorney can be general or limited to one task, like closing the sale of a house. Unless it is [durable](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney), it ends if the principal loses capacity. All powers of attorney end at the principal's death.

## Example

Jerome, traveling abroad for a year, signs a limited power of attorney letting his brother sign closing papers on his condo. The document ends when the sale closes. Separately, Jerome has a durable one that stays in place for his finances should anything happen.

## Why it matters

A power of attorney is one of the few documents that can keep your family out of court if you become ill. Not having one can lead to a costly [Conservatorship](/glossary/conservatorship). It also carries risk, because the agent controls your assets, so name someone dependable and require reporting if you wish. See [the power of attorney overview](/learn/power-of-attorney) and [springing vs. immediate](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).
