A probate court generally has power only over property inside its own state. If a person dies owning a house, land, or sometimes a vehicle in a second state, the executor must usually open an ancillary case there, in addition to the [main probate](/glossary/probate) in the state of the person's home. The second case follows that state's rules and deadlines, often requires a local attorney, and may need copies of the main court's papers, authenticated by the court.

## Example

Raymond lives in Ohio and owns a lake cabin in Michigan. After he dies, his daughter, as executor, opens probate in Ohio, then files an ancillary case in the Michigan county where the cabin sits so that she has authority to sell it and sign a deed.

## Why it matters

Two proceedings mean two sets of filing fees, possibly two attorneys, and months of added delay. Heirs often find out only when they try to sell the out-of-state property. The cost can be avoided by holding the property in a funded [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) or by using a [Transfer-on-death deed](/glossary/transfer-on-death-deed) where the state allows one. For the process in detail, see [ancillary probate explained](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) and [owning property in another state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).
