A will names an [Executor](/glossary/executor), but naming is not enough. After a judge accepts the will and approves the appointment, the clerk issues letters, often with an official seal. They prove to banks, brokers, title companies, and agencies that the executor can legally collect assets, sell property, and sign for the estate. When there is no will, the equivalent document, usually called letters of administration, is issued to an [Administrator](/glossary/administrator). Some courts limit letters by time or by action, and banks may require a recent certified copy.

## Example

Maya is named executor in her father's will. After a short hearing, the clerk issues letters testamentary dated that week. Maya takes three certified copies to the bank, which opens an estate account and releases the balance of her father's checking account.

## Why it matters

Without letters, an executor often cannot touch estate assets at all, so delay in filing can hold up everything else, including paying bills and selling the house. Order several certified copies, since each institution may keep one, and watch for expiration dates.
