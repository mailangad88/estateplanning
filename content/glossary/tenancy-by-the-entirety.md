Only about half of states recognize this form, and some limit it to real estate. Each spouse is treated as owning the whole property together, so neither can sell or mortgage it alone, and neither can force a sale. When one spouse dies, the other owns it outright with no probate. In many states, creditors of only one spouse cannot reach the property, though debts owed by both spouses can. The arrangement generally ends if the couple divorces or both agree to end it. Compare [Joint tenancy with right of survivorship](/glossary/joint-tenancy), where an owner can usually sell their share.

## Example

Pete and Dana, married, own their house as tenants by the entirety. When Pete is sued over a business debt of his own, the creditor cannot force a sale of the house, because Dana also owns all of it. If Pete dies, Dana holds it alone.

## Why it matters

Entireties ownership offers simple probate avoidance and some creditor protection, but it ignores your wishes at the survivor's death and may leave the property exposed if both spouses owe a debt. It also can complicate planning for tax and for Medicaid.
