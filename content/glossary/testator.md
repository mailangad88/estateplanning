The word comes from the Latin for "to bear witness." Estate lawyers use it for the author of a will, as opposed to a [Grantor (settlor, trustor)](/glossary/grantor), who creates a trust. To make a valid will, a testator must generally be an adult (usually 18) and have [Testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity), which means understanding what a will is, what property they own, and who their natural heirs are. The will must reflect the testator's own free choices, without [Undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence). It becomes effective only at death, so the testator may change or revoke it at any time before.

## Example

Oscar, 62, meets with an attorney and signs a will before two witnesses. As testator, he states his wishes: his house to his daughter, his tools to his grandson. Two years later he signs a new will that replaces the first, as he is free to do.

## Why it matters

Because the testator cannot explain anything after death, courts depend on the document and the circumstances of signing. A well-run signing, with clear wording and a record of capacity, makes the testator's wishes much more likely to be carried out.
