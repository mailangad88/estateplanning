Most states that do not use [Community property](/glossary/community-property) rules protect a surviving spouse with an elective share. If a will leaves the spouse less than a set fraction of the estate, often between one-third and one-half depending on the state and sometimes on the length of the marriage, the spouse can file a claim with the court and take that share. Some states count only probate assets; others also count trusts, joint accounts, and transfers made shortly before death. Deadlines for electing are short, commonly a few months after the will is admitted.

## Example

Jack, in his second marriage, leaves his entire estate to his two children from his first marriage and nothing to his wife Carol. After he dies, Carol files an election with the probate court and receives her statutory fraction of the estate, which reduces what his children take.

## Why it matters

People are often surprised that a spouse cannot be fully disinherited in most states without the spouse's written waiver. A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement can waive this right. In blended families, planners often use trusts to provide for a spouse while keeping the principal for children.
