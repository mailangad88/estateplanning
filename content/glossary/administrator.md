An administrator does the same job an [Executor](/glossary/executor) does, but the court chooses the person instead of the deceased person naming them in a will. Courts appoint administrators when someone dies [Intestate](/glossary/intestate), when the will names no executor, or when the named executor has died, declined, or cannot serve. State law usually ranks who gets first call, often starting with a surviving spouse and then adult children. Many courts require the administrator to post a bond, which is a type of insurance protecting the estate, and to receive [official court letters](/glossary/letters-testamentary) (often called letters of administration) before acting.

## Example

Hector dies at 71 with no will. His wife Lucia asks the court to appoint her, and the judge issues letters of administration after she posts a modest bond. With those letters, Lucia can open an estate bank account, collect his final paycheck, sell his truck, and pay his debts before dividing what remains under state intestacy rules.

## Why it matters

An administrator has no instructions from the deceased, so every choice about who gets what follows a statute rather than a person's wishes. The process is usually slower and costs more than serving under a will, because of the bond and added court oversight. Naming an executor in a will lets you pick someone you trust and often waives the bond.
