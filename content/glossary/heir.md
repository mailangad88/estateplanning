Strictly speaking, heirs are the relatives whom the state's intestacy statute names as entitled to inherit when there is no will. Order of priority typically runs from spouse and children to parents, siblings, and more distant kin. Heirs are different from a [Beneficiary](/glossary/beneficiary), who is someone named in a will, trust, or account. In everyday speech the words are used loosely, and a will's beneficiary may be called an heir. Still, when a will exists, an heir who is left out generally has no right to a share unless a special rule applies.

## Example

Greta dies with no will, a husband, and two adult children. Under her state's rules, her husband and children are her heirs and divide her probate property according to a fixed formula. Her best friend, whom she loved like a sister, receives nothing.

## Why it matters

Heirs often have to be located and given notice in probate, which can extend the timeline when a family tree is large or relatives cannot be found. Making a will or trust lets you choose who inherits instead of leaving it to a formula.
