Article XIa of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/11a-3) allows a plenary guardianship only when the court finds, by clear and convincing evidence, that the adult is totally without capacity to care for self or manage property. The guardian of the person makes health and living decisions, and the guardian of the estate manages money under court reporting. The guardian reports annually and needs court approval for major steps, such as selling a home. Because it removes rights, the statute tells courts to use limited guardianship instead whenever that is enough.

## Example

After a severe brain injury, Warren, 52, cannot recognize family or understand any decision. A physician's report supports a plenary guardianship, and his sister is appointed guardian of both his person and his estate.

## Why it matters

Plenary orders are uncommon, since most adults keep some abilities. The ward may ask the court to change the order if capacity improves. A less restrictive path, such as a power of attorney, may avoid court.
