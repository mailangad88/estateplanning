In community property states, income and assets acquired during a marriage generally belong half to each spouse, no matter whose name is on the title. Property owned before marriage, plus gifts and inheritances received by one spouse, is usually separate property. Nine states, including California, Texas, and Arizona, follow this system, and a few others offer an optional version. Most other states use "common law" rules, where title matters more and surviving spouses are protected by an [Elective share](/glossary/elective-share).

## Example

Dev and Mia live in Arizona. Dev earns the salary and the house is in his name alone. Because they bought it during the marriage, the house is community property and Mia owns half. In his will, Dev can leave only his half to someone else.

## Why it matters

Community property changes who can leave what. A spouse generally cannot give away the other spouse's half in a will. It also affects taxes: at the first spouse's death, both halves of community property typically get a [Step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which can cut capital gains tax for the survivor. Moving between states can change how assets are classified.
