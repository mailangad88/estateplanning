Many beneficiary forms have a line for a primary beneficiary and a line for contingent ones. The contingent beneficiary receives nothing unless the primary beneficiary dies before the owner, cannot be found, or [disclaims](/glossary/disclaimer) the gift. You can name several contingent beneficiaries and assign percentages. If every named person is gone, the account usually falls to default rules in the plan or to your [Probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate), which can mean delay and, for retirement accounts, faster taxes.

## Example

Sofia names her husband as primary beneficiary of her $400,000 IRA and leaves the contingent line blank. They are in a car accident and he dies first. With no backup, the IRA goes to her estate under the plan's default rules instead of directly to her two children.

## Why it matters

A blank contingent line is a common, avoidable gap. Name backups on every retirement account, life insurance policy, and bank account that allows it, and think about what happens if a backup is a minor child, who cannot manage money directly. A trust or a custodian can solve that.
