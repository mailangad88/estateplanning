A will commonly says that the named executor serves without surety on the bond. Under the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/12), the court generally honors that direction, although it may still require a bond if there is cause, such as doubts about the executor's honesty or finances, or if a creditor or heir shows a need. When there is no will, heirs may sign consents, but the court decides. Waiving the bond saves the premium and underwriting time but removes a layer of protection for beneficiaries.

## Example

Maya's will names her brother Jon as executor and says he need not post a surety bond. The court issues letters of office after he takes the oath, and he avoids a bond premium that would have run about $500 a year.

## Why it matters

The waiver reflects trust, and it should be a deliberate choice rather than boilerplate. A testator with doubts about the executor, or with many warring heirs, may prefer to keep the bond.
