Yesterday you listed what you own. Today you check one column of that list: who is named as beneficiary.

This is often the most eye-opening day of the course.

## Why beneficiary forms matter so much

A beneficiary designation is a form you fill out with a bank, insurer or retirement plan. It says who gets the account when you die.

That form usually wins over your will. If your will leaves everything to your spouse, but your life insurance names your brother, your brother usually gets the insurance. The guide to [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) explains why.

For many people, retirement accounts and life insurance are their largest assets. So these forms can matter more than any other document.

## Which accounts have beneficiary forms

- Life insurance, including policies through work
- 401(k), 403(b) and 457 plans
- IRAs and Roth IRAs
- Pensions and annuities
- Bank accounts with a payable-on-death beneficiary
- Brokerage accounts with a transfer-on-death beneficiary
- Health savings accounts

Some states also allow [transfer-on-death](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death) designations for cars or real estate.

## What to look for

When you look up each form, check four things:

1. **Who is the primary beneficiary?** This is the first person in line.
2. **Is there a backup?** This is called a contingent beneficiary. It matters if the first person dies before you.
3. **Is anyone out of date?** Look for former spouses, deceased relatives or people you no longer want to include.
4. **Is a minor named?** A child under 18 usually cannot receive the money directly. A court may need to appoint someone to manage it. See [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Common surprises

People often find:

- An ex-spouse still listed on an old policy
- A parent named on a 401(k) from their first job
- No beneficiary at all, so the account goes to the estate
- Young children named directly
- A form that does not match what their will says

Do not change anything yet unless you are certain. Some changes, such as naming a trust, are worth discussing with an attorney first. If you are going through a divorce, there may be a court order that limits changes.

## How to find the information

Most accounts let you check online. Look for "beneficiaries" in your account settings. For workplace plans, ask HR or check the plan's website. For life insurance, call the company or check your annual statement.

If you cannot find it, write "unknown" and note the company. That is useful too.

## A quick example

Ben checks his five accounts in about 40 minutes:

| Account | Primary | Backup | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Work 401(k) | Wife | None | Add the kids as backup? |
| Old IRA | Ex-wife | None | Needs fixing |
| Term life | Wife | Kids | Kids are 6 and 9 |
| Savings | None | None | Goes to estate |
| HSA | Wife | None | Fine |

Two items stand out. His ex-wife is still on the IRA. His young children are named on the life insurance without a trust. Both go on his list for the attorney.

## Today's task

Look up the current beneficiary on each retirement account and life insurance policy and write it down. Add bank and brokerage accounts if you have time.
