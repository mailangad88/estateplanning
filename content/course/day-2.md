Yesterday you wrote down what you want your plan to do. Today you list the people it is for.

This sounds simple. It is also one of the most useful things you can bring to an attorney.

## Why your people come first

Almost every decision in an estate plan is about a person. Who inherits. Who manages things. Who raises your children. Who speaks for you at the hospital.

Your family structure also changes what the law assumes. A spouse, children from an earlier relationship, stepchildren or a partner you are not married to can each be treated very differently. State law decides who inherits if you have no plan, and that varies by state.

## Who to include on your list

### Your immediate family

- Spouse or partner, and whether you are married
- Children, including stepchildren and adopted children
- Grandchildren, if you might include them

For each, note their full legal name, age or date of birth, and city. For children, note whether they are minors.

### Others who depend on you

Think beyond your household. A parent you support, a sibling with a disability, or a friend you help each month may matter for your plan.

If someone on your list has a disability or receives benefits, make a note. An inheritance can affect benefits like Medicaid or SSI. A [special needs trust](/guides/special-needs-trusts) is one way families commonly handle this.

### People and causes you want to include

This could be a close friend, a godchild, a church or a charity. Write down the name, and for a charity, its full name and city.

### People you want to leave out

If you plan to leave out a child or a close relative, note it. Attorneys usually want to know so the plan can be drafted clearly.

## Things to flag for your attorney

Some family situations need more care. Mark any that apply:

- A blended family, with children from more than one relationship
- A child or beneficiary with special needs
- A beneficiary who struggles with money, addiction or debt
- Family members who do not get along
- A partner you are not married to
- Family living outside the country

None of these are problems. They just shape the plan. The guide to [estate planning for blended families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families) covers one of the most common ones.

## A quick example

Tom is 55 and remarried. His list looks like this:

| Name | Relationship | Age | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Karen Lee | Wife | 52 | Married 2019 |
| Alex Lee | Son, first marriage | 24 | Lives in Denver |
| Sophie Lee | Daughter, first marriage | 19 | In college |
| Ryan Park | Stepson | 16 | Karen's son |
| Margaret Lee | Mother | 81 | I help with her rent |

Next to Sophie he wrote: "not good with money yet." Next to Margaret he wrote: "would she be OK if I died first?"

Those notes are exactly what an attorney wants to hear about.

## Today's task

Make a list of your family members and anyone else you might include in your plan. Add full names and contact details. A simple table like Tom's works well.

Keep it honest and private. You do not need to decide anything yet. Tomorrow you will look at what you own.
