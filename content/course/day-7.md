You made it to day seven. Over the past week you have done the groundwork most people put off for years.

Today you put it all in one place. Then we walk through what usually happens when you meet an estate planning attorney.

## What you have so far

If you did each day's task, you now have:

1. **Your goals:** the three things you most want your plan to do.
2. **Your people:** family, beneficiaries and anyone who depends on you.
3. **Your assets:** what you own and owe, and how each is held.
4. **Your beneficiaries:** who each account names today.
5. **Your decision-makers:** a first choice and backup for each role.
6. **Your health care wishes:** a few sentences on the care you want.

If you missed a day, that is fine. Bring what you have.

## Build your folder

Put everything in one folder, paper or digital. Add copies of these if you have them:

- Any existing will, trust or powers of attorney
- Recent statements for major accounts
- Deeds to any real estate
- Life insurance policy summaries
- Business documents, such as an operating agreement
- A prenuptial agreement or divorce decree, if you have one

The [documents to gather before your consult](/checklists/documents-to-gather-before-your-consult) checklist lists these in one place.

## Write down your questions

Your questions are as important as your lists. Some people commonly ask:

- Do I need a will, a trust, or both?
- How would probate work in my state?
- How do I protect money left to my children?
- Should I change any of my beneficiary forms?
- Do I need to think about estate or state inheritance taxes?
- How often should I review my plan?
- How are your fees set, and what is included?

## What happens at a first consult

Every firm works a little differently. A first meeting commonly follows this pattern.

### The attorney learns about you

They ask about your family, your assets and your goals. Your folder answers most of this quickly.

### The attorney explains your options

They describe the documents that might fit, and why. They point out issues, such as an outdated beneficiary or a minor named directly.

### You talk about cost and timing

Many estate planning attorneys charge a flat fee for a set of documents. Ask what is included and what is not.

### Drafting and signing

If you go ahead, the attorney drafts your documents. You review drafts and ask for changes. Then you sign, usually with witnesses and a notary. Signing rules vary by state, which is one reason to work with an attorney licensed where you live.

### After signing

If you have a trust, assets often need to be moved into it. This is called funding. The guide to [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) explains it. You may also update beneficiary forms to match your new plan.

## Keep your plan current

A plan is not a one-time task. People commonly review it every few years and after big changes, such as a marriage, a birth, a divorce, a move or a death. The guide to [updating your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) covers when to look again. The [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) can nudge you.

## A quick example

Rachel finished the course in nine days, not seven. Her folder held her six lists, an old will, two account statements and her house deed. She added five questions.

At her consult, the attorney spotted that her old will named her ex-boyfriend as executor. Her 401(k) had no backup beneficiary. They talked through a will versus a trust. The meeting took an hour, and she left with a clear plan and a quote.
