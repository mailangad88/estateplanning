Welcome to day one. Over the next seven days, you will do one small task each day. By day seven, you will have most of what an attorney asks for at a first meeting.

Today is about the big picture. What does an estate plan actually do?

## An estate plan answers four questions

Most plans come down to four questions:

1. **Who gets your things when you die?**
2. **Who is in charge of handing them out?**
3. **Who raises your children if you cannot?**
4. **Who makes decisions for you if you are alive but unable to?**

The fourth question surprises people. Many think estate planning is only about death. A large part of it is about illness, accidents and old age.

## The documents most plans include

### A will

A [will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will) says who gets your property and names an executor to handle your estate. If you have minor children, it is also where you name a guardian. A will usually goes through probate, a court process for settling an estate.

### A revocable living trust

Some people use a [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained) as the main document instead. You keep control while you are alive. When you die, your trustee can usually pass assets on without probate. Whether a trust makes sense depends on your assets, your family and your state.

### Powers of attorney

A [financial power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) names someone to manage your money if you cannot. Without one, your family may need a court to give them that power.

### A health care directive

A [health care directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) names someone to make medical decisions for you. It can also say what treatment you want near the end of life. A HIPAA release usually goes with it, so doctors can talk to your family.

### Beneficiary designations

Life insurance, retirement accounts and many bank accounts pass by a beneficiary form. These forms often control more money than the will. Day four covers them.

## What happens without a plan

If you die without a will, your state's law decides who inherits. It may not match what you would have chosen. The guide on [what happens if you die without a will](/guides/what-happens-if-you-die-without-a-will) walks through it.

If you become unable to make decisions without powers of attorney, a court may appoint a guardian or conservator. That process can be slow, costly and public.

Rules vary a lot by state. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you how they apply to you.

## A quick example

Nina is 41, married, with one child. She owns a home with her husband and has a 401(k) at work. She has never signed anything.

When she writes down her three priorities, they are:

1. Make sure her sister raises her son if both parents die.
2. Make sure her husband can manage everything if she is in a coma.
3. Keep her son's inheritance safe until he is older.

Those three lines already tell an attorney a lot. They point to a will with a guardian, powers of attorney, and some kind of trust for her son.

## Today's task

Write down the three things you most want your estate plan to take care of. Use plain words. There are no wrong answers.

Some prompts if you are stuck:

- Who depends on you today?
- What would worry you most if you were in the hospital for a month?
- Is there anyone you want to make sure is included, or left out?
- Is there a home, business or family item you care about?

Keep your list somewhere you will find it. You will use it on day seven.
