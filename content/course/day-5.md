So far you have listed your goals, your people, your assets and your beneficiaries. Today you choose who will be in charge.

These choices often take the most thought. You do not need to be final today. A first draft is enough.

## The roles in most plans

### Executor

Your executor settles your estate after you die. They gather assets, pay bills and taxes, and distribute what is left under your will. The guide to [choosing an executor](/guides/choosing-an-executor) covers the role in detail.

### Trustee

If you have a trust, your successor trustee manages it after you die or if you cannot. If you leave money in trust for children, the trustee may serve for years. See [choosing a trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee).

### Guardian for minor children

If you have children under 18, your guardian would raise them if both parents die. The guide to [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children) explains how courts treat your choice.

### Financial agent

Under a [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney), your agent manages your money if you cannot. They might pay bills, deal with the bank, or sell property.

### Health care agent

Your health care agent makes medical decisions if you cannot. Day six covers this role in more depth.

## What makes a good choice

The best person is not always the closest relative. People commonly look for:

- **Trustworthiness.** They will follow your wishes, not their own.
- **Good judgment.** They can make decisions under stress.
- **Organisation.** Executors and trustees deal with paperwork and deadlines.
- **Availability.** They have time and are healthy enough to serve.
- **Location.** Some roles are easier for someone nearby. Some states have rules for out-of-state executors.
- **Relationships.** They can work with the rest of the family.

One person can fill several roles. Or you can split them. A common split is one person as guardian and another as trustee, so one raises the children and another manages their money.

## Always name a backup

People move, get sick, or decide they cannot serve. A backup for each role avoids a gap. Without one, a court may choose.

## Ask before you name

Many people talk to their choices before naming them. It is a big job. Some people say no, and it is better to know now.

## A quick example

Mia and Jon have two young children. Their draft looks like this:

| Role | First choice | Backup |
|---|---|---|
| Guardian | Mia's sister, Ana | Jon's parents |
| Trustee for kids | Jon's brother, Eli | Mia's friend Dana |
| Executor | Each other | Eli |
| Financial agent | Each other | Ana |
| Health care agent | Each other | Ana |

They chose Eli as trustee because he is careful with money. Ana is warm and lives near the kids' school, but she has said money stresses her out. Splitting the roles plays to each person's strengths.

## Today's task

Write down a first choice and a backup for each role in your plan. Add a short note on why. Skip any role that does not apply to you.
