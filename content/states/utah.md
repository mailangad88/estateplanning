Utah is a Uniform Probate Code state, which means most estates can be settled with limited court involvement. Combined with no state estate or inheritance tax, that makes planning here less about tax and more about keeping the process simple and the family organized.

For the national guide, see our [probate overview](/learn/probate).

## Wills in Utah

A Utah will is valid when you sign it in front of two witnesses who also sign. Notarization is not needed, but a [self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) signed before a notary allows the court to accept the will without witness testimony. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Utah recognizes a [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will) when the signature and the material provisions are in your own handwriting. A Utah court may accept it with evidence that it is your handwriting, but disputes about handwriting are common. If you wrote a will yourself, have it re-signed with witnesses. Our article on [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) explains the risks.

Utah is a common law property state. A surviving spouse is protected by an elective share of the augmented estate, a concept that counts some transfers made during life. The share is scaled to the length of the marriage, so a longer marriage means a larger protected share. Confirm the percentage with an attorney.

With no will, the spouse inherits everything only when all the decedent's children are also the spouse's. If the deceased had a child from another relationship, the spouse receives a reduced share and that child inherits part directly. That is a frequent surprise for stepfamilies, and a reason to read [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Utah

Probate is filed in the district court of the county where the person lived, under the Utah Uniform Probate Code. Utah allows both informal and formal probate.

**Informal probate** is handled through the court clerk, called the registrar. The applicant files the original will and an application, and the registrar appoints the personal representative without a hearing. The representative must send notice to heirs and publish notice to creditors. Creditors have a limited time to submit claims.

**Formal probate** is a court proceeding with a hearing, used when the will is challenged or when someone wants a binding ruling.

An executor can also request **supervised administration**, though it is rare. Under the Uniform Probate Code, the personal representative generally has the power to sell, pay and distribute without asking the court, which shortens timelines. Our guide to [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) lays out the usual steps.

Utah offers a small estate affidavit for modest estates. As of our last check the limit is around $100,000, though you should confirm the figure and any waiting period. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

What slows Utah estates down is usually real estate, creditor notice periods and family conflict, not court backlog. Utah families often own land in multiple states, so check [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Utah

Utah's informal probate is relatively inexpensive, so avoiding probate is mostly about privacy and incapacity planning. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) keeps the terms private, because a will becomes a public record when filed. It also lets a successor trustee step in if you become incapacitated. Utah has adopted the Uniform Trust Code, so trustees have clear duties.

Beneficiary designations, payable-on-death accounts and joint ownership pass property outside probate. Utah also has procedures for real estate beneficiary transfers, but confirm the current rules and recording requirements before relying on one; see [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed). For other tools, see [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

Many Utah families have large families, young children and a mix of retirement accounts, so naming a [guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) and a trustee for minors is often the most important part of the plan. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).

## Taxes

Utah has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026, and most Utah families will not reach it. Heirs of retirement accounts may owe income tax when they withdraw. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Utah

A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) in Utah must be properly signed and acknowledged before a notary. Utah has a statute governing agent duties and powers, and gifting or real estate powers should be spelled out.

For healthcare, Utah has an Advance Health Care Directive form in statute that names an agent and states treatment wishes, and a separate POLST form for people with serious illness. Utah's directive must be signed before two witnesses or a notary. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).

Neighbors with different rules include [Idaho](/estate-planning/idaho) and [Colorado](/estate-planning/colorado).
