Florida is a popular state for retirees, and its estate laws reflect that. The constitution restricts how you can leave your home, handwritten wills are invalid, and a special deed fills the gap left by the lack of transfer-on-death deeds. This guide covers what is specific to Florida.

## Wills in Florida

A Florida will must be signed by the testator, who must be at least 18 and of sound mind, in front of two witnesses. The witnesses must sign in the testator's presence and in each other's presence. No notary is required, though a self-proving affidavit signed before a notary makes probate smoother. Florida does not accept handwritten wills that lack witnesses, so see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) and [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills). Florida has also enacted an Electronic Wills Act, which allows electronically signed wills with specific safeguards.

A surviving spouse can claim an elective share of 30 percent of the elective estate. A spouse who was not mentioned in a will signed before the marriage may also have a claim.

The homestead rule is the most unusual part. If you own a home in Florida and are survived by a spouse or a minor child, you generally cannot leave the home to anyone else by will. A spouse may take a life estate with the children taking the remainder, unless the spouse waived those rights in writing. This is a common trap in second marriages; see protecting children from a first marriage.

With no will, a spouse takes the whole estate when all children are also the spouse's, and one half otherwise. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Florida

Probate is handled in the circuit court's probate division under Chapters 731 to 735 of the Florida Statutes. Florida has some rules that surprise families. Anyone who holds the original will must deposit it with the clerk within ten days after learning of the death. A personal representative must be represented by a Florida attorney in a formal administration. A nonresident can serve only if closely related to the decedent. Creditors generally have three months after publication to file claims, and the estate must also deal with unknown creditors and Medicaid estate recovery; see dealing with debts in probate.

Florida offers summary administration for smaller estates, or when the decedent has been dead more than two years, and a short process to release a decedent's property when the only probate asset is a modest one. These involve a petition and a court order. Check the current dollar limit with an attorney. See [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for how simplified procedures work in general, and the [probate pillar](/learn/probate) for formal administration.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Florida

Florida's Trust Code follows the Uniform Trust Code, and a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is widely used, especially by people who split time between states (out-of-state property). Fund the trust (how to fund a trust).

Because Florida lacks a statutory TOD deed, many homeowners use an **enhanced life estate deed**, nicknamed a Lady Bird deed. You keep the right to live in, sell or mortgage the home, and at death the property passes to the named beneficiary without probate. A Lady Bird deed can also be useful in Medicaid planning because the home may avoid estate recovery, though that is a legal question to resolve with a Florida elder law attorney. Compare the general tradeoffs in [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

Married couples often hold the home as tenants by the entirety, which passes automatically to the survivor and protects against one spouse's creditors; see [tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety).

Florida's seasonal residents should make sure their documents and domicile line up. If you spend winters in Florida and summers elsewhere, your state of domicile controls which probate court has authority, and a declaration of domicile filed with the county clerk can help show your intent. Also have your documents reviewed after moving to Florida, since an out-of-state power of attorney or health care form may not meet Florida's signing rules.

## Taxes

Florida has no estate tax, no inheritance tax and no state income tax. Only the federal estate tax can apply, above $15,000,000 per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Florida

A Florida [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) must be signed in front of two witnesses and a notary. Florida no longer allows springing powers of attorney that take effect only upon incapacity, so a Florida power of attorney is effective when signed; see springing vs immediate power of attorney. For health care, Florida uses a designation of health care surrogate and a living will, each signed before two witnesses, and a yellow Do Not Resuscitate Order form for outside the hospital. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and living will.
