## Wills in Maryland

A Maryland will must be in writing, signed by a testator who is at least 18 and of sound mind, and signed by two credible witnesses who see you sign. No notary is required. Unlike many states, Maryland does not have a formal self-proving affidavit, so witnesses may need to be located if a will is contested. Pick younger, reachable witnesses who will not inherit. Our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) covers the national basics.

Maryland does not recognize an ordinary handwritten will unless it is witnessed. There are narrow exceptions for service members, so an unwitnessed note rarely works.

If you die without a will, the result depends on your children. A spouse takes half when any child is a minor, with the children splitting the rest, but takes everything when all children are adults. Read [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for the general rules.

A surviving spouse who is unhappy with the will can elect against it and take one-third of the net estate when there are surviving descendants, or one-half when there are none.

## Probate in Maryland

Maryland probate begins at the **Register of Wills** in the county, an elected official who accepts the will and opens the estate. Contested matters go to the Orphans' Court, whose judges are elected in most places, while in Montgomery and Harford counties the Circuit Court sits as the Orphans' Court. Most estates are **regular estates**, open for at least a few months and often longer, and the personal representative must follow a set schedule: appointing, notifying heirs, publishing notice to creditors, filing an inventory, and filing accounts. The creditor claim period is generally six months after the date of appointment.

Maryland also has a **small estate** track. When the value of the probate estate is $50,000 or less, or $100,000 or less if the spouse is the sole heir, the family can use a simplified filing with a shorter timeline and lower fees. The estate is valued at the amount of probate assets only, not joint property or accounts with beneficiaries. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for the general idea, and the [probate guide](/learn/probate) for the larger process.

Because Maryland's inheritance tax is collected through the Register of Wills, the personal representative often pays the tax before distributing, which can add steps.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Maryland

Maryland has adopted the Maryland Trust Act, based on the Uniform Trust Code, so a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is a standard option. A trust avoids probate, but it does not avoid the inheritance tax, because the tax applies to non-probate transfers too. That surprises many families.

Married couples often hold a home as **tenants by the entirety**, which passes the home to the survivor without probate and protects against one spouse's creditors. Whether Maryland offers a transfer on death deed is a point to confirm with an attorney. See [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) and [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) for your options.

## Taxes

Maryland's tax picture is the most unusual in the country. It has **both**:

| Tax | Who pays | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Estate tax | The estate | Applies to estates over $5 million (not indexed to the federal exemption) |
| Inheritance tax | The heir | 10% on heirs who are not exempt close relatives; applies at any estate size |

An estate worth $5.5 million can owe Maryland tax even though it owes no federal tax, since the federal exemption is $15 million per person in 2026. Maryland also allows a state-level portability election for married couples, explained in [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples). If you leave something to a niece, a friend or an unmarried partner, 10% comes out of that gift unless your documents say otherwise. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Maryland

Maryland has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which governs what an agent can do and requires the document to be signed and notarized or witnessed. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

For health decisions, Maryland's Health Care Decisions Act lets you appoint a health care agent and give instructions in an advance directive, which generally requires two witnesses. Maryland also uses MOLST forms, which are medical orders signed by a clinician for people with serious illness. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).
