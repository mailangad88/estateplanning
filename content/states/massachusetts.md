## Wills in Massachusetts

A Massachusetts will must be in writing, signed by you at 18 or older and of sound mind, and signed by two witnesses. No notary is required, but the Massachusetts Uniform Probate Code permits a self-proving affidavit that lets the court accept the will without witness testimony. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for a general walkthrough.

Massachusetts does not honor an unwitnessed handwritten will, so a note in your drawer will not control who gets your home.

Without a will, M.G.L. c. 190B section 2-102 decides how a spouse and children share. The spouse's share is a fixed first amount plus part of the balance, and it depends on whether the children are also the spouse's, so check the current numbers with an attorney. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

A spouse who is not satisfied with the will can waive it and claim a statutory share. If the decedent left children, the share is one-third of the estate. If there are no children, the spouse gets a fixed first amount plus half of the balance. The election has a short deadline.

## Probate in Massachusetts

Massachusetts adopted its Uniform Probate Code in 2012. Cases are filed in the **Probate and Family Court**, a department of the trial court with a division in each county and a Registry of Probate. The code offers two tracks:

1. **Informal probate.** The personal representative files forms with the Registry and gets appointed without a hearing if no one objects. Most routine estates use this track.
2. **Formal probate.** A judge decides on the will and the appointment after notice to heirs, used when there is a dispute or a need for a binding order.

Informal proceedings are not the same as unsupervised ones. Even so, the personal representative must give notice to heirs, publish notice, file an inventory within 90 days, and file accounts later. Creditors generally have one year from death to bring claims.

For very small estates, **voluntary administration** is available. When personal property is $25,000 or less, not counting one motor vehicle, a person can use a short statement to collect assets. Real estate is not included, and a house usually changes the answer. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) and the [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Massachusetts

Revocable trusts are especially common in Massachusetts, for three reasons. They avoid a court record of your will, they help with incapacity, and they are a tool for larger estates that face the state estate tax. The Massachusetts Uniform Trust Code, Chapter 203E, governs them. Read about the [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) and why a trust alone does not reduce tax in [does a trust avoid taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes).

Massachusetts has not historically offered a general transfer on death deed, so confirm current law with an attorney before using one. Many families hold a home as tenants by the entirety or in a trust. Massachusetts also offers a declared homestead that protects a home from some creditors, a separate topic from probate. For other tools, see [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

## Taxes

Massachusetts has no inheritance tax, but its **estate tax** reaches modest estates. Since 2023 the threshold has been $2 million, and a credit limits the tax on estates just above that amount. The threshold is not tied to the federal exemption of $15 million per person in 2026, so a family with a home, retirement savings and life insurance can owe Massachusetts tax with no federal tax. Massachusetts does not offer portability, so a married couple who leaves everything to the survivor may waste the first spouse's $2 million exemption, which is why couples often use a credit shelter trust. The return is due nine months after death. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Massachusetts

The Massachusetts Uniform Probate Code governs durable powers of attorney in Article V. A durable power of attorney does not need a statutory form, and it should be notarized so that banks and the Registry of Deeds accept it. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

The main medical document is the **health care proxy** under M.G.L. c. 201D, which requires two adult witnesses and names an agent with a backup. Massachusetts has no living will statute, but you can add a statement of wishes, and the state uses MOLST forms as medical orders for people with serious illness. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).
