Texas is one of the most flexible states for probate and one of the most confusing for community property. The state has no estate tax, a fast path to transfer a home, and options for both formal and nearly invisible probate. How you use them depends on whether you are married, what you own and how much you trust the people you are naming.

For the national background, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate) and article on [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

## Wills in Texas

A Texas will must be in writing, signed by you, and witnessed by two people who are at least 14 and who sign in your presence. Notarization is not required, but adding a self-proving affidavit before a notary lets the court admit the will without calling witnesses. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Texas recognizes a [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will) if the whole document is in your handwriting and signed. A self-proving affidavit is possible for it too. Even so, a witnessed will is easier to prove, and mistakes in handwritten language lead to disputes.

Texas is a [community property](/glossary/community-property) state, which changes how a will works. Property acquired during marriage is generally owned half by each spouse, so you can only leave your half of community property in your will. Separate property, such as inherited items or property owned before marriage, is entirely yours to give. Texas has no elective share; spouses are protected by community property, homestead rights, and family allowances.

With no will, the answer depends on the type of property. When all children are from both spouses, community property goes to the surviving spouse. When the deceased had children from another relationship, the spouse keeps half of the community property and those children inherit the rest. Separate property is split in more complicated ways, with the spouse often receiving only a life estate in real property. For blended families, that is a large reason to have a will. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Texas

Texas offers several levels of probate, which is why the process is faster than in many states.

**Independent administration** is the usual route. A will can name an independent executor, and after the court issues letters, the executor acts with minimal supervision, filing an inventory and giving notice to creditors but not asking the judge before most actions. Without a will, heirs can often agree to independent administration.

**Muniment of title** is a probate that does not appoint an executor. The court admits the will as proof that the named beneficiaries own the property. It requires that the estate has no debts other than those secured by real estate, and it is quick and inexpensive.

**Small estate affidavit** lets heirs claim assets of estates of up to $75,000, not counting the homestead and exempt property, by swearing to the facts before a judge. Confirm current limits with an attorney. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

Probate courts differ by county: a county court, constitutional county court, or a statutory probate court, depending on size. Texas generally requires that a will be offered for probate within four years of death. The most common timeline drivers are creditor notices, the need for a lawyer for each step, and heirs who disagree.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Texas

Texas has one of the most useful tools for homeowners: the **transfer on death deed**, authorized under Estates Code Chapter 114. You sign and record a deed that names a beneficiary, you keep full control while you are alive, and the property passes when you die without probate. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

Married couples can sign a **community property with right of survivorship agreement**. It lets the survivor take community assets without probate. Bank accounts, retirement accounts and life insurance pass by beneficiary designation.

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is still useful for larger estates, for people with property in several states, and for managing property if you become incapacitated. Texas is also well regarded for business ownership structures, so owners of LLCs should read [LLC ownership at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death).

## Taxes

Texas has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026. Community property gets a full [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) on both halves at the first spouse's death, a notable advantage of living in a community property state. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for how Texas compares.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Texas

Texas provides a statutory durable power of attorney form, and the document must be signed and notarized. Texas also gives agents statutory duties and powers that can be listed on the form. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

For health care, Texas uses three forms under its Health and Safety Code: a Medical Power of Attorney, a Directive to Physicians and Family or Surrogates (its living will), and an out-of-hospital DNR. The medical power of attorney is signed before two qualified witnesses or a notary. Texas also lets family members make decisions in a defined order when no document exists. See [DNR and POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).

Neighbors with different rules include [Oklahoma](/estate-planning/oklahoma) and [Louisiana](/estate-planning/louisiana).
