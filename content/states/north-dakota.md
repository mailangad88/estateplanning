North Dakota's laws are friendly to modern planning. It is a Uniform Probate Code state, it lets a notary stand in for witnesses on a will, it has an electronic wills law, and it offers an optional community property trust for married couples. Many of these rules are newer than the ones in neighboring states.

## Wills in North Dakota

A North Dakota will must be signed by you and either witnessed by two people or acknowledged by you before a notary public. That is a real difference from most states, where two witnesses are required. If you use witnesses, adding a self-proving affidavit lets the court accept the will without contacting them. The [general signing rules](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) still help, such as picking witnesses who do not inherit.

A will is valid with no witnesses if your signature and the material portions are in your handwriting. See [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) for the risks. North Dakota also adopted the Uniform Electronic Wills Act, so a will can be an electronic record that is signed and either witnessed by two people or notarized. North Dakota has no harmless-error rule, so mistakes cannot be repaired by proving what you meant.

If you die without a will, your spouse takes everything when all your children are also the spouse's. In a [blended family](/learn/blended-families), the spouse takes a fixed amount plus half of the remainder, and your children take the rest. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will). A spouse also has an elective share that grows with the length of the marriage, plus homestead and family allowances.

## Probate in North Dakota

Probate goes through the district court in the county of residence under Title 30.1, North Dakota's Uniform Probate Code. Informal probate is available, where a registrar appoints the personal representative on an application, and formal probate is used when someone objects. See [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) for the general sequence.

North Dakota raised its small estate threshold to $100,000 beginning in August 2025. A successor can present an affidavit to the person holding the decedent's personal property after a 30-day waiting period, with no court filing. The affidavit does not work if the estate includes any real property, however small the interest. A family with a house or farmland will typically use informal probate. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit). Many North Dakota families also own minerals and land in several counties or in Montana or Minnesota, which can bring [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) and mineral-rights questions worth discussing with a lawyer.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in North Dakota

North Dakota authorizes transfer-on-death deeds, so a homeowner can name a beneficiary while keeping control, and revoke the deed later. It must be recorded before death. Our guide to [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) covers the tradeoffs. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is still the better tool for farm families, owners of land in other states, and parents with young or vulnerable heirs. Retirement account and life insurance beneficiary designations pass outside probate.

North Dakota also has an opt-in community property trust law. Spouses who transfer assets into a trust that elects community property treatment may obtain a full [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) on both halves at the first death, which can reduce capital gains tax for highly appreciated assets such as farmland. This is a complex structure, and confirm the requirements with an attorney.

## Taxes

North Dakota has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax applies, to estates above the federal exemption. Families with large farm or ranch operations may still be affected by federal rules and income-tax basis questions. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for how state systems differ.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in North Dakota

North Dakota has a statute governing powers of attorney and a statutory health care directive under chapter 23-06.5, which combines naming a health care agent and stating treatment wishes. The directive generally needs two witnesses or a notary. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) should be notarized so it works for land transactions. A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) names the person who can decide when you cannot. Confirm current forms with a North Dakota attorney.
