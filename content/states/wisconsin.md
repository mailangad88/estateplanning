Wisconsin is different from nearly every neighboring state: it treats married couples' assets as marital property, a system modeled on community property. That affects how a will works, how property passes at the first death and which documents a married couple should sign.

For the national picture, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate) and the glossary entry on [community property](/glossary/community-property).

## Wills in Wisconsin

A Wisconsin will must be in writing, signed by you, and witnessed by two people who watched you sign and who sign within a reasonable time. Notarization is not needed. A notarized [self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) lets the court accept the will without witnesses. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Wisconsin does not generally recognize a handwritten, unwitnessed will. It does, however, offer a feature many states lack: you can deposit your signed will with the register in probate in your county for a modest fee. The court keeps it safe, and your family can find it after your death. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

The Marital Property Act means that, during marriage, income and property acquired by either spouse is generally owned half by each. A will can dispose of your half of the marital property and your individual property, which includes gifts, inheritances and assets from before the marriage. Spouses can change the classification with a marital property agreement, which is a common planning tool. A surviving spouse also has a deferred marital property elective share that can reach one-half of the combined deferred marital property.

With no will, the spouse takes all of the decedent's marital property. For individual property, the spouse takes all if all the children are the spouse's, and one-half if the decedent had children from another relationship. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Wisconsin

Probate is handled by the circuit court through the register in probate in the county where the person lived. Wisconsin offers formal administration, informal administration for estates that need less court involvement, and several shortcuts.

In informal administration, the personal representative acts with limited court supervision, after the court issues letters. The estate must give notice to creditors, and Wisconsin sets a claims deadline that is announced by the court. The representative also files an inventory with the court and, in larger estates, a final account. Our guide to [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) describes the stages.

For smaller estates, Wisconsin offers a **transfer by affidavit** for estates of about $50,000 or less, and **summary settlement** and **summary assignment** for other modest estates. Confirm the current limits with an attorney. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

Wisconsin estates are often delayed by the claims period, real estate that must be sold, and out-of-state property. See [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Wisconsin

Wisconsin has a rich set of non-probate tools. A **survivorship marital property agreement** passes the couple's marital property to the survivor at the first death without probate. A **transfer-on-death beneficiary designation for real estate** under Wis. Stat. 705.15 lets you name a beneficiary for a home or land and record the form before death. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

Payable-on-death accounts and beneficiary designations work as they do elsewhere. Wisconsin also has a **Transfer on Death** registration for securities. Our guide to [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) lists the main tools.

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is still helpful for people with property in more than one state, for blended families, or for those who want to manage property if they become incapacitated. Wisconsin adopted a Uniform Trust Code in Chapter 701.

## Taxes

Wisconsin has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026. Marital property gets a full [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) on both halves at the first death, a notable benefit of Wisconsin's system. If you own land in Minnesota, where the estate tax applies at a much lower threshold, check [Minnesota](/estate-planning/minnesota). See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Wisconsin

Wisconsin has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act in Chapter 244. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) must be signed before a notary or two witnesses. Wisconsin also provides statutory forms for **Power of Attorney for Health Care** and **Authorization for Final Disposition**. Healthcare documents are signed before two witnesses.

Wisconsin also has a Declaration to Physicians, its living will form, and a separate form for a do-not-resuscitate order that uses a bracelet. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [DNR and POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).
