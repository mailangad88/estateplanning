## Wills in Illinois

An Illinois will must be in writing, signed by you (or by someone else at your direction and in your presence), and attested by two credible witnesses who see you sign. There is no notary requirement. Most Illinois attorneys still add an attestation clause, where the witnesses state they saw you sign and believed you of sound mind, because that clause lets the court admit the will without calling the witnesses. Our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) walks through the general steps.

Illinois does not recognize [holographic wills](/glossary/holographic-will). A handwritten note that nobody witnessed will not count, which matters for people who rely on a [handwritten or DIY will](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills). Illinois also permits electronic wills and remote witnessing under its Electronic Wills and Remote Witnesses Act, but the rules for each method are strict, so ask an attorney before signing anything on screen.

If there is no will, Illinois law decides. A married person with children leaves half to the spouse and half to the children, which surprises families who assume everything goes to the spouse. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains the order of heirs.

A spouse who is left out or given little can renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if the decedent left descendants, or one-half if not. The spouse must act within a limited time after the will is admitted.

## Probate in Illinois

Probate takes place in the circuit court of the county where the decedent lived, under the Illinois Probate Act of 1975. Illinois has a few features that families should know:

1. **Filing the will is mandatory.** Anyone holding the original will must deliver it to the circuit clerk within 30 days after learning of the death.
2. **Independent administration is the usual route.** If the will allows it and no one objects, the executor can handle most steps without court orders at each stage, which saves time and money.
3. **Creditor claims run on a fixed clock.** After the executor publishes notice, creditors have a limited window of at least six months from first publication to file claims, and that window is a major reason estates stay open for most of a year.
4. **Real estate drives the timeline.** A house in the decedent's sole name typically means opening a case.

For estates with no real estate, Illinois offers a small estate affidavit under 755 ILCS 5/25-1. For deaths on or after August 15, 2025, the limit is $150,000, and vehicles are handled separately through the Secretary of State and no longer count toward it. Our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains how affidavits work generally. For the full picture of the process, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Illinois

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is common in Illinois for people who own a house, want privacy, or want a plan for incapacity. The Illinois Trust Code, based on the Uniform Trust Code, governs these trusts.

Illinois also allows a **transfer on death instrument** for residential real estate of one to four units. You sign it like a deed, record it with the county recorder before you die, and keep the right to sell or revoke it. Compare it with other tools in our guide to [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) and the seven methods in [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate). Joint tenancy and beneficiary designations also work, but they carry risks covered there.

## Taxes

Illinois is one of a small group of states with its own estate tax, and its $4 million exclusion is much lower than the federal exclusion of $15 million per person in 2026. The Illinois computation is unusual, so even estates a little above $4 million should be run through the state calculator. Illinois has no inheritance tax. Read our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and the article on [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples), since the federal portability election does not give a second Illinois exclusion.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Illinois

Illinois uses its own Power of Attorney Act with a statutory short form for property and a statutory short form for health care. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) in Illinois has formal signing requirements, and the rules changed in recent years, so use a current form. Confirm the witness and notary requirements with an attorney.

For medical decisions, Illinois recognizes a power of attorney for health care, a living will declaration, and a POLST form for people with serious illness. Illinois also has a Health Care Surrogate Act that names who may decide when no agent exists. Read about the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and the [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).
