Alaska's estate planning laws differ from most of the country in three ways. You can validate a will with a notary, handwritten wills count, and couples can choose community property. This page explains those features and links to our national guides for the basics.

## Wills in Alaska

An adult of sound mind can make a will in Alaska by signing it in front of two witnesses, or by acknowledging it before a notary. A self-proving affidavit lets the court accept the will without tracking down witnesses. Alaska also honors a [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will) written and signed entirely in the testator's own hand, if the material provisions are handwritten. Our guide to [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) explains why you should still be careful: proof of handwriting is the first thing a relative challenges.

For the signing steps themselves, see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will). If someone dies without a will, Alaska follows UPC-style intestacy: a surviving spouse takes the whole estate when every child is also the spouse's, and a smaller share when the decedent had children with someone else. Our [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) guide describes how that plays out.

## Probate in Alaska

Probate is handled by the Superior Court. Alaska follows the Uniform Probate Code's two tracks: informal probate, handled through the court's registrar with little hearing, and formal probate, used when there is a dispute or a need for a judge's ruling. Informal probate is common when the family agrees.

For small estates, Alaska allows a successor to collect personal property with an affidavit after a waiting period, as long as the value stays under the statutory limits. Vehicles have their own limit. Real estate is excluded, so a home generally needs probate or an advance transfer plan. Confirm current limits with an attorney, as they are adjusted from time to time. Our [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) article explains the general procedure, and the [probate pillar](/learn/probate) covers the full process.

Alaskans often own property in more than one state, such as a cabin or a Lower 48 home. A property in another state may require ancillary probate there, which is one reason a trust is popular.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Alaska

Alaska has been a leader in trust law. The Alaska Trust Act allows self-settled domestic asset protection trusts, and Alaska trusts are often used by people who do not live in Alaska. Most families, however, simply need a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) to avoid probate and plan for incapacity.

The most distinctive Alaska tool is community property. Under the Alaska Community Property Act, spouses can sign a community property agreement or a community property trust. The benefit is tax-related: when the first spouse dies, both halves of community property can receive a step-up in basis, which can reduce capital gains tax when the survivor sells appreciated assets. This is a technical choice, and spouses who move from a common law state should have an Alaska attorney review it. Whether Alaska allows a transfer-on-death deed is a point to confirm with an attorney; see how to avoid probate for the general options.

Distance is a recurring Alaska issue. Executors and heirs often live outside the state, and some assets, such as a remote cabin, Native corporation shares or a fishing permit, have their own transfer rules. List every unusual asset in your planning file and ask an attorney how each one passes. Mailing delays and travel to the court registrar are another reason many Alaskans prefer a trust and a named successor trustee over a long administration.

## Taxes

Alaska does not tax estates or inheritances, and it has no state income tax. The federal estate tax applies only above $15,000,000 per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Alaska

Alaska's power of attorney statutes allow a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) that continues after incapacity; Alaska generally expects it to be acknowledged before a notary. The Alaska Health Care Decisions Act governs advance directives, including naming a health care agent and stating end-of-life wishes, and a directive is signed before a notary or witnesses. Learn the difference between the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and the [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) before you sign.
