## Wills in Louisiana

Louisiana has a different legal tradition from the rest of the country, based on French and Spanish civil law, and that shows up in how wills work. The word "will" is usually "testament," and there are two main forms.

- **Notarial testament.** You sign in front of a notary and two witnesses, and the document must include a specific attestation. It is the form most attorneys use because it is hard to challenge on technical grounds.
- **Olographic testament.** The document must be entirely in your handwriting, dated, and signed. No witnesses or notary are needed, but the court will require proof that the handwriting is yours. A typed will signed by you is not valid in Louisiana.

For the general national rules, see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will), and for the risks of the handwritten route, see [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills). Because Louisiana's requirements are strict about form, a will that works in another state may fail here.

### Forced heirship

The feature that most surprises newcomers is **forced heirship**. Children who are 23 or younger, or of any age if they have a permanent disability that prevents them from caring for themselves, are forced heirs. The law reserves a portion of your estate for them: 25% if there is one forced heir and 50% if there are two or more. You cannot override this by will unless Louisiana law recognizes a specific ground to disinherit. Adult, healthy children can be disinherited by will, which is different from civil-law countries where all children are protected.

### Intestacy and the surviving spouse

Without a will, the result depends on whether property is community or separate. Louisiana is a [community property](/glossary/community-property) state, and the decedent's half of community property goes to the children, subject to the surviving spouse's usufruct, which lasts until the spouse dies or remarries. Separate property passes to the children when there are any. With no children, the surviving spouse inherits more, depending on the family. A surviving spouse in need may also claim a marital portion in some situations. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) gives the national baseline.

## Succession in Louisiana (probate)

Louisiana calls probate a **succession**, and it is handled by the district court in the parish where the decedent lived. The process ends with a **judgment of possession**, which names heirs or legatees and puts them in possession of the property. Heirs can accept an inheritance simply or with the benefit of an inventory to protect themselves from the decedent's debts, a choice with no close counterpart elsewhere.

For smaller estates, a **small succession by affidavit** allows heirs to transfer property without a full court case if the gross value of the estate is below a ceiling set in the Code of Civil Procedure. The legislature has raised that ceiling recently, so confirm the current figure with a Louisiana attorney. If the decedent owned Louisiana property but lived elsewhere, an ancillary process may be needed; see [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) and [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property). The [probate guide](/learn/probate) explains the general process.

## Trusts and avoiding succession in Louisiana

Louisiana has its own Trust Code, and trusts work differently from the common-law version. In particular, forced heirship limits how much a trust can take away from forced heirs. Louisiana does not offer transfer on death deeds, so [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) elsewhere have no counterpart here. A common substitute is a **donation inter vivos with a reserved usufruct**, where the owner donates a home to children but keeps the right to live in it for life. Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance work normally.

## Taxes

Louisiana has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The federal estate tax applies only to estates above $15 million per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Louisiana

A power of attorney in Louisiana is called a **mandate**, and the Civil Code sets how it is created. A mandate to deal with real estate must be in writing, and many are made as notarized documents with witnesses. Confirm the formal requirements with an attorney.

For medical decisions, Louisiana recognizes a declaration (its living will) witnessed by two people, and a medical power of attorney, as well as a POLST form. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
