## Wills in Maine

Maine's probate law is based on the Uniform Probate Code and is found in Title 18-C of the Maine Revised Statutes, which took effect on September 1, 2019. To make a valid will, you must be at least 18 and of sound mind, sign the will (or direct someone to sign for you), and have two witnesses sign in your presence. A notary is not required, but a notarized self-proving affidavit lets the Probate Court admit the will without finding the witnesses. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the general steps.

Maine's rules for handwritten wills are a point to confirm with an attorney. Do not assume a handwritten note without witnesses will be honored.

If there is no will, Title 18-C decides who inherits. Under the Uniform Probate Code approach, the surviving spouse's share depends on whether all of the children are also the spouse's children, so a blended family may end up with a very different result than a first-marriage family. A spouse can also elect against a will and take an elective share of the augmented estate. The augmented estate concept counts certain non-probate assets, which makes it harder to cut a spouse out by moving property into joint names.

## Probate in Maine

Maine does not use one statewide probate court. Each of Maine's 16 counties has its own **Probate Court**, led by an elected judge and an elected register of probate. Because Maine follows the Uniform Probate Code, it offers both **informal probate**, handled by the register of probate for uncontested wills, and **formal probate**, which involves a hearing and is used when there is a dispute or a need for a binding court order. Informal probate is faster and cheaper, and many estates use it. After appointment, the personal representative gives notice to creditors, files an inventory and handles claims.

For modest estates, Maine allows a small estate affidavit to collect property without full probate. The dollar limit has changed over time, so confirm the current amount with an attorney. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for how the general idea works. In practice, Maine's timeline is shaped by the creditor notice period and by estate tax filing for larger estates, as covered in [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take). The [probate guide](/learn/probate) shows the whole process.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Maine

Maine has enacted the Uniform Trust Code as Title 18-B, so a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) follows familiar rules. Trusts are popular with Maine families who own a home on the coast or a camp on a lake, particularly if children live out of state, because a trust avoids a Maine probate case and a second ancillary case if property sits in more than one state. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) for how that works.

Maine also lets owners use joint ownership, beneficiary designations and payable on death accounts. Whether Maine allows a transfer on death deed in the form used in some other states is a point to confirm with an attorney, so check current law and read [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) for the tradeoffs of each tool.

## Taxes

Maine is one of a dozen or so states with its own estate tax, and its exemption is a fraction of the federal $15 million per person in 2026. As of recent years the Maine exemption has been about $7 million, with graduated rates, and it may be adjusted, so confirm the current figure. Maine has no inheritance tax. The state estate tax does not allow a "portability" election the way federal law does, so married couples often plan with trusts to use both spouses' Maine exemptions; see [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples). Our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) compares Maine with other states.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Maine

Maine has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act within Title 18-C, which sets default rules for agents and requires proper signing. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

For medical decisions, Maine uses an advance health care directive that can name an agent and state your wishes in one document. The signing rules include witnesses or a notary, so confirm them. Maine also has a POLST form for people with serious illness. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
