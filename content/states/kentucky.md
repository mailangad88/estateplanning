## Wills in Kentucky

Kentucky allows two kinds of will. A **witnessed will** must be written, signed by you, and signed by two witnesses in your presence. You must be 18 or older and of sound mind. No notary is required, though a notarized self-proving affidavit lets the court admit the will on paper. A **holographic will** is valid if it is entirely in your handwriting and signed, even with no witnesses. Few states are as flexible, but flexibility has a cost: families regularly fight over whether a handwritten page is genuine. See [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) for the risks, and [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the standard approach.

Kentucky also has special spousal protection through dower and curtesy. A surviving spouse who is dissatisfied with the will can reject it and take a statutory share, which includes an interest in real estate you owned at death. Prenuptial agreements often address this.

If you die without a will, a surviving spouse and children share according to Kentucky's intestacy statute, which gives the spouse a portion of the real and personal property. The details are specific to Kentucky, so confirm them with an attorney. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for the general idea.

## Probate in Kentucky

Probate takes place in district court in the county where the person lived. The executor presents the will, is appointed, and in most cases posts a bond unless the will waives it. Kentucky has two features worth knowing:

1. **The inheritance tax return is part of the process.** The personal representative generally needs to file an inheritance tax return with the Kentucky Department of Revenue, even when most heirs are exempt, and a certificate may be needed to clear certain assets.
2. **Settlement is often handled in stages.** The fiduciary files an inventory, handles claims, and later files a settlement before the estate closes.

Small estates can sometimes skip full administration by petitioning the court to dispense with it. The dollar threshold has changed, so confirm it with an attorney before relying on it. Our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains how these shortcuts generally work. For what typically delays an estate, see [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) and the [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Kentucky

Kentucky has adopted the Uniform Trust Code, so a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) works the usual way. Many Kentuckians also use a **survivorship deed**, which is a deed that names the owners and states clearly that the survivor takes the whole property. It avoids probate but exposes the property to a co-owner's creditors, as explained in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

Whether Kentucky offers a transfer on death deed is a point to confirm with an attorney, because the rules have been changing in many states; see [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) for how they work in states that allow them. Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance remain the simplest tools; read [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

## Taxes

Kentucky has no estate tax but does have an **inheritance tax**, which makes it one of only a handful of states with one. The beneficiary's relationship decides the outcome:

| Class | Who | Tax |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Spouse, parents, children, grandchildren, siblings | Exempt |
| B | Nieces, nephews, aunts, uncles, daughters- and sons-in-law and others | 4% to 16% after a $1,000 exemption |
| C | Everyone else, including friends and unrelated people | 6% to 16% after a $500 exemption |

Life insurance and retirement accounts have special treatment, so ask an attorney how the tax applies to a specific plan. Read [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) for context.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Kentucky

Kentucky adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which governs the agent's powers and duties and expects the document to be signed and either notarized or witnessed. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) for what a good document covers.

For medical choices, Kentucky law provides a living will directive and a way to designate a health care surrogate, along with a MOST form for seriously ill patients. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).
