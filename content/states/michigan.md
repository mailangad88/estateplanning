## Wills in Michigan

Michigan recognizes three kinds of will. A **witnessed will** is signed by you and by two witnesses who watched you sign or heard you acknowledge the signature, and no notary is needed. A **holographic will** is valid without witnesses if it is dated, signed, and the material portions are in your handwriting. And a **statutory will** is a short form written into Michigan law (MCL 700.2519) that simple estates can use. A notarized self-proving affidavit can be added to a witnessed will so the court can admit it without calling the witnesses.

The handwritten route is real but fragile; read [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) before relying on it. For the standard approach, see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Michigan also lets you deposit your will with the probate court in your county for safekeeping during your life. That solves a problem many families face: nobody can find the original. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) for other options.

Without a will, a spouse inherits the whole estate if all of the decedent's descendants are also the spouse's. If some descendants are not, the spouse receives a fixed first amount, adjusted over time, plus one-half of the remainder. A spouse can also elect a statutory share instead of the will, with deadlines tied to the inventory and the date of death.

## Probate in Michigan

Michigan's Estates and Protected Individuals Code (EPIC) governs probate in the **Probate Court** of each county. Michigan offers **informal proceedings**, where the register handles appointment of an uncontested estate, and **formal proceedings** before a judge. Personal representatives usually have broad power to act with little court supervision, and the court mostly checks in at the beginning and end. Creditors must present claims within four months after notice or the claim is barred, which in practice sets the minimum length of most estates.

For small estates, Michigan allows heirs to collect property by affidavit after a waiting period when the estate is below an indexed dollar limit. The limit is adjusted each year, so ask the probate court for the current figure. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) and the [probate guide](/learn/probate). Real estate typically requires a formal appointment or a deed-based tool, so a house changes the equation.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Michigan

The best-known Michigan tool is the **Lady Bird deed**, also called an enhanced life estate deed. You deed your home to a beneficiary but reserve a life estate with the power to sell, mortgage or change the beneficiary, and at death the property passes without probate. Michigan has not adopted a general transfer on death deed, so this deed is how many families do the same thing. Compare it with the model described in [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed). A Lady Bird deed can raise questions about Medicaid eligibility, so ask an attorney if long-term care is a concern.

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is also widely used, particularly for families with property in more than one state or with children who live far away. Michigan's trust code is based on the Uniform Trust Code. See also [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

## Taxes

Michigan imposes no estate tax and no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax applies, and it applies to estates above $15 million per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for a comparison with states that still tax. Michigan property tax can be reassessed when a home changes hands, so a transfer at death may raise the bill, a point worth checking with the local assessor.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Michigan

Michigan adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which sets default rules for agents and expects the document to be signed and notarized or witnessed. Confirm the current signing and effective-date rules with an attorney. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

Michigan's medical document is called a **patient advocate designation**. It names someone to make medical decisions once two people (usually a physician and another clinician) decide you cannot. It requires two witnesses who are not family members or your health care providers, and the patient advocate must sign an acceptance before acting. Michigan has no living will statute, but the designation can state your wishes. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).
