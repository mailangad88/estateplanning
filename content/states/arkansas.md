Arkansas estate planning has two local quirks worth knowing: spouses have dower and curtesy rights that can override a will, and the small estate affidavit limit is $100,000. Everything else follows familiar patterns, which this guide links to.

## Wills in Arkansas

An Arkansas will is valid when a testator who is at least 18 signs it in front of two witnesses. A notary is not needed, but a self-proving affidavit notarized after signing saves your executor from finding the witnesses later. Arkansas also accepts a will written and signed in the testator's own handwriting without witnesses. Read [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) before relying on one, and use [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the signing steps.

The unusual feature is dower and curtesy. Instead of an elective share based on the whole estate, Arkansas gives a surviving spouse a life interest in one-third of the deceased spouse's real property, and a share of personal property, whatever the will says. It means a will that leaves everything to children from a first marriage can be undercut. Our guide on what a will cannot do discusses this kind of limit, and protecting children from a first marriage shows how to plan around it.

Without a will, Arkansas intestacy blends the spouse's dower rights with the children's inheritance, and the result differs for real estate and personal property. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) and confirm the specifics with an attorney.

## Probate in Arkansas

Probate is handled in the probate division of the county's circuit court under Title 28 of the Arkansas Code. The executor files the will and a petition, and the judge appoints a personal representative. After the appointment, the representative must publish notice to creditors, who generally have a short window to file claims. Debts, taxes and fees are paid before distribution; see dealing with debts in probate.

Small estates have a shortcut. When the probate estate is $100,000 or less, heirs can use an affidavit filed with the circuit clerk after a waiting period of about 45 days, which lets them collect assets without full administration. Real estate in a small estate involves added steps and a creditor claim period, so confirm the process with an Arkansas attorney. Our [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) article explains the common structure, and the [probate pillar](/learn/probate) covers formal administration.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Arkansas

The Arkansas Trust Code follows the Uniform Trust Code, so a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) works smoothly, as long as it is funded ([how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust)). Retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death bank accounts pass by beneficiary designation.

Whether Arkansas offers a transfer-on-death deed for real estate is something to confirm with an Arkansas attorney. If it does not, a trust is the usual way to move land outside probate. See how to avoid probate for the options that work in any state.

Dower rights also affect real estate planning: a spouse who did not sign a deed or trust funding transfer may still have dower in land, which a title company will notice. Spouses typically sign together when they retitle property.

If you own farmland or timber, plan with extra care. Dower rights and shared family ownership of land can complicate who may sell, and heirs who inherit land in equal shares can be forced into a partition sale if they disagree. A will or trust that names who manages the land, and how a buyout works, is often more useful than simply dividing it equally. An Arkansas attorney can review how title is currently held.

## Taxes

Arkansas has no estate or inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax can apply, and it begins above $15,000,000 per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Arkansas

An Arkansas [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) should be signed before a notary, which banks and county clerks expect, particularly for real estate. For health care, Arkansas law allows a living will (a declaration about end-of-life treatment) and a health care power of attorney, each with its own signing requirements, and recognizes a durable DNR order. Compare the living will and the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) before choosing. Confirm the current statutory forms with an Arkansas attorney.
