Tennessee is known for having no income tax on wages and no inheritance tax, but estate planning here is still more than filling in a form. Probate is organized differently from county to county, handwritten wills are allowed with proof, and married couples have a tax planning option that most states do not offer.

For national background, see our [wills guide](/learn/wills) and [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## Wills in Tennessee

A Tennessee will must be signed by you in front of two witnesses, who also sign in your presence. Notarization is not required for the will to be valid, but it is how a [self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) works: the witnesses and you sign an affidavit before a notary so the court does not need live testimony.

Tennessee does recognize a [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will). The whole document must be in your handwriting and signed, and two witnesses must swear that it is your handwriting. That means a handwritten note found after a death is not self-sufficient; someone has to be available to prove it. See [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills).

Tennessee protects a surviving spouse with an elective share. Unlike a flat fraction, the share is a percentage of the net estate that grows with the length of the marriage, so a spouse of a few years receives much less than a spouse of decades. The spouse also has a homestead allowance and a year's support allowance. Confirm the percentages with an attorney.

If there is no will, the spouse takes the same share as one child but never less than one-third, and the children take the rest. A married person with one child and a house titled alone can see the child end up with a large part, so a will often fits the family better. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains the national rules.

## Probate in Tennessee

Tennessee has no single probate court. Depending on the county, probate is handled by a probate court, a chancery court, or a circuit court, and filings go through the county clerk. In some counties a will must be admitted to probate with the testimony of its witnesses unless it is self-proved.

The executor files the will and a petition, takes an oath, and receives letters testamentary. The estate must publish notice to creditors and notify known creditors, and Tennessee law sets a deadline for claims that runs from the notice and from the date of death. The estate generally stays open until the claims period ends. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

Tennessee also has a small estate procedure with a simplified process for modest estates. The limit has been around $50,000, but confirm the current number. Our guide to [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains how they usually work.

The most common timeline driver in Tennessee is real estate: land titles in older counties can be messy, and a house that must be sold can hold the estate open. Our article on [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) covers other causes.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Tennessee

Beneficiary designations, payable-on-death accounts and jointly owned property pass outside probate. For the home, Tennessee has not clearly authorized transfer-on-death deeds, so confirm with an attorney whether one is available. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) holds title to the home reliably, and Tennessee's Uniform Trust Code governs trustees.

Two Tennessee features are unusual. First, the Tennessee Community Property Trust Act lets married couples, including couples who live elsewhere, hold property in a trust that is treated as community property. The advantage is that both halves can receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) when the first spouse dies, which can reduce capital gains tax on appreciated assets. Second, Tennessee's Investment Services Trust Act allows self-settled asset protection trusts with strict requirements, which is a complex tool.

## Taxes

Tennessee repealed its inheritance tax for deaths on or after January 1, 2016, and it has no estate tax. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026, so most families owe no estate tax. Heirs of retirement accounts may owe income tax when they withdraw. Read our guide to [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

If you own land in a neighboring state with its own tax, such as [Kentucky](/estate-planning/kentucky), that state may tax that property.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Tennessee

A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) is governed by Tennessee statutes and must be signed with the required formalities, which usually means a notary. Real estate and gift powers are worth spelling out.

For healthcare, Tennessee lets you complete an advance care plan that names a health care agent and states your wishes. It must be signed before two witnesses or a notary, and a medical orders form called POST can be used for serious illness. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [DNR orders and POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).
