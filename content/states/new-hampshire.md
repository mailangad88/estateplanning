New Hampshire is a low-tax state with a strict approach to wills. Its famous no-estate-tax status is real, but many families discover only after a death that a will signed with a missing witness may be invalid.

## Wills in New Hampshire

You must be at least 18, of sound mind, and sign the will in front of at least two credible witnesses, who sign in your presence. Notarization is not required for validity. A notary matters only for the optional self-proving affidavit, which lets the probate court accept the will without calling the witnesses. Our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) explains what a witness should be and why beneficiaries make poor witnesses.

New Hampshire is unforgiving about errors. It does not recognize a [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will) made in the state, so a handwritten will without witnesses is invalid. It also has no general harmless-error rule, and the statute provides that its witnessing rules do not allow electronic wills. Remote witnessing by video is allowed only in narrow situations, with a New Hampshire-licensed attorney and a notarial officer taking part. The article on [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) shows how these small defects cause the biggest problems.

The original will must also be found. Since New Hampshire needs the signed original, [where you keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) matters.

If there is no will, the intestacy statute decides, and a spouse's share depends on whether the children are also the spouse's. The intestate rules are in RSA 561:1; confirm with an attorney how they would apply to your family.

A surviving spouse also has an elective share, reported as one-third of the estate, which limits how much a will can leave elsewhere.

## Probate in New Hampshire

Probate is handled by the Probate Division of the Circuit Court. The court appoints an executor or administrator, who files an inventory and manages creditor claims before distributing property. [What probate involves](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) is similar everywhere, but New Hampshire has its own approach to simple estates.

Rather than a dollar-limit affidavit, New Hampshire uses a waiver of administration. If there is a sole heir who is also the administrator, or all heirs agree, the court can waive inventory, bond and accounting. The case is still opened and the executor still needs court appointment, and the estate generally cannot be closed until six months after appointment. Read [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) to see how other states handle this differently. The old dollar-based law was repealed in 2006.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in New Hampshire

Because the probate waiver is not a true bypass, many New Hampshire families rely on non-probate tools. Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance and payable-on-death accounts pass directly. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), governed by New Hampshire's Uniform Trust Code (RSA 564-B), keeps the transfer private and out of probate court. Real estate is the special problem: sources conflict on whether New Hampshire recently authorized transfer-on-death deeds, so confirm with an attorney before relying on one and see our general article on [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

Joint ownership with a spouse or child works but exposes the property to the co-owner's creditors and can cause tax and Medicaid problems; our guide to [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) compares the options.

## Taxes

New Hampshire has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. It once had a legacy and succession tax, but that was repealed for deaths after 2002. Only the federal estate tax can apply to estates above the federal exemption. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes). If you own property in Massachusetts or another state with its own estate tax, that state's rules can still reach you.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in New Hampshire

New Hampshire's durable power of attorney statute is RSA 564-E, and its advance directive statute, RSA 137-J, covers health care agents and living wills. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) should be notarized, and an advance directive generally requires two witnesses or a notary. A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) names the person who can decide when you cannot. Confirm the current statutory forms with a New Hampshire attorney.
