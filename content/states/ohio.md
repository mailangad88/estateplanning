Ohio's probate system is built around county probate courts that are used to working with families directly. The rules are traditional rather than uniform-code style, and two features surprise people: there is no self-proving affidavit for a will, and real estate passes without probate through a recorded designation affidavit rather than a deed.

## Wills in Ohio

An Ohio will must be in writing, signed at the end by you, and witnessed by two competent people who see you sign or hear you acknowledge the signature, and who sign in your conscious presence. Telephone or video presence does not count. A notary is not required, and since Ohio has no self-proving affidavit, the witnesses' signatures are what the probate court looks to. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will). Choosing witnesses who do not inherit avoids a statutory presumption against any gift to a witness.

Handwriting is no substitute for witnesses here. Ohio does not recognize [holographic wills](/glossary/holographic-will). A limited rule lets the probate court admit a defective will when clear and convincing evidence shows that the will was prepared by the testator and signed in front of two witnesses, but the two-witness signing itself cannot be waived. Read [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills).

If you die without a will, your spouse takes everything when all the children are also the spouse's. If any child is from another relationship, the spouse takes the first $20,000 plus half the rest. Our guide to [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains the order of heirs. A spouse who is unhappy with a will can elect against it, taking the intestate share but no more than half of the net estate, or one-third if two or more children survive.

## Probate in Ohio

Probate is handled by the probate court of the county where the person lived. Many estates are opened by a short application to admit the will and appoint the executor, then the executor files an inventory within three months and gives notice to creditors, who have six months from death to present claims. Ohio estates frequently stay open for at least six months to a year for that reason. See [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) and the [executor's duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

Ohio's small estate tools run through the court. Release from administration is available when the estate is worth $35,000 or less, or up to $100,000 when the surviving spouse takes the entire estate. A summary release handles very small amounts, such as funeral bills. Ohio has no private affidavit that avoids the court entirely. Confirm current limits and eligibility with your county probate court. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Ohio

Ohio no longer uses a transfer-on-death deed. Instead, a homeowner signs and records a transfer-on-death designation affidavit under ORC 5302.22, naming who takes the property. It has to be recorded before death, and it can be revoked or changed later. The practical effects are similar to a TOD deed, and our general article on [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) covers the tradeoffs. The affidavit does not remove Medicaid estate recovery in the way some families expect, so seek advice before relying on it.

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) under the Ohio Trust Code is the usual choice for people with several properties, minor children or blended families. Beneficiary designations and payable-on-death accounts also bypass probate. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) for the assets that do not follow your will.

## Taxes

Ohio repealed its estate tax for deaths on or after January 1, 2013, and it has no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax applies, to estates over the federal exemption. Heirs can owe income tax on inherited retirement accounts. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Ohio

Ohio has statutory forms for a health care power of attorney (ORC 1337.12) and a living will declaration (ORC 2133.02), and each must be either signed before a notary or witnessed by two adults who meet the statute's conflict rules. Ohio's financial power of attorney should be notarized, particularly if it will be used for real estate. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living wills](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will). A DNR comfort-care order is a separate medical form; see [DNR and POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst). Confirm current forms with an Ohio attorney.
