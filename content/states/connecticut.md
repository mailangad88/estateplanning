Connecticut stands out for taxes. It is the only state with its own gift tax, and its estate tax is tied to the federal exemption. This guide sets out what is specific to Connecticut and links to the national guides for general rules.

## Wills in Connecticut

A Connecticut will must be in writing, signed by an adult testator, and signed by two witnesses in the testator's presence. Notarization is not required for validity, though a self-proving affidavit completed before a notary lets the probate court accept the will without questioning witnesses. Connecticut does not recognize an unwitnessed handwritten will as a valid will, so a [handwritten or DIY will](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) needs the same formalities. Walk through the steps in [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

A surviving spouse has a statutory right to a life use of one-third of the estate, and the rule can limit disinheriting a spouse. For anyone without a will, the intestacy rule is specific: when all of the children are also the spouse's, the spouse takes the first $100,000 plus three-quarters of the balance, and the children take the remaining quarter. If some children are from another relationship, the shares are split more evenly. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Connecticut

Connecticut probate is handled by local probate courts, organized in districts, under Title 45a of the General Statutes. Rather than a county judge, you appear before a probate judge for your district. The executor files the will and an application, the court appoints the fiduciary, and an inventory is filed. Court fees are set by a statutory schedule based on the size of the taxable estate, rather than being a flat filing fee. Creditors have a limited claim window after publication of notice. See what is probate and probate costs.

For small estates, Connecticut allows simplified settlement of estates with personal property of $40,000 or less, through the probate court with a decree authorizing transfers; if the decedent owned real estate, the shortcut generally does not apply. Our [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) article explains the common structure, and the [probate pillar](/learn/probate) describes standard administration. Also, the state Department of Administrative Services may need notice, as a creditor, if the decedent received certain benefits.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Connecticut

Connecticut has adopted the Uniform Trust Code, and a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is a common way to avoid probate and keep affairs private. Probate files in Connecticut are public. Joint ownership, beneficiary designations and payable-on-death accounts also bypass probate; see how to avoid probate. Whether Connecticut offers a transfer-on-death deed for real estate is something to confirm with an attorney; the national guide on [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) explains how they work where available.

Because Connecticut combines a high exemption with a cap on the tax, the families most affected are those with estates near or above $15,000,000, including closely held business owners and holders of appreciated real estate. For most households the practical Connecticut questions are different: whether the probate court will be public, how long fees and notice periods take, and whether a trust keeps the family's affairs private. Ask about all three when you review your plan.

## Taxes

Connecticut imposes its own estate tax. For 2026 the exemption is $15,000,000, matching the federal estate tax exemption, and a flat 12 percent applies above that amount. The tax is capped at $15,000,000, so extremely large estates have a ceiling. Connecticut also imposes a gift tax on taxable lifetime gifts, and the state estate and gift tax exemptions are shared, so large gifts can use up the exemption. Connecticut has no inheritance tax. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and the gift tax annual exclusion. Because the amounts are high, most families owe nothing.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Connecticut

Connecticut has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) is signed before a notary and two witnesses, which is more than many states require. For health care, Connecticut allows you to appoint a health care representative and to state treatment wishes in health care instructions, with witness requirements to confirm. Related guides: [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and living will.
