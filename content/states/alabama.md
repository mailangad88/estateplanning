Estate planning in Alabama comes down to a few local facts: a will needs two witnesses, the county probate court supervises estates, and survivorship has to be spelled out in writing. This guide covers the rules that are specific to Alabama and links to our national guides for everything that works the same in every state.

## Wills in Alabama

An Alabama will must be signed by the testator, who is at least 18 and of sound mind, and by two witnesses who sign in the testator's presence. Notarization is not required. The practical advice is to add a self-proving affidavit, a short sworn statement signed before a notary that lets the probate judge accept the will without finding the witnesses years later. See our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the step-by-step signing process.

Because a handwritten, unwitnessed will is not given special treatment, a [handwritten or DIY will](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) is risky here. Alabama also protects a surviving spouse who is left out or left little: the spouse can claim an elective share of the augmented estate, and there are separate homestead, exempt-property and family allowances. A spouse can use these even if the will says otherwise.

If someone dies [without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will), Alabama's intestacy statute decides who inherits. A spouse and children usually share, with the spouse taking a fixed amount first when the children are all the spouse's own. If the decedent had children from another relationship, the spouse's share drops, which is a common source of conflict in blended families.

## Probate in Alabama

Alabama probate is handled by the probate court in the county where the person lived, and the probate judge also oversees related matters in that county. The executor files the original will and a petition for letters testamentary. The court admits the will, issues letters, and the executor then gathers assets, notifies creditors and pays debts before distributing what is left. Many estates can be administered without constant court supervision, but the court still reviews the inventory and the final settlement. Our [probate pillar](/learn/probate) explains each stage, and how long probate takes covers what slows it down.

Alabama has simplified procedures for small estates, usually by petition to the probate court, with a waiting period and notice to creditors and the Alabama Medicaid Agency. Real estate is generally outside the simplified process, so a house usually still needs a regular administration or a planned transfer. Confirm the current dollar limit with an attorney, since it can change. Our guide to the [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains the general idea.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Alabama

Alabama has adopted the Uniform Trust Code, so a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is well supported, provided you retitle assets into it (see how to fund a trust). Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death bank accounts also bypass probate.

Survivorship deserves extra attention. In Alabama, a deed or account held by two people does not automatically pass to the survivor unless the document expressly says so. A couple who bought a house "as joint tenants" without survivorship language may find half of it in the probate estate. Review the wording on every deed and account, and read our guide to joint tenancy risks. Whether Alabama offers a transfer-on-death deed is a point to confirm with an Alabama attorney; our national article on [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) explains how they work where they exist.

A practical point for Alabama families: because the probate court is local and elected, procedures and forms can differ a little from county to county. Call the probate court clerk in the county where your parent or spouse lived before you file anything, and bring a certified death certificate and the original will. Keep the original will somewhere your executor can find it quickly; a copy is much harder to admit than the original.

## Taxes

Alabama levies no estate tax and no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax can apply, and as of 2026 the federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for how other states compare.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Alabama

Alabama has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which sets default rules for [durable financial powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney). A power of attorney is signed before a notary or two witnesses. For medical decisions, Alabama's Natural Death Act provides an advance directive for health care that combines a living will with the appointment of a health care proxy; it requires two adult witnesses. Read about the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and living will to decide what to include.
