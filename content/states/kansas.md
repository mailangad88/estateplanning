## Wills in Kansas

A Kansas will must be in writing, signed by a testator who is at least 18 and of sound mind, and witnessed by two competent people who see the signing or hear you acknowledge it. No notary is required. Witnesses can later sign an affidavit before a notary to make the will self-proving, which lets the court admit it without finding them. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the general steps.

Kansas does not recognize a handwritten will that lacks two witnesses. Our article on [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) explains why those fail.

Intestacy is a standout Kansas issue. If you die married with children and no will, K.S.A. 59-504 gives one-half to your spouse and one-half to your children, even if all the children are also the spouse's. A family that assumed "everything goes to Mom" may find that adult children, or guardians for minor children, hold half. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

A surviving spouse who dislikes the will can elect against it. In Kansas the elective share grows with the length of the marriage, so a long marriage protects the spouse more.

## Probate in Kansas

Kansas has no separate probate court. Estates are handled by the district court in the county where the person lived, under Chapter 59 of the Kansas Statutes. The distinctive feature is the **six-month deadline**: Kansas generally expects a will to be offered for probate within six months of death, and a will filed after that point may not be admitted. People often discover a will in a drawer long after a funeral, so the deadline is a trap. After the executor is appointed, creditors have a limited claim period running from the first published notice.

Most Kansas estates are handled with the court's involvement at the opening and closing, with a petition to approve the final accounting before the executor distributes. That gives beneficiaries more visibility but takes longer than unsupervised systems. See [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take).

For small estates, Kansas has an affidavit procedure for personal property that was recently raised to $75,000. Confirm the current limit with an attorney before relying on it. The general approach is in [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit), and the full process is in our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

A practical example: Dale, a widower in Salina, dies and his daughter finds a signed will in a filing cabinet eight months later. Because Kansas expects wills to be offered within six months, she may face questions about whether the will can still be admitted and how property that passes under it will be handled. Had Dale also recorded a transfer on death deed for his farmhouse, that property would not depend on the probate deadline at all. Telling your executor where the will is, as our article on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) suggests, avoids the problem.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Kansas

Kansas offers a **transfer on death deed** for real estate under the Kansas Nonprobate Transfers on Death Act. You sign and notarize the deed, record it with the register of deeds in the county where the land sits before you die, and name beneficiaries who receive the property automatically. You can revoke it and keep control while you live. Learn the details in [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

For farmland or ranchland, a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) may fit better, particularly if the property is shared among siblings and you want rules for management. Joint tenancy, payable on death accounts and beneficiary designations also work. Read [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) for the comparison.

## Taxes

Kansas has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The only estate tax that could apply is the federal one, which starts above $15 million per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) to compare.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Kansas

Kansas has its own Power of Attorney Act (K.S.A. 58-650 and following) instead of the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, and it sets formal signing rules that include notarization or witnesses. Confirm current requirements with an attorney. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) for what a good document covers.

For medical decisions, Kansas provides a durable power of attorney for health care decisions and a declaration under the Natural Death Act, which is Kansas's living will statute. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).
