Rhode Island is small, but its rules have two features that catch families off guard: probate happens at the city or town level, and the state taxes estates well below the federal threshold. If you own a home in Providence or on the coast, plus retirement savings and life insurance, your estate may be larger than you think.

This page covers the points that are specific to Rhode Island. For the general picture, see our [estate planning overview](/learn/basics) and the national guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

## Wills in Rhode Island

A Rhode Island will must be in writing, signed by you, and witnessed by two competent people. The witnesses should watch you sign and then sign themselves. Choose witnesses who are not beneficiaries, because a gift to a witness can be challenged.

Notarization is not required for the will to be valid. Still, most attorneys add a self-proving affidavit signed in front of a notary. The [self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) lets the probate judge accept the will without tracking down witnesses years later, which matters if one of them has moved or died.

Rhode Island does not generally honor a handwritten will that lacks witnesses. Our article on [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) explains why those documents often fail, and the same caution applies here.

Rhode Island is a common law property state, so each spouse owns what is titled in their name. A surviving spouse has statutory protections, including rights in the deceased spouse's real estate, so disinheriting a spouse is difficult. Confirm with an attorney how those rights apply to your situation.

If you die without a will, state law decides who inherits. A spouse and children usually share, and the split can differ for real estate and personal property. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for the national picture, then confirm Rhode Island's current split with an attorney.

## Probate in Rhode Island

Rhode Island has no single statewide probate court. Each of the 39 cities and towns runs its own probate court, with a judge appointed by the city or town council and a clerk who handles the filings. A will is filed in the municipality where the person lived. If the person owned real estate in other Rhode Island towns, the process is still run from the home town.

The general steps match what we describe in [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate): file the will and a petition, the court appoints the executor and issues a certificate, the executor notifies creditors, pays debts and taxes, and distributes what remains. Because each court sets its own pace and local practice, timelines vary from town to town.

For very small estates, Rhode Island offers a voluntary or informal administration that avoids full court supervision. The ceiling is low, around $15,000 for personal property in the sources we reviewed, and the amount can change, so confirm with an attorney. Our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains how these shortcuts usually work.

Three things commonly slow Rhode Island estates: a will with no self-proving affidavit, real estate that needs to be sold or retitled, and an estate tax return that must be filed before the court will close the estate.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Rhode Island

Assets with a named beneficiary pass outside probate: life insurance, retirement accounts, and bank accounts with payable-on-death designations. Property owned jointly with survivorship rights also passes directly. Our guide to [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) lists the main tools.

For a home, the most dependable route in Rhode Island is a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) that holds title to the property. Whether a transfer-on-death deed is available is something to confirm with an attorney before relying on one; our article on [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) explains how they work where they exist.

A trust also matters because Rhode Island property sits in a town probate court. If you own a vacation property in another state as well, a trust can spare your family a second proceeding. See [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

## Taxes

Rhode Island collects its own estate tax. For deaths in 2026, the first $1,838,056 of the estate is exempt, and the figure is indexed annually. Above it, graduated rates apply. There is no inheritance tax. The federal exemption is $15 million per person in 2026, so Rhode Island's lower threshold is the one that matters for most families here.

A married couple can use the marital deduction to defer tax until the second death, but the state exemption is not automatically portable between spouses, so the plan for the second death matters. Read more in our guide to [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Rhode Island

A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets someone you choose manage money if you cannot. Rhode Island has a statute governing these documents and lets you name an agent and a backup. Banks sometimes balk at older forms, so refresh yours every few years.

For medical decisions, Rhode Island recognizes a health care power of attorney and a living will, signed with the formalities the statute requires. A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) names who speaks for you; a living will states your wishes. Rhode Island also uses a medical orders form for people with serious illness. Confirm the current statutory forms with a Rhode Island attorney.

Neighboring [Connecticut](/estate-planning/connecticut) and [Massachusetts](/estate-planning/massachusetts) have their own forms, so move your documents when you move.
