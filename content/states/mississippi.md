## Wills in Mississippi

Mississippi accepts two kinds of will. A **witnessed will** is in writing, signed by you at 18 or older and of sound mind, and signed by two credible witnesses who see you sign. A **holographic will** is valid if it is entirely in your own handwriting and signed, with no witnesses. The catch is proof: after death, two witnesses must testify that the handwriting is yours. Read [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) for the risks, and [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the standard approach.

A witnessed will can be proved by the testimony or affidavit of the witnesses. Mississippi's rules on formal self-proving affidavits are a point to confirm with an attorney, so choose witnesses who will be easy to find.

Intestacy in Mississippi treats a spouse like another child. If you die married with children and no will, the estate is divided equally among the spouse and the children, and the spouse takes at least one child's share. With one child, that is half. With four children, the spouse gets one-fifth. That is very different from states that give a spouse everything. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

A spouse who is left too little can renounce the will and claim the statutory share instead.

## Probate in Mississippi

Mississippi does not have a separate probate court. Estates go to the **chancery court** in the county, a court of equity with broad authority over wills, estates, guardianships and conservatorships. The chancery clerk accepts the will, and the chancellor appoints the executor or administrator. The same court hears will contests and guardianship cases, so some families deal with one clerk's office for several matters.

After appointment, the executor publishes notice to creditors, who generally have 90 days after first publication to file claims, then inventories assets and closes the estate. Mississippi practice favors keeping the chancery court's involvement light for uncontested estates, but a contested estate can take much longer. See [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take).

For smaller estates, Mississippi offers an **affidavit procedure** for collecting personal property after a waiting period. The dollar limit has been raised in recent years, so confirm the current amount with the chancery clerk or an attorney. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) and the [probate guide](/learn/probate).

A practical point: because chancery court also hears guardianships and conservatorships, a family that has not signed a power of attorney can end up in the same courthouse for an incapacity case before a probate case. An advance power of attorney and health directive can keep the first one out of court entirely. The chancery clerk's office in your county can explain filing fees and local procedures, which vary from county to county.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Mississippi

Mississippi has adopted the Uniform Trust Code, so a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) works in a standard way. Many Mississippi families own land that has been handed down for generations, sometimes titled in the names of long-deceased relatives, and a trust or properly updated title helps avoid stacking multiple estates.

Whether Mississippi offers a transfer on death deed is a point to confirm with an attorney. The common tools are joint tenancy with right of survivorship, payable on death accounts and beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance. For a comparison of the options, see [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) and [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

## Taxes

Mississippi has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax can apply, and it reaches estates above $15 million per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) to compare with states that still tax.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Mississippi

Mississippi's rules for a financial power of attorney should be checked with an attorney, including the signing and notary requirements and whether the document remains valid if you become incapacitated. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) for what to cover.

For medical decisions, Mississippi's Uniform Health-Care Decisions Act lets you appoint an agent and give instructions in an advance health-care directive. It also recognizes a default surrogate if no agent is named. Mississippi also uses a POLST form for people with serious illness. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).
