## Wills in Indiana

To be valid in Indiana, a will must be in writing and signed by a testator who is at least 18 and of sound mind, in front of two witnesses who also sign. A notary is not required. Many Indiana lawyers add a self-proving clause at the signing, which lets the court admit the will on the paperwork alone. Our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) covers the national basics. Choose witnesses who will not inherit, so no one can argue they had a stake.

Indiana does not accept an unwitnessed handwritten will, so the reasoning in our article on [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) applies fully. Indiana also has a statute for electronic wills with its own signing and witnessing rules.

If a married person dies without a will and leaves children, the spouse takes one-half of the net estate and the children share the rest. That is a problem for a parent who assumed a spouse would get everything, and it is worse when some children are from an earlier relationship. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

A spouse can elect against the will and generally take one-third of the net estate. Indiana's formula can differ in some second marriages, so confirm with an attorney if that applies to you.

## Probate in Indiana

Probate is handled by the circuit or superior court (a probate court in a few counties) under Title 29 of the Indiana Code. Indiana's most useful feature is **unsupervised administration**. When the personal representative qualifies for it, they can sell, pay and distribute without asking the judge at every step, and the estate closes with a sworn statement rather than a formal accounting. Supervised administration is available when someone asks for court oversight.

Typical timeline drivers are the creditor claim period, which runs for months after notice is published, the preparation of an inventory, and any dispute among heirs. Our article on [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) lists the usual delays.

For smaller estates, Indiana offers an affidavit that lets a successor collect personal property. For deaths after June 30, 2022, the limit is $100,000, and the claimant must wait the statutory period after death before using it. Real estate is not collected by affidavit, so a house usually changes the picture. Details on the general procedure are in [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit), and the entire process appears in our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Indiana

Indiana's Transfer on Death Property Act is the most direct tool. It lets you name beneficiaries for real estate, bank accounts and securities. For real estate, you sign and record an authorization, similar to a deed, in the county recorder's office before you die. Read more in [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) and [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) remains the choice for people who want a plan for incapacity, control over when heirs receive money, or one document for property in more than one state. Indiana's trust code is based on the Uniform Trust Code. Indiana also recognizes tenancy by the entirety for married couples, which can protect a home from a single spouse's creditors, but it does not avoid probate if the spouse dies and the survivor later dies without a plan.

## Taxes

Indiana has no estate tax and repealed its inheritance tax for deaths after December 31, 2012. That leaves the federal estate tax, which affects only estates above $15 million per person in 2026. Our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) shows how Indiana compares with neighbors that still tax estates.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Indiana

Indiana's powers of attorney are governed by Article 30-5 of the Indiana Code, and a power of attorney must be signed and either notarized or witnessed under the statute. Confirm the current signing requirements with an attorney, and see our [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) article for what to include.

For health decisions, Indiana's advance directive law allows you to name a health care representative and sign a living will declaration. Indiana also recognizes POST forms for people with serious illness. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living wills](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) for how each works.
