## Wills in Minnesota

Under Minnesota's Uniform Probate Code, a will must be in writing, signed by you at age 18 or older and of sound mind, and signed by two witnesses. No notary is required. The self-proving affidavit, signed before a notary at the signing, lets the court accept the will without tracking down the witnesses. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for national basics.

Minnesota does not honor an unwitnessed handwritten will. That matters because a lot of people write their wishes on paper and assume it counts.

Intestacy follows the Uniform Probate Code pattern. If all of your children are also your spouse's children, the spouse takes the whole estate. If you have a child from another relationship, the spouse takes a first fixed amount plus half of the rest, and the children share the remainder. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

Minnesota's **elective share** is also modern. A spouse can claim a percentage of the augmented estate, which counts both probate and certain non-probate assets, and the percentage rises with the length of the marriage until it reaches one-half after 15 years. A couple married for three years has a much smaller claim than a couple married for 20.

## Probate in Minnesota

Probate is handled by the district court in the county where the person lived. Because Minnesota follows the Uniform Probate Code, the most common path is **informal probate**: the personal representative applies to the court registrar, who can appoint without a hearing if no one objects. **Formal probate** is available when there is a dispute or a need for a binding order. Once appointed, the personal representative publishes notice to creditors, who have four months to file claims, and sends notice to known creditors and heirs.

Minnesota also has a **small estate affidavit** under Minn. Stat. 524.3-1201. When the value of the probate estate is $75,000 or less, not counting certain exempt property, heirs can collect assets after a 30-day wait. Real estate has a separate path. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) and the [probate guide](/learn/probate).

For estates that must file a Minnesota estate tax return, the due date is nine months after death, and the tax is a major reason larger estates stay open for a year or more. See [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Minnesota

Minnesota allows a **transfer on death deed** (Minn. Stat. 507.071). The owner signs and acknowledges the deed, records it before death, and keeps the right to sell, mortgage or revoke. After death, the beneficiary records a certificate of survivorship or similar proof. The property remains subject to claims against the estate in some cases. See [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) and [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is also common, including for families who own a lake cabin and want to hand it down. Minnesota's Uniform Trust Code is in Chapter 501C.

## Taxes

Minnesota is one of a dozen or so states with its own estate tax, and its **$3 million exemption** is not indexed and is far below the federal exemption. Rates are graduated, topping out at 16%. Minnesota has no inheritance tax. It also counts certain gifts made within three years of death when figuring the estate, so late-in-life gifts can be pulled back in. Unlike federal law, Minnesota has no portability election, so a married couple who leaves everything to the survivor can waste the first spouse's exemption. See [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) and [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes). Farm and small business owners may have special options, so ask an attorney.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Minnesota

Minnesota's powers of attorney are governed by Chapter 523, which includes a statutory short form. The signing rules require a notary acknowledgment or witnesses, and the rules were updated recently, so use a current form. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

Minnesota's **health care directive** combines the agent appointment and living will in one document under Chapter 145C. It must be signed by you and either notarized or witnessed by two people. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will). Minnesota also uses POLST forms.
