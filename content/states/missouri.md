## Wills in Missouri

A Missouri will must be in writing, signed by you at 18 or older and of sound mind, and signed by two witnesses who see you sign or hear you acknowledge it. No notary is required. Most Missouri attorneys attach a notarized **self-proving affidavit** under RSMo 474.337, so the Probate Division can accept the will without calling the witnesses. Choose witnesses who will not inherit. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Missouri does not recognize a purely handwritten will without witnesses, so a will on a legal pad needs two witnesses to count.

If there is no will, RSMo 474.010 governs. A spouse takes a fixed first amount plus a share of the rest when there are children, and the children share the balance. The exact split depends on the family, so confirm it with an attorney, and see [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

A spouse who is left too little can elect against the will and take one-third of the estate if there are descendants, or one-half if not. Missouri's Nonprobate Transfers Law affects how much counts toward that share, which makes the calculation technical.

## Probate in Missouri

Probate is handled by the **Probate Division of the Circuit Court** in the county where the person lived. The personal representative is appointed by the court under Chapters 472 to 475. Missouri has two features worth noting:

1. **Creditor claims run on a hard clock.** After notice is published, creditors generally have six months from first publication to file, and claims are barred after one year from death. The six-month window is why many estates stay open for most of a year.
2. **Court supervision is light for most estates.** Once the personal representative is appointed, they can generally sell, pay and distribute without asking the judge at each step, and the court mostly reviews the settlement at the end.

For small estates, an affidavit procedure under RSMo 473.097 lets heirs collect personal property of $40,000 or less after waiting 30 days. Real estate is not collected by affidavit. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) and the [probate guide](/learn/probate). For estates with a house, plan for the full process; see [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take).

Consider Ruth, a widow in Springfield who owns a house and a $30,000 savings account in her own name. If she leaves a beneficiary deed on the house and names a payable on death beneficiary on the account, her children may never need to open an estate. Without them, the house alone forces a full case, since the small estate affidavit does not reach real estate. A short list of what is titled in your sole name, with no beneficiary, shows quickly whether your family faces probate.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Missouri

Missouri's **beneficiary deed** (RSMo 461.025) is the most direct way to pass real estate outside probate. You sign it, have it notarized, and record it with the recorder of deeds in the county before you die. You keep full control and can sell or revoke the property during life, and the beneficiary takes title at death by recording proof of death. See [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) for the general concept.

Missouri's Nonprobate Transfers Law also covers payable on death accounts and similar designations. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is another option, and Missouri's Uniform Trust Code governs it. Married couples often hold a home as tenants by the entirety, which has special creditor protection. See [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) for a comparison.

## Taxes

Missouri has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax applies, and it affects estates above $15 million per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes). Heirs should still consider income tax on inherited retirement accounts.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Missouri

Missouri has its own durable power of attorney law in Chapter 404, which expects a written, signed and notarized document, and it has no statutory form for general financial powers. Confirm the current requirements with an attorney. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

For medical decisions, Missouri uses a durable power of attorney for health care and a declaration (its living will) with two witnesses. Missouri's living will statute applies only in certain situations, so a health care agent is more flexible. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).
