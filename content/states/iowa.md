## Wills in Iowa

An Iowa will must be written, signed by you (or by another person at your direction), and witnessed by two competent people who also sign. You must be at least 18 and of sound mind. A notary is not needed for the will to be valid, but Iowa lawyers routinely add a notarized self-proving affidavit so the court does not need to track down the witnesses later. Our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) explains the general process.

Iowa does not accept an unwitnessed handwritten will. A note in your own handwriting that no one witnessed will not control who gets your property.

Without a will, Iowa law decides. When a married person dies and all children are also the spouse's children, the spouse inherits everything. When any child is from another relationship, the spouse takes the first $50,000 plus one-half of the rest, and the children split the balance. Parents in blended families should read about [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) because this default can move more to a stepparent than expected.

A surviving spouse can elect against the will and take a statutory elective share, generally one-third. Prenuptial agreements often address this.

## Probate in Iowa

Iowa probate is handled by the district court in the county where the person lived, under Chapter 633 of the Iowa Code. A few Iowa-specific points shape the process:

- **Formal and informal paths.** Many estates are opened with a simple filing and handled by the executor with limited court involvement. A court hearing is needed for contested matters.
- **Inventory and claims.** The executor files an inventory with the court and handles creditor claims during a four-month claim period after notice is published. Iowa's required notices to heirs and the surviving spouse are a frequent source of mistakes for first-time executors.
- **Real estate.** Iowa often requires a final report and a court order before real estate title can be cleared for sale, which is one reason a house can add weeks to the process.

Iowa also has a simplified procedure for collecting personal property by affidavit when the estate is small and has no real estate, and a small estate administration for modest estates. The dollar limits have changed over time, so confirm current figures. Our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains the general approach, and the [probate guide](/learn/probate) covers the whole process.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Iowa

Iowa's Trust Code, Chapter 633A, is based on the Uniform Trust Code, and it is the framework for a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust). A trust is a practical tool for Iowans who own farmland or other real estate, because the trust owns the land and a successor trustee can manage or sell it without a probate case. For farm families, see also our article on [passing a family business to children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children).

Iowa has not historically offered a general transfer on death deed for real estate, so confirm the current rule with an attorney before relying on one. The usual probate-avoidance tools are joint tenancy with right of survivorship, beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and insurance, and payable on death bank accounts. Read [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) for the tradeoffs of each.

## Taxes

Iowa has no estate tax. Its inheritance tax, which once charged between 1% and 15% on heirs other than a spouse, child or grandchild, was fully repealed for deaths on or after January 1, 2025. That leaves only the federal estate tax, which applies above $15 million per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for how Iowa compares with states that still tax.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Iowa

Iowa has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act as Chapter 633B, which sets default rules for what an agent can and cannot do and requires the document to be signed and acknowledged before a notary. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) for what to cover.

For healthcare, Iowa uses a durable power of attorney for health care (Chapter 144B) and a living will declaration (Chapter 144A). Iowa also has the IPOST form for people with serious illness. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) for how to choose an agent.
