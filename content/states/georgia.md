Georgia keeps estate administration close to home, in the county Probate Court, and has two vocabulary words worth learning: common form and solemn form. This guide covers what is specific to Georgia.

## Wills in Georgia

To make a Georgia will, the testator must be at least 14, of sound mind, and sign in front of two witnesses who sign in the testator's presence. No notary is required. A self-proving affidavit, signed before a notary at the signing ceremony, lets the court admit the will without finding the witnesses. A handwritten will without two witnesses is not valid, so see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) and [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills).

Georgia has no elective share. Instead, a surviving spouse and minor children can petition for year's support, an allowance from the estate set by the Probate Court based on the family's standard of living. Year's support is paid ahead of most other claims, and a will cannot override it. A spouse can also claim a limited statutory right to remain in the home during the first months. See what a will cannot do.

Without a will, Georgia's intestacy law divides the estate equally among the surviving spouse and children, but the spouse gets at least one-third. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Georgia

Probate is handled by the Probate Court of the county where the decedent lived, under Title 53 of the Georgia Code. Georgia has two ways to probate a will. **Common form** is the routine, uncontested process: the executor petitions, the court admits the will and issues letters testamentary, usually without a hearing. **Solemn form** requires notice to all heirs and a hearing; it takes longer but gives the will a final stamp that blocks later challenges. Executors commonly choose solemn form when family members might object. See contesting a will for how disputes unfold.

Georgia has unsupervised administration, so many estates move without regular court review. When the will names an executor and waives bond, the executor can often act with limited court oversight. The estate then publishes notice to creditors and pays claims in a statutory order; see dealing with debts in probate.

For small estates, Georgia does not use a dollar-limited affidavit for most assets. Instead, when there are no debts or the heirs agree, the heirs can ask the Probate Court for an order that no administration is necessary, which allows them to deal with the property, including real estate, without a formal administration. A separate affidavit procedure allows collection of up to $15,000 from bank deposits. Our [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) article explains the general idea, and the [probate pillar](/learn/probate) covers full administration.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Georgia

Georgia has adopted the Uniform Trust Code, and a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is a common way to avoid probate and plan for incapacity; fund it using the steps in how to fund a trust. Beneficiary designations, payable-on-death accounts and joint ownership with survivorship also pass assets outside probate; see how to avoid probate.

Whether Georgia offers a transfer-on-death deed for real estate is a point to confirm with a Georgia attorney. Because Georgia law has historically relied on trusts and survivorship deeds for real estate, ask before assuming a TOD deed is available. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) for the general idea.

Georgia families often hold land that has passed through several generations. Check whether past estates were ever formally administered, because unclear title, sometimes called heirs property, can make it hard to sell, borrow against or insure land. If your family owns land with many co-owners, ask about clearing title now while the owners are alive and able to sign, which is far easier than sorting it out after another death.

## Taxes

Georgia has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax can apply, and it begins above $15,000,000 per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Georgia

Georgia has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, and a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) should be signed before a notary and two witnesses, at least one of whom is not the agent. For health care, Georgia's Advance Directive for Health Care lets you name a health care agent and state treatment preferences in one document; it requires two witnesses and no notary. Georgia also uses POLST forms. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney), living will and DNR and POLST.
