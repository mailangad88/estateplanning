Arizona estate planning starts with community property, which changes who owns what before a will is ever read. Add a handy deed that skips probate and a registry for medical directives, and Arizona is quite different from a typical common law state.

## Wills in Arizona

A will is valid in Arizona when an adult signs it in front of two witnesses who also sign; no notary is needed. A notarized self-proving affidavit lets the court accept it without witness testimony. Arizona also recognizes a handwritten will if the signature and the key terms are in the testator's handwriting, though proving that handwriting can become a fight (see [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills)). The signing steps are in our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Community property matters here. Each spouse can leave only their own half of community property, plus separate property, through a will. Arizona has no elective share because the survivor already owns half. Read our glossary entry on [community property](/glossary/community-property) for the basics.

Without a will, the surviving spouse takes the decedent's community property. For separate property, the spouse takes everything only if all the children are also the spouse's; otherwise the spouse and the other children share. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Arizona

Probate takes place in the Superior Court of the county where the person lived. Arizona follows the Uniform Probate Code, which offers an informal, largely unsupervised process managed by a personal representative. Most estates use this track and appear before a judge only if something is contested. Creditors get a four-month window after notice is published, a major driver of the timeline; see how long probate takes.

Arizona's small estate rules are split. A successor can collect personal property with an affidavit after a 30-day waiting period when the estate is below the statutory limit, currently $200,000. A separate affidavit for real property, with a higher limit of $300,000 and a longer waiting period of about six months, is filed with the court and recorded. Both limits changed in 2025 and may change again, so confirm the current numbers. Our [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) article walks through the general process, and the [probate pillar](/learn/probate) covers formal administration.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Arizona

Arizona offers some of the simplest non-probate tools in the country.

- **Beneficiary deed.** A recorded deed that transfers real estate at death with no probate. You can revoke it at any time. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).
- **Community property with right of survivorship.** Spouses can title property so that it passes automatically to the survivor, and both halves keep a full basis step-up at the first death.
- **Revocable living trust.** Useful for larger estates, out-of-state property and incapacity planning. See [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) and how to fund a trust.

Retirement accounts and life insurance pass by beneficiary designation, so check them against your plan.

Arizona's retirement population means many families have ties to other states. If you moved to Arizona from a common law state, ask whether property you bought there is treated as quasi-community property, and whether an old will still reflects your wishes. Property in a revocable trust keeps its community or separate character, so the trust should say which is which. Keeping simple records of what you owned before marriage can save heirs from an expensive argument later.

## Taxes

Arizona has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The federal estate tax applies only above $15,000,000 per person in 2026. Because community property gets a full step-up in basis at the first spouse's death, the survivor often faces little capital gains tax on appreciated property. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Arizona

An Arizona [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) is signed in front of a notary and one witness who is not the agent or the agent's spouse. For medical decisions, Arizona has statutory forms for a health care power of attorney and a living will, and the Secretary of State's registry stores them. Arizona also has a separate prehospital medical care directive, commonly printed on orange paper, which tells paramedics not to resuscitate. Read about the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and DNR and POLST.
