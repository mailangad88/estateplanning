Virginia's estate system is straightforward in some ways and quirky in others. Probate starts at a clerk's counter, not a judge's courtroom, and the state has no estate or inheritance tax. But a mistake in how a will is signed, or an assumption about how a spouse inherits, can cost a blended family a lot.

For national context, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## Wills in Virginia

A Virginia will must be in writing and signed by you. Two competent witnesses must see you sign or hear you acknowledge the signature, and they must both be present at the same time. Notarization is not required. A self-proving affidavit, signed before a notary, lets the clerk admit the will without calling witnesses. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Virginia also recognizes a [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will) that is wholly in your handwriting and signed. Two disinterested witnesses must testify that it is your handwriting. That works only if such witnesses exist after you die, which is why handwritten documents are a last resort. See [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills).

Virginia is a common law state with an augmented estate elective share. A surviving spouse who is left out or left little may claim a share of the augmented estate that grows with the length of the marriage, reaching up to 50 percent for long marriages. The augmented estate counts certain transfers made before death, so moving assets into joint names does not defeat it. The spouse also has family allowance and exempt property rights.

The no-will rule is specific. If all of your descendants are also your spouse's, the spouse inherits everything. If you have a child from another relationship, the spouse gets one-third and your descendants split two-thirds. For a blended family, that can leave a spouse short or a child unexpectedly inheriting a house. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Virginia

In Virginia, probate starts at the circuit court clerk's office in the county or city where the person lived. A clerk, or deputy clerk, can admit a will and appoint an executor without a hearing, which is a quicker path than many states.

The executor, called a personal representative, takes an oath, posts a bond unless the will waives it, and receives a certificate of qualification. Within four months the executor must file an inventory with the commissioner of accounts, an officer of the court who reviews accountings. That oversight is a distinctive Virginia feature: the commissioner of accounts reviews the inventory and later accountings, and the process has deadlines. See [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

Virginia also has a small estate affidavit that lets heirs collect personal property when the estate is below a statutory limit, around $50,000. Confirm the current figure. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

Typical timeline drivers include real estate that must be sold, the creditor claim process, and delays with the commissioner of accounts. Our guide to [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) covers the usual causes.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Virginia

Beneficiary designations, payable-on-death accounts and survivorship ownership pass property without probate. Virginia law also allows married couples to own property as tenants by the entirety, which passes automatically to the survivor and also protects against one spouse's creditors. See [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) and [tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety).

For real estate, Virginia has authorized transfer-on-death or beneficiary deeds, but the rules on form and recording are specific, so confirm with an attorney before using one. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is another way to avoid probate and is useful for families with property in more than one state or with minor children. Virginia has a Uniform Trust Code.

Northern Virginia families often own property in the District of Columbia or Maryland, which can trigger [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

## Taxes

Virginia has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026. Neighboring [Maryland](/estate-planning/maryland) taxes both estates and inheritances, so a Virginia resident who owns Maryland property may owe tax there. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Virginia

Virginia has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) must be signed and acknowledged before a notary, and certain powers, such as making gifts or changing beneficiaries, must be listed expressly.

For health care, Virginia lets you sign an Advance Medical Directive that combines a living will and a health care agent designation, witnessed by two people, and the Health Care Decisions Act sets a priority list of decision-makers when no directive exists. Virginia also recognizes Durable Do Not Resuscitate orders. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
