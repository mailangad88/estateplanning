Colorado makes some steps easy: a notary can stand in for witnesses on a will, and a beneficiary deed moves a house outside probate. This guide covers what is specific to Colorado and links to the national guides for the rest.

## Wills in Colorado

A Colorado will is valid when an adult signs it and either two witnesses sign too or the testator acknowledges it before a notary. In practice an attorney uses the witnesses and a notary together, because that also creates a self-proving affidavit and the court can then admit the will without testimony. Colorado also recognizes a handwritten will if the signature and material provisions are in the testator's handwriting, but see [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) for the problems that come with them. Signing steps are in [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Colorado is a common law state with an elective share. The percentage of the augmented estate a spouse can claim rises with the length of the marriage, reaching 50 percent for long marriages. That affects second marriages, where leaving everything to children from the first marriage can be challenged (protecting children from a first marriage).

With no will, the spouse takes the whole estate when every child is also the spouse's, and a fixed amount plus a share when some children are from elsewhere. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in Colorado

Probate takes place in the District Court of the county, or the Denver Probate Court for Denver residents, under Title 15 of the Colorado Revised Statutes. The Colorado Probate Code follows the Uniform Probate Code, which offers informal probate through the court's registrar and formal probate when a judge must rule. Informal, unsupervised probate is the default for most families. The personal representative has to give creditors notice; unknown creditors generally have a limited window after a published notice. See what is probate and how long probate takes.

Colorado offers an affidavit for collecting personal property after a short waiting period, up to a threshold that is adjusted each year for inflation. It does not cover real estate, so a house may need either a recorded beneficiary deed or a small estate probate. Check the current limit with an attorney. See [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for the general procedure and the [probate pillar](/learn/probate) for full administration.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in Colorado

Colorado adopted the Uniform Trust Code, so a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is straightforward to set up. For real estate, however, many Coloradans skip the trust and use a **beneficiary deed**. You sign and record it before death, retain full control, can revoke it, and the named beneficiary takes title at death. Pairing a beneficiary deed with a named contingent beneficiary avoids lapses (contingent beneficiaries). The general tradeoffs are in [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed). A trust is still better when you want conditions, protection for young beneficiaries or planning for incapacity (how to fund a trust).

Many Colorado residents own a second property in the mountains or in another state. Each property that is not in a trust or covered by a beneficiary deed can trigger its own probate in the state where it sits. Review how every deed is titled, and record any beneficiary deed in the county where the land is located, because a deed that is signed but never recorded does nothing. Keep recording receipts with your will.

## Taxes

Colorado has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. The federal estate tax applies only above $15,000,000 per person in 2026. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in Colorado

Colorado has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, and a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) is signed before a notary (or two witnesses in some cases). For health care, Colorado uses a medical durable power of attorney, and a living will or declaration about life-sustaining treatment is available. The state also uses a CPR directive for emergency responders and a MOST form, the Colorado equivalent of a POLST, for seriously ill patients; see DNR and POLST. Compare the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and the living will.
