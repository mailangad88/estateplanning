New Jersey is a rare state where the biggest estate-planning question is not the estate tax but who your heirs are. A New Jersey inheritance tax applies depending on relationship, and it catches families who leave money to siblings, nieces and nephews, or close friends.

## Wills in New Jersey

A New Jersey will must be in writing, signed by you, and signed by two witnesses who saw you sign or heard you acknowledge the will; they can sign afterward within a reasonable time. A notary is not required. An optional self-proving affidavit, signed before a notary or attorney, lets the Surrogate admit the will without contacting the witnesses. The [signing rules in our national guide](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) apply, and a witness who is also a beneficiary can cause avoidable trouble.

New Jersey also recognizes a handwritten will when your signature and the material portions are in your hand, with no date required, and it allows a court to accept an imperfect document if clear and convincing evidence shows you intended it as a will. Electronic wills are not currently permitted, although legislation has been introduced.

With no will, a surviving spouse or partner takes the first $50,000 and half of the rest when all the children are also theirs; children from another relationship reduce the spouse's share. A will is the only way to change that outcome. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will). A surviving spouse is also protected by an elective share of one-third of the augmented estate.

## Probate in New Jersey

Uncontested probate starts at the Surrogate's Court in the county where the person lived. The Surrogate admits the will and issues letters to the executor or administrator; disputes move to the Superior Court's Probate Part. The process mirrors the steps in [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate), with one New Jersey twist: the executor often has to wait for the Division of Taxation's release before banks and title companies will transfer assets, because of the inheritance tax.

The small estate shortcut is narrow. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:10-3 and 3B:10-4, a short affidavit process covers only intestate estates: reportedly $50,000 with a surviving spouse or partner and $20,000 otherwise, with written consent from other heirs in the second case. An estate with a will cannot use it. Our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains the national pattern.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in New Jersey

New Jersey does not currently authorize transfer-on-death deeds for real estate, so the house is the asset most likely to need a plan. Options include a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), joint ownership with a spouse (often as tenants by the entirety, which lets the survivor take automatically), and a lifetime deed with a retained life estate. Each has trade-offs for Medicaid and taxes; see [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate). Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance pass outside probate, but note that New Jersey inheritance tax can still reach some of them.

## Taxes

New Jersey has no estate tax, repealed for deaths after 2017. It does have an inheritance tax with four classes. Class A (spouses, civil union and domestic partners, children, grandchildren and parents) pays nothing. Class C (siblings and children-in-law) pays 11 to 16 percent on amounts above $25,000. Class D (everyone else, including friends, nieces and nephews) pays 15 to 16 percent from the first dollar, and charities are exempt. Life insurance payable to a named beneficiary is generally outside this tax.

The practical effect is that how you title assets and which beneficiaries you name can change the bill. Read [do you pay taxes on an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) and our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes). Confirm current rates with a New Jersey attorney.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in New Jersey

New Jersey adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act in 2016. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) should be signed and acknowledged before a notary so it can be used for real estate. For medical decisions, New Jersey recognizes a proxy directive naming a health care representative and an instruction directive, which is a living will; a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) is the key document. Those documents need either two witnesses or a notary. Practitioners often add a [POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) for serious illness. Confirm current forms with a New Jersey attorney.
