The District of Columbia is not a state, but it has its own estate planning rules, and its estate tax is the feature residents are most surprised by. This guide covers what is specific to DC.

## Wills in the District of Columbia

A DC will must be in writing, signed by an adult testator and signed by two witnesses in the testator's presence. Notarization is not required for validity. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the signing steps. DC does not give handwritten wills a special status, so a [handwritten or DIY will](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) should be treated as needing witnesses. Confirm whether a self-proving affidavit is available with a DC attorney.

A surviving spouse has a statutory elective share and cannot be entirely disinherited by a will. Without a will, a spouse and children generally divide the estate with the spouse taking about one-third and the children two-thirds. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Probate in the District of Columbia

Probate is handled in the Probate Division of the Superior Court, with the Register of Wills handling filing and appointment of the personal representative. DC allows both supervised and unsupervised administration. Unsupervised is common when the beneficiaries agree. The personal representative must give notice to creditors and heirs, file an inventory and account for the estate. See what is probate and probate costs.

DC has two simplified tracks for small estates. An estate of $40,000 or less can use a small estate affidavit after a 60-day wait, without a full court case, and a separate court-supervised simplified track covers estates up to $80,000. Real property is excluded from both tracks, so a home generally needs another route. See [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for the general process and the [probate pillar](/learn/probate) for full administration.

Many DC residents own property in Maryland or Virginia as well. Real estate in another jurisdiction may require ancillary probate, which a funded trust can avoid. See out-of-state property.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in the District of Columbia

DC has adopted the Uniform Trust Code, so a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is well supported. Because DC's estate tax exemption is much lower than the federal one, trusts do more than avoid probate: married couples often use them to preserve both spouses' DC exemptions. Remember that a trust must be funded ([how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust)).

Tenancy by the entirety is available to married couples, giving automatic survivorship and creditor protection from one spouse's debts; see the glossary entry on [tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety). Other probate-avoidance tools include beneficiary designations and payable-on-death accounts; see how to avoid probate. Whether DC offers a transfer-on-death deed is something to confirm with a DC attorney.

Because DC sits beside Maryland and Virginia, many residents work in one jurisdiction, live in another and own property in a third. Domicile, meaning where you intend to make your permanent home, decides which jurisdiction's probate and estate tax rules apply, so keep your voter registration, driver license and tax filings consistent. Document your domicile clearly, especially if you split time between a DC residence and a vacation home.

## Taxes

DC imposes its own estate tax. The exemption is roughly $4.9 million as indexed, far below the federal $15,000,000 for 2026, and rates above the exemption are graduated. That means a DC homeowner with a house, retirement accounts and life insurance can cross the line even if no federal tax is due. DC has no inheritance tax. Confirm the exact 2026 exemption and rates with a DC attorney, and see [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and portability for married couples, noting that federal portability does not apply to the DC exemption.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in the District of Columbia

DC has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, so a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) continues after incapacity. Confirm the current signing requirements with an attorney. For health care, DC's Health-Care Decisions Act allows a durable power of attorney for health care and a living will, and DC uses a MOST form for seriously ill patients. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney), living will and DNR and POLST.
