New York gets more attention than most states because of three features: a state estate tax with a cliff, Surrogate's Court, which handles all estates and trusts, and a statutory power of attorney with its own signing requirements. Each affects how a family plans.

## Wills in New York

A New York will must be signed at the end by you and witnessed by two people. The witnesses do not have to be in the room at the same time, but both must sign within 30 days of each other. A notary is not required. Witnesses can later sign an affidavit under SCPA 1406 to make the will self-proving; without it, the court may contact the witnesses. Our guide on [making a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) covers who should and should not witness.

Unwitnessed handwritten wills are generally invalid in New York. Only armed forces members in active service and mariners at sea can make them, and they lapse after a limited period. An electronic wills law has been enacted but is not yet in effect, so wills remain paper documents.

Without a will, a spouse with children takes the first $50,000 plus half the rest, and the children share the other half. A [blended family](/learn/blended-families) may not want that result. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

A surviving spouse has a right of election: the greater of $50,000 or one-third of the net estate, which counts certain non-probate transfers such as joint accounts and trusts. It must be claimed on a deadline, and you cannot waive it by accident.

## Probate in New York

Probate happens in the Surrogate's Court of the county where the person lived. The executor files a petition with the will, the court issues letters testamentary, and distributees (those who would inherit by intestacy) must be served or must waive. That is why New York probate often takes longer when relatives are missing or estranged. See [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) and [how long it takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take).

New York's small estate option is called voluntary administration. If the personal property is $50,000 or less, an eligible person can apply to the Surrogate's Court clerk, with no waiting period, and collect assets without a full estate. It does not cover real estate. Read [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for the national comparison.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in New York

The usual tools apply: beneficiary designations, payable-on-death accounts and joint ownership. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is widely used in New York to avoid delay with Surrogate's Court, especially for homeowners and owners of out-of-state property. New York has enacted a transfer-on-death deed statute, but it is new and the rules about recording and ownership change are still being tested, so ask a New York attorney before relying on one; see [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

Medicaid planning is a major reason New Yorkers use trusts. Home care and nursing home costs are high, and the look-back period for transfers matters. See [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## Taxes

New York has its own estate tax and no inheritance tax. The basic exclusion amount is $7,350,000 for deaths in 2026, well below the $15 million federal figure. The cliff is the unusual part: if the taxable estate is more than 105 percent of the exclusion, the exclusion phases out and the whole estate is taxed from the first dollar. Estates just above the line can pay far more than their excess warrants. Lifetime gifts made within three years of death can be pulled back into the New York estate. The tax rates rise to 16 percent.

A married couple can use the [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction), but New York does not recognize portability, so a married couple's plan often uses credit shelter trusts. Read [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) and [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes). Confirm current rules with a New York attorney.

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in New York

New York has a Statutory Short Form Power of Attorney, and since 2021 it must be signed by the principal, acknowledged before a notary and witnessed by two people. Lenders and banks commonly reject forms with signing errors; see [when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney). For medical decisions, New York uses a Health Care Proxy under the Public Health Law, witnessed by two adults, and a separate living will. A MOLST form is used for people with serious illness. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
