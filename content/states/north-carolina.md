North Carolina is a tax-friendly state with a clerk-centered probate system and some of the strictest signing rules for medical documents. Many families are surprised by two things: there is no transfer-on-death deed for the house, and a health care power of attorney needs both witnesses and a notary.

## Wills in North Carolina

A North Carolina will must be signed by you and by two competent witnesses, each of whom signs in your presence. The witnesses do not need to sign in each other's presence. Notarization is not required. The optional self-proving affidavit, signed before a notary at the time of the will or later, lets the clerk admit the will without witness testimony. Our guide to [making a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) covers the general rules, and North Carolina enforces them strictly, with no harmless-error exception.

North Carolina recognizes a [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will) that is written entirely in your handwriting and signed. Two people must later testify that it is in your handwriting. Because the rule requires the entire will be handwritten, a typed form with handwritten blanks is not enough. Read [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills). Electronic wills are not permitted, though attorneys may now store executed paper wills electronically.

Without a will, a spouse takes half if one child survives and one-third if two or more children survive. That can be a shock in [blended families](/learn/blended-families). See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will). A surviving spouse also has an elective share of the augmented net estate, scaled by length of marriage, and a year's allowance paid ahead of most creditors.

## Probate in North Carolina

Probate is run by the Clerk of Superior Court in the county of residence, not by a judge. The clerk admits the will, issues letters, reviews the inventory and accounts, and decides many disputes over fees and removals. See [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate). North Carolina requires publishing notice to creditors and sets a deadline of at least three months after the first notice, which drives much of the timeline. Our [probate timeline article](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) explains what slows cases.

For small estates, a successor can collect personal property by affidavit after 30 days. The limit is reported at $20,000 of personal property, or $30,000 when the affiant is a surviving spouse who is the sole heir, with a copy filed with the clerk. Surviving spouses also have a summary administration route. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit). Real estate is not covered by the affidavit.

## Trusts and avoiding probate in North Carolina

North Carolina does not currently authorize transfer-on-death deeds for real estate, so people use other tools for the home. A married couple can hold title as tenants by the entirety, so the survivor takes it automatically and creditors of only one spouse generally cannot reach it. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) works for single owners, couples with children from earlier marriages, and owners of land in more than one state. A [life estate deed](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) is another option but involves loss of control and tax consequences. Our article on [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) compares the choices.

## Taxes

North Carolina repealed its estate tax in 2013 and has no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax applies, which in 2026 affects estates above $15 million per person. Heirs may owe income tax on inherited IRAs; see [do you pay taxes on an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) and our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in North Carolina

North Carolina adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act in Chapter 32C. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) should be notarized. For health care, the state has a statutory Health Care Power of Attorney form (G.S. 32A-25.1) and an Advance Directive for a Natural Death (living will) under G.S. 90-321. Both generally require two qualified witnesses and a notary, and the rules about who can witness are narrow: relatives, heirs and the health care provider generally cannot. A signing mistake is the usual reason a hospital questions these documents. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living wills](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will). Confirm current forms with a North Carolina attorney.
