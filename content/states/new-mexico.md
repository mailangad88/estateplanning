New Mexico combines Uniform Probate Code procedures with community property ownership. The result is a system that is efficient for many families, but one where the details of how you hold property, and whether you signed in front of two witnesses, decide what your family can do.

## Wills in New Mexico

A New Mexico will must be in writing and signed by you in front of two witnesses. Both must watch you sign, and each witness signs while you and the other witness are present. There is no option to simply acknowledge an earlier signature. A notary is not required and cannot serve as a witness. An optional self-proving affidavit lets the court accept the will without testimony. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the general rules.

New Mexico is stricter than many neighbors about informal documents. It does not recognize [holographic wills](/glossary/holographic-will) made in the state, and there is no harmless-error rule to save a defective will. Arizona and Colorado handle this differently, and that matters for people who moved here. A handwritten will signed in another state where it was valid may still be honored. Read [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) before using a template.

If someone dies without a will, a surviving spouse generally keeps all community property and takes a share of separate property when children survive. The exact shares depend on whether the children are all the spouse's, so confirm with an attorney. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for how heirs are ranked.

## Probate in New Mexico

Probate takes place in district court under the New Mexico version of the Uniform Probate Code. An informal process, started with the court clerk or probate judge, appoints a personal representative with limited supervision. The formal process is used for contested estates. The steps are the ones in [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate), and [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) depends largely on the creditor claim period and whether real estate must be sold.

The small estate affidavit under NMSA 45-3-1201 lets a successor collect personal property after a 30-day wait. The estate must be worth $50,000 or less after liens and encumbrances, and the affidavit goes to whoever holds the asset rather than being filed in court. It does not transfer real estate. If the home is in the estate, you may need a different procedure. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

## Trusts and avoiding probate in New Mexico

New Mexico adopted the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act, which authorizes a transfer-on-death deed. It lets a homeowner name a beneficiary while keeping full control and the right to revoke, and it must be recorded before death. Our guide to [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) covers the pitfalls, such as naming a minor or failing to name a backup.

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is the other main option, especially if you own property in several states or want to control timing for young beneficiaries. Because of community property, a couple's trust should state how the community and separate property are characterized. Retirement accounts and life insurance pass by beneficiary designation. Our [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) article compares the tools.

## Taxes

New Mexico has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. Only the federal estate tax can apply. Community property also brings a tax benefit: at the first spouse's death, both halves of community property can receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), which can reduce capital gains if the survivor later sells. See our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Powers of attorney and healthcare directives in New Mexico

New Mexico has adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act for financial agents and the Uniform Health-Care Decisions Act, which includes an optional statutory form for naming a health care agent and stating treatment wishes. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) should be notarized so it can be used for real estate. A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) names the agent who can speak with doctors. Confirm the current statutory forms and signing rules with a New Mexico attorney.
