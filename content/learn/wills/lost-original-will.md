Someone has died, and the family agrees there was a will. A sister remembers it. A son saw it once. But the folder is empty, the desk drawer holds nothing, and the safe deposit box has a passport and a few old coins. Now what?

This page sets out how to search properly, what the law says about a missing original, how a copy can be admitted, and what happens if it can't.

## Where should you look for a missing will?

Start with the places where originals are actually kept, before assuming it's gone. Work through this list in order.

1. **The lawyer who drafted it.** Call the firm. Many hold original wills in a vault. If the firm closed, check the state bar, which can often tell you who took over the files.
2. **The probate court.** In some places a will can be deposited with the court for safekeeping during life. Call the clerk in the county where the person lived.
3. **The safe deposit box.** Banks have rules for access after death. Some allow a family member to open the box to look for a will, under supervision. Others require a court order.
4. **Home files.** A fireproof box, a desk, a filing cabinet, a folder marked "important," a closet shelf. Look at files related to taxes, insurance and the house deed.
5. **The executor named in the will, and the family.** Ask whether anyone was handed an original.
6. **Financial advisers and accountants.** They sometimes keep copies and sometimes originals.
7. **Email and cloud storage.** A scan will not be the original, but it may show the date, the attorney and the witnesses.
8. **Other homes.** A vacation house, a storage unit, a relative's house.

Write down every place you search and when. If the matter goes to court, a judge will want to know how hard you looked. The list in [where to keep your original will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) shows where originals tend to be stored, which is a useful map for the search.

## Why does the law presume a missing will was revoked?

Because a will found missing at death is often a will the maker destroyed on purpose. Courts in most states apply a presumption: if the original was last in the maker's possession and can't be found after death, the maker is presumed to have destroyed it, intending to revoke it. People do tear up wills after a falling out or a change of heart.

The presumption is not final. The family can rebut it with evidence that the will was lost, misplaced, stolen or destroyed by accident, or that the maker still stood by its terms. Some of the strongest evidence is how the maker spoke about the will in the last years. Did they say "my will is with the lawyer" or "I changed my mind and ripped it up"?

The presumption is weaker when the original was never in the maker's hands. A will held at the lawyer's office, then lost when the office moved, is not likely to have been revoked by its maker.

## Can a copy of a will be probated?

Often, yes, but it takes a petition and proof. The usual elements are:

- **The will was validly executed.** It was properly signed and witnessed. The drafting attorney's file or the witnesses can show this. A [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) on the copy is very helpful.
- **The will was lost, not revoked.** Evidence rebuts the presumption.
- **The contents are proved.** A clean copy is the best proof. Some states also allow testimony from people who read the original, and some require more than one witness.
- **The standard of proof is met.** Many states require "clear and convincing" evidence, which is higher than the ordinary civil standard.

The petition is filed with the probate court and notice goes to the heirs. Anyone who would do better without the will can object, and the case can turn into a hearing. A copy that the family agrees on moves faster than one that is disputed. See [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) for how the usual process starts.

### An example

Eleanor, 77, signed a will drafted by her lawyer and kept at the firm. When she died, her son Rick learned the lawyer had retired and sold the practice. The new firm's vault held no will, but the old file contained a signed copy, a conformed copy with the witnesses' names, and a billing note about the signing. One of the witnesses was still working in the same office. Rick petitioned the court, the witness testified and the file was entered into evidence. Eleanor's daughter, who would have inherited more without the will, did not object after seeing the file. The court admitted the copy. It took about two months longer than an ordinary filing.

## What if the court will not accept a copy?

Then the estate passes as if the lost will did not exist. In practice that means one of two things:

- **An earlier valid will,** if there is one. This could be a will from a prior marriage, naming a beneficiary the person later cut out. Strange outcomes can result, as in [what happens if you die with an outdated will](/learn/what-if/outdated-will).
- **State intestacy rules,** which give property to the spouse, children and other relatives in fixed shares. [Dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains how that works.

Neither result reflects the person's actual wishes, which is why the effort of proving the will is often worth it. Sometimes the heirs, all of whom are adults and in agreement, decide to divide the estate as the copy describes, in a settlement approved by the court. That depends on everyone cooperating.

## What should the executor do while the search goes on?

Move carefully. The named [executor](/glossary/executor) has no formal authority until a court appoints them, and acting too early can create liability. In the meantime:

- Secure the home and any valuables, and keep the mail.
- Get a stack of death certificates.
- Keep a written log of the search.
- Do not distribute property or pay anything beyond urgent bills such as insurance and utilities.
- Ask an attorney early. Many courts have a specific process for a lost will, and some require notice to everyone who would inherit by intestacy.

The sequence of tasks after a death is covered in [what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) and [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

## How do you keep this from happening to your own will?

Make the original easy to find and hard to lose.

| Choice | Strength | Weak spot |
|---|---|---|
| Lawyer's vault | Professional storage, a record of custody | Firm may close; keep a receipt |
| Safe deposit box | Fire and theft protection | Access after death can be slow |
| Fireproof home safe | Immediate access | Key or combination may not be shared |
| Court deposit (where offered) | Official record | Not available in every county |
| Desk drawer | Convenient | Easily lost, damaged or destroyed |

Whichever you choose, tell your executor in writing where the original is, keep a copy elsewhere, and put the location in your [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction). A signed copy with a self-proving affidavit is the best backup evidence. Each time you sign a new will, collect the old one only when the new one is safely stored, and note the date.

If your original is already missing and you are alive, you do not need to prove anything. Sign a new will. That is usually faster and cheaper than any legal action your family would face later. Read [changing a will](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) to see how to replace one cleanly.
