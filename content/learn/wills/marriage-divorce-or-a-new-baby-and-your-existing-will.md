A will is a snapshot. The day you sign it, the people in it are the people in your life. Then you marry, divorce, have a child, or lose a spouse, and the picture changes. The law notices some of those changes on its own. It ignores others entirely. The gap between the two is where families get hurt.

This page goes through the three big events one at a time. It explains what the law does automatically, what it leaves to you, and what to do about it. For the mechanics of making changes, see [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils), and the [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers the larger picture.

## The short version

| Event | What the law usually does to your will | What you still must do |
|---|---|---|
| Marriage | Leaves it in place, gives your new spouse rights against it | Rewrite it to include your spouse and reconsider your executor and guardian |
| Divorce | Cancels gifts and roles for the ex-spouse in most states | Rewrite it, and fix every beneficiary form |
| Baby or adoption | May protect the child as an omitted heir | Name the child, name a guardian, create a trust |
| Death of a spouse | Does nothing | Redo everything |

## Does marriage cancel a will?

Not in most states. Marriage does not revoke a will you signed beforehand, so a will written when you were single still says what it said. In a few states, a spouse who marries after the will is signed may take a share as though there were no will, unless the will shows it was written in anticipation of the marriage.

The bigger issue is the spouse's own rights. Most states give a surviving spouse an elective share, a fraction of the estate they can claim even if the will gives them less. Community property states approach it differently by treating half of marital earnings as the spouse's already. The page on [the spousal elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) explains how far these protections go.

Take Aaron, who wrote a will at 30 leaving everything to his brother, then married at 36. He assumed the will still worked. At his death, his wife can claim a share, the brother receives less than Aaron intended, and the family spends a year sorting it out. A marriage is the best reason to revisit the executor and guardian choices too, since the person you named at 30 may no longer be the right one. Our newlyweds page, [estate planning for newlyweds](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-newlyweds), has a checklist.

## Do I need to update my will after a divorce?

Yes, even though the law helps. In most states, a divorce or annulment automatically revokes gifts to the former spouse and removes the ex as executor or trustee, treating the ex as if they had died before you. Some states do not, and a few apply the rule only after the final decree, not while the case is pending.

Three things the automatic rule often misses:

1. **Beneficiary forms.** A life insurance policy or retirement account that still names the ex can be paid to them. Some states cancel those too, but federal rules can override the state rule for employer plans. See [ex-spouse still named as beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).
2. **The ex's family.** Gifts to an ex-stepchild, or an ex-in-law named as backup executor, may stay in place.
3. **Timing.** If you die while the divorce is pending, the will usually remains fully in force.

Waiting for the final decree and then updating is the simplest course. Read [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) for the full list.

## What happens if I have a baby after I make my will?

Many states protect a child born or adopted after the will by treating the child as a pretermitted heir, which means an heir the maker skipped by omission, not choice. The child may receive what they would have received had you died without a will, or a share equal to the other children.

The protection has limits. It often applies only if the will mentions no children at all, or only if you did not provide for the child through other means, such as life insurance. It may also be defeated by a clause stating that you intend to leave out any future children, which is a clause nobody means to include. Some states give a new child very little.

Priya and Joel signed a will before their first daughter was born, leaving everything to each other, and then to Priya's sister. A second child arrives. In some states, the children would claim a share if both parents died. In others, the sister would inherit. Either way, nobody has named a guardian, and a will is the only document that can.

The practical steps for a new family:

1. Name the child, and every future child, in the will by class ("my children").
2. Name a guardian and a backup.
3. Set an age for distribution, or create a [trust for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children), instead of handing over everything at 18.
4. Update the life insurance beneficiary.

Our page on [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) goes further, and [updating your plan when your family grows](/learn/life-stages/updating-your-plan-when-your-family-grows) covers later children and stepchildren.

## What happens to an old will when I make a new one?

The new will controls. If it contains a clause revoking all earlier wills, the old ones are void. If it does not, only the parts that conflict are cancelled, and courts have to figure out what survives. That is the reason to sign a fresh, complete will rather than patch an old one. A [codicil](/glossary/codicil) is fine for a small change like replacing an executor, but it is a poor tool after a marriage or a birth.

Never destroy the old will before the new one is properly signed. If the new will turns out to be defective, you want the old one as a fallback. Once the new one is done, ask your attorney how to retire the old original.

## What if my spouse dies or I remarry later?

A spouse's death changes everything and the will notices none of it. If your spouse was your sole beneficiary and executor, the will now points at someone who cannot act, and unless it names backups, the court fills the gap. Redo the will promptly, and use the moment to reconsider guardians for any minor children. [Estate planning after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies) walks through the order.

Remarriage mixes the two problems above. The new spouse gains rights against your estate, and your children from before may lose ground if the will is not rewritten. Many people in this position set up a trust so the new spouse is looked after for life and the children inherit afterward. The page on [remarrying without updating your will](/learn/blended-families/remarried-without-updating-your-will) describes how that plays out.

A good habit: put a reminder in your calendar for the month after any wedding, decree or birth certificate arrives. Waiting for a convenient time is how wills end up two marriages behind.

## What do the rules look like in practice?

The patterns above hold in most places, but the details differ a great deal, including whether divorce revokes only the gifts or also the appointments, how an omitted child is treated, and whether the elective share reaches non-probate property. The [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) summarize each state's approach, and our [plan finder](/plan-finder) helps you decide what to review first.
