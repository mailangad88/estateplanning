If you are pricing a will, you have likely seen offers from "no cost" online forms to law-firm packages that cost several hundred dollars or more. That spread is wide because the products are different, not just the prices. A form supplies words. A lawyer supplies words plus advice about your state, your family and the rest of your plan.

This article gives typical national ranges, explains what moves the price, and shows what to ask before you pay. The ranges are general market figures, not a quote. Prices vary by state, city and provider, so use them to judge whether an offer is in a reasonable range, not to predict your own bill. If you need the cost of the whole plan, see [how much estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost).

## What does a will usually cost?

For most people the choice is among four routes, and the typical costs run roughly like this:

| Option | Typical use | Strengths | Risks | Attorney review? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DIY form or template | Very simple situations | Lowest cost, quick | Easy to sign wrong; no advice on your assets or state | No |
| Online will service | Simple situations, comfortable with software | Guided questions, generally in the low hundreds of dollars or less | Does not check beneficiary forms or titling; limited help if there is a problem | Usually not, unless an add-on |
| Lawyer, flat fee | Most families, especially with children or property | Tailored, signed with proper formalities, coordinated with the rest of the plan | Higher upfront cost; confirm what is included | Yes |
| Lawyer, hourly | Complex or unusual situations | Pays for the time the problem needs | Harder to predict; ask for an estimate | Yes |

As a rough national picture, a lawyer-drafted simple will for one person often costs in the range of a few hundred dollars up to about $1,000, and a couple's wills often cost a bit less than twice that. A bundle that adds a financial power of attorney and healthcare directives commonly runs higher, often from several hundred to a couple of thousand dollars depending on the market. These are typical ranges, not a survey result, and a given lawyer can fall well outside them.

## What changes the price of a will?

Four factors do most of the work.

1. **Complexity of the family.** A single person leaving everything to a sibling is simple. A married parent of three minor children needs guardian nominations, a trust for the children's inheritance, and a backup plan if both parents die. A blended family needs careful language about who receives what and when. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).
2. **What you own.** Real estate in two states, a business interest, a rental property or a large retirement account raises the amount of analysis. Out-of-state land may need local input. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).
3. **Who drafts and how.** A solo lawyer in a small town, a large firm in a city and an online company all price differently. Remote signing and in-person signing may also differ.
4. **What is bundled.** Some packages are just a will. Others include a power of attorney, a healthcare directive, a living will, a HIPAA release and a review of beneficiary forms. Compare like with like. See the [estate planning documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist).

## What should you ask any provider?

Ask these questions and compare the answers in writing:

- Is the price flat or hourly? Does it include revisions?
- Which documents are included? Power of attorney? Healthcare directive?
- Who supervises the signing, and are witnesses and a notary included?
- Does the provider review how my accounts and house are titled?
- If I change my mind in a year, is there a charge to update?
- Can I talk to a person if a question comes up?

## What does a will not do, whatever it costs?

Many people pay for a will believing it controls everything. It does not. A will controls property in your name alone that has no beneficiary designation. It goes through probate, which has its own fees. And it does not control assets that pass by beneficiary form, joint ownership or trust. Your retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death accounts go to whoever is named on the forms, no matter what the will says. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).

So the real cost of a will is the cost to write it plus what probate may cost later. Our article on [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) explains the components. If the combined picture is expensive in your state, a [living trust](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost) may or may not be a better value.

## When is a will alone not enough?

A will is often enough for modest estates in simple families. It leaves gaps when:

- You have **minor children.** A will names guardians, but money left outright can end up in a court-supervised account. A trust can control timing.
- You have a **blended family.** A will cannot easily protect children from a first marriage after a new spouse takes control.
- You own **property in more than one state.** Each state may require a separate probate.
- You worry about **incapacity.** A will does nothing while you are alive. A power of attorney and healthcare directive are needed for that.
- You expect a **contest.** A well-drafted will with proper signing and documentation is harder to challenge. See [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## What is the cost of getting it wrong?

Cheap and wrong is the most expensive path. Common errors that surface after death:

- **Invalid signing.** Too few witnesses, a witness who is also a beneficiary, or missing a notary where one is needed. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).
- **Ambiguity.** "My jewelry to my kids" without saying how it is divided.
- **Forgotten assets.** A will that does not mention a house bought after it was signed, or that conflicts with beneficiary forms.
- **Missing alternates.** If the executor or a beneficiary has died or cannot serve and no backup is named, the court decides.

The American Bar Association has a resource on the risks of do-it-yourself estate planning at [americanbar.org](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/real_property_trust_estate/resources/estate-planning/diy-estate-planning/), which is a reasonable neutral place to read more.

Consider Priya, 44, a single mother. She uses a template to write a will leaving everything to her 9-year-old son, and she names her brother as guardian. She forgets that her life insurance still names her ex-husband as beneficiary. At her death, the insurance proceeds go to the ex-husband, not her son, no matter what the will says. A lawyer's review of her forms would have caught it. For more on this mismatch, see [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

## Is a cheaper will a mistake?

Not always. If you are single with no children, own little, and have beneficiaries on your accounts, a basic will from a reputable source may be all you need. Pay for more only when your situation calls for more. Our compare page [online will vs. estate attorney](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney) walks through the trade-offs. For wills prepared without a lawyer, read [handwritten, online and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills).

## What other costs come with a will?

- **Notary and witnesses.** A lawyer's office provides these. Do-it-yourself signing may require finding witnesses and a notary.
- **Safekeeping.** Storing the original can cost little or nothing, and some courts and law offices offer storage. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
- **Updates.** Marriage, divorce, a birth or a move should trigger a review.
- **Probate at death.** Court fees, attorney fees and executor expenses are paid from the estate.
