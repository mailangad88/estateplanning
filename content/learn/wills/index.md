A will is the best-known estate planning document, and also the most misunderstood. People assume it settles everything, avoids court, or protects them if they get sick. It does none of those on its own. What a will does well is narrower and still essential: it lets you choose the people who inherit, who runs your estate, and who raises your young children.

This pillar guide covers the whole subject in one place. You will learn what a will does, what it leaves out, how to make one that holds up, and how it fits with trusts, probate, beneficiary forms and incapacity planning. Each section points to a deeper article.

## What is a will and what does it do?

A will is a written, signed document in which a person, called the [testator](/glossary/testator), states how their property should be distributed after death. It takes effect only when you die, and you can change it at any time while you have capacity.

A typical will does five things:

1. **Names an executor.** The [executor](/glossary/executor) is the person who collects assets, pays debts and taxes, and distributes what remains.
2. **Leaves gifts.** Specific gifts, such as a watch or a sum of money, and the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate), which is everything else.
3. **Names a guardian** for any minor children. A will is the usual place to make this choice.
4. **Creates trusts for heirs** who should not receive money outright, such as young children or a person with disabilities.
5. **Gives instructions** on paying debts and taxes and on the executor's powers.

If you want the full picture of everything a plan needs, the [estate planning basics guide](/learn/basics) lists the full set of documents.

## What happens if you don't have a will?

If you die without a valid will, state law decides who inherits and who manages your estate. A court appoints an administrator, and your property goes to relatives in a set order: usually a spouse and children first, then parents, siblings and more distant family. Unmarried partners, stepchildren, friends and charities receive nothing.

The result can be very different from what you would have chosen, and a court rather than you picks the guardian of your minor children. Even families who think the default rules will work often find surprises, especially in blended families. Read [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for how the rules work for spouses, children and other relatives, and who is left out.

## What makes a will legally valid?

Each state sets its own requirements, but the pattern is consistent. The testator must be an adult with [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity). The will must be in writing, signed by the testator, and signed in the presence of witnesses, usually two adults who are not beneficiaries. A [self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) signed before a notary lets the court accept the will without tracking down the witnesses.

The signing is where otherwise careful people go wrong. A missing witness, a witness who is also a beneficiary, or a signature added later can create months of litigation. The details, including a step-by-step signing guide, are in [how to make a legally valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

Some states also recognize handwritten wills. Those are valid in about half of states, with narrow rules, and they are riskier than they look. The article on [handwritten, online and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) explains when a template is enough and the patterns that make DIY wills fail.

## Who should be your executor?

The executor is the person who does the work. They file the will, get court authority, secure property, pay bills, file tax returns and distribute assets. It is a fiduciary role, held to legal duties of loyalty and care, and it can take a year or more.

Choosing the right executor matters more than most people expect. Traits that count include organization, honesty, willingness and the ability to stay calm around grieving family. Birth order is not a qualification. [How to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) covers the tradeoffs among a spouse, an adult child, a friend, co-executors and a professional, and what to do when no one in the family is a good fit.

If you are already in the role, [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) in the after-a-death section lays out the work in order.

## What does a will not control?

This is the section most people need to read twice. A will governs only your probate estate, which means property in your name alone with no beneficiary or survivorship feature.

It does not control:

- Retirement accounts and life insurance, which go to the named beneficiary.
- Jointly owned property with survivorship rights.
- Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts.
- Assets titled to a living trust.

It also does nothing during your life. If you are incapacitated, your will is irrelevant, and you need a power of attorney and a healthcare directive.

The most common estate planning failure is a will that disagrees with the account forms that actually control the money. [What a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) shows what passes outside the will and gives a method for lining everything up. For the account side of the story, see the [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Does a will avoid probate?

No, and this surprises many people. A will is the document that probate enforces. Property passing under the will goes through [probate](/glossary/probate), a court-supervised process in which the will is validated, debts are paid and assets are distributed. The process may be simple and quick, or lengthy and costly depending on the state and the estate.

Probate runs on a general sequence:

1. The executor files the will and a petition with the court.
2. The court validates the will and appoints the executor.
3. The executor notifies heirs and creditors and inventories assets.
4. Debts, taxes and expenses are paid.
5. The remaining property is distributed and the estate closed.

People often want to avoid this, and several tools exist. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) covers how it works, how long it takes, what it costs and how to avoid it. Smaller estates may qualify for a streamlined process.

## How do trusts and wills fit together?

A will and a [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) are not rivals. A living trust holds assets during your life and passes them privately at death without probate, but only if you transfer the assets into it. A will is cheaper, simple and names guardians, but sends assets through court.

| | Will | Revocable living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Takes effect | At death | When signed, if funded |
| Avoids probate | No | For assets retitled to it |
| Handles incapacity | No | Yes, through the successor trustee |
| Public record | Yes, once filed | No |
| Names guardian for minors | Yes | No |
| Typical cost | Lower | Higher |

Many people use both. The will that backs up a trust is the [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will), which sweeps any forgotten or newly acquired assets into the trust. The article explains why trust-based plans still need one and how it can leave assets in probate if the trust is not funded. To compare tools for your own situation, read the [trusts guide](/learn/trusts), starting with the question of will versus trust.

## How do you protect a will from challenges?

A will contest is a lawsuit asking a court to declare a will invalid. Only people with a financial stake can bring one, and only on narrow grounds: lack of capacity, undue influence, fraud, forgery or improper signing. Unfairness alone is not a legal reason.

Contests are expensive and divisive, and a few planning habits make them less likely to succeed. These include using an attorney, documenting capacity, signing with neutral witnesses, explaining unusual decisions in a letter, and talking with your family in advance. [Will contests: who can challenge a will and how to make yours harder to contest](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) walks through the grounds, deadlines, no-contest clauses and disinheritance.

## What about children and guardians?

If you have minor children, the will is typically where you name a guardian, the person who would raise them. The court usually follows your choice unless it is clearly not in the child's best interests. Without a nominated guardian, relatives may compete and the court decides.

Money is a separate question. A child cannot manage a large inheritance, and leaving it outright can mean a court-supervised account and a lump-sum payment at 18. Many parents prefer a trust that delays or stages payments. See the [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship) for how to choose a guardian, what happens when none is named, and the difference between a guardian and a trustee.

## Where should you keep your will?

A will no one can find is useless. Courts generally require the original signed document, and when it cannot be found, many states presume you revoked it. Good storage means a place that is safe from fire and theft and still reachable by your executor, such as a fireproof home safe, an attorney's vault or a court deposit service. A safe deposit box protects the paper but can slow access after death.

[Where to keep your original will so it can actually be found](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) compares each option and gives a short checklist for what to tell your executor.

## What about incapacity?

A will starts working at death, but the most likely planning problem is not death, it is a long period of illness. If you cannot manage your own affairs, someone must pay your bills and handle accounts, and someone must make medical decisions. Those jobs belong to a durable financial power of attorney and a healthcare power of attorney.

Without them, your family may need to petition a court for a guardianship or conservatorship, which is public, slower and costly. Learn how to protect yourself in the [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), and the [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives) for medical decisions.

If you plan to leave one child less than another, or nothing, read [how to disinherit someone or leave unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) for the spouse protections and contest risks that apply.

## How do you build a will that fits your life?

Use this checklist when you plan or review your will.

For typical prices and what to ask providers, see [how much a will costs](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost).

1. **List your assets and how each is titled:** alone, joint, with a beneficiary or in a trust.
2. **Decide who gets what,** including backups if someone dies before you.
3. **Choose an executor and a backup,** and ask each if they are willing.
4. **Name a guardian and backup** if you have minor children.
5. **Decide if any heir needs a trust** because of age, disability, creditor problems or a blended family.
6. **Match beneficiary forms** on retirement accounts and insurance to your plan.
7. **Sign with the correct formalities,** including a self-proving affidavit.
8. **Store the original** and tell your executor where it is.
9. **Add incapacity documents,** a power of attorney and healthcare directive.
10. **Review every three to five years** and after any major change.

Life stage changes which items matter most. If you are a new parent, the guardian decision comes first. If you are retired, beneficiary forms and incapacity planning may matter more. People with a second marriage, a business or a child with special needs need more than a basic will, and the right structure depends on the facts.
