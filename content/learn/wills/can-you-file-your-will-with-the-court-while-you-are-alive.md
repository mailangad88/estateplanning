Plenty of people believe a will has to be filed with a court or a state office the way a deed is recorded at the county. It does not. If you live in Illinois or New Jersey and your will is signed and witnessed correctly, you are done with the legal part. Nothing needs to be sent to anyone.

That leaves a practical question: should you put the original somewhere official anyway? This page covers what filing actually means, what some county offices offer, and how to decide where your original will lives. The wider picture of wills is in our [wills guide](/learn/wills).

## Do you have to file or register your will before you die?

You do not. A will becomes legally effective because of how it was signed, not because of where it is kept. Both states look at the same few things after you die: that you were an adult with [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity), that you signed, and that the right number of witnesses saw you do it. If you want the details of those signing rules, the [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) and [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) pages walk through them.

Some countries keep national will registries. The United States does not have one for ordinary wills, and neither state asks you to record your will in a central database. If a company or website offers to "register" your will for a fee, that is a private storage product. It can be useful as a locator, but it has no legal effect on validity.

## What does "filing" a will mean after death?

Filing means handing the original to the court that will open the estate. This is where the legal duty sits, and it falls on whoever has the will, not on you.

| Step | Illinois | New Jersey |
|---|---|---|
| Who receives the original | The circuit court clerk in the county of residence | The county Surrogate |
| Who must deliver it | Anyone holding the will, immediately after the death | The person holding it, so probate can start |
| Earliest probate can finish | No set waiting period for filing the will | Probate and letters are not completed until the tenth day after death |
| Notice to family afterward | Heirs and legatees get notice within 14 days after the court admits the will | Executor sends a notice of probate within 60 days |

In Illinois the statute is blunt. A person who willfully hides a will for 30 days after learning of the death commits a felony, which is covered in our article on [finding a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois). In New Jersey the Surrogate's office handles the file, as described in the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process).

The key point: all of that is about the original document arriving at the court within days of death. Depositing a will early is just a way of making sure it is already in the right building.

## Will any court hold your will for safekeeping?

Some will. Courts in various states accept sealed wills from living people for a modest fee, and some Illinois circuit clerk and New Jersey Surrogate offices may do so. Availability and fees vary by county and can change, so a phone call is the only reliable answer. Ask these questions:

1. Do you accept a will for safekeeping from a living person?
2. What does it cost, and is the fee one time or yearly?
3. Is the will kept sealed, and who can ask for it?
4. How do I get it back if I want to change it?
5. What proof of death do you need to release it to my executor?

Write down the answers and keep them with your estate planning papers. If the office says no, you have lost nothing.

### What is good and bad about court safekeeping?

On the good side, a court office is fireproof, staffed and not going anywhere. A will held there cannot be quietly thrown out during a house cleanout, and it cannot be hidden by a relative who dislikes the contents. Your executor does not have to hunt.

On the other side, the court does not know you died. Nobody there will call your family. Unless your executor already knows the will is on deposit, the original may sit in a vault for years. Retrieving it to make changes is also a chore, and some people stop updating their will because of the hassle. That is a poor trade, because an outdated will causes far more trouble than a will kept in a drawer. See [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) before you decide.

## Does filing the will early make it private?

No, and this is the misunderstanding that trips up the most people. A sealed will on deposit is private while you are alive. Once you die and the will goes into probate, it becomes part of a court file, and in both states you should assume anyone can ask to read it. Our article on [whether probate is public](/learn/probate/is-probate-public) explains what a stranger can see.

If privacy is your goal, the fix is not early filing. People who want details kept out of the record usually move assets into a living trust, which does not pass through the court file, and leave only a short pour-over will behind. The [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) page shows how that works.

## Where should the original live, then?

For most Illinois and New Jersey families, three places make sense. Choose one and note it.

| Location | Works well when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Your attorney's vault | You used an attorney to draft the will and expect to keep the relationship | The firm moves or closes; your family has to know the firm's name |
| Fireproof home safe | Your executor knows the combination or where the key is | Fire ratings vary and a safe in a bedroom closet is easy to lose track of |
| County court deposit | Your county accepts it and you will update rarely | Nobody at the court tells your family; retrieval takes a trip |

A bank safe deposit box is the one many people pick first and then regret. In Illinois a bank may open a box so a family member can look for a will, but only for that purpose, as our [Illinois will-finding article](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) explains. If your executor is not named on the box, getting in can take longer than anyone wants. The article on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) compares all the options in more depth.

## A worked example

Example only: Walter, 71, lives in Bergen County, New Jersey. His attorney drafted a will in 2019. Walter calls the Surrogate's office and learns it will hold a sealed will for safekeeping, but he would have to come in person to take it back. He is also thinking about adding his granddaughter to the will next year. He decides to keep the original in his attorney's vault, leaves a copy in his desk marked "original at the law office," and tells his daughter Beth, the executor, the firm's name.

Two years later he signs a short codicil. His attorney files the new original with the old one in the same envelope. When Walter dies, Beth makes one phone call and carries both originals to the Surrogate. Nothing about the plan involved a court until the moment it needed one.

## How to make sure your family finds it

A short checklist beats any filing strategy:

- Write one sentence stating where the original is, and who holds any key or code.
- Give that sentence to your executor and to one backup person.
- Keep an unsigned copy with your other papers, labeled as a copy.
- Put a note in your [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) listing every document location.
- Review the note whenever you move the will.

If the original is lost anyway, the family can often still ask the court to admit a copy, which is covered in [what to do if the original will is lost](/learn/wills/lost-original-will).
