Sooner or later someone in the family says it out loud. Mom told us she had a will, but the box is empty. Or: the will that turned up leaves everything to the one child who lived with her, and the signature looks off. Or: a brother admits he threw away the papers "to keep the peace."

Those are three different problems with three different fixes. This page sorts them out, explains what a court wants to see, and lists the first steps for a family that suspects something went wrong.

## The short version

- Cancelling a will is the will-maker's choice alone. A relative cannot do it by destroying paper.
- A missing will can often be proven through a copy, a lawyer's file or witnesses, but the court will ask hard questions.
- A forged will can be set aside, but you have to bring the challenge on time and bring evidence.
- Hiding or destroying a will can be a crime, and the person who does it can lose the inheritance they were trying to protect.
- The first week matters. Preserve everything and write down what you saw.

## What if the person who made the will destroyed it?

If Ray, 80, takes his will out of the desk drawer, tears it in half and says "I'm done with this," that is usually a revocation. The law looks for two things: a physical act, such as tearing, burning, cancelling or obliterating, and an intent to revoke. Ray meets both.

What happens next depends on what else exists. If Ray had an earlier will, some states bring it back, some do not, and some ask what Ray wanted. If there is no earlier will, Ray's state's default heirs inherit, which the [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) page explains. That may be exactly what Ray was trying to avoid.

Two quieter situations lead to arguments:

- **A torn will found after death.** Did Ray tear it, or did it happen in a move? A family member who finds a ripped page cannot tell. Courts often presume a will that was last in the maker's hands and cannot be found was revoked by the maker. That presumption can be rebutted, but you need evidence.
- **Half-cancelled wills.** A line through one clause may revoke the clause or the whole will, depending on the state. Handwritten edits on a signed will are a known source of fights, as the [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) page explains.

## What if someone else hides or destroys the will?

Then the will is not revoked. This is the rule that protects families, and it surprises people. If your sister burns your mother's will after she dies, Mom's gifts still stand. The court's task shifts from "read the will" to "prove what the will said."

Proof usually comes from four places.

1. **A copy.** A photocopy, a scan, a photo on a phone, an email attachment. A court may accept it if you can show the original existed, was signed correctly, and was not destroyed by the maker.
2. **The drafting attorney's file.** Many attorneys keep a signed copy or the draft and notes. The [lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will) page explains how those papers are used.
3. **Witnesses.** The people who watched the signing can describe what happened and, with luck, what the will said. A self-proving affidavit, covered in the [self-proving affidavit explained](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) page, can lock in their account early.
4. **Circumstances.** Who had the key, who benefits from the will being gone, who was in the house after the death.

The standard of proof for a missing will is higher in many states than for an ordinary one. That is why "I remember Mom saying the lake house went to the girls" is not enough, while "here is the signed copy from the lawyer's file and both witnesses" is.

## What if the will looks forged?

A forged will is a fake document passed off as the real thing, or a real will with pages swapped or a signature added. The remedy is a will contest. The [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) page covers who can challenge and on what grounds, and the state pages on [Illinois will contests](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) and [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats) cover the timing rules there.

Courts look at the same handful of signals:

| Signal | What it suggests | How it is tested |
|---|---|---|
| Signature differs from known samples | Possible forgery | Handwriting examiner compares checks, letters and deeds |
| Witnesses cannot be reached or deny signing | Signing may not have happened as stated | Depositions, notary records |
| Document appeared only after death | Possible fabrication | Metadata, drafting history, who found it |
| Gifts depart sharply from stated plans | Possible fraud or undue influence | Statements to friends, prior wills, lawyer's notes |
| Benefiting person arranged the drafting | Suspicious circumstances | Who paid, who attended, who gave instructions |

A forged will is not the same as an unfair one. A will that cuts out a child is legally valid if the maker had capacity and signed properly. Forgery, fraud and [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) are about the document's authenticity or the maker's free choice, not about whether the result seems fair.

## What are the first steps if you suspect misconduct?

Move in this order. Speed matters because evidence disappears and deadlines run.

1. **Write down what you know.** Dates, who said what, where papers were last seen. Do it now, while memory is fresh.
2. **Preserve everything.** Do not clean out the house. Keep the desk, the files, the computer and the phone as they are. Photograph the rooms.
3. **Ask the drafting attorney.** If you know who prepared the will, ask whether they hold the original or a copy. Many attorneys keep originals in a vault, as the [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) page notes.
4. **Check the court.** A will may have been deposited or filed. Search the county probate court records. The [family cannot find your will](/learn/what-if/family-cannot-find-your-will-or-open-the-safe-deposit-box) page describes where wills turn up.
5. **Ask for the will to be produced.** In most states, anyone holding a will must deliver it to the court soon after death, often within weeks. A written demand creates a record.
6. **Talk to a probate attorney before you accuse anyone.** A wrong accusation can damage family relationships and, depending on the state, trigger penalties under a no-contest clause. See the [no-contest clause](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) page.
7. **Watch the deadlines.** Windows to challenge a will can be short, and some states let you file a notice early to be heard before the will is admitted.

## What happens to the person who did it?

The consequences are layered. A court can refuse to admit a doctored will, order that the true will be admitted, remove an executor who engaged in the misconduct, and assign property that was obtained through fraud to the rightful heirs. In many states, someone who commits fraud or destroys a will loses any gift they would have received. Criminal charges for forgery or destruction of a will are possible, though prosecutors vary in how often they bring them.

Families often care more about getting the plan restored than punishing anyone. A judge's ability to look at the whole picture, and the expense of litigation, usually pushes people toward settlement once the evidence is clear. The [executor not doing their job](/learn/probate/executor-not-doing-their-job) page covers remedies when the problem is an executor who stalls or conceals.

## How do you protect your own will from this?

Most of the risk is solved by planning, not litigation.

- Keep the signed original with your attorney or in a secure place your executor can reach.
- Give your executor a clear copy and tell two trusted people where the original is.
- Use a self-proving affidavit so the witnesses' statements are on file.
- Date every revision and destroy old drafts deliberately, not by accident.
- Consider a trust for larger or family-sensitive plans, since a trust avoids some of the exposure of a public will filing.
