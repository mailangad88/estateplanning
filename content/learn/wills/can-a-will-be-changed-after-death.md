The will has been read, and a family member is unhappy with it. Maybe the house went to one child. Maybe a bequest to a cousin makes no sense to anyone. The question comes quickly: now that Mom is gone, can we just change it?

The honest answer is no, not the words. But the outcome often can change, and families do it lawfully every year. This page explains the difference, who can do what, and where people get into trouble.

## The short version

- The words of a will cannot be edited after death by anyone.
- A beneficiary alone can refuse a gift. That is the one change they can make by themselves.
- A group of heirs who agree can often reshuffle their shares by written agreement.
- A court can read an unclear will, and sometimes fix a clear mistake.
- A newer will, if one exists, replaces the old one.

## Why can't a will be changed after the writer dies?

A will speaks for the person who made it, and it only counts if it was signed with the formalities your state requires. Those formalities are the safeguard. If anyone could add a sentence or cross out a name after death, the document would carry no weight. The person who wrote it can no longer confirm anything, so the law treats the signed version as final.

An executor or family member who alters the paper, swaps a page, or backdates a document is committing fraud, and in most places a crime. The court can remove an executor for this, void gifts to anyone who took part and refer the matter for prosecution. It is also easy to catch, since originals are checked against the filed copy and witnesses can be asked.

If you are the one writing a will and hoping to avoid this problem, the better time to fix wording is now, with a [codicil or a new will](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils).

## What lawful options do heirs have?

There are four routes, from simplest to most involved.

| Option | Who acts | What it does | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disclaimer | One beneficiary alone | Refuses a gift; it passes as if they had died first | Cannot direct where it goes, and must be done before accepting benefits. |
| Family settlement agreement | All affected adults together | Divides assets differently than the will says | Everyone must agree; minors and charities complicate it. |
| Court interpretation or reformation | A judge, on petition | Clarifies unclear text, corrects proven mistakes | Needs evidence; rules vary by state. |
| A will contest | An interested person | Asks the court to throw out the will | Needs legal grounds, such as undue influence. |

### Disclaiming an inheritance

A [disclaimer](/glossary/disclaimer) is a formal refusal. It must be in writing, delivered to the executor and signed before you accept the gift or use it. For federal tax purposes, a qualified disclaimer has to happen within nine months of the death, and state law adds its own filing steps. The refused gift is treated as if you died before the person who left it, so it goes where the will says for that case, or to your own children if state law redirects it.

Why do it? Some people refuse because they do not need the money and would rather it went to their kids. Others disclaim a house with a large repair bill, or a gift that would disqualify them from needs-based benefits. The catch: you cannot pick the new recipient. If your aunt left you $50,000 and the will names your sister as backup, your disclaimer sends it to her. If you do not like that result, a disclaimer is the wrong tool.

### A family settlement agreement

If every heir agrees on a different split, many courts allow it. Suppose Lena's father left his lake cabin to Lena and the rest of his estate to her brother Owen. Lena does not want the cabin, and Owen does. They sign a written agreement under which Owen takes the cabin and Lena takes more of the cash. The court reviews it and approves it. Nothing in the will's text changed. The siblings simply used their own legal rights to trade what they received.

This works best when:

- Everyone affected is an adult of sound mind.
- Everyone signs freely, with the chance to ask a lawyer of their own.
- No one is a minor, unborn or a charity, or a court approves for them.
- The will does not include a no-contest clause that treats the agreement as a challenge. See [no-contest clauses](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will).

Watch for gift tax. If one heir gives property to another after receiving it, the IRS may treat that as a gift from the first heir. Gifts are tax-free up to the [annual exclusion of $19,000 per recipient in 2026](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion), and above that a gift tax return is required, though little tax may actually be due. A well-drafted agreement or a qualified disclaimer can avoid the problem, so ask before signing.

### Asking a court to read or correct the will

Sometimes the will is unclear. Language such as "my personal property to my children" leaves open whether it includes a car, or what "children" means in a blended family. A judge can interpret the text using the will as a whole and the writer's likely intent.

A different step is reformation, where a court rewrites a clear mistake, such as a wrong lot number or an omitted word. Standards differ. A growing number of states allow it with clear and convincing proof of what the writer meant, and others stay close to the literal words. Do not assume it will work without a lawyer's view of your state.

## What if you suspect the will is not valid?

That is a different question from changing it. If you believe the person lacked capacity, was pressured, or the signing was defective, you may have grounds for a [will contest](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will). A contest asks the court to set aside the document entirely, and the estate then passes under an older will or by intestate succession. Deadlines are short, often a few months after the will is admitted, so do not wait.

## What happens if a later will turns up?

A properly signed newer will revokes the old one to the extent they conflict. If the family finds it after probate has opened, whoever holds it should give it to the court quickly. Courts can reopen a case, though the longer the delay, the harder it gets, and distributions already made may be difficult to reverse.

Telling your executor where your latest will is kept helps. [Where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) covers it.

## Steps if you are unhappy with a will

1. **Get a copy** of the will and read it in full, including any codicils.
2. **Check the deadlines** for contests and claims in your county. Waiting can end your rights.
3. **List every person** with a stake, including children, charities and backup beneficiaries.
4. **Decide your goal.** Refuse a gift, trade shares with a sibling, fix a mistake or challenge validity each use a different tool.
5. **Do not touch the money** until you decide. Accepting a benefit can ruin a disclaimer.
6. **Talk to a probate attorney** before signing anything. You often only get one chance.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how much time a disclaimer or contest allows, whether a court can correct a mistaken will, how a settlement agreement is approved and what anti-lapse rules apply. If the estate has property in more than one state, more than one set of rules may apply.
