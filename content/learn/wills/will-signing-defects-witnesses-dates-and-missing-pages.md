Wills fail on small things. A witness who left the room before the signature. A page that went missing in a copy shop. An unsigned last page that was supposed to follow. The words are clear and the family agrees what the person meant, and still a judge says the document does not count.

This page goes defect by defect. For each one, you will see how serious it is, whether it can be fixed, and what the court looks at. The overall rules for a valid document sit in [how to make a legally valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will), and the [wills guide](/learn/wills) ties the rest together.

## The short version

- Fatal in most states: no signature, or too few witnesses.
- Usually harmless: no date, a typed or printed form, a missing notary stamp.
- Damaging to one person: a beneficiary witness.
- Risky because of doubt: a missing page, handwritten edits, staple holes.
- Fixable in some states: a signing error excused by clear evidence of intent.

## Is a will valid without a signature?

Almost never. The signature shows that the person adopted the document as their will. A will with every page filled and no signature at the end is, in most places, only a draft.

The detail is usually about where the signature sits. Many states want it at the end, after all the dispositive text. A signature on the first page of a five-page will, with text continuing below, can lead a court to disregard what appears after the signature. Another common problem is signing by someone else. If a person's hand is too weak, another adult can often guide it or sign at the maker's direction and in their presence, but that has to be done right, and the witnesses have to see it.

Some states now let a court rescue a will that was not properly signed if there is clear and convincing evidence the maker intended it as a will. It is not something to rely on. The page on [what happens when someone dies before signing](/learn/what-if/dies-before-signing-their-will) shows how narrow that rescue is.

## Can a will be signed without witnesses?

Generally not, with two exceptions. Some states recognize a [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will), written and signed entirely in the maker's hand, without witnesses. And a handful of states allow a will to be signed before a notary in place of witnesses. Everywhere else, a typed will needs two adult witnesses who watch the signing, or who hear the maker acknowledge the signature, and then sign themselves.

The mistakes follow a pattern. Witnesses sign on a different day. A witness signs on behalf of an absent colleague. One witness is a nurse who stepped out. Courts look hard at each of these because the rules exist to prevent fraud.

## Can a spouse or beneficiary be a witness?

Who witnesses matters as much as how many. The safest witnesses are adults who inherit nothing and are not related to anyone who does. In many states, a beneficiary who witnesses does not invalidate the will but may forfeit their gift, or any part of it above what they would have received without the will. Some states treat a beneficiary's spouse the same way. In states that follow the uniform code, an interested witness does not invalidate the will at all.

Consider Elena, who asks her son's wife to witness her will because she is at the kitchen table. If the will gives the house to that son, a challenger can argue the daughter-in-law had a stake. Even if the will survives, the arguments cost months. Ask two friendly neighbors instead. They sign, they go home, and nobody has a reason to remember the afternoon differently.

The same caution applies to the person who drafts the will. A relative who types it up and then sits in on the signing, especially one who gains under it, hands a challenger an argument about pressure that a neutral setting avoids.

## What is the difference between a witnessed and a notarized will?

They do different jobs, and people mix them up.

| Item | What it does | Needed for validity? |
|---|---|---|
| Two witnesses | Watch you sign and confirm you seemed capable and free of pressure | Yes, in most states |
| Notary | Confirms identity, takes oath in the affidavit | Usually no |
| Self-proving affidavit | Lets the court accept the will without calling the witnesses | No, but strongly advised |

A notary stamp alone on a will without witnesses may not make it valid. A will with witnesses but no notary is valid in most states, and it will only be harder to prove after the witnesses move or die. Details of how the affidavit works are in [the self-proving affidavit explained](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained).

## Can a will be valid without a date?

Yes. The law generally does not require a date. The absence rarely matters when a person has only one will. The date matters if two wills conflict, since the later one usually controls, or if a question comes up about whether the person had capacity at the time. Put the date on every page that bears a signature, and have the witnesses date their own lines.

## Can a will be invalid if a page is missing?

It can, and this is one of the nastier surprises. If the original is missing a page, a court has to decide whether the missing text mattered and whether the page was removed on purpose. Unnumbered pages are the worst case. Someone can slip in a different page, or take one out, and no one can tell.

Protect against it with simple habits:

1. Number every page "Page 2 of 6".
2. Have the maker and witnesses initial each page.
3. Use a binding, not a loose clip. Removing staples to copy pages invites doubt.
4. Keep the original unmarked. Avoid highlighting, pen notes and sticky tabs.
5. Store it with the attorney or in a safe place described in [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

If a page really is missing, courts may admit what remains if it shows a complete and consistent plan. The more the missing page matters, the more difficult the proof, as covered on the page about a [lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will).

## When can a will be thrown out for other reasons?

Even a flawlessly signed will can be challenged on substance. The common grounds are that the maker lacked [capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity), that someone used [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence), that the document was forged, or that a later will revoked it. Each requires evidence, and the burden is generally on the person challenging. The details, deadlines and costs are in [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will). Careful signing habits, including a calm meeting with an attorney and no one who benefits in the room, make challenges harder to win.

## What to do if you suspect your will has a defect

1. Do not mark on the original or try to repair it.
2. Find the drafting lawyer and ask for the file, including any signing notes.
3. Identify the witnesses and notary, and note how to reach them.
4. Sign a new will, correctly, as soon as you can. A new valid will is almost always cheaper than fighting over an old one.
5. Destroy the old original only after the new one is signed, or ask your attorney how to revoke it.
