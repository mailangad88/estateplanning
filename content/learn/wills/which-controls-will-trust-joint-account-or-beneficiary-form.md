Most people own a mix. A house in joint names, a 401(k) with a beneficiary, a savings account in their name alone, maybe a living trust, and a will to tie it together. Then the question arises: if these documents say different things, which one wins?

The short answer is that no single document sits on top. Each controls the assets it was designed for. This page shows you how to tell which is which. For the foundation, see the [wills guide](/learn/wills), and for the narrower list of what a will cannot touch, read [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).

## What is the order of precedence?

Think of it as a series of questions you ask about each asset, in this order.

1. **Is the asset held in a trust?** If the trust owns it, the trust document controls.
2. **Does it have a joint owner with survivorship?** If so, it passes to the surviving owner by operation of law.
3. **Does it have a named beneficiary or transfer-on-death designation?** If so, that form controls.
4. **Is it in your sole name with nothing else attached?** Then the will controls, and if there is no will, your state's intestacy law does.

A will lands last. That surprises people, since it is the document most often called "the plan." In reality it governs what is left after the others have taken their share.

| Document | What it controls | When it takes effect |
|---|---|---|
| Trust | Assets titled in the trust | Right away, and continues after death |
| Joint account with survivorship | The account itself | At the first owner's death |
| Beneficiary form | That account or policy | At death |
| Will | Sole-name assets without a form | At death, after court approval |
| Power of attorney | Your finances while alive | Ends at death |

## Does a will override a joint account?

No. A joint account with right of survivorship goes straight to the surviving owner. The bank does not look at the will, and the account does not pass through probate. Gerald's will might leave his savings equally to his three children, but if the main account is joint with his youngest son, that son owns it when Gerald dies.

The exception is the convenience account. Some parents add an adult child to an account only to help pay bills, with no intention of giving the child the money. Courts in many states let the family show that intent, but it takes evidence, and the other children usually need a lawyer. Naming one child on an account because it is easier, and then leaving everything "equally" in a will, is one of the most common ways to produce an unequal result. The page on [the joint account surprise](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) explains the risks of co-ownership.

## Does a will override a beneficiary form?

No. The form on file with the insurer or plan administrator wins for that asset. This is true whether the form was signed in 1998 or last week, and whether or not the person who signed it remembers it.

A will saying "my life insurance goes to my wife" does nothing if the policy names a former girlfriend. Retirement plans governed by federal law make this even stricter, and the plan can be required to follow its own form. If you want the will and the forms to agree, change the forms. Our guide on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and the page about an [ex-spouse still named as beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) cover the trouble spots.

## Does a will override a trust?

No, but the two are designed to work together. If your revocable trust owns the house, the will cannot give the house to someone else, because the house is not yours as an individual anymore. If you forgot to retitle something into the trust, a pour-over will sends it into the trust at death, so the trust's terms apply. That detour usually requires probate, which is the cost of not funding the trust.

Say Nina's trust leaves everything to her two daughters equally. Her will, written years earlier, gives a rental condo to her son. The condo was never retitled. Because the condo is in her name alone, the will controls it and her son gets it, even though the trust says something different about "everything." If the condo had been in the trust, the trust would have controlled. Which document wins therefore depends on the title, not on the date.

Read more in [will versus trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) and the [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) page.

## Which takes precedence, a will or a power of attorney?

They do not compete, because they work at different times. A power of attorney is for your life. The agent can pay bills, manage investments and sign documents, but only for you, and only until you die. At that moment the authority ends. The executor named in your will takes over, with court authority. An agent who keeps spending after death is acting without authority.

The agent also cannot change your will, make or revoke one on your behalf, or change a beneficiary form unless the power expressly allows it and state law permits. [Power of attorney versus executor](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-executor) explains the handoff, and our page on [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) lists the limits.

## What if two documents truly conflict?

If two documents appear to cover the same asset, the first job is to find out who owns it. Pull the deed, the account statement and the beneficiary confirmation. Whoever holds title or sits on the form typically prevails, and the competing document simply fails as to that asset.

Genuine conflicts where an executor, a trustee and a beneficiary each claim the same property do happen. They tend to involve unsigned changes, forms completed on paper and never processed, a trust that was signed but never funded, or a change made while the owner lacked capacity. Those usually end in court, and the court looks at the documents and the evidence of intent. The [family dispute](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate) steps are the same ones you would take for any estate fight.

## Why do these conflicts happen so often?

Because paperwork piles up in layers. A person opens a bank account in 2003 and adds a daughter for convenience. Takes a job and names a spouse on the 401(k). Signs a will in 2011 and a trust in 2019. Each step made sense then, and nobody lined them up afterward. After a divorce or a death, the forms are the last thing anyone remembers.

The fix is rarely more documents. It is fewer surprises: one list, checked against what you actually want, with the oldest forms looked at first.

## How do you make the documents agree?

A short routine fixes most problems. Do it every few years, and after any family change.

1. List every account, policy and property with the way each is titled and who is named.
2. Mark the ones that pass outside the will.
3. Compare that list with what you want. Is the result equal when you meant equal?
4. Update forms and titles first, then the will.
5. Name backups on every form, so a death does not send the account to the estate.

The [probate asset sorter](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) helps with step two, and [how to organize your documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents) covers where to keep the results.
