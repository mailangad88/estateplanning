Gail named her brother Neil as executor in a [will](/learn/wills) signed in 2014. Neil died last spring. Gail's will still says "my brother Neil," with no one behind him, and she has been meaning to fix it. If Gail dies tomorrow, her daughter will have to ask a judge to be appointed.

This situation is more common than people expect. The executor you choose today may be unable or unwilling when the day comes. This page covers each way an executor can drop out and what the court does.

## What happens if the named executor cannot serve?

The court appoints a replacement, starting with the backup executor in the will. If the will names none, or all are gone, the court appoints an administrator, often with the label "with the will annexed." That person works from your will, not from state default rules, so your gifts are still honored. The difference is only in who runs the process, and how long it takes.

The cost of having no backup is not dramatic, but it is real: a hearing, notice to heirs, sometimes a bond, and weeks added at the start. Our page on [what happens if there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) walks through that path.

Here are the main ways an executor falls out and what follows.

| Situation | What usually happens |
|---|---|
| Named executor died before you | Backup serves; if none, court appoints an administrator |
| Named executor dies after you but before finishing | Successor named in the will, or a court-appointed replacement |
| Named executor declines | Treated the same as unable to serve; backup takes over |
| Executor accepted, then wants out | Needs court permission to resign |
| Executor is disqualified (age, felony, incapacity) | Court appoints the backup |
| Executor is removed for misconduct | Court appoints a replacement, see [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) |

## Do you have to say yes if you're named?

No. Being named in a will is an invitation. Many people decline because they live far away, are overwhelmed by their own grief, or know the family will fight. Declining is not rude and it is not a breach of anything.

To decline cleanly:

1. Do not act as executor. Paying a bill from the estate, taking possession of property or filing the will as executor can be treated as accepting.
2. Sign a written declination. Many probate courts have a short form, often called a renunciation or declination of the right to serve.
3. File it with the court, or give it to the person who will file the will.
4. Tell the family, and the backup executor if there is one, so they do not wait on you.

If you have already started, stepping down is harder. You may file a petition to resign and ask the court to approve an accounting of what you have done. Our Illinois readers can see [how refusing works in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-refuse-to-be-executor-in-illinois).

## Who can't serve at all?

Most states bar minors, and many bar people who have been convicted of a serious crime, are not mentally capable, or in some cases are not residents. The detail varies. A convicted person may be allowed if the judge agrees. A non-resident often can serve with a bond or a local agent. Anyone under guardianship is out. If you are naming a family member with a record, a debt problem or a health condition, ask whether your state's rule affects them, and name a backup either way.

A spouse is a fine executor if they are capable. The weakness is timing, since spouses are often similar in age and may be unwell or overwhelmed after a death. Name a younger backup, such as an adult child. For the traits that make a good executor, see [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).

## Can the executor refuse to be paid?

Yes. An executor is entitled to reasonable compensation under state law, and many family members choose to waive it. There is a tax reason. Executor fees count as taxable income to the executor, while an inheritance generally does not. An adult child who is also the main heir may be better off taking nothing as executor and receiving the same value as part of the estate.

A will may say the executor serves without compensation, but an executor who wants fees may still ask the court to set them. If you want to waive them, do it before you start billing and put it in writing. The rules for paying an executor are explained in [what an executor can take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate).

## Should you name a bank or professional?

For complicated estates or families that do not get along, a professional can be the calmer option. A bank trust department or professional fiduciary can serve as executor or backup. You pay for it, and some will not take small estates. A reasonable pattern is to name a family member first and a professional as the backup, so the estate is never left without someone.

Sofia, 59, has two sons who barely speak. She names her older son as executor and a professional fiduciary as the backup, and she explains to both sons why she did it. When the older son declines after her death, the professional steps in without either brother having to choose a side.

## What if the executor is willing but slow, or in over their head?

Some executors accept and then stall. They miss deadlines, ignore beneficiaries' calls, or realize the job is bigger than they thought. Heirs have options short of removal: ask in writing for a status report, ask the executor to hire a probate attorney paid by the estate, or ask the executor to resign voluntarily and let the backup take over. Courts can compel an accounting and, if the delay harms the estate, remove an executor. That is a last step, and our page on [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) explains the grounds.

Many of these problems are avoided by the choice you make early. An executor who has been told what the job involves, knows where the documents are, and has agreed in advance is far less likely to freeze. Give your executor a copy of the will's location, a list of accounts and the name of your attorney, and ask them directly whether they are willing.

## How do you write a good succession clause?

Name people in order, give their relationship and location, and say what happens if all of them fail. Here is a pattern.

1. First choice: "my daughter, Carla Mendes."
2. Second choice: "my brother, Neil Walsh, if Carla cannot or will not serve."
3. Third choice: "a corporate fiduciary selected by a majority of my adult children."
4. A sentence waiving a bond if your state allows it, so the executor does not have to pay for one.

Then review it every few years. Executors age, move and fall out with families. When one of your people dies or is no longer suitable, [update your will](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) rather than assuming it will sort itself out. And when the person named eventually takes on the job, [what executors actually do](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) is worth reading ahead of time.
