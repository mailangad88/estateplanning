Married couples often ask for one will, together. It sounds tidy: one document, both names, everything to the survivor and then to the kids. Some lawyers and some online services still offer it. But the tidiness hides a question that matters a great deal later. After the first spouse dies, is the survivor free to change the plan?

This page explains the difference between a joint will and mutual wills, where couples get into trouble, and what most of them use instead.

## What is a joint will?

A joint will is one document, signed by two people, that serves as the will of each. It usually says that when the first person dies, everything goes to the other, and when the second dies, everything goes to named beneficiaries, often the children.

The oddity is that it is one piece of paper covering two deaths. It gets filed with the court when the first spouse dies, and in many places again at the second death. It has to be stored for a long time, possibly decades, in one place that both people trust.

## What are mutual wills?

Mutual wills (also called reciprocal wills) are two separate wills, one for each person, with terms that match or fit together. Each spouse leaves property to the other, and then to the same final beneficiaries.

The word "mutual" often carries a legal meaning beyond the similarity. Courts use it for wills made under an agreement that neither person will revoke or change them without the other. If that agreement exists, it is a contract. A will that merely looks like the other spouse's, with no promise attached, is better called a mirror will.

## How do joint, mutual and mirror wills compare?

| Feature | Joint will | Mutual will | Mirror will |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of documents | One | Two | Two |
| Matching terms | Yes | Usually | Yes |
| Contract not to change | Possible | Usually yes | No |
| Survivor free to change | Unclear, depends on wording | Often not | Yes |
| Probate | May be probated twice | Each probated at own death | Each probated at own death |
| Revoked by new will while both alive | Yes, if both agree | Yes, if both agree | Each can revoke their own |
| Fits blended families | Rarely well | Sometimes | No protection for first-marriage kids |
| Common today | Rare | Rare | Very common |

Cost is not a meaningful difference. The [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers the basics behind all of these. A pair of separate wills is a standard job for an estate planning attorney, and a trust costs more. See [how much a will costs](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost) for ranges.

## Why can a joint or mutual will lock in the survivor?

If the spouses agreed that neither would change the plan, a court may treat the agreement as a contract that survives the first death. Once one spouse is gone, the survivor may be held to it. Lawyers call this the "contract not to revoke."

The consequences can be harsh.

- The survivor may not be able to leave property to a new spouse or to a child who needs more help.
- Selling or giving away the house can raise claims from the children named in the first plan.
- Children can sue to enforce the agreement, and sometimes to recover property the survivor gave away.
- The survivor may not know what they can safely do, and neither may the bank.

Whether a binding contract exists is the key battleground. In many states, a joint will does not prove the agreement on its own. The law requires that the will itself say there is a contract, or that a separate signed writing exist. That rule exists because people sign joint wills without ever discussing whether the survivor is locked in, and courts do not want to create a lock by accident.

### An example

Walt and Carol, both in their sixties, signed a joint will in 2009. It left everything to the survivor and then equally to Walt's two sons and Carol's daughter. Walt died in 2021. Carol, 71, needed to sell the house and move closer to her daughter, and later wanted to leave her daughter a bit more because she had been her caregiver. Walt's sons objected, pointing to language in the joint will saying the couple "agreed that this will shall not be revoked." Carol spent a year and a considerable sum of money getting clarity. She could sell the house, the court said, but she could not shift the final shares.

## When does a joint or mutual will make sense?

Rarely, and mostly when a couple has a specific reason to want the survivor held to a promise. Even then, there are better ways to do it.

- **Second marriages where each spouse wants the first family protected.** The motive is understandable. A binding will is a blunt tool, though. A [QTIP or marital trust](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) gives the survivor income and sometimes principal while fixing who ultimately inherits. For more on this, see [how to make sure children from a first marriage inherit](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage).
- **Couples who want a single document.** If that is the whole appeal, a revocable living trust that both spouses sign also uses one document, and it avoids the court filing. Read [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).
- **Jurisdictions or situations where a professional recommends it.** This is uncommon. Ask why, and ask what happens at the second death.

## What do most couples use instead?

For most couples with a straightforward family, two separate wills is the answer. Each spouse's will leaves property to the other, names a backup beneficiary and an [executor](/glossary/executor), and sets out the final plan. Each is free to change their own will, and neither document reaches into the other's. That freedom is a feature: if you outlive your spouse, you can respond to what life has done, such as a child's divorce, a grandchild with special needs or a new relationship.

If you worry about the survivor changing the plan in a way you would not want, say so to your attorney. A trust can split the plan into pieces, with some assets locked for the children and others free for the survivor. This is also the point at which a trust usually beats a will. [Will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) compares them.

## Common mistakes with joint and mutual wills

- **Signing a joint will from a template without understanding the contract language.** One sentence can turn a joint will into a binding agreement.
- **Assuming a joint will avoids probate.** It does not.
- **Forgetting that account forms and joint ownership override the will.** Property held as joint tenants passes to the survivor outside the will, and beneficiary forms pass outside it too. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).
- **Letting the plan go stale.** A joint will signed by newlyweds can be a poor fit by the time a child, a business or a second marriage appears. Cover this in your [review schedule](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).
- **Not realizing a spouse has rights anyway.** In most states, a surviving spouse can claim an [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) against the will, regardless of what it says.

## What if you already have a joint will?

Take it to an attorney and ask three things: does it contain a contract, where is the original, and what do you want to change. If you both are alive and capable, you can usually replace it with two separate wills or a trust. Doing that after one spouse has died is much harder. You can read about changing an existing will in [changing a will: codicils, new wills and revocation](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils).
