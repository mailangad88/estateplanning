Booking the appointment is often the hardest step. Once it is on the calendar, the next worry is showing up unprepared, or being asked questions you cannot answer. You do not need to be a finance person. You need a handful of facts on paper, and an honest idea of who you want to look after your family.

This page lists what to bring to a will appointment with a lawyer in Illinois or New Jersey, what you will be asked, the reasons a lawyer might say no, and what signing day looks like. For the broader subject, start with our [wills guide](/learn/wills).

## What should you bring to a lawyer to make a will?

Bring documents and names. Everything on this list helps, and none of it has to be perfect.

**Documents**

- Photo identification.
- Any existing will, trust, power of attorney or healthcare directive, including old ones.
- The most recent statement for each bank, investment and retirement account, or just a list with the institution and the approximate balance.
- Your deed, or the address and how title is held (one owner, two owners, "joint tenants," "tenants by the entirety").
- Life insurance policy names, or the insurer and policy number.
- A copy of the beneficiary form for each retirement account and policy, if you have it. If not, the name of the company is enough to get started.
- A prenuptial agreement or divorce decree, if either exists.
- Business ownership papers, if you own part of a business.

**Names and decisions**

- Full legal names of your spouse, children and anyone else you want to include, with birth dates for minors.
- Your first and backup choices for executor, and for guardian of any minor child.
- Anyone you want to leave out, and a sentence on why. You do not need to defend the reason, but the lawyer needs to know.
- Specific gifts you already know you want: a piece of jewelry, a car, a sum of money for a grandchild.
- Whether any beneficiary has a disability, a divorce, a debt problem or a gambling or substance issue.

A one-page asset list is the single most helpful thing you can bring. Our article on [how to find a person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) shows what families wish had been written down. Writing it for yourself now saves them that hunt later.

## What will the lawyer ask you?

Expect questions that sound personal because the answers shape the document. Typical ones:

1. Who is in your family, and is everyone getting along?
2. Who do you trust to be executor if your first choice cannot serve?
3. What happens to your house, and does anyone live in it with you?
4. Do you want your property to go outright to your children, or held for them until they are older?
5. Do any of the accounts you listed already name a beneficiary?
6. Has anything been promised to someone, in writing or verbally?
7. Do you want your funeral or burial wishes recorded somewhere?

The last answer may surprise you, since a will is a poor place for funeral instructions because it is often read days after the service. The lawyer will usually suggest a separate document. The article on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) is helpful homework before the meeting.

## Why might a lawyer decline to draft your will?

A lawyer can say no, and the reasons are usually sound ones. These are the ones that come up most.

| Reason | What it looks like | What usually happens next |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity doubts | You cannot say what you own, who your children are, or what the document does | The lawyer may ask for a doctor's letter, schedule a second meeting at a better time of day, or decline |
| Undue influence | A relative speaks for you, answers questions, or wants to be in the room | The lawyer insists on meeting you alone and may refuse if the pattern continues |
| Conflict of interest | The lawyer also represents someone with opposing interests, such as the beneficiary who is paying | The lawyer can refer you to separate counsel, which is a normal and proper outcome |
| Unlawful request | You want a gift conditioned on breaking the law, or a clause that violates public policy | The lawyer explains what can be done instead |
| Scope | The matter is outside what the firm handles, such as a complex business sale | The lawyer tells you at the start |

None of these are personal judgments. They protect the validity of your will. A will that is signed under questionable circumstances can be challenged later, and the person who pays for that is your family. Our article on [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure) discusses the steps lawyers take to document capacity.

## Is it worth paying for a will?

It depends on what is at stake. A single adult renting an apartment with a savings account and a beneficiary form on everything may be fine with a basic form. A homeowner with young children, a second marriage or a family business is a different case, because the cost of a mistake is much higher than the fee.

What the lawyer adds is not the typing. It is checking that your account titles and beneficiary forms agree with the will, picking language that works in your state, supervising the signing, and noticing the thing you did not ask about. Our [online will versus estate attorney comparison](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney) lays out the trade-offs, and the [cost of a will](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost) article gives realistic ranges.

## What happens on signing day?

The signing is short and more formal than most people expect. In both Illinois and New Jersey, your will must be signed in front of witnesses, and the firm will normally provide two who have no interest in your estate. A self-proving affidavit is usually signed in front of a notary at the same time. The [self-proving affidavit explained](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) page tells you why it matters.

You should expect to:

1. Confirm your name, address and that you are signing willingly.
2. Hear the will summarized, or read it aloud if you prefer.
3. Sign or initial where indicated, with the witnesses present.
4. Watch the witnesses sign, then the notary complete the affidavit.
5. Leave with a plan for where the original is stored.

Do not take the original home and add edits in the margin. Handwritten changes after signing can create doubt about the whole document.

## A worked example

Example only: Priya, 44, lives in Union County, New Jersey. She has two children under ten, a house with her husband Dev, a 401(k) and a term life policy through work. She walks into the first meeting with a single page listing the house, two bank accounts, the 401(k) and the policy. She has written Dev's sister Anita as her pick for guardian and her own brother Rohan as backup.

The lawyer notices the 401(k) beneficiary is her mother, who died three years ago. That form would send the account to whoever the plan says is next, which may be her estate. Fixing one form, which would not have been in any will, protects a large share of what her children will inherit. The meeting took an hour, and that single catch was worth more than the fee.
