Most people picture one kind of will: typed pages, a signature, two witnesses. That is the right default. But the law has a few side doors, and families run into them at odd moments. A sailor shipping out in a week. A man in a hospital bed who says what he wants to a nurse. A retiree who owns a cottage in Portugal.

This page walks through the special wills you may hear about, what each one really does, and how rarely any of them is the right choice. This is part of our [wills guide](/learn/wills). If you are making a normal will, start with [how to make a legally valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

## What is an oral (nuncupative) will?

An oral will, formally called a nuncupative will, is a spoken statement of how to divide property after death. Only a minority of states allow one, and those that do wrap it in tight conditions.

The typical conditions look like this:

- The speaker believes death is near, or is on active military duty or at sea.
- Two or three witnesses hear the statement and are asked to listen.
- Someone writes down the words within days, and the witnesses sign or swear to them.
- Only personal property up to a modest dollar cap can pass this way. Real estate is almost never included.
- The will lapses if the speaker survives the danger and lives beyond a short period.

Say Hector, 79, is in intensive care. He tells his daughter and a nurse, "Give the truck to Marcus and split the rest between my kids." If his state allows oral wills and every condition is met, the truck might pass to Marcus. If any piece fails, the statement is just a conversation, and the state's [intestacy rules](/glossary/intestate-succession) divide everything.

Because the stakes are the whole estate and the proof is a memory, courts treat oral wills with suspicion, and relatives who were left out will challenge them. If someone you love is dying and has no will, the better move is a quick written will signed in front of proper witnesses. Our page on [what happens if someone dies before signing their will](/learn/what-if/dies-before-signing-their-will) covers how fast that can sometimes be done.

## What is a military will, and can it be oral?

A military will is an ordinary written will made for someone on active duty, usually through the base legal assistance office. Federal law gives wills prepared and signed under military legal supervision a special status, so a service member stationed far from home does not have to worry that a state's witness rules were missed.

Several points are worth knowing:

1. **It is free to service members.** Legal assistance attorneys prepare simple wills, powers of attorney and healthcare directives at no charge.
2. **It is usually a simple will.** It works well for a single person or a couple with straightforward wishes. It is not built for trusts, businesses or blended families.
3. **Oral military wills are rare.** A few states let someone in actual service declare wishes before witnesses, but the declaration often expires soon after discharge. Nobody should plan around it.
4. **Deployment is the moment to update.** Sign a new will, name a guardian for children, and check that the beneficiary forms for Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance and the Thrift Savings Plan match the will. Those forms control, as the section below explains.

Because a spoken statement made before a deployment is so easy to lose, most service members are better served by a written will, a power of attorney and an [advance healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) signed before they leave.

### What about a will for a veteran?

A veteran uses the same kind of will as anyone else. There is no separate "veteran's will" document. What changes is the paperwork around it. Burial in a national cemetery, a flag, survivor benefits and some health coverage for a spouse or child are handled through the Department of Veterans Affairs and need their own applications. Keep the discharge papers (DD-214) where your executor can find them, and mention them in your [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction).

## What is a statutory will?

A statutory will is a will you fill in on a form that your state legislature wrote. California is the best-known example, and a few other states have similar forms. You check boxes, enter names and sign it before the required witnesses.

The appeal is obvious: it is cheap, it is clearly valid if completed correctly, and no one can argue that the language is ambiguous. The limits are just as obvious.

| Feature | Statutory will | Attorney-drafted will |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Low or free | Flat fee, varies by area |
| Wording | Fixed by the state | Tailored to you |
| Trusts for children | Usually limited or none | Available |
| Blended family or unequal shares | Poor fit | Designed for it |
| Backup plan if a beneficiary dies | A few preset options | Whatever you choose |
| Review for tax or asset problems | None | Included in the process |

The term also has a second meaning in some places: a court-approved will written on behalf of an adult who has lost capacity and cannot sign one. That is a court process, not a form, and it is uncommon.

A statutory will is a reasonable choice for a young single adult with a modest estate who wants something in place this week. For a family with children, a house and a retirement account, it is a placeholder.

## What is an international will?

An international will follows a format created by a 1973 convention so that a will signed in one country is accepted as formally valid in the others that adopted it. A number of U.S. states have adopted the uniform law, though not all.

The format is stricter than a normal will: it must be in writing, signed in front of two witnesses and an authorized person (in the United States that is generally an attorney or a notary), and it carries a certificate attesting that the formalities were met.

What an international will does not do is change the substance. It will not stop a foreign country's inheritance rules from applying to land there. Countries with forced heirship, which reserve a portion of an estate for children, may override your wishes for property inside their borders. The page on [wills when you move states or live abroad](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) explains how to coordinate a home-country will with a local one.

Priya owns a flat in Lisbon and a house in Ohio. A cleaner setup is often two wills drafted together, each stating that it does not revoke the other, rather than a single document that one country's court may not recognize.

## Do these special wills avoid probate?

No. Every form in this article is still a will, and a will goes through probate. An oral will often has a harder time in court than a written one, because the judge needs witness testimony before accepting it. If avoiding court is your goal, look at a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) and beneficiary designations, and read [does a will avoid probate](/learn/wills/does-a-will-avoid-probate).

## Which special will fits which situation?

Use this as a rough map.

1. **You are about to deploy.** Go to the legal assistance office and sign a written will, a power of attorney and a healthcare directive.
2. **You are young, single and have little property.** A statutory form or an inexpensive attorney-drafted simple will is fine. See [how much a will costs](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost).
3. **You own property in two countries.** Ask about coordinated local wills, with an international will as one possible tool.
4. **Someone is dying and has nothing signed.** Arrange written signing quickly. Treat an oral statement as a backup, not a plan.

## What if you are not sure a special will is valid?

Write down what happened while memories are fresh, who was present, what was said and when. Keep every scrap of paper. Then ask an attorney in the state where the person lived whether the document can be admitted to probate. Standards for oral and out-of-state documents vary a great deal, and the deadline to file a will with the court can be short.
