Tomas, 74, wants his old pickup to go to his nephew, $5,000 to a neighbor who drove him to dialysis, forgiveness of a loan to his brother, and everything else split between his two daughters. He thinks that is a simple list. It is, but each item is a different kind of gift in the law's eyes, and each fails or survives in its own way.

This page explains the three gift types and how to word them, then covers what happens when the estate is short.

## What are the three kinds of gifts in a will?

A [will](/learn/wills) uses specific, general and residuary gifts, and the labels decide what happens when something goes wrong. If everything goes smoothly, you will not notice the difference.

| Gift type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Specific | A particular, identifiable item or account | "My 2018 Ford F-150 to my nephew Luis" |
| General | A fixed sum or quantity, paid from the estate as a whole | "$5,000 to Marlene Ortiz" |
| Demonstrative | A sum paid from a named source | "$10,000, to be paid from my savings account at First Credit Union" |
| Residuary | Everything left after debts, expenses and other gifts | "The rest of my estate to my daughters, in equal shares" |

The residuary clause is the most important paragraph in most wills, even though it is the shortest. It catches the house you forgot to mention, the gift that lapsed, and the account you opened last year. Our glossary entry on the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate) covers the term, and [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) lists the assets that skip it.

## What happens if the item is gone when you die?

A specific gift fails if you no longer own the item, a result called ademption. If Tomas sells the pickup in 2028 and buys a van, the gift to his nephew has nothing to attach to. The nephew does not receive the sale price or the van. In many states, a court looks for your intent, and a few let the beneficiary take unpaid sale proceeds, or an insurance payment if the truck was totaled and the check had not yet been paid. Do not count on it. There is also a short rule worth knowing: the more specific the item, the more likely it is to be gone by the time you die.

General gifts do not have this problem, because they are paid in dollars from whatever the estate holds. That makes them sturdier when your assets change often. If the gift matters more than the exact object, use a sum of money. Or write that a replacement passes the same way: "my pickup truck, or any vehicle I own at my death that replaces it."

## How do you leave a car, a ring or a collection?

Describe it so a stranger could identify it, and say who gets it if the first person cannot. Here are the habits that work.

1. **Name the item by type and a distinguishing detail.** "My wedding ring" is better than "my jewelry." A VIN or an engraving is better still for high-value items.
2. **Avoid values.** "The $10,000 painting" invites an argument over the appraisal.
3. **Name the person fully.** A first name alone is how two nieces end up claiming one gift.
4. **Add a backup.** See [what happens when a beneficiary dies first](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death).
5. **Say who pays for shipping and storage.** An executor has to hold a grand piano until the recipient arrives.

A car needs a note of its own. It still has a title and often a loan. Whoever receives it takes it subject to the debt, unless the will says the estate should pay it off, and many wills do not. Our page on [transferring a car, boat or RV after death](/learn/property-and-assets/transfer-a-car-boat-or-rv-after-death) walks through retitling. Houses follow similar rules, covered in [leaving a house in a will](/learn/wills/leaving-a-house-in-a-will).

## Can you leave a gift to a friend, a partner or a charity?

Yes. You can leave a gift to nearly anyone, including friends, an unmarried partner, a non-citizen, a business or a charity, using the same wording you would use for relatives. A will does not need a reason. The practical points are different:

- **Friends and partners** are outside the default inheritance rules, so a will is often the only way they inherit at all. Name them clearly and add a backup, because anti-lapse laws rarely protect them.
- **Charities** should be named by their full legal name and address. A gift to "the cancer society" has been fought over by three organizations.
- **Non-citizens** can inherit. Only a non-citizen spouse faces special estate tax treatment, covered under the [QDOT rules](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot).
- **Retirement accounts** usually pass by beneficiary form, not by will, so a charity gift is often cheaper from an IRA. See [QCDs and donor-advised funds](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds).

## How do you forgive a debt in a will?

You say so in a sentence, and the executor then does not collect it. "I forgive the debt that my brother Carl owes me on the promissory note dated June 2021, and direct my executor to cancel it." The note is an asset of your estate, so forgiving it is a gift to Carl. If Carl owes $20,000 and your other child receives nothing comparable, the shares are unequal. Some people reduce the other child's share to match, or deduct the loan from Carl's inheritance instead of forgiving it. Both are valid. Choose one and write it down.

## What happens when there is not enough money for every gift?

The estate pays debts, taxes and expenses first. Then gifts are cut back in a set order, called abatement, unless the will says otherwise. The usual order is residuary first, then general gifts, then specific gifts. A specific gift is the last to be touched.

Imagine Tomas leaves $5,000 to his neighbor, the truck to his nephew, and the rest to his daughters. If the estate owes more than expected, his daughters' share shrinks first. If the debts exceed the daughters' share, the neighbor's $5,000 is reduced next. The truck is last. Our page on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains which creditors are paid first.

If a mortgage or car loan is attached to a gift, the beneficiary takes it with the debt unless the will provides otherwise. If you want the estate to pay the loan off, say so in the will, and check that the estate has the cash to do it.

## How do you divide personal property among children?

The fairest method is the one your children will not argue over, and that varies. A written list assigned by item works for many families. Others have each child choose in rotation, or sell everything and divide the proceeds. A will can refer to a separate signed list in many states, but not all. Our page on [dividing personal property and family heirlooms](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms) covers the options.
