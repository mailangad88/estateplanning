You find your will in a folder. The date on it is 2004. Maybe you signed it with a lawyer when your kids were small, maybe at a kitchen table with two neighbors. The question that follows is a fair one: does a will expire, or is this thing still good?

The short answer is that it is still good, with a large asterisk. The paper stays valid. The plan inside may not. This page explains the difference and gives you a checklist for deciding whether your old will deserves a fresh look. For the full background on wills, see our [wills guide](/learn/wills).

## Does a will ever expire?

No. Wills do not carry an expiration date in Illinois, New Jersey or anywhere else in the country. Once you sign it correctly, a will remains your will until one of three things happens:

1. You revoke it, usually by signing a later will that says so.
2. You physically destroy it with the intent to revoke it.
3. A change of law or circumstance cancels part of it, such as a divorce.

You can read how revocation works in each state in our articles on [changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois) and [in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-new-jersey). Time passing is not on the list. A court will admit a 40-year-old will in the same way it admits one signed last month, provided someone can show it was properly executed.

That last condition is the practical catch. If your witnesses have died or cannot be found and your will has no [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained), your family may need extra steps to prove the signatures. A will signed years ago without that affidavit is worth checking for this reason alone.

## What does a will not "expire," but still stop working?

A will keeps its legal force while its content drifts away from your life. These are the changes our attorneys see most often when they open a decades-old will.

| What changed | What the old will does now |
|---|---|
| The executor you named has died or moved away | The court picks a substitute, usually a named backup if there is one |
| A beneficiary died before you | That gift may fail or go to their children, depending on the wording and state law |
| You sold the house or account you left to someone | The gift is simply gone; the person gets nothing in its place |
| You divorced | Gifts and appointments for the former spouse are generally cancelled by state law |
| You had a child after signing | The child may have rights that your will did not plan for |
| The guardian for your children moved, divorced or died | The court is not bound to a person you chose a decade ago when circumstances have changed |
| You acquired new assets | They fall into the residue, which may not be what you meant |

The page on [lapsed gifts and simultaneous death](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death) explains what happens to a gift when the beneficiary dies first. The article on [marriage, divorce or a new baby and your existing will](/learn/wills/marriage-divorce-or-a-new-baby-and-your-existing-will) covers the life events in the table above.

## Will an old will from another state still work?

Generally yes. A will that was validly signed under the law where you lived at the time is treated as valid in most states, and that includes a will signed in a different state and then brought to Illinois or New Jersey. The article on [moving states or living abroad with a will](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) covers the details.

The trouble comes elsewhere. An old will may name an executor who lives two states away, refer to laws or forms that no longer exist, or leave out provisions your new state's courts expect to see. It still works, but your family may spend more time and money getting it admitted.

## A worked example

Example only: Dolores, 79, signed a will in 2003 in Illinois. It leaves her house to her husband Frank, then equally to her three children. It names Frank as executor and her brother Leo as backup.

Today, Frank has died, Leo has died, and her youngest daughter Carla has moved to a new state. Dolores moved to a condo, sold the house, and put part of the sale price in an account with her son Marcus as payable-on-death beneficiary.

The will is still valid. But the house gift is gone because the house no longer exists. Neither named executor can serve. The account with Marcus passes outside the will, so he gets that money and the other two children do not, which is a result Dolores never planned. The will itself will leave her remaining property to her three children equally, so some of what she wanted survives. A two-page replacement will, and a quick review of the beneficiary form, would fix all of this.

## How can you tell if your will needs updating?

Pull out your will and work through this list. A single "no" or "not sure" is a reason to book a review.

- Are the executor and the backup both alive, willing and living near enough to do the job?
- Is every beneficiary alive, and is each still someone you want to benefit?
- Do you still own the property that your will gives away by name?
- Is the guardian for your minor children still the right choice?
- Has your marital status changed since you signed?
- Have you had or adopted children since you signed?
- Do your beneficiary forms agree with the will? The [beneficiary designation](/learn/beneficiary-designations) on an account overrides the will for that account.
- Does the will include a self-proving affidavit?
- Do you still live in the state where it was drafted?

For a longer list, our [estate planning documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) walks through the whole plan.

## How often should you actually review a will?

Instead of an age cutoff, pick triggers. Review after a marriage or divorce, a birth or adoption, a death in the family, a move to another state, the sale of a home or business, or a large inheritance. Without any trigger, every five years is plenty. A will does not rot, so the calendar is not what drives the review. Your life is.

### Codicil or new will?

A [codicil](/glossary/codicil) is an amendment signed with the same formalities as a will. It works well for one small change, like swapping an executor. If you have more than one or two changes, or if the original will is more than a decade old, a new will is cleaner. Piling codicils on an old will leaves your family to reconcile several documents, and conflicts between them are a common source of will disputes. The article on [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) helps you pick.

## What about the old original?

When you sign a new will, the old one does not need to be burned, but keep it away from the new one. Some people store old originals with the lawyer marked "revoked and replaced." Confusion over which document is current is what you want to prevent. Never write notes on an existing will. If you lose track of the current original, see [lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will).
