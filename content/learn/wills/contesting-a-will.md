Few things hurt a family like a lawsuit over a will. Maybe you are worried that one of your children will challenge your plan. Or you have just read a will that seems unfair and wonder whether you have any recourse. Both sides need the same facts: who can contest, what a court will actually listen to, and how the process runs.

This guide covers the legal grounds, the deadlines, what contests cost, and the planning steps that make a will much harder to knock down.

## What is a will contest?

A will contest is a lawsuit in probate court asking a judge to declare a will, or part of it, invalid. If the challenger wins, the court usually applies an earlier will if there is a valid one, or falls back on intestacy under state law. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for how that default works.

Contests are less common than people fear, and most fail. Courts start from a presumption that a properly signed will reflects the maker's wishes. The challenger carries the burden of proof, and the evidence needed is substantial.

## Who has standing to contest a will?

Only someone with a financial interest in the outcome can sue. Lawyers call this standing. Typically it includes:

- **Heirs.** A child, spouse or other relative who would inherit if there were no will.
- **Beneficiaries of an earlier will.** Someone who received more under a prior version.
- **Sometimes creditors or others** with a claim against the estate, in narrow situations.

A friend, a caregiver, or a distant cousin who would not inherit either way has no standing. A beneficiary who is happy with their gift usually has no reason to sue, though they may be dragged in to defend.

## What are the legal grounds for a contest?

Courts accept a short list of reasons.

### Lack of testamentary capacity

The [testator](/glossary/testator) must have had [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) when signing. This standard is lower than most people expect. It asks whether the person knew they were making a will, roughly what they owned, who their closest family were, and how those pieces fit together. A diagnosis such as dementia does not by itself defeat a will, because capacity is judged at the signing, and some people have lucid periods.

### Undue influence

[Undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) means someone overpowered the testator's own judgment, so the will reflects the influencer's wishes instead. Courts look for warning signs: a caregiver or new friend who isolates the person, a beneficiary who arranged the lawyer visit or was present at the signing, a sudden change that benefits that person, and a person who was dependent and vulnerable. In many states, if a person in a position of trust receives a large gift, the burden may shift to them to explain it.

### Fraud, forgery or mistake

Fraud in the execution means the testator was tricked into signing a will they did not understand, such as being told a document was something else. Fraud in the inducement means lies were used to cause a particular gift, for example convincing a parent that a sibling had stolen from them. Forgery means the signature is not genuine.

### Improper execution

If the will lacks required witnesses, a signature or other formalities, it may be invalid. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the usual rules. A self-proving affidavit often helps establish proper execution.

### Revocation

A challenger may argue a later will exists, or that the testator revoked the document by destroying it. This is common with lost originals.

## What does not work as a ground?

Being surprised, hurt or left out is not enough. A parent is generally free to leave unequal amounts to children, disinherit a child or leave everything to a charity. The question for the court is whether the will was valid, not whether it was fair. Disinheritance is covered below.

## How does a contest work, and how long do you have?

A typical contest follows these stages.

1. **The will is filed** with the probate court and heirs receive notice.
2. **A deadline starts.** In many states this is a matter of months from admission or notice, though it may be longer in some states. Missing it can end the challenge.
3. **The challenger files an objection or petition** stating the grounds.
4. **Discovery.** Lawyers gather medical records, drafts of the will, notes from the drafting attorney and testimony from witnesses and doctors.
5. **Settlement talks or trial.** Most contests settle. Some go to a judge, and in some states a jury.
6. **A ruling.** If the will stands, the estate proceeds. If not, a prior will or intestacy applies.

Contests are expensive. Legal fees can exceed the amount in dispute, they delay distributions to everyone, and they strain relationships permanently. A family that contests a will often loses more than it gains. If you are a potential challenger, speak with an attorney early, because the clock begins before you are ready.

## How do you make your will harder to contest?

You cannot stop someone from suing. You can make it much harder to win. The best steps are practical.

1. **Use an attorney,** especially for any plan that treats heirs unequally or cuts someone out. A lawyer who meets the client alone, explains the plan, and keeps notes is a strong witness.
2. **Sign with full formalities** and a self-proving affidavit, using neutral witnesses.
3. **Document capacity.** If you are older or ill, ask your physician for a short letter or evaluation near the signing date. Have the attorney record the questions asked and your answers.
4. **Keep beneficiaries out of the room.** The person who benefits should not drive you to the office, attend the meeting or hold the paperwork.
5. **Explain your reasons.** A brief letter to your family, or a clause in the will, can turn a mystery into an understood decision. A disinherited child who sees a calm explanation is less likely to claim fraud.
6. **Say it plainly.** If you intend to leave a child nothing, name them and state it. A silent omission can look like an oversight.
7. **Talk to your family while you can.** See [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan). Surprises drive lawsuits.
8. **Consider a living trust.** A [revocable trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can be harder to challenge in practice, since it is private and creates no public probate hearing, though trusts can still be contested on the same grounds.
9. **Choose the right executor.** A neutral person lowers the temperature. See [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).

### Example: an explained disinheritance

Carl, 74, decided to leave his estate to two of his three children because the third, Ron, had not spoken to him in fifteen years. Carl's attorney met with him alone, asked him to describe his family and assets, and had him sign with a self-proving affidavit. The will named Ron, stated that the omission was intentional, and Carl left Ron a letter. Ron could still sue, but there was little evidence of incapacity, influence or error.

## Do no-contest clauses work?

A [no-contest clause](/glossary/no-contest-clause), also called an in terrorem clause, says that anyone who challenges the will forfeits what they would otherwise receive. It can be a deterrent when the beneficiary stands to lose something meaningful. It does nothing against a person who gets nothing under the will, since there is nothing to forfeit, which is why it is not a defense against a disinherited child.

States differ on enforcement. Some enforce these clauses strictly. Many enforce them only if the challenge lacked probable cause, and a few states do not enforce them at all. An attorney can advise how your state treats them and whether to include one.

## Can you disinherit someone?

Usually, within limits. In most states you can leave a child out of your will, provided you do so deliberately. Louisiana has forced heirship for some young or disabled children. A surviving spouse generally cannot be cut out entirely, because of the [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) or community property rules, unless a valid prenuptial or postnuptial agreement says otherwise. If you have children from a prior marriage, specific planning is essential. See [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage). The planning side is covered in [how to disinherit someone or leave unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).

## What if you are considering a contest?

Ask yourself four questions. Do I have standing? Is there a real legal ground, with evidence? What is the deadline? What is the likely cost compared with the gain? Then consult an attorney quickly, before any deadline passes. Many states allow you to request copies of the will and estate documents, and you may be able to check the court file yourself.
