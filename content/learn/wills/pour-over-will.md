If you have a living trust, or you are thinking about one, you will probably hear an attorney say you also need a "pour-over will." That sounds like a duplicate. It is not. The two documents do different jobs, and the second one is the backstop for the first.

This guide explains what a pour-over will does, how assets move through it, what it cannot fix, and how to set it up so your trust plan works the way you expect.

## What is a pour-over will?

A [pour-over will](/glossary/pour-over-will) is a will whose main instruction is simple: everything I own that is not already in my trust goes to my trust. The assets "pour over" from your estate into the trust, where the trustee manages and distributes them under the trust's terms.

Most pour-over wills are short, often two or three pages. They typically include:

- A clause leaving the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate) to the trustee of your living trust.
- An executor and backup executor.
- A guardian nomination for minor children, because trusts cannot appoint guardians.
- Boilerplate on debts, taxes and the executor's powers.

The will is the signing-and-witnessing kind, with the same formalities described in [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will). A trust does not replace those rules for the will.

## Why would you need a will if you have a trust?

A [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) works only for assets titled in the name of the trust. Real life leaves gaps.

- You open a new bank account and forget to title it to the trust.
- You inherit money or receive a legal settlement shortly before death.
- You buy a car, or a second property, and it is titled to you personally.
- A refund, final paycheck or tax refund arrives after you die.
- You become incapacitated before finishing the retitling.

Without a pour-over will, those assets would pass under state intestacy law, possibly to relatives you did not choose, and not through your trust. With one, they are directed to the trustee and then handled under your own instructions. The will also provides the only place to name a guardian for your minor children, a point covered in [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian).

### Example: where a pour-over will helps

Helen, 67, set up a revocable trust and moved her house and brokerage account into it. Two years later her brother died and left her $90,000, which landed in a bank account titled in her own name. She died before retitling it. Because her pour-over will directed the account into her trust, the executor collected the $90,000 through probate and handed it to the successor trustee, who distributed it to Helen's grandchildren in the trusts she had set up. Without the will, the money would have passed to Helen's nearest heirs under the statute, not her plan.

## How does a pour-over will work after death?

The sequence runs in order.

1. **The executor files the will** with the probate court, and the court confirms the appointment.
2. **The executor identifies the probate assets,** which are those not in the trust and without a beneficiary or survivorship feature.
3. **Debts, expenses and taxes are paid** from the probate estate.
4. **What remains is transferred to the trustee,** who adds it to the trust.
5. **The trustee distributes everything** according to the trust. If the trust is already being administered, the added assets simply join the rest.

The key point is step 1: assets passing by pour-over will generally go through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) before reaching the trust. If the probate estate is small enough, a simplified procedure may be available, and the cost is limited. If large assets are left outside the trust, you could face the delay and cost that a trust was designed to avoid.

## Does a pour-over will avoid probate?

No. A pour-over will does not skip court, since it is a will. It only makes sure that anything caught by probate lands in the right place. Avoiding probate requires funding the trust during your life, as explained in [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust), and using tools such as beneficiary designations and transfer-on-death registrations. See [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) for seven options.

Think of the plan as two lines of defense. Funding your trust is the first. The pour-over will is the second, and you hope it has little to do.

## What can't a pour-over will fix?

It does not override beneficiary forms. A retirement account naming your sister passes to your sister, whatever the will says. It does not reach jointly owned property that passes to a surviving co-owner. It does not give you privacy for assets that go through probate, since the will becomes a public court record once filed. And it cannot restructure a plan that was never coordinated. The companion piece, [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do), explains these limits.

Also remember that the trust the will pours into must be valid. If the trust is never signed, or its terms are unclear, the pour-over will has nowhere to send the assets.

## Does the will or the trust come first?

Most states follow a uniform approach that lets a will pour into a trust that exists when the will is signed, or one created at the same time. Many states also allow a will to pour into a trust that is amended later. This matters because it lets you update the trust as life changes without re-signing the will.

A few states have narrower rules. Attorneys usually sign the trust and pour-over will in the same session, which removes any doubt about timing. If you inherit a document or move, have the combination reviewed under your new state's law.

## Who should have a pour-over will?

Anyone with a living trust. It is also worth considering when you are planning to create a trust but have not finished funding it, since it works as a placeholder. The decision between the two documents is covered in [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust). A trust is not right for everyone, but for those who choose one, the pour-over will is a normal and inexpensive companion, usually included in the same package.

## How do you keep the pour-over will from doing too much?

Use a short checklist once a year or after any change.

- List every account and asset, and mark whether it is titled to the trust, has a beneficiary, or is in your name alone.
- Retitle any bank, brokerage or real estate accounts that were left out.
- Name your trust or your family as beneficiary on accounts where that fits your plan. A retirement account is special because of tax rules, so check before changing it.
- Keep the original will and trust somewhere your executor and trustee can find, as discussed in [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
- Look at the [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) overview if you want a refresher on how the pieces fit.

The goal is for the pour-over will to catch only small leftovers, not a house.
