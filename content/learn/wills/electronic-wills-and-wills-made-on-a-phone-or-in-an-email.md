Plenty of people have written down who gets what on their phone at 2 a.m. after a scare. Others have emailed a "last wishes" message to a brother or typed a will in a word processor and never printed it. The question that follows is a fair one: does any of that count?

This page explains where electronic wills are legal, what a state usually demands before it will accept one, why the phone-note version fails, and a safe way to use technology without betting your family's plan on a gap in the law.

## The short version

- A will has to be a signed writing, as the [wills guide](/learn/wills) explains. Most states add two adult witnesses who watch you sign.
- Electronic wills are allowed in a growing list of states, each with its own steps. They are not allowed everywhere.
- A note, text or email sent to yourself or a relative almost never meets those steps.
- If a court is later asked to rescue a sloppy document, the cost lands on the people you meant to protect.
- Drafting on a screen is fine. Signing is where the law is picky.

## What is an electronic will?

An electronic will is a will that exists only as a digital file and is signed with an electronic signature, instead of ink on paper. It is a more formal thing than the note on your phone. A true e-will usually comes out of a process: an attorney or platform prepares the document, you sign it electronically, and witnesses sign electronically too, with a record of who was present and how identity was checked.

Think about what the old paper rules were guarding against. A will takes effect when the only person who knows what it was meant to say is gone. The witnesses, the signature and the original page are all evidence that you really made this document, understood it and were not tricked. A state that allows electronic wills still wants that evidence. It just accepts it in a different form: a tamper-evident file, an audit trail, a recorded video.

## Which states allow them?

A number of states have passed electronic will laws, many based on a uniform act written for that purpose, and others had temporary emergency rules that were later made permanent or allowed to lapse. Some states recognize only paper wills. Some recognize a will that was valid where it was signed, so a document signed correctly in one state can often be honored in another.

Because this area has moved quickly, you should treat any general statement, including this one, as a starting point. Illinois, for example, has a statute covering electronic wills and video witnessing, and the details are on the [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) page. For New Jersey, the [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) page lays out what a will needs there. If you live somewhere else, ask the drafting attorney which rule applies on the day you sign.

## Why does a note on a phone usually fail?

Compare what an e-will statute asks for with what a notes app gives you.

| Requirement | Typical e-will process | Note, text or email |
|---|---|---|
| Writing | A document in a readable file | Yes |
| Signature | Electronic signature with intent to sign | Often none, or a typed name at the bottom |
| Witnesses | Two, present in person or by approved video | None |
| Identity check | Photo ID or similar step recorded | None |
| Tamper protection | File sealed or stored by a custodian | Anyone with the phone can edit it |
| Storage | Named custodian or attorney | Wherever the phone happens to be |

The last line catches people off guard. A note can be edited by anyone who knows your passcode. Suppose Dana, 63, leaves a note saying the lake cabin goes to her daughter Priya. If her son Marcus later says he saw the note, said otherwise, and changed it, there is no way to prove who typed what. A court has no reason to trust it.

### The harmless error exception

A few states have a rule often called harmless error. It lets a judge accept a document as a will, even though it missed a formality, if there is clear and convincing proof you meant it to be your will. New Jersey is among the states with a rule along those lines. Judges have used these rules for unsigned drafts, for a will with one witness instead of two, and occasionally for electronic documents.

This is a rescue, not a plan. Somebody has to ask the court, the other heirs get to argue, and the judge has to hear evidence about your intent. The note on the phone may win, or it may not. For anyone who wants to see how that plays out for a document nobody signed, the [unsigned will what-if](/learn/what-if/dies-before-signing-their-will) walks through it.

## How do you sign an electronic will correctly?

If you want the convenience of a screen, here is a sensible order of operations.

1. **Ask which rules apply.** The state where you live decides, and so can a state where you own land or where you might move. Ask the attorney, in writing, whether an electronic will is recognized and what witnessing method is allowed.
2. **Use a real drafting process.** The text still has to say what you want, name an executor, and cover a backup if a beneficiary dies first. The [parts of a will](/learn/wills/parts-of-a-will-what-each-clause-does) page covers what belongs in it.
3. **Sign in the approved way.** That might mean a platform that records your signature and the witnesses' signatures, or a video session where your identity is checked on camera.
4. **Add a self-proving affidavit if the state allows it.** This is a sworn statement by the witnesses, and it shortens court proof later. The [self-proving affidavit explained](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) page shows how it works.
5. **Decide where the original lives.** Tell your executor who holds the electronic file or the signed paper copy, and how to reach the custodian.
6. **Check it after moves and big changes.** A will that was signed in the approved way in Illinois may be treated differently if you later live elsewhere, as the [moving states or living abroad](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) page explains.

## Can you just print it and sign it?

Yes, and for many people this is the cleanest answer. Draft on the computer, print the pages, sign in ink in front of two qualified witnesses, and keep the paper original. A signed paper will works in every state, and the courts have centuries of habit around it. The [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) page lists the steps that matter most.

The same is true of documents beyond the will. If you are signing a power of attorney or health directive, the [signing estate planning documents electronically](/learn/basics/sign-estate-planning-documents-electronically) page covers which ones tolerate electronic signing and which are fussy.

## What about voice notes, videos and texts to family?

None of them replace a will, and several can cause trouble. A video where you say "everything goes to the kids equally" can be played at a hearing by a child who was left a smaller share, by a stepchild, or by a cousin hoping to show you were confused. A text to your daughter that says "the house is yours" may be read as a promise and can conflict with a signed will that says something else.

The better use of recordings is as support for a real will. Some attorneys film the signing ceremony, with the person answering simple questions about their family and property, to show later that they understood what they did. That belongs in the file, with the signed will, and it is covered in [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).

For wishes that are not about money, such as music at a service or who takes the dog, a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) is the right home. It guides people without trying to be the will.

## What if you already made a phone note or an email?

Do not delete it in a panic, and do not rely on it either. Take it to an attorney, since it shows what you were thinking, and have a proper will prepared and signed. Once the new will is signed, the note is a historical curiosity. If you die before you get that done, your family can ask a court whether the note qualifies, and the odds depend on the state and on the proof.

One more practical point. Passwords and logins are a separate problem from the will. If your executor cannot get into your phone, they cannot find the note, and they cannot see the accounts you meant to leave. The [digital assets planning](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan) guide explains how to make that access lawful and workable.
