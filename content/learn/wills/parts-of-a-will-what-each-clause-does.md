Neil, 48, has been using a will template from the internet. It has fourteen paragraphs, three of which he does not understand and one that mentions "my personal representative" when he thought he was naming an executor. He wants to know whether the clauses matter, or whether he can sign and move on.

They matter, but there are not as many as the template makes it look. This page goes through a standard will in the order a lawyer would draft it, says what each part does and shows where mistakes tend to hide. It belongs with the other pages in our [wills guide](/learn/wills), and it assumes you want to understand a document, not memorize one.

## The short version

- Every will does five things: it revokes older wills, appoints people, makes gifts, handles backups and proves itself.
- The residuary clause is the one that decides what happens to everything you forgot.
- A will controls only property in your name alone. Accounts with a beneficiary, joint property and trust assets pass outside it.
- Short and clear beats long and clever. A clause nobody can apply is worse than no clause.

## What are the parts of a will?

Most wills run in the same order. The wording varies, but the structure rarely does.

| Clause | What it does | Where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Opening declaration | Names you, your county and state, and says the document is your will | A nickname or a missing legal name invites a later dispute |
| Revocation | Cancels every earlier will and codicil | Omitted, it can leave an old will partly in force |
| Family information | Lists spouse, children and sometimes deceased children | A child left out by name can later claim to have been forgotten |
| Executor | Names who administers the estate, and a backup | One name only, and the person has died or declined |
| Guardian | Names who raises minor children | Outdated after the family moves or changes |
| Specific gifts | Leaves named items or sums to named people | The item was sold, or the person died first |
| Residuary gift | Leaves everything left over | Written as "my children" with no plan for a child who dies first |
| Survivorship | Requires a beneficiary to outlive you by a stated period | Missing, so property passes through two estates |
| Powers of the executor | Lets the executor sell, lease or distribute without court approval | Too narrow, so every step needs a judge |
| Debts and taxes | Says what is paid from where | Silent, so state default rules apply |
| Signature and testimonium | Records that you signed, with date and place | Signed on the wrong page or with a page missing |
| Attestation and affidavit | Witnesses state they watched you sign | Witnesses who are also beneficiaries |

## What does the beginning of a will do?

The opening names you and says that this is your will and that it revokes every earlier one. The revocation sentence is dull and powerful. Without it, a new will cancels an old one only where the two conflict, so an old gift you forgot can survive. Our page on [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) covers how the mechanics work if you are updating.

Next comes a short paragraph about your family: your spouse, your children by name, and sometimes a note about a child who has died and leaves children of their own. This is not sentiment. It lets the court see that you knew who your relatives were. A person who is omitted on purpose should still be mentioned, so no one can later say you simply forgot them. See [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) for how to do that.

## Who do you appoint in a will?

You appoint three kinds of people, and you should name a backup for each.

1. **An executor.** This is the person who files the will with the court, collects the assets, pays the debts and distributes what remains. The law calls them different names in different states, such as personal representative. See [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) for what to weigh.
2. **A guardian for minor children.** Only a will can do this in most states. If both parents die, the court looks first at the person named. Without one, a judge chooses.
3. **A trustee, if the will creates a trust.** A will for young children often holds their inheritance in a trust until they reach an age you choose. That is a more advanced will, and the trustee needs the same care as an executor.

Add a line waiving the bond if you trust the executor. A court may otherwise require the executor to buy an insurance bond, which costs the estate money.

## How are gifts written?

Gifts come in three kinds: specific gifts of named things, general gifts of a sum of money, and the residuary gift of everything else. The distinctions affect what happens when money runs short, and they are laid out in [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts).

Two drafting habits are worth copying.

**Describe gifts so a stranger could find them.** "My grandmother's ring" is a gift that a court has to guess at. "The gold ring with a sapphire that my grandmother Ruth Alvarez wore, which is kept in my bedroom dresser" is a gift that a nephew can claim. For a long list of belongings, a signed written list kept with the will can be recognized in some states and ignored in others. Ask before depending on it.

**Write the residuary gift as carefully as the rest.** The residuary clause is the net under all the others. If it fails because the named person died first, the estate may pass by default to your relatives under state law. A good clause names a first taker, a backup, and a final taker, perhaps a charity. In our experience, the fights come more often from something the person forgot to mention than from something they wrote.

## What is a survivorship clause?

It is a clause that treats a beneficiary as having died before you if they do not outlive you by a stated period. Commonly that is 30, 60 or 120 days, and the number is a choice.

Without it, property can be passed to a beneficiary who dies a few hours or days after you, and then through that person's estate to their heirs, perhaps with two rounds of costs. It also settles which person died first in an accident. [Lapsed gifts and simultaneous death](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death) explains the fallback rules if the will says nothing.

## What does the end of a will contain?

The ending carries the signature and the proof. It usually has three pieces.

- **Executor powers and general provisions.** These give the executor authority to sell property, hire an accountant or lawyer, and divide property in kind. A will with no powers clause forces the executor to ask the court for permission more often.
- **Testimonium and signature.** Your signature, the date and the place. Sign at the end, after the last line of text, not at the top.
- **Attestation and self-proving affidavit.** Two witnesses sign beneath a paragraph saying they saw you sign and that you appeared to be of sound mind. A [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained), notarized, lets the court accept the will without calling the witnesses back. Illinois and New Jersey both set out witness rules, which you can read in our [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) and [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) guides.

## What should you leave out?

A will is a public court document in most states once filed, and it takes effect only after your death. Keep out anything that is private or that comes too late to be useful.

- **Funeral wishes.** Families often see the will after the service. Put them in a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) and tell people.
- **Passwords and account numbers.** If the will is filed, so are they.
- **Gifts of assets that pass elsewhere.** A life insurance policy with a named beneficiary, a retirement account and a jointly owned house all ignore the will. [Joint ownership vs. a beneficiary designation](/learn/beneficiary-designations/joint-ownership-vs-beneficiary-designation) explains which wins.
- **Conditions that cannot be met or enforced.** A gift conditioned on someone marrying a particular person, or never marrying, is the sort of term a court may strike.
- **Instructions about your children's upbringing beyond the guardian.** Hopes can go in a letter. A court will not enforce them.

## Is a simple will enough?

For many people, yes. A simple will is a short will that gives property outright and names an executor, a backup and, if needed, a guardian. It works when you own a house and some savings, your family is not complicated, and the heirs are adults who can manage money.

It is less suited to a few situations: children who are minors and need a trust, a blended family, a child with a disability, a business, property in more than one state or an estate near a tax threshold. In those cases the extra clauses, often a trust inside the will, do real work.
