Many people think of a will as the master document that decides everything. It is not. A will is powerful in one narrow territory, and large amounts of money often sit outside it. Families discover this at the worst moment, when the will says "split everything equally" and a bank pays the entire account to one child.

This article draws the map. You will see which assets a will controls, which it does not, what it cannot do for you while you are alive, and how to make the pieces agree.

## What does a will actually control?

A will controls your probate estate. That is property in your name alone, with no beneficiary or survivorship feature, at the moment of death. Typical examples are a house titled only to you, a car, a checking or savings account with no payable-on-death designation, personal belongings and a business interest held individually.

The will's instructions only become effective when a court admits the will to probate and appoints your executor. For more on how this works, see [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) and [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate).

## Which assets pass outside your will?

These pass directly to a named person or owner, and the will has no say.

| Asset or arrangement | Who gets it | What controls it |
|---|---|---|
| 401(k), 403(b), IRA | Named beneficiary | Beneficiary form on file |
| Life insurance and annuities | Named beneficiary | Beneficiary form on file |
| Payable-on-death (POD) bank account | Named person | POD designation |
| Transfer-on-death (TOD) brokerage or deed | Named person | TOD registration or recorded deed |
| Jointly owned property with survivorship | Surviving owner | How title is held |
| Tenancy by the entirety | Surviving spouse | Marital title in states that allow it |
| Assets in a living trust | Trust beneficiaries | Trust terms |
| Property given away during life | Recipient | The gift itself |

### Beneficiary designations

Retirement accounts and life insurance are often the largest assets a person owns. Each has its own form, and the person named on that form gets the money. The will is not consulted. If you named your spouse in 1998 and later divorced, the form may still say that, and in some states the old designation survives the divorce. Read [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) to see how to keep these forms current, and also consider naming contingent beneficiaries.

### Joint ownership

If you own your home with someone as [joint tenants with right of survivorship](/glossary/joint-tenancy), or as tenants by the entirety, the surviving owner takes the property automatically. A will cannot leave your share to someone else. Parents who add an adult child to the deed for convenience often create unintended results, as covered in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

### Trust assets

Anything properly titled to a [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) follows the trust, not the will. That is the whole idea. A pour-over will sends leftovers to the trust, as explained in [pour-over wills](/learn/wills/pour-over-will).

### Example: the will that did not work

Angela, 63, had a will leaving everything equally to her three children. Her largest asset, a $400,000 IRA, still named her late husband as primary beneficiary and had no backup. With no living named beneficiary, the plan's default rules sent the account to her estate, and the account's tax treatment became less favorable. Her second-largest asset, a $250,000 brokerage account, was jointly titled with her son Mark. He received it outright. The will governed her house and a small savings account. Her other two children got a fraction of what Angela intended, and nothing in the will could fix that.

## What can't a will do while you're alive?

A will does nothing until you die. It cannot:

- **Manage your money during incapacity.** If you have a stroke or develop dementia, your will is irrelevant. You need a [durable power of attorney](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney) so someone can pay bills and manage accounts. Without it, your family may need to ask a court to appoint a conservator. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
- **Make medical decisions.** Healthcare wishes belong in an advance directive and a healthcare agent appointment, covered in the [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives).
- **Give you control over your care.** Nursing home planning, Medicaid eligibility and asset protection require separate planning.

## What else can a will not do?

### Avoid probate

A will is the reason probate exists. If you have assets in your name alone, a will makes sure they follow your instructions, but they still go through the court process. Avoiding probate requires other tools, listed in [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

### Keep your affairs private

A will becomes a public record after filing. Anyone can read it at the court, and the inventory of assets may also be public in some states.

### Disinherit a spouse in most states

Most states protect a surviving spouse with an [elective share](/glossary/elective-share), letting the spouse claim a percentage of the estate, or with community property rules. A will alone cannot fully remove that right. A valid prenuptial or postnuptial agreement is how couples adjust it.

### Impose illegal or unreasonable conditions

A will can set conditions on a gift, such as "when she turns 25" or "if he finishes college," but courts will not enforce conditions that break the law or public policy, such as discouraging marriage altogether or requiring unlawful acts. Vague conditions invite litigation too.

### Provide ongoing management for a vulnerable heir

A will can pay a gift outright, but it cannot manage that gift over time unless it creates a trust. A child with special needs who receives an inheritance directly may lose public benefits. That calls for a [special needs trust](/glossary/special-needs-trust) or a testamentary trust, not a simple bequest.

### Reduce estate or income taxes by itself

A will leaves property, but on its own it does not lower taxes. Federal estate tax applies only above the exemption, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. Tax planning uses tools like trusts, gifting and beneficiary choices. For basics, see the [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

### Control a beneficiary form or a joint account

Repeating the point because it causes the most harm: a will cannot override the beneficiary form. It is a mistake to assume that updating one document updates all of them.

## How do you make everything line up?

Use a short review.

1. **List every asset** and how each is titled: yours alone, joint, with a beneficiary, or in a trust.
2. **Pull the beneficiary forms** for every retirement account, life insurance policy and annuity. Confirm the primary and contingent names.
3. **Check bank and brokerage titling** for POD or TOD designations.
4. **Look at the deed** to your home and any other real estate.
5. **Compare the results with your will.** If your will says equal shares and the accounts say otherwise, decide which you actually want.
6. **Adjust the weakest link.** Sometimes it is easier to change the account forms than to rewrite the will.
7. **Add incapacity documents,** a durable power of attorney and healthcare directive, so a plan exists while you are alive.
8. **Review after major events.** Marriage, divorce, births, deaths and moves all create mismatches.

Intentional differences are fine, as when a parent leaves a retirement account to one child and the house to another to balance the value. The issue is accidental mismatch.
