You can write a flawless will and have it fail for a simple reason: nobody can find it. The family searches for days, hires a locksmith, or learns after the funeral that the original was thrown out during a house cleanout. The question of where to put the will is as important as what it says.

This article compares the common storage options, explains why originals matter, and gives a short plan so your executor can find the document when it counts.

## Why does the original will matter?

Courts want the original signed document. The witness signatures and the self-proving affidavit are on the original, and the court wants assurance that it is the last will you signed. A photocopy might not be accepted without additional proof.

If the original cannot be found, many states presume that you destroyed it intentionally to revoke it, especially if it was last in your possession. The family then has to overcome that presumption, with testimony or evidence that the will was lost by accident. That means more cost and more room for a [challenge](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will). Some states have more flexible rules for proving a lost will, but the extra hearing is still a burden you can avoid.

The same principle applies to a [codicil](/glossary/codicil) and to any trust documents. Keep the originals together, clearly labeled.

## What are your storage options?

| Option | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| Fireproof home safe | Immediate access, no fee, private | Executor needs the key or code, fire ratings vary, burglary risk |
| Attorney's vault | Professional storage, known to the firm, often free | Must remember the firm, firm may close or move |
| Court deposit or registry | Some states let you file your will with the probate court during your life | Not available everywhere, fee, you must know the process |
| Bank safe deposit box | Fire and theft protection | Access can be delayed after death, you may need court permission |
| Desk drawer or file cabinet | Easy | Unsafe from fire, theft and cleanouts, easy to lose |
| Online storage or scanned copy | Handy for copies | Not a substitute for the original, may not satisfy the court |

### Fireproof home safe

A home safe is the most convenient choice. Choose one rated for both fire and water, bolt it down, and put the key or combination where your executor can reach it, such as in a sealed envelope with your attorney or in a password manager you have shared. A safe does no good if nobody can open it, and a locksmith can be an unpleasant surprise.

### Your attorney's office

Many estate planning attorneys keep original wills in a vault. It is secure, and the family has a natural first call. If you use this approach, tell your executor the firm's name and keep a copy of the receipt. Ask what the firm does if it closes, and confirm in writing how to retrieve the original. Lawyers also have rules about releasing originals, so understand the process before you need it.

### Court filing

Some states let you deposit a will with the probate court for safekeeping during your life, sometimes for a modest fee. The will stays sealed until your death. It is secure and officially known, but it can be less convenient if you want to revise it. Check whether your state or county offers this service.

### Safe deposit boxes

A box protects against fire and theft, but there is a catch. When the owner dies, a bank may freeze access until someone shows proper authority. Many states have a procedure that lets a named person or the family open the box under supervision to look for a will, or limits the first look to retrieving it. The process differs by state and bank. If you use a box, add your executor or a trusted person as an authorized signer, and make sure they know the box exists. A box that nobody knows about, or that is titled only to you, can add delay.

## What about digital copies?

A scan or photo is helpful as a backup, not as a replacement. Keep a copy in a secure folder and list its location where your executor can find it. If passwords protect your files, see [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) for how to share them safely. Be careful with email attachments sent to multiple family members, which can spark arguments over the contents before you are ready.

Some states now allow electronic wills with specific requirements. Unless your will was signed that way, treat paper as the original.

## Who needs to know where it is?

At minimum, your executor, and ideally a backup person too. You do not have to share what the will says. Telling someone where it is kept does not reveal the contents.

Consider this simple approach. Tell your executor the location and how to access it. Keep a one-page "where things are" sheet with the location of the will, safe or box key, name of the attorney, insurance policies, accounts and passwords. Review the sheet once a year. The [estate planning documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) lists the other papers to include.

## What should you do when you move or change plans?

When you update or replace a will, retrieve the old original and destroy it only after the new one is signed and stored, and note the date of the change. Leaving old originals around can cause confusion about which is current. If you moved to another state, ask an attorney whether your will still fits.

Wills held by a law firm should be updated in the firm's file, and the old version marked as superseded. If you use a safe deposit box, update the list of authorized signers when people change.

## What happens if the original is lost?

Do not panic. Options exist, though they vary by state.

1. **Search thoroughly.** Check the home, safe, files, the attorney who drafted it, the bank, and the county probate court.
2. **Ask the drafting attorney** for a conformed copy or file copy.
3. **Offer a copy to the court** along with evidence that the original was lost, not revoked. This might include witness testimony or a statement from the attorney.
4. **Expect heirs to be notified,** because a lost-will petition lets anyone who would inherit without the will object.
5. **If the court denies it,** the estate passes under intestate rules, covered in [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

If you are the family member searching, the [after-death checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) walks through what to do in the first weeks, including how to look for estate documents.

## A short plan for storing your will

1. Sign the will with proper formalities. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).
2. Choose one primary storage place from the table above.
3. Tell your executor where it is and how to open it. See [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
4. Keep a copy at home in a different place, labeled "copy."
5. Write the location on your "where things are" sheet.
6. Review every year, and after major life changes.
