For most people the house is the biggest thing they own and the thing their family cares about most. So the question comes up early at an estate planning meeting: can I just say in my will who gets the house?

You can. But "just say it" skips a few real problems: the mortgage, how the house is titled, a spouse's rights, and what happens if the plan goes stale. This page walks through each one with examples, so the sentence in your will does what you meant.

## The short version

- Yes, you can leave a house to anyone by will, and you can leave it in shares.
- Your will controls only what you own in your own name. Joint tenancy, a trust or a transfer-on-death deed override it.
- A mortgage passes with the house. A will can also name where the money to pay it should come from.
- The house normally goes through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) before the heir can sell or retitle it.
- Spouses, and in some places children, have legal protections you cannot simply write around.

## How do you leave a house in a will?

A gift of specific property, such as a house, is called a [bequest](/glossary/bequest). A specific bequest names the property and the person: "I leave my home at 14 Alder Lane to my daughter, Priya." You can identify the house by street address or by a phrase like "my primary residence at the time of my death," which survives a move.

A good gift of a house usually answers these questions:

1. **Who gets it, and who gets it if they die first?** Name a backup person or say the gift goes to their children, otherwise it may fall back into the rest of your estate.
2. **Does the house come with money?** A house is expensive to keep. Property tax, insurance and repairs start the day you die. Some people leave a small cash gift to the heir for upkeep.
3. **Who pays off the mortgage, if there is one?** The will can say it is paid from other estate money, or the heir takes it as is.
4. **What if the house has been sold?** Say whether the heir gets the proceeds or nothing.
5. **Who is the executor?** That person handles the house until the court lets them transfer title. See [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).

## What about the mortgage?

The mortgage stays on the house. A will cannot erase a loan, and the lender keeps its lien. If your daughter inherits a $140,000 loan along with the house, she can keep paying it, refinance it or sell the house and settle it from the sale.

Many people worry the bank will demand the whole balance at once. Federal law restricts that in common cases, such as when the home passes to a relative after the owner's death, so a lender cannot usually call the loan due simply because of the transfer. The heir still has to make the payments or face foreclosure. Ask the loan servicer about its process early; the executor often needs the death certificate and court papers before the lender will talk.

If there is not enough cash in the estate for the payments in the months it takes to open probate, someone will need to cover them. A family that has not discussed this tends to find out at the worst moment.

### Example: Walter's house and a $90,000 loan

Walter, 77, left his house to his son Ben and his savings, about $90,000, to his daughter Carla. The house had a loan of almost the same size. Walter assumed the two gifts were equal. They were not, because Ben also had to pay taxes and insurance and keep the loan current, while Carla's inheritance came without a bill. A clause saying Walter's savings would pay off the mortgage would have made his equal-shares plan real. A better plan might have been a shared formula that treats the house at its net value.

## When the will does not control the house

A will only passes property that is yours alone at death. Four setups send the house elsewhere.

| How the house is held | What happens at your death | Does the will control it? |
|---|---|---|
| Your name alone | Probate, then the heir | Yes. |
| Joint tenancy or tenancy by the entirety | Surviving owner takes it | No. |
| Transfer-on-death deed | Named person takes it | No. |
| Held in a living trust | Trustee follows the trust | No. |
| Tenants in common | Your share goes by will | Only your share. |

If your will says "my house to Ben" and the deed lists you and your second spouse as joint tenants, Ben receives nothing. Your spouse owns the house the moment you die. People who remarry often make this mistake, and it is covered in [the family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage). A [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) can move a house without court, but it can conflict with the will, so read the deed before drafting.

## Do a spouse's rights limit a gift of the house?

Often, yes. The rules depend on the state, so this is a place to check before signing.

- In many states a surviving spouse can claim an **elective share**, a set portion of the estate, instead of what the will gives. Our guide on [the spouse's elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) explains how it works.
- Some states also give a spouse a **homestead right** to stay in the home, or a right to live there for life or a period of time.
- In community property states, half of what was earned during the marriage already belongs to the spouse.

None of this stops you from leaving the house to a child. It means the plan has to account for the spouse, usually by giving the spouse a right to live in the home or by balancing the gift with other assets.

## What happens after you die?

Here is the usual path for a house passing under a will.

1. The executor files the will and is appointed by the court.
2. The executor secures the property, keeps insurance and utilities active, and keeps the loan current if cash is available.
3. Creditors have a period to make claims, which the executor handles.
4. When the court permits, the executor signs a deed transferring the house to the heir.
5. The heir records the deed and updates insurance, taxes and the mortgage.

An heir generally cannot sell until step 4, with a few exceptions covered in [can heirs sell the house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate). The house usually gets a stepped-up tax basis at death, which can cut the capital gains tax if it is sold soon after. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## Leaving a house to more than one person

Giving a house to three children equally sounds fair. It often is not. One child lives close and wants to keep it, one wants cash, and one cannot pay their share of the tax bill. Without instructions, co-owners must agree on every decision, and when they cannot, any of them can ask a court to order a sale.

Better options include:

- Naming one child to receive the house and giving the others equal value in cash or life insurance.
- Leaving the house to a trust that sets rules on use, costs and buyouts.
- Giving the executor power to sell and divide the money.

If a family drifts toward arguing, [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) shows how it plays out. For unequal shares between children, see [how to leave unequal shares without a fight](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).
