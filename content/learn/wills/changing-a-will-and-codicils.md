The [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers the whole subject. Your life moves, and a will signed years ago does not. The executor you picked has moved abroad. A grandchild has been born. A brother you once named is no longer speaking to you. Now you want to change a sentence or two, and you are wondering if you can grab a pen.

Please don't. This page walks through the right ways to change a will, how to choose between them, and what to do about the changes that happen on their own.

## Can you change a will after you sign it?

Yes. A will does nothing until you die, so you can revise or replace it as often as you like, as long as you have the mental capacity to make a will at the time. What the law cares about is that every change follows the same formalities as the original. A will is a formal document, and its changes must be too.

There are four recognized ways to change or cancel one:

1. Sign a **codicil**.
2. Sign a **new will** that revokes the old one.
3. **Physically destroy** the will, intending to revoke it.
4. Let the **law change it for you**, as happens with divorce in most states.

## What is a codicil and when should you use one?

A [codicil](/glossary/codicil) is a short supplement that amends a will. It names the will it changes by date, says what is being changed, and confirms that the rest stays in force. It is signed by you before two witnesses, usually with a notarized affidavit, in the same way as the will.

A codicil fits a narrow, simple change:

- Replacing the [executor](/glossary/executor) or adding a backup.
- Changing a specific [bequest](/glossary/bequest), such as who gets a piece of jewelry or a sum of money.
- Updating a guardian for minor children.
- Fixing a name or address.

### When a codicil is a bad idea

Use a new will if any of these is true:

- You already have one or more codicils. A stack of them makes it hard to tell what controls, and they can contradict one another.
- The change touches the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate), meaning everything not specifically given away, since that is the heart of the plan.
- You are changing several things.
- You have moved to another state, since state rules on signing and on certain gifts vary.
- The original is old, and you would like to modernize the whole document.

An attorney can often prepare a full new will for little more than a codicil would cost. For prices, see [how much a will costs](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost).

## How do you sign a codicil or a new will?

Follow the same steps as the first time.

1. **Draft the document.** A codicil names the original will by date, states each change in plain words, and says "in all other respects I confirm my will." A new will opens by revoking all earlier wills and codicils.
2. **Gather two witnesses** who are not beneficiaries under the will or the change. Do not reuse a witness who benefits.
3. **Declare it** to the witnesses and sign in front of them.
4. **Witnesses sign** in your presence.
5. **Add a self-proving affidavit** before a notary. See [self-proving affidavits](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained).
6. **Store the original new document** with the original will, or in place of it. Do not destroy the old will until the new one is signed and safe. A gap is worse than a stale will.
7. **Tell your executor** where the originals are. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

Remember that the formalities are state law. [How to make a legally valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) explains the signing in detail.

## What happens if you write changes on the will itself?

Usually nothing you intended. A handwritten note, cross-out, or margin edit made after signing is generally ineffective unless it is signed and witnessed in the way a will must be. It can also backfire. If the markings suggest you intended to cancel something, a court may find that part revoked, while the replacement language is ignored. That leaves a gap. And a stapled note or a sticky label is just evidence that someone, possibly not you, tampered with the document.

Marta, 66, crossed out her brother's name as executor and wrote her daughter's name above it, initialed it, and put the will back in the drawer. After her death, the court found the edit unwitnessed. It treated the crossed-out name as revoked, but could not make the daughter executor under the will. A judge appointed a person under state priority rules, which was a third child Marta had not wanted. A thirty-minute codicil would have settled it.

## How do you revoke a will completely?

You can revoke a will by signing a new one that says so, by signing a separate written revocation with the formalities of a will, or by destroying it on purpose. Burning, tearing, cutting or shredding the document, while intending to revoke it, is effective in most states. You can also direct another person to do it in your presence.

That also means accidents can matter. A will torn by mistake is not meant to be revoked, but proving that after you are gone is another matter. If an original is damaged or missing, see [what to do if the original will is lost](/learn/wills/lost-original-will).

Revoking all your wills without a replacement leaves you [without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will), so intestacy rules take over. Make the new one first.

## What changes happen without your signing anything?

Some life events change how a will works whether you act or not. The rules vary by state, so treat the list as a guide.

| Event | What typically happens | What you should do |
|---|---|---|
| Divorce | Gifts to the ex-spouse are canceled and the ex is treated as having died first | Rewrite the will, update beneficiary forms; see [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) |
| Marriage | A will made earlier may not be revoked, but a new spouse may have a claim | Make a new will after marrying |
| Birth or adoption of a child | In many states, a child born after the will may get a share if the will did not anticipate it | Update gifts and the guardian |
| Death of a beneficiary | The gift may lapse or pass under state anti-lapse rules | Name backups and update |
| Move to another state | A will valid where signed is usually still honored | Have it reviewed under the new state's law |

Marriage and divorce rules differ most. Do not assume. Ask.

## Can a will be changed after someone dies?

No. After death, the will is fixed. The executor must follow it, and a court will not rewrite it to be fairer. A few things can alter the outcome though:

- A court may invalidate all or part of it in a [will contest](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).
- A beneficiary can [disclaim](/glossary/disclaimer) a gift, letting it pass as if they had died first.
- A surviving spouse may take an elective share instead of what the will gives.
- Beneficiaries sometimes sign a settlement agreement, approved by the court, that redistributes property.

None of these is the same as changing the will. If you want something done, do it while you can.

## How often should you review your will?

Look at it after any major event and at least every few years. The usual triggers are a marriage, divorce, birth, death, a move, a large change in assets, or a falling-out with someone named in the document. The checklist in [when to review and update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) helps. If you never updated, [what happens if you die with an outdated will](/learn/what-if/outdated-will) shows what can go wrong.
