You may be thinking about a quick will so you are covered. A website promises a will in twenty minutes for a small fee, or you could simply write one out by hand tonight. For some people that is a sensible first step. For others it creates a document that looks complete and fails when it counts.

This guide explains how handwritten, online and fill-in-the-blank wills work, what courts do with them, the specific mistakes that sink them, and how to tell which category you are in.

## What is a handwritten (holographic) will?

A [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will) is a will written by the testator, in their own handwriting, and signed by them. About half of states recognize them in some form. Rules differ in three ways:

- **How much must be handwritten.** Some states require the entire will in your hand. Others accept a will where the "material provisions," meaning the parts that actually give property away, are handwritten, even if the rest is a printed form.
- **Witnesses.** Many states that allow holographic wills do not require witnesses. States that do not recognize them at all insist on the standard two witnesses, so a handwritten will signed alone is invalid there.
- **Proof.** The court has to be satisfied the handwriting and signature are yours. Family members or handwriting experts may be called, which adds time and cost.

Even where valid, a handwritten will invites problems. People write when they are stressed, ill or in a hurry, and tend to leave out the pieces a lawyer would add: a backup executor, a residuary clause, a guardian for children, and a direction for what happens if a beneficiary dies first.

### A cautionary example

Walter, 79, wrote a note on a legal pad saying, "My house goes to my son Greg, and the rest is for my daughters." He signed it, but did not date it, and never said who would handle the estate. After Walter died, his daughters argued that "the rest" meant half of the house's value, and the court had to decide whether the note was a valid will at all, what "the rest" meant, and who should be appointed to run the estate. The legal fees came out of the estate that Walter was trying to leave behind.

## Do online wills work?

Online will services let you answer questions and print a will. The documents are real, and many people use them for straightforward situations. The service gives you the form. It cannot supervise your signing, tell you what your state requires for witnesses, or check whether your will clashes with the rest of your finances.

A will from an online service is legal when:

1. It meets your state's requirements for content and signing.
2. You follow the signing instructions exactly, with the right number of qualified witnesses.
3. The terms match your family reality, such as names, relationships and property.
4. It is not undone by something outside the will, like beneficiary forms or joint ownership.

Some states have passed laws allowing electronic wills and remote witnessing through approved platforms. These laws are specific and technical, and a will that fails them can be rejected. If you plan to sign electronically, confirm that your state has such a law and that the provider follows it. Otherwise print and sign on paper in front of witnesses, as described in [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

## How do DIY wills fail?

Most failures fall into a few repeat patterns.

1. **Signing mistakes.** The witnesses are missing, are beneficiaries, signed later, or did not see you sign. In a close call, courts may accept the document under a "harmless error" or substantial compliance rule, but only after a hearing.
2. **No self-proving affidavit.** Without it, the court may need a witness to testify, and witnesses can be hard to find years later.
3. **Vague or conflicting gifts.** "My jewelry to my kids" does not say how to split it. "My car to Anna" does not say what happens if you sell the car or Anna dies first.
4. **No backup plans.** A will that leaves everything to one person fails partly or entirely if that person dies first.
5. **Beneficiary conflicts.** A will says "everything equally to my three children" while a life insurance policy still names a former spouse. Your will does not override these designations. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).
6. **Wrong tools for the situation.** A will cannot protect an inheritance for a child with disabilities, shield assets in a second marriage, or manage a business without extra planning.
7. **Out-of-date or unfindable documents.** A will in a drawer that nobody knows about is as unhelpful as no will. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
8. **Overlooking taxes and debts.** A DIY will rarely says which assets pay debts, or what happens if one gift is large enough to trigger problems.

## When is a DIY will a reasonable choice?

For some people, an online or fill-in will is a fair stopgap. It tends to work when all of these are true:

- You are single or married with no children, or your children are adults.
- Your assets are modest and mostly pass by beneficiary designation.
- You have a simple plan, such as everything to your spouse and then to your children equally.
- You own property in one state only.
- You do not expect a family dispute.

Priya, 26, rents an apartment, has a retirement account with a named beneficiary and some savings. An online will that names her sister as executor and beneficiary is a sensible step. The cost is small, and it keeps her state's default rules from deciding who gets her belongings and who handles her affairs.

## When should you skip DIY and see an attorney?

Move beyond a template if any of these apply:

- You own a home or other real estate, especially in more than one state.
- You own a business or professional practice.
- You have minor children, or a child with special needs.
- You have a blended family, or you want to treat children differently.
- You want to disinherit someone, which raises [contest risks](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).
- Your estate may be large enough for federal or state estate tax. The federal exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, but some states tax much smaller estates.
- You are considering a trust to avoid probate. That is a different tool, covered in the [trusts guide](/learn/trusts).
- You are ill, or developing memory loss. Capacity evidence is easier to collect with an attorney involved.

A middle path is to draft with an online tool and pay a lawyer for a flat-fee review. You can compare typical prices in [how much an estate plan costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost).

## What should you do if you already have a DIY will?

Here is a short audit to run through.

1. Read the signing page. Do you see your signature, two witness signatures and, ideally, a notarized self-proving affidavit?
2. Check names, spellings and any gifts to people who have died or whom you no longer want to name.
3. Compare the will with your beneficiary forms on retirement accounts, life insurance and bank accounts.
4. Confirm there is an executor and a backup.
5. If you have children under 18, check that a guardian is named.
6. Note the date. If it is more than five years old, or if you have married, divorced, moved or had a child since, it needs a refresh.
7. Make sure someone knows where the original is.

If the will fails any of these, a new one is usually simpler than a codicil. Other mistakes people make are listed in [common estate planning mistakes](/learn/basics/common-estate-planning-mistakes).
