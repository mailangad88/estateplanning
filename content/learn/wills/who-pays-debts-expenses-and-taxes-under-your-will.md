Priya, 70, writes a will that leaves her condo to her son Anand and her savings to her daughter Leela. She has a $140,000 mortgage on the condo, a $6,000 credit card balance and a funeral plan she has not yet paid for. She assumes her children will each get what she named. Nobody has asked who covers the bills.

That question matters because the answer is mechanical. Unless the will says something different, the estate's debts come out of one pocket, and often it is the pocket Priya meant for Leela. This page explains the default order, what clauses change it, and how to write instructions that do what you intend. It sits within our [wills guide](/learn/wills). For the creditor side of the process, see [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

## The short version

- The estate pays its debts and costs first. Heirs get what is left.
- Heirs are not personally responsible for a deceased person's debts, unless they co-signed or owned the debt jointly.
- With no instruction, the residuary gift usually pays first, so one heir can carry the cost for everyone.
- A mortgage follows the house, but your will can shift it to other estate money.
- Tax can be charged to the estate or to the heirs, and a clause is how you choose.

## What does the estate pay before anyone inherits?

An executor works through bills in a rough order. State law sets the details, but the categories are familiar.

| Priority | What it covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Costs of administration | Court fees, executor commissions where allowed, attorney and accountant fees |
| 2 | Funeral and burial | Often capped or limited to a reasonable amount in some states |
| 3 | Taxes | Income tax owed by the person, and any estate tax |
| 4 | Secured and priority debts | Mortgage and car loan are paid from their collateral. Medical bills in the final illness are often treated with priority |
| 5 | General unsecured debts | Credit cards, personal loans, unpaid bills |

Only after those do heirs receive their shares. The executor must also give creditors a chance to file claims. In many states that window runs a few months, and handing out gifts before it closes can leave the executor personally exposed. See [the probate creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) for how that works.

An estate with more debts than assets is called insolvent. Creditors are paid in priority order until the money runs out, and the rest generally goes unpaid. Heirs do not make up the difference.

## Which gift pays when money runs short?

If the estate cannot cover the bills and every gift, the law cuts gifts back in a fixed order unless the will says otherwise. That process is called abatement. Residuary gifts shrink first, then general gifts of money, then specific gifts of named items. Our page on [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) explains the categories.

Back to Priya. Say her estate on paper is the condo ($320,000), savings ($90,000), and the car ($10,000). The will gives the condo to Anand, savings to Leela and the car to a cousin. The debts and costs are the credit card, the funeral, legal fees and her final tax return, together perhaps $30,000. They come from the savings, which is the residue or general money. Leela gets $60,000. Anand gets a condo with a $140,000 mortgage still attached. Each child gets what the will names, but not the amounts Priya probably had in mind.

That example shows how things look equal on paper and aren't in practice. If she had meant for the two to share the burden, the will should say so.

## What happens to a mortgage on a house left in the will?

The mortgage stays with the house. The heir takes title subject to the loan, and can keep paying, refinance or sell. Federal law restricts a lender from calling the whole loan due merely because the house passed to a relative on the owner's death, which gives heirs time to arrange something. Our pages on [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) and [leaving a house in a will](/learn/wills/leaving-a-house-in-a-will) go further.

A will can change the default with an exoneration clause. It says that a named mortgage is to be paid from the rest of the estate, so the heir takes the house free and clear. That is generous and sometimes unfair to the others, so be sure the estate has the cash. If it does not, the clause fails and the loan follows the house anyway.

## What clauses can you write?

Four short clauses cover most needs. Each one is a sentence or two, and an attorney will tailor the wording to your state.

1. **A payment of debts clause.** It authorizes the executor to pay valid debts, funeral costs and administration expenses, and may say from which assets.
2. **A mortgage clause.** It says whether a mortgaged property passes with the debt or free of it, and from what money.
3. **A tax clause.** It says who bears estate and inheritance taxes, and whether life insurance and other assets outside the will share in them.
4. **An expense reserve.** It tells the executor to hold back a stated amount before the residue is divided, so the last heir is not left waiting for a refund of costs.

In a modest estate the practical version is simply: pay debts and costs from the residuary gift, and divide the rest as written. That puts the burden on the residuary takers. If you want it shared, use percentages.

## Who pays estate and inheritance tax?

Federal estate tax applies only to estates above the exclusion, $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so it will not touch most families. State rules are different. Illinois has an estate tax on estates over $4 million, paid by the estate. New Jersey has no estate tax but charges an inheritance tax on certain heirs, and it is generally figured on the share each heir receives, with a tax waiver step for real estate. Our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) shows how states differ, and the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) can tell you whether your state has one.

If your estate is taxable, the question of who bears the tax becomes real. The default may be that the residue pays it all, or that every beneficiary pays a share. State law differs, and the rules often reach assets outside the will, such as life insurance. A tax clause avoids the guesswork.

Consider a childless widower who leaves $500,000 to a nephew by will and $500,000 of life insurance to a friend. If the will says nothing, a state rule might make the nephew pay all of a state tax out of his share. A tax clause might split it between the two in proportion to what each gets. The same estate yields different outcomes depending on one sentence.

## Can the debts reach assets outside the will?

Sometimes. Life insurance, retirement accounts and payable-on-death accounts pass to named people without probate, and creditors generally cannot reach them, because they never become part of the probate estate. Some states, however, allow a creditor or the executor to reach them when the probate estate is too small to pay the bills. The details vary and exceptions apply for certain assets.

For planning, that means three things. A beneficiary form does not erase debts. A large debt can still erode what you meant to leave to the people who inherit under the will. And if you want an heir to receive an account free of the estate's expenses, say how the estate will pay them. Our page on [which controls: will, trust or beneficiary form](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form) shows how the documents interact.

## How should you plan, step by step?

1. **List what you owe.** Mortgage, car loans, cards, medical bills, taxes. Include the funeral.
2. **List who gets what.** Match each gift against the debts that will fall on it.
3. **Test the split.** Subtract likely debts from the residue and see what each person ends up with.
4. **Decide where the burden should sit.** On one person, on all in proportion, or on a reserve.
5. **Write it down.** Ask your attorney for payment, mortgage and tax clauses that fit your state.
6. **Review after big changes.** A new mortgage, an inheritance or a move can shift the arithmetic. See [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral) if you want to settle that item early.
