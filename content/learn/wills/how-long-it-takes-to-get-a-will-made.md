People put off making a will because they imagine a drawn-out ordeal: several meetings, mountains of paper, a bill that grows. The actual process is shorter than most expect. It is also less about legal work than about decisions you have not yet made, and those decisions are where the time goes.

This page walks through what happens from the first phone call to the signed will, how long each part tends to take, and what speeds it up or slows it down. For the rules that make the will valid once it is signed, see the [guide to making a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will). For the cost side, see [how much a will costs](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost). The [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers everything else.

## What are the steps from first call to signed will?

| Step | What happens | Typical elapsed time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. First contact | You book a meeting and answer a short intake form | A few days to wait for a slot |
| 2. Intake meeting | You discuss family, assets, executor, guardian and gifts | 45 to 90 minutes |
| 3. Drafting | The attorney prepares the will and any related documents | A few days to a week |
| 4. Review | You read the draft and ask for changes | Your pace, often 2 to 5 days |
| 5. Signing | You sign before witnesses, with a notary for the affidavit | About 30 minutes |
| 6. Storage | You keep the original safe and tell your executor | Same day |

For a straightforward will, the full run is commonly one to three weeks. The attorney's hands-on time is often just a few hours spread across steps two to five. Everything else is the calendar.

## What happens at the intake meeting?

The attorney asks about your family first, because the family determines most of the will. Expect questions about your spouse or partner, your children and their ages, anyone who depends on you and anyone you want to leave out. Then come assets: the house, the accounts, the retirement plans, life insurance, a business. Last come choices: who will be executor, who will be guardian, what happens if someone dies before you.

Good attorneys also ask about what is not in the will. They want to know who is named on your IRA and your life policy, because a will does not control those. That conversation is where many people find an ex-spouse still listed or a minor child named outright. The [guide on what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) explains why it matters.

If you arrive with answers, the meeting is short. If you arrive saying, "I haven't decided who should raise the kids," the meeting becomes a conversation about that, and a draft has to wait.

## What slows a will down?

Most delays have a human cause.

- **Undecided key roles.** Choosing an executor and backup, and a guardian for children, takes some people weeks. Our [guide to choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) can shorten it.
- **Family complexity.** A second marriage, children from two relationships, a child with a disability or a business partner means more drafting and often a trust inside the will.
- **Missing information.** A list of account types and beneficiaries is enough. You do not need exact balances.
- **Back and forth on the draft.** Each round of changes adds days.
- **Scheduling the signing.** You need two witnesses and often a notary at the same time. A signing slot can be a week out.

## Can a will be made in one day?

Yes, and it is done more often than people think. A patient scheduled for surgery on Friday might call on Wednesday. The attorney meets that day, drafts overnight and holds the signing on Thursday, with staff as witnesses. A shortened process works when three things are true: the person knows their wishes, the family picture is simple, and no one is pressuring them.

The risks of speed are real. A rushed will can leave out a backup executor, miss an asset or skip a formality. If capacity may be questioned, take the extra time to document it, as described in the guide to [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).

## When does a will take longer than a few weeks?

Plan on a month or more when the will is only one piece of a larger plan. A couple with a young child and a house may want a trust for the child's inheritance and a pour-over will behind a living trust. A business owner needs the will to fit with a buy-sell agreement. A parent of a child with a disability needs a special needs trust, drafted carefully so benefits are not lost. In each case the will is quick and the surrounding documents are not. Ask at the first meeting whether your situation is a will-only job, and see the [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) if you are not sure.

## How long does an online will take?

A form-based will can be done in under an hour: you answer questions, the program generates a document, and you print it. What it does not do is fit the document to your family, spot a beneficiary form that conflicts with your plan, or supervise the signing. The printed pages are only a draft until two witnesses watch you sign.

Priya, 41, finished an online will on a Sunday night in forty minutes. She printed it and signed it alone, intending to find witnesses later. Three years on, it was still sitting unwitnessed in a folder. The fast part of the process was done. The part that makes the will valid never happened. The [guide to handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) lists the usual failure points.

## How do you speed things up?

Before the first meeting, write down five things:

1. The full legal names of your spouse or partner and every child.
2. Your first and second choice for executor.
3. Your first and second choice for guardian, if you have minor children.
4. The big assets and who is named as beneficiary on each.
5. Any specific gift you want to make, such as a car, a ring or a sum to a charity.

The [consultation checklist](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) covers the longer version. If you are choosing a firm, the guide on [how to choose an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) suggests what to ask.

## What happens after the signing?

The original goes in a safe place, and your executor knows where. A bank safe deposit box can be a problem if no one else has access, so see [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will). Update your beneficiary forms if the meeting turned up mismatches. Put a reminder on the calendar to review the will after a marriage, divorce, birth, move or large change in assets.
