You have decided that part of what you leave behind should go to a hospital, a food pantry, a school or the church you have attended for thirty years. The decision is the easy part. The sentence in the will is where gifts go wrong, because a clause that sounds clear to you can be unclear to a probate judge.

This page explains how to leave money to charity in your will, which kind of gift to choose, what the clause should say, and why some assets should not go through the will at all. For the whole picture of what a will does, start with our [wills guide](/learn/wills).

## How do you leave money to a charity in a will?

You add a clause, called a bequest, that names the charity and describes the gift. It does not need special language, but it does need to be precise. Four details do most of the work:

1. **The charity's legal name.** Use the name on its IRS determination letter, not the name on its billboard.
2. **The type of gift.** A dollar amount, a percentage, a specific asset or whatever is left over.
3. **Any restriction.** If the money must go to a scholarship fund or a building project, say so, and ask the charity if it will accept the condition.
4. **A fallback.** Say what happens if the charity no longer exists when you die.

The will must be signed and witnessed exactly like any other will. Our article on [making a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) covers those steps. A charity clause added in the margin of an old will, or in a separate note, is not a safe way to make the change. If you only want to add the gift, a short [codicil or an updated will](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) is the correct tool.

## Which kind of gift should you choose?

The three common choices behave very differently, and the table below shows how.

| Type of gift | Example wording | Works well when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed dollar amount | "$25,000 to the Lakeview Food Pantry" | The estate is stable and you want a known number | The estate shrinks and the gift takes too large a share, or grows and the gift looks stingy |
| Percentage of the estate | "Ten percent of my residuary estate" | You want the gift to rise and fall with the estate | You must say whether debts and taxes come off first |
| Specific item | "My collection of first-edition books to the county library" | The item has a natural home | The charity may refuse it, and the item may be sold before you die |
| Residuary share | "Half of everything left after other gifts" | Charity is a main beneficiary | Other gifts must be spelled out so the remainder is clear |

Our article on [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) explains how the order of payment works if the estate turns out smaller than expected.

### A worked example with real-looking numbers

This is an illustration, not a prediction. Dolores, a widow in Naperville, leaves $50,000 to the public library and the rest equally to her two daughters. When she writes the will, her estate is about $600,000. Over ten years she pays for care, sells the house and the estate falls to $180,000. The library still gets $50,000, which is now more than a quarter of the estate, and each daughter receives $65,000. If Dolores had written "ten percent to the library," the library would get $18,000 and each daughter $81,000. Neither result is wrong, but only one of them is what Dolores wanted. She had never decided which, because she never considered the estate could shrink.

## What should the charity clause say?

A good clause is short. For example: "I give ten percent of my residuary estate to Riverside Community Health Foundation, a nonprofit corporation, Employer Identification Number 00-0000000, for its general purposes. If that organization is not in existence or is not a qualified charitable organization when I die, I give this share to the Riverside Medical Center Auxiliary." The number is a placeholder.

A few points practitioners see over and over:

- **Check the exact entity.** Many large charities run their gift programs through a separate foundation. A will that leaves money to "the hospital" may be read against the hospital's operating company, a foundation or a parent system.
- **Ask whether the name has changed.** Mergers are common. A named small charity that merged into a larger one often has its gift go to the successor, but not always.
- **Say "for its general purposes" when you mean it.** An unrestricted gift is the most useful to the charity and the least likely to be refused.
- **Restricted gifts need the charity's agreement.** If the money must fund a named scholarship, call the charity first and ask for its gift acceptance policy.

Our article on what happens when a [charity named in a will no longer exists](/learn/what-if/charity-named-in-will-no-longer-exists) walks through the court process if no backup is named.

## Should retirement accounts go through the will?

Usually not. An IRA, 401(k) or similar account passes by beneficiary form, and the form controls over the will. That is also why those accounts are often the first assets people consider for charity. A child who inherits a traditional IRA generally has to withdraw it within 10 years and pay income tax on each withdrawal. A charity pays no income tax, so the same dollar goes further when the charity receives the IRA, and your children receive other assets that carry a stepped-up basis instead.

A common plan looks like this: name the charity as a beneficiary of a fixed percentage of the IRA, and leave your house and bank accounts to your children through the will. The details are in our article on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries), and the form wording is covered in [beneficiary form wording for trusts and charities](/learn/beneficiary-designations/beneficiary-form-wording-for-trusts-charities-and-overseas-beneficiaries). A [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) shows which of your accounts will override the will.

## Do charitable gifts save estate tax?

Only for the small number of estates large enough to owe it. As of 2026 the federal estate tax exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, so most families never pay. Where an estate is above that, gifts to qualifying charities are deductible and there is no dollar cap on the deduction. Our full article on [charitable gifts and bequests](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-gifts-and-bequests-gift-tax-estate-tax-and-income-tax) covers the tax side in detail.

State taxes are a separate question. Illinois has its own estate tax with a lower threshold than the federal one, and New Jersey taxes some inheritances but exempts charitable organizations from its inheritance tax. If you live in either state, see the [Illinois estate planning guide](/estate-planning/illinois) or the [New Jersey guide](/estate-planning/new-jersey) for what applies to you.

## What do families tend to miss?

Charitable gifts rarely cause family fights, but they do cause surprises. Here are the situations that come up most often.

- **A spouse's rights.** In both Illinois and New Jersey a surviving spouse can claim a share of the estate regardless of the will. A large charitable bequest can be reduced if the spouse takes an elective share. See [the elective share explained](/glossary/elective-share).
- **Loved ones named as co-beneficiaries without knowing.** If a charity receives a percentage of an account along with two children, tell the children. Unexplained delays while a charity processes paperwork are easier to bear when everyone expected them.
- **Gifts of property the charity cannot take.** Land with an environmental problem, a car or a share in a closely held business may be declined. A cash gift is easier.
- **Memorial gifts that become stale.** A bequest "in memory of my husband for the cancer wing" may fail if the wing is renamed. Add language that lets the charity use the money for the same general purpose.

## What to do next

If you are drafting a will now, make a list of each charity with its legal name, address and tax ID, and decide whether each gift is a number or a share. If you already have a will, check each charity named in it, since an old gift can be out of date after a merger.
