You want your will to hold up when it matters most, which is long after you can explain what you meant. A will is only as good as its signing. A flawless document that fails a state formality can be rejected, leaving your family to the default rules for dying without a will.

This guide covers who can make a will, what the document must say, the exact signing ceremony most states require, what a notary does and does not do, and how to change a will later.

## Who can legally make a will?

You can make a will if you are an adult, usually 18 or older, and have what the law calls [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity). That is a lower bar than the ability to manage a business. It generally means you understand that you are making a will, you know roughly what you own, you know who your close family members are, and you can connect those pieces into a plan.

Capacity is judged at the moment of signing. Someone with early dementia may still have a good day and make a valid will, and attorneys often document that moment carefully with notes, a medical letter or a witness statement. If you are making a will for yourself later in life, see [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) for how to plan while capacity is clear. Minors and people under certain limited exceptions, like some emancipated or military minors in a few states, are handled differently.

## What must a valid will contain?

There is no magic wording, but a sound will normally includes these parts.

1. **Identification.** Your full legal name, county and a statement that this is your will, revoking earlier ones.
2. **Executor.** The person you name to carry out the will, plus a backup. See [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
3. **Gifts.** Specific gifts (a ring, a sum of money) and the residuary clause, which says who gets everything else. The [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate) is where most of the money goes, so it deserves the most care.
4. **Guardian.** If you have minor children, your choice of [guardian](/glossary/guardian) and backup.
5. **Trusts for young or vulnerable heirs,** if needed, often called testamentary trusts.
6. **Debts and taxes.** Directions for paying expenses, and which assets pay first.
7. **Signature and witness block.** Where you and the witnesses sign and date.

A will that names a beneficiary by relationship only, such as "my children," should account for what happens if a child dies first. Naming backups is one of the cheapest ways to avoid litigation.

## How do you sign a will correctly?

The ceremony matters because the court will ask whether each formality was met. This is the standard path in most states. Confirm local details before you start.

1. **Print the final will.** Check that every page is present, numbered and that nothing is added in ink afterward.
2. **Gather two witnesses,** adults who are not named in the will and who are comfortable testifying later if asked.
3. **Declare it.** Tell the witnesses, "This is my will," in their presence. Some states require this "publication," others do not, and it is harmless.
4. **Sign.** Sign your full usual signature at the end of the will, with the witnesses watching you do it.
5. **Witnesses sign.** Each witness signs in your presence and in each other's presence, and prints their name and address. Some states do not require them to see each other sign, but doing it all in one room is safest.
6. **Complete the self-proving affidavit** before a notary, if your state allows one. Everyone signs again on the affidavit, and the notary notarizes it. It usually costs little or nothing at a bank or law office.
7. **Store the original carefully.** See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

### Example: how a sloppy signing causes trouble

Tom, 71, typed a will, signed it alone at his kitchen table, and had his neighbor sign later when she stopped by. After Tom died, a court reviewed the will and found the witness had not seen Tom sign and he had not acknowledged his signature to her. Depending on the state, that could have been fatal. Because the court could not call on a self-proving affidavit, his neighbor had to testify, and the family spent months on a question that a 15-minute meeting would have avoided.

## What does a notary do for a will?

A notary confirms identity and administers an oath, but in most states notarization does not itself make a will valid. What counts are the witnesses. The notary comes in because of the self-proving affidavit, a short statement signed by you and your witnesses, under oath, that the will was signed properly.

Without the affidavit, the court may require a witness to appear or file a sworn statement before it accepts the will. Witnesses move, forget, or die. With it, the court accepts the will with less proof, which can shorten [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate). A few states do not recognize self-proving affidavits in all situations, and a handful treat notarization differently, so use a lawyer's form where in doubt.

## Who should serve as a witness?

Choose two adults who are not beneficiaries and who are unlikely to move away or be unavailable. A paralegal, a coworker or a neighbor works well. Avoid your spouse, your children, your executor and anyone named in the will.

Many states void, or reduce, a gift given to a person who also witnessed the will, unless there are enough other disinterested witnesses. Even where the gift survives, a challenger can use the overlap to suggest pressure. A neutral witness protects your wishes. For the broader picture of challenges, read [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## What are the types of valid wills?

Most people use a **witnessed attested will**, the type described above. There are exceptions that change the steps.

- **Handwritten (holographic) wills.** About half of states accept a will written and signed entirely in the testator's hand, sometimes without witnesses. The rules are narrow, and mistakes are common. See [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills).
- **Oral or nuncupative wills.** A few states permit them in extremely narrow circumstances, such as a deathbed statement with limited property, and the results are unreliable.
- **Electronic wills.** A growing number of states have adopted laws allowing electronic signatures and remote witnessing. Requirements vary and are strict.
- **Wills made in another state or country.** Many states honor a will that was valid where it was signed. Even so, if you move, have an attorney review it.

## How do you change or revoke a will?

You can change a will at any time while you have capacity. The two safe ways are:

- **A new will** that states it revokes all earlier wills. This is cleanest for large changes.
- **A [codicil](/glossary/codicil),** which is a short amendment signed with the same formalities as a will. It suits a single change, like a new executor, but multiple codicils can create confusion.

Do not cross out words, write in the margin or staple notes to a signed will. Courts may ignore the markings or, worse, treat the whole document as revoked. Tearing up or burning the will with the intent to revoke it also revokes it, so keep the original safe from accidents.

Life events also change a will's effect even if you do nothing. In most states, divorce removes a former spouse as a beneficiary and as executor. Marriage or the birth of a child may entitle that person to a share if the will predates them. A review every few years, and after any big event, keeps the plan current. ## What are the most common mistakes?

- Not having enough witnesses, or using beneficiaries as witnesses.
- Signing before the witnesses arrive.
- Adding handwritten changes after signing.
- Leaving out a backup executor or backup beneficiary.
- Assuming the will controls accounts that already have a named beneficiary. It does not, as explained in [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).
- Using a form from another state without checking the rules.
- Losing the original, which can lead the court to presume it was revoked.
