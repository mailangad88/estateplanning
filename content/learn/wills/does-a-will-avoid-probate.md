Many people sign a will believing the job is done and their family will never see a courtroom. That belief is one of the most common surprises we hear from executors. The will is signed, the funeral is over, and then a letter arrives from the probate court.

This page explains what a will does and does not do about probate, when it actually starts working, and which tools really keep property out of court.

## The short version

- A will goes *through* probate. It does not go around it.
- Property that passes by beneficiary form, joint title or trust ownership generally skips probate. The will does not control those assets at all.
- The will "takes effect" at death, but nobody can act on it until a court says so.
- Smaller estates may qualify for a faster process, but that depends on value and state law, not on whether you have a will.

## Does a will avoid probate?

A will is the opposite of a probate-avoidance tool, which is why our [wills guide](/learn/wills) treats the two as separate topics. Probate is the court process that proves a will is valid, appoints the [executor](/glossary/executor), pays debts and transfers what is left. Without a will, probate still happens; the court just uses state law to decide who gets what. With a will, probate happens and the court follows your instructions instead.

Think of it this way. A will answers the question *who gets it.* Probate answers the question *who has the legal power to hand it over.* A signed will does the first job and none of the second.

Does that mean every family with a will spends a year in court? No. Plenty of estates are settled in a few months. The point is that the will brings the probate estate into court, and the [full probate process](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) follows. How long and how costly depends on the state, the size of the estate and whether anyone objects. Our guide to [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) goes through what slows it down.

## When does a will go into effect?

A will takes effect only at death. Before that, you can change it, cancel it, sell everything it mentions, and nobody has any claim on your property because of it. A beneficiary named in your will has a hope, not a right.

At death, two things happen on different clocks.

1. **Ownership shifts in principle.** Title to your probate assets is held for the people named in the will, subject to debts and expenses.
2. **Authority waits for the court.** The executor must file the will, often within a deadline set by state law, and ask the court to appoint them. The court then issues *letters testamentary*, the paper that banks and title companies want to see.

Until letters issue, the executor generally cannot sell the house, close a bank account or distribute a single item. That gap often lasts weeks, and in some courts months.

### What an executor can do before appointment

Not everything has to wait. Before letters are issued, a named executor can usually:

- Arrange the funeral or burial, if the will or family asks them to.
- Locate the original will and take it to the court clerk.
- Secure the house, collect mail and cancel subscriptions.
- Notify the Social Security Administration and ask a funeral home for certified death certificates.
- Gather a list of accounts, debts and property.

They generally should not move money, sell property or pay creditors. Several states allow limited "emergency" or "special" appointments when an asset truly cannot wait, such as a business that needs its payroll run. Ask the probate clerk what your county offers.

## What a will controls and what it doesn't

The easiest way to see who needs probate is to sort assets by how they are titled. The will governs only the probate column.

| How the asset is held | Does the will control it? | Usually goes through probate? |
|---|---|---|
| House in your name alone | Yes | Yes. |
| Bank account in your name alone | Yes | Yes. |
| Bank account with a payable-on-death payee | No | No. |
| IRA or 401(k) with a named beneficiary | No | No. |
| Life insurance with a named beneficiary | No | No. |
| Joint tenancy with right of survivorship | No, passes to the surviving owner | No. |
| Home with a transfer-on-death deed | No | No. |
| Property titled in a living trust | No, the trust controls it | No. |
| Personal items, car, jewelry in your name | Yes | Often, unless the estate is small. |

The pattern is simple. If a document other than the will says who gets the asset, that document wins. Our guide to [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) lists more examples, and [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) covers the traps, including the ex-spouse who is still on a beneficiary form.

### Example: a will, a joint account and a surprise

Dolores, 72, signed a will leaving everything equally to her two sons, Marcus and Theo. Years earlier she added Theo to her checking account to help with bills, and the account was set up with survivorship rights. When she died, that $84,000 went straight to Theo. The will, which said equal shares, never touched it. Marcus's share of the rest of the estate was far smaller than Dolores expected. No court could fix it, because the account was never part of the probate estate.

Dolores did the right thing by having a will. She just did not match the rest of her paperwork to it.

## Which tools actually skip probate?

If the goal is to keep your family out of court, the work happens in how you hold property. The main tools:

1. **Beneficiary designations** on retirement accounts and life insurance.
2. **Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death registrations** on bank and brokerage accounts.
3. **A transfer-on-death deed** for real estate, in states that allow one.
4. **A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust)**, funded by retitling assets into it.
5. **Joint ownership with survivorship**, which works but has real risks, such as exposing the asset to the co-owner's creditors.

Our overview of [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) compares these side by side. Nearly every plan with a trust also includes a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will), because anything missed still falls back into the will and into probate.

## Can a small estate skip probate even with a will?

Often yes, but the will is not the reason. Most states let heirs collect a modest estate by affidavit, without a full court case. The cutoff varies widely, and some states treat real estate differently from bank accounts and cars. The [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) guide walks through how it works, and our [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) gives a rough read on whether your family might qualify.

Even when no court case is needed, the will still matters. It tells the family who should receive things, and some states require you to deliver a will to the court even when nothing else is filed.

## Mistakes people make about wills and probate

- **Assuming "I have a will" means "no probate."** Check how each asset is titled, then decide whether you are comfortable with court.
- **Leaving the original where no one can find it.** The court usually needs the original, so see [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
- **Naming an executor without talking to them.** The job is real work, as described in [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
- **Forgetting that a trust needs funding.** A trust that is signed but empty leaves the will, and probate, doing all the work.
- **Skipping the will because the estate seems small.** Even with a trust and a few beneficiary forms, a will still names the guardian for children. It is cheap insurance.

## How state law changes this

Probate is a state court process, so the rules differ. States set their own deadlines for filing a will, the dollar limits for small estates, whether independent administration is available, and how much court supervision follows. A few states have streamlined systems that make probate quick for many families, while others run slowly and cost more. Our state guides, such as the one for [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), show how one state handles it, or ask an attorney who works in the county where the estate would be filed.
