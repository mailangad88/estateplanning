People tend to ask this in one of two moods. Some are young and healthy and feel a will belongs to an older person with a mansion. Others are comfortable and organized and suspect their beneficiary forms already cover everything. Both groups are partly right, and both usually miss one thing.

Below are seven common situations and what a will does in each. For the broad picture, the [wills guide](/learn/wills) is the place to start, and the [plan finder](/plan-finder) helps if you want to see which documents match your life.

## The short version

- Children under 18 settle the question. Write the will.
- A blank or outdated beneficiary form leaves the account to the court's default rules.
- Joint ownership only works until the second owner dies.
- A will is cheap compared with the cost of its absence.
- If you truly have no one to name and nothing to leave, a will is optional. Few people qualify.

## Do I need a will if I have minor children?

Yes, and this is the clearest case. Only a will lets you name a guardian for children under 18. If both parents die without naming one, a judge chooses, usually after a hearing and sometimes after relatives disagree. The court may pick well, but it will not know that you wanted your sister, not your brother, because of how he talks to your kids.

The will can also set up a trust so that a child does not receive a lump sum at 18. Our page on [what happens when no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) explains the court process, and [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) helps with the harder part, deciding who.

## Do I need a will if I am married?

Usually, yes. People assume a spouse takes everything. In many states that is true only if all your children are also the surviving spouse's children. If you have a child from an earlier relationship, state law often splits the estate between the spouse and children. If you have no will and your spouse is also hurt in the same accident, the whole estate falls to the default list of relatives.

Take Dana and Lewis, both 41, with two kids and a house they own jointly. The house passes to the survivor. But if they die together, the house goes to a court-supervised process, and the court picks the guardian. A mirror pair of simple wills fixes all of it. For how wills for couples differ, read [joint wills versus mutual wills](/learn/wills/joint-will-vs-mutual-will).

## Do I need a will if everything has a beneficiary?

Not for those accounts, but you are more exposed than you think. Retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death bank accounts go to the person named on the form. A will cannot change that, and it is not needed for it.

The gaps show up in three places:

1. **The named person died first** and nobody updated the form, so the account falls into the estate.
2. **A form was left blank** when the account was opened.
3. **Things without forms exist**: the car, personal property, a tax refund, the last paycheck, a digital account, an old brokerage account.

A will catches all of those. The [guide to which accounts can have a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/which-accounts-can-have-a-beneficiary) shows what sits outside a will, and the [probate asset sorter](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains which side each asset falls on.

## Do I need a will if I rent, have a small estate or little money?

Probably, if anyone depends on you or you care who gets what you own. A renter still has a vehicle, a bank balance, a laptop full of photos and a security deposit. A small estate often qualifies for a simplified court process, which is good news, but someone still has to be authorized to start it, and the law decides who without a will.

If you have a pet, a will can name the person who takes the animal, and a short gift can fund its care. If a relative has a disability and receives benefits, a will can route money to a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) rather than to the person directly, which might otherwise jeopardize their benefits.

For a person with almost nothing, a different set of priorities applies. The healthcare directive and power of attorney often matter more than the will. The page on [estate planning with few assets](/learn/basics/do-i-need-an-estate-plan-with-few-assets) sets out that order.

## Do I need a will if I am young?

If you are 18 or older, you can have one, and the case grows quickly with each milestone. A first job brings a 401(k) and a beneficiary form. A first apartment brings a lease. A partner brings the question of whether you are married in the eyes of the law.

Marcus, 26, is single, rents, and has a retirement account naming his mother. He does not need a long will. He needs a one-page will naming an executor, a power of attorney, and a healthcare directive, because at his age the likelier risk is an accident that leaves him unable to speak, not an early death. See [estate planning for young adults](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults) for the short list.

## Do I need a will if I am unmarried?

Yes, more than a married person does. State law generally does not recognize an unmarried partner as an heir. Without a will, your partner of twenty years can receive nothing, and the house you share may go to your siblings. A will is the starting point, and a trust or beneficiary changes may be needed too. The story is told in detail on the page about [an unmarried partner who dies without a will](/learn/what-if/unmarried-partner-dies-without-a-will).

## What does a will do that nothing else does?

Three jobs belong to the will alone. It names the guardian. It names the executor, so the court does not pick an administrator for you. And it catches whatever no other document reaches. Everything else on your plan, from a beneficiary form to a trust, handles a specific asset. The will handles the leftovers, and in most households the leftovers include the things that cause arguments: the car, the furniture, the family photos, the jewelry that two siblings both remember being promised.

It also records your wishes in your own words. A judge, a bank or a sibling who doubts the story can read what you chose, and that alone settles more family disputes than people expect.

## When can you safely skip a will?

Consider skipping only if all of these are true:

| Question | Safe-to-skip answer |
|---|---|
| Minor or dependent children? | None |
| Every account has a living primary and a backup beneficiary? | Yes |
| Everything else is co-owned with a person who will outlive you? | Yes |
| You accept your state's default heirs for anything left? | Yes |
| You have no pet, business, or property in another state? | Yes |

If you answered the last column to every row, a will is optional. Check again after any birth, marriage, divorce or purchase.

## How much trouble is it to get one?

Less than most people expect. A simple will can often be drafted and signed in one visit. The harder parts are choosing the executor and guardian, not the paperwork. The page on [what a will costs](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost) gives realistic ranges, and [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) shows the signing steps.
