Marguerite, 57, has lived with Joan for nineteen years. They share a house in Joan's name and a dog in nobody's. Marguerite has two brothers she sees at Christmas. If Marguerite dies without a will, her brothers inherit what she owns. Joan, who has cooked her dinner every night for two decades, inherits nothing unless the law gives her another route.

Estate law is built around relatives. Marriage and blood put you on the default list, and everyone else is a stranger until you say otherwise. This article is for the person who wants a will to say otherwise: for a partner, a friend, a charity or an heir who lives abroad. It covers what a will can do, where the limits are, and the drafting steps that keep the gift from failing. For the full overview, see our [wills guide](/learn/wills).

## The short version

- A will can name anyone, if you own the property alone and nobody has a legal claim on it.
- Without a will, an unmarried partner and a friend get nothing under the default rules.
- A spouse is the one person who can push back against your will.
- Taxes differ by state, and a partner may be taxed like a stranger.
- Joint ownership and beneficiary forms can do the job faster than a will.

## What happens if you do nothing?

The state's intestacy statute decides, and it ranks relatives. A spouse is first, then children, then parents and siblings, and so on outward. An unmarried partner is not on the list, however long the relationship. A friend is not either. If the statute finds no relative at all, the property goes to the state, a result called escheat.

That is why the unmarried partners we see most often are the ones in trouble. The survivor may have cared for the other through illness, shared every expense and still be asked to leave the house by a brother who lives three states away. Our page on [what happens when an unmarried partner dies without a will](/learn/what-if/unmarried-partner-dies-without-a-will) walks through the sequence, and [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) covers the rest of the plan.

## How do you leave property to a partner or friend?

Four routes exist, and most couples use more than one.

1. **A will.** It controls property in your sole name. It goes through probate, so the partner waits and may face a challenge from relatives.
2. **Joint ownership with survivorship.** A house or bank account held jointly passes to the survivor outside the will. Both owners have present rights, so this is a bigger step than it looks. See [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).
3. **Beneficiary designations.** Retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death accounts go to whoever is named. The name on the form beats the name in the will.
4. **A trust.** A revocable trust can hold the home and investments, avoid probate and set terms, such as lifetime use of the house for the partner and then a remainder to your family.

In the will, name the person by full legal name and describe their relationship, so a court cannot confuse them with someone else. "My partner, Joan A. Whitfield, of 14 Mill Street" beats "Joan." Add a backup, and a clause saying what happens if the partner and you die close together.

### What about the shared house?

If the house is in your name alone, your will can leave it to your partner, but relatives can contest the will and the probate process delays the transfer. If it is in both names as joint tenants, it goes to the survivor automatically. If it is in both names as tenants in common, your half passes by your will. The differences are covered in [joint tenants vs. tenants in common](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenants-vs-tenants-in-common-vs-entirety).

A common compromise leaves the partner the right to live in the house for life, with the remainder to your relatives, through a trust or a life estate. It protects the partner and keeps faith with the family, though it leaves the partner to pay taxes and upkeep unless the plan sets aside money.

## Does the state tax a partner or friend more?

Sometimes. There is no federal inheritance tax, and the federal estate tax applies only above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most estates owe nothing federally. A few states are different.

In New Jersey, the inheritance tax sorts heirs into classes. Spouses, civil union and domestic partners, children, grandchildren and parents are exempt. Siblings and children-in-law pay on amounts above the first $25,000. Everyone else, including an unmarried partner who has not registered and a friend, falls in Class D and pays 15 percent on the first $700,000 and 16 percent above. Charities are exempt. Details are on our page about [New Jersey's inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax), and registration is covered in [domestic partners and civil union partners in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/domestic-partners-and-civil-union-partners-in-new-jersey-estate-law).

Illinois has no inheritance tax. It has an estate tax that applies when an estate passes $4 million. A gift to a partner or friend counts the same as a gift to a child for that threshold, and a gift to a surviving spouse is deductible.

Many other states have no inheritance tax at all, and a few have one with a similar class structure. Check yours with the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker).

## Can a non-citizen inherit under a will?

Yes. Nothing in federal law stops a person from inheriting because they are not a citizen. The practical questions are different.

- **Where do they live?** An heir abroad needs a bank account that can receive money, ID that satisfies the bank, and possibly a power of attorney in the U.S.
- **Which taxes apply?** The U.S. does not impose an inheritance tax on the recipient, but the heir's home country might tax or require them to report it.
- **What if the heir is your spouse?** A citizen spouse's inheritance qualifies for an unlimited marital deduction. A non-citizen spouse's does not unless the property goes into a special trust, called a qualified domestic trust. See [the non-citizen spouse and the QDOT](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot).
- **What about immigration status?** Inheriting does not change status in itself. Questions about residency belong with an immigration lawyer, not an estate lawyer.

Our guide to [estate planning for immigrants and non-citizens](/learn/basics/estate-planning-for-immigrants-and-non-citizens) goes further, including the case where you are the non-citizen.

## Who can stand in the way?

A will gives you freedom, but some people have claims that sit above it.

| Person or rule | What it does | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Surviving spouse | Can claim an elective share, a minimum fraction of the estate, against a will that leaves them less | Plan for it, or agree in writing with the spouse |
| Minor children | In some states they have support claims or a protected share of the home | Ask about your state's rules |
| Relatives | Can challenge the will for undue influence or lack of capacity | Document capacity, use disinterested witnesses |
| Foreign forced-heirship law | Can reserve shares of property abroad for children | Get a local view on foreign property |
| Creditors | Are paid before gifts | Count debts before promising amounts |

The [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) deserves emphasis. A married person cannot leave everything to a friend and nothing to a spouse in most states, because the spouse can elect against the will. If you are married and want to leave a significant portion to someone else, talk to an attorney first. A signed agreement from the spouse before or during marriage can sometimes waive the right.

For relatives who may be hurt, a short explanation helps. Our page on [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) explains how to draft the will and what to say.

## What if the person receives benefits?

A direct gift to someone on SSI or Medicaid may disqualify them, because those programs limit countable assets. A special needs trust can hold the gift and pay for extras without ending benefits. This applies equally to a partner, a friend or a relative. If you do not know your beneficiary's situation, ask.

## What about charities?

A charity can be named in the same way, but get the legal name and tax identification number right. "The Humane Society" without a city and a number can lead to a dispute over which of several groups gets the gift. Contact the organization if the gift is large. Tax effects of charitable gifts are described in [charitable gifts and bequests](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-gifts-and-bequests-gift-tax-estate-tax-and-income-tax).
