Dolores, 71, leaves her lake cottage to her son Marcus in a will she signed eleven years ago. Marcus died three winters ago. Dolores never went back and changed the will. The cottage gift is now dead on the page, and what happens next depends on a sentence she never wrote.

Gifts in a [will](/learn/wills) have a hidden condition: the person has to be alive when you die. This page covers what happens when they are not, whether the beneficiary's children step in, and how to handle the harder case where two people die in the same accident.

## What is a lapsed gift?

A lapsed gift is a bequest that fails because the beneficiary died before you did. A will only speaks when you die. If the person named is already gone, there is no one to receive the property, so the gift lapses.

The property does not go to the dead person's estate or heirs just because they were named. What happens instead is decided by three layers, checked in this order:

1. **The will itself.** If you named a backup, the backup takes. This is the cleanest outcome.
2. **State anti-lapse law.** If there is no backup, many states substitute certain relatives of the dead beneficiary.
3. **The residuary clause.** If neither applies, the gift falls into whatever you left to "the rest of my estate." If that also fails, state intestacy law decides.

## When do anti-lapse laws save the gift?

Anti-lapse statutes redirect a failed gift to the dead beneficiary's descendants, but only when the beneficiary was a close relative of yours. The exact list differs. In many states it covers your children and grandchildren. In others it also covers your siblings or their children. Most do not reach friends, a partner you were not married to, a charity that has dissolved, or stepchildren.

Here is how it plays out for Dolores. If her state's anti-lapse law covers children, the cottage passes to Marcus's two daughters in equal shares, unless the will says otherwise. If she had instead left the cottage to her friend Pat and Pat had died, nobody steps in. The cottage drops into the residue.

A few details trip people up:

- **The will can switch the statute off.** A line like "if my son does not survive me, this gift fails" or "to my son Marcus, only if he survives me" blocks substitution.
- **Class gifts behave differently.** A gift "to my children" usually adjusts automatically: a child who died first is simply not counted, though their descendants may be added back by statute.
- **Stepchildren and in-laws are the gap.** Families with blended households often assume the law will treat a stepdaughter like a daughter. Usually it will not. See [leaving an inheritance to stepchildren](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance).

## What if the residuary gift lapses too?

If the person who gets "everything else" has died, and there is no backup, the leftover property may go through intestacy as if you had no will for that part. That is how a carefully planned will turns into a partial intestacy.

The usual fix is a chain of backups in the residuary clause: to my spouse; if my spouse does not survive me, to my children in equal shares; if a child does not survive me, to that child's descendants; and if none survive, to a named charity or relative. That ordering is ordinary drafting, not exotic.

For account-based assets the same logic applies on the form. A life insurance policy or IRA with a dead primary beneficiary and no contingent beneficiary goes to the default under the contract, often your estate. Our page on [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) explains why the form needs the same backup chain.

## What if two people die at the same time?

When people die in the same accident, the order of death can be impossible to prove, and the order decides who inherits from whom. Most states follow a version of the Uniform Simultaneous Death Act. Under it, a beneficiary must survive you by a short, fixed period, commonly 120 hours (five days), shown by clear and convincing evidence. If they do not, they are treated as having died first and the gift passes to your backup.

Without such a rule, you can end up with a chain of strange transfers. Husband and wife are in a car crash. The wife is declared dead at the scene. The husband lives two days. Everything she owned passes to him, and then out through his will or intestacy to his relatives, perhaps a brother she never spoke to, while her own family receives nothing.

### What does a common disaster clause do?

A common disaster clause lets you choose the presumption yourself, so you are not relying on a default. Typical wording says that if a beneficiary and the will-maker die in circumstances where the order is uncertain, the beneficiary is treated as having died first. Pair it with a survivorship period.

| Provision | What it does | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|
| Default simultaneous death rule | Beneficiary must outlive you by a fixed short period | Often 120 hours in states following the Uniform Act |
| Survivorship clause in your will | Beneficiary must outlive you by a longer period you choose | 30 to 60 days is common |
| Common disaster clause | Presumes the beneficiary died first if the order is unclear | Written into the will |
| Backup beneficiary | Names who takes if the first choice fails | One backup per major gift |

The survivorship clause has a cost: if the beneficiary does live through the period, they wait that long to receive property that sits in probate anyway, so the delay rarely matters in practice. Keep it shorter than you think you want, because a 6-month period can stall the entire estate.

## What should parents with young children do?

They should write the backup plan for the worst day first. When both parents die together, the questions pile up: who raises the children, who manages the money, who gets the house. If the will names no guardian, a judge picks. If the children inherit directly, the money may reach them at 18 or 21 in a lump sum. Our page on [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) walks through the options, and [what happens when no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) shows the court path.

Priya and Dan, both 38, have a four-year-old. Their wills leave everything to each other, and then to their daughter "when she turns 25." They have named a guardian but never wrote what happens if the guardian's household is later unavailable. A backup guardian and a trust for the daughter, managed by a trustee they trust, closes both gaps.

## How do you check your own will for these gaps?

Read each gift and ask what happens if the person is gone. Here is a short checklist to use:

1. List every named beneficiary and note whether each is alive and still in your life.
2. Mark each gift that has no backup beneficiary.
3. Check whether your state's anti-lapse rule would catch each person, and whether you want it to.
4. Confirm the will has a survivorship period and a common disaster clause.
5. Compare the will with your beneficiary forms so both name the same backups.
6. Update through a [codicil or a new will](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) when anyone on the list has died, divorced you or fallen out with you.

If you are a married couple, review both wills together, because the clauses interact. Details differ by state, and our [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) cover local rules; the [plan finder](/plan-finder) can point you to the right starting place.
