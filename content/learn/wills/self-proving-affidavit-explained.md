Here is a small detail that decides whether the first week of probate is a formality or a scavenger hunt. You have a will, signed in front of two witnesses. Years later, your executor takes it to the court. The judge wants proof the will was signed correctly. The question is whether that proof is already sitting in the file or has to be gathered from people who may have moved, married, forgotten or died.

A self-proving affidavit is that proof, prepared in advance. This page covers what it says, how it is signed, what it saves and what happens when a will does not have one.

## What is a self-proving affidavit?

A [self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) is a sworn statement attached to a will. In it, you (the [testator](/glossary/testator)) and your two witnesses state under oath, in front of a notary, that you signed the will willingly, that you appeared to be of sound mind, and that the witnesses watched you sign and then signed themselves. The notary adds a stamp and signature confirming that the people who swore really are who they say they are.

In states that recognize it, a will with this affidavit is called "self-proved." The court can admit the will to [probate](/glossary/probate) on the paperwork alone, unless someone contests it. Nobody has to be called to say, years later, what happened on a Tuesday afternoon in a lawyer's conference room.

It is worth being clear about what the affidavit is not. It does not make an invalid will valid. If only one witness signed, or you signed after the witnesses left, a notary's stamp will not fix that. The ceremony itself is what creates a valid will, as laid out in [how to make a legally valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will). The affidavit only records that the ceremony happened.

## How is a self-proving affidavit signed?

It is usually done in the same sitting as the will, with the same people in the room.

1. **Sign the will first.** You sign at the end, in front of both witnesses, and tell them it is your will.
2. **The witnesses sign the will** in your presence, printing their names and addresses.
3. **Everyone moves to the affidavit,** which is often a page attached to the will or printed on its last page.
4. **You and both witnesses sign the affidavit** in front of the notary, who has checked each person's photo ID.
5. **The notary completes the certificate:** the date, the county, a statement that the signers swore or affirmed, and the notary's commission details and seal.
6. **Keep the whole thing together.** Do not unstaple the pages. A will with pages missing raises questions of its own.

The notary and the witnesses can be the same person in some states, but that mixes roles and invites challenge. Using separate people is the better habit. Law offices usually have staff who can do all three.

## What does a self-proving affidavit actually say?

The exact wording is set by state law, and a good form follows it closely. The substance is usually the same:

- The will was signed by the testator in the presence of the witnesses.
- The testator said it was their will and signed willingly.
- The witnesses signed in the testator's presence, and in each other's presence in states that require it.
- The testator was at least 18 and, as far as the witnesses could tell, of sound mind and under no pressure.

A few states let the sworn language sit on the same page as the signatures, so there is only one signing moment instead of two. This is sometimes called a combined or simplified form. It works the same way in practice.

## What happens if a will has no affidavit?

The will can still be valid and still be admitted, but the court needs another route to proof. Which route depends on the state and the facts.

| Situation | What the court usually needs |
|---|---|
| Self-proved will, no challenge | The will and the affidavit. Often no hearing. |
| No affidavit, witnesses available | A sworn statement or testimony from one or both witnesses. |
| No affidavit, witnesses dead or missing | Proof of the witnesses' signatures and handwriting, plus proof the signing was proper. |
| Handwritten will in a state that accepts them | Evidence that the handwriting and signature are the testator's. |
| Any will, with a contest filed | Live testimony from witnesses, even if the will is self-proved. |

That last row matters. The affidavit eases the ordinary case. If a relative files a contest on the grounds of capacity or [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence), the judge still hears evidence, and the witnesses may still be asked to testify. See [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) for how those cases run.

### A worked example

Rosa, 68, signed her will with two coworkers of her lawyer as witnesses and a notary down the hall. The affidavit took six minutes. When she died eleven years later, her son Marcus filed the will and affidavit with the county court and was appointed executor without a hearing. Neither witness was ever contacted. Both had left the firm.

Compare Dale, 74, who signed a typed will at his kitchen table with his neighbors Ines and Paul watching. No affidavit. After Dale died, Ines had moved away and Paul had died. His daughter Colleen needed a sworn statement from Ines, found her through an old holiday card, and waited five weeks for a notarized declaration from another state. The will was admitted in the end. The delay cost her the closing date on the sale of Dale's condo.

## Do you need a notary for the will itself?

No, in most states. The witnesses are what make the will valid. Notarizing the will page alone does not substitute for witnesses, and it does not make the will self-proved unless the notary certificate uses the sworn statement your state recognizes. People often assume the stamp is the magic part. It is the affidavit language and the oath that count.

Some situations are different. Certain countries or agencies ask for notarized or apostilled documents, and a will used abroad may need extra steps. If you have property overseas, mention it to the attorney drafting your will.

## Common mistakes with self-proving affidavits

- **Signing the affidavit without the witnesses.** Both witnesses have to swear to it, not just the testator.
- **Using a witness who is also a beneficiary.** The affidavit is valid, but the gift may be reduced or voided in many states, and a challenger will point to it.
- **Mismatched dates and names.** The affidavit should match the will's date and the names exactly. A misspelled name invites a second look.
- **Printing a stock form for the wrong state.** Language that works in one state can fall short in another.
- **Separating the pages.** Keep the affidavit stapled or bound with the will. Store the original as described in [where to keep your original will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
- **Assuming online forms handled it.** Many do-it-yourself services provide an affidavit page, but it only works if you actually find a notary and do the signing. See [handwritten, online and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills).

## Can electronic or remote signing be self-proved?

In some states, yes. A growing number have passed laws on electronic wills and remote notarization, and a few allow an electronic self-proving affidavit. The rules about where each person must be, how the video is recorded and which platform may be used are specific, and a mistake can invalidate the document. Unless you have confirmed your state's law, the safer route is paper, in person.

## How does this fit into the probate timeline?

When an executor files a will, the first question the clerk or judge asks is whether it is the real thing and was signed properly. The affidavit answers that at the start. The rest of the process, such as notifying heirs, inventorying assets and paying debts, goes on as usual. A smooth start does not make a long estate short, but it removes one source of delay. For the full sequence, read [what probate is and how it works](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) and [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate).
