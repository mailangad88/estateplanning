It is the question nobody wants to ask out loud. A husband is charged in his wife's death, her will leaves him everything, and the family wants to know whether he still inherits. It sounds like a plot from television, but probate courts see real versions of it, and the answer is written into statute.

This page explains the slayer rule, how it treats each kind of property, how it differs from a will contest, and what you can do in your own plan so the question never has to be argued. It sits in our [wills guide](/learn/wills), which covers the rest of how a will works.

## What is the slayer rule?

The slayer rule says a person cannot profit from intentionally and unlawfully killing someone. Illinois and New Jersey have it in their probate statutes, and nearly every other state does too. The usual mechanism is simple: the law treats the killer as having died before the victim. Everything that would have passed to the killer instead passes as if the killer were not there to take it.

That "died first" fiction does a lot of work. A will gift to the killer fails, and the gift goes to the person named as a backup, or falls into the residuary estate. If there is no will, the killer is skipped in the order of heirs. A joint account the two owned is treated as if the killer had no survivorship right. The rule does not punish the killer's children; it just removes the killer from the line.

## What kind of killing triggers it?

Only a killing that is both intentional and unlawful. Murder and voluntary manslaughter fit. Accidents, most car crashes without criminal intent, and killings justified by self-defense generally do not. An involuntary manslaughter conviction sits in the gray zone, and courts differ on whether it counts.

The practical test is the court's own finding. In many states, a conviction for murder or voluntary manslaughter is conclusive proof for the probate case. Without a conviction, the executor or an heir can ask the probate court to decide, and the standard is usually the ordinary civil one, "more likely than not." That is why a person found not guilty in criminal court can still lose an inheritance. The two courts ask different questions with different burdens.

## How does it work with each kind of property?

Each asset has its own path, so check them one by one.

| Asset | What the slayer rule usually does |
| --- | --- |
| Will gift to the killer | Fails. Passes to the named backup or the residuary estate. |
| Intestate share | Killer is skipped; other heirs take. |
| Joint account or house with survivorship | Killer's survivorship right is cut off; the victim's share generally passes through the estate. |
| Life insurance naming the killer | Killer is usually barred; proceeds go to the contingent beneficiary or the estate. |
| Revocable trust gift to the killer | Treated like a will gift under most trust statutes. |
| Retirement account naming the killer | Governed by plan rules and state law; confirm with the plan administrator. |

Plan documents are where it gets uneven. A federally regulated retirement plan follows its own plan terms and federal rules, and an insurer may pay a named beneficiary until a court order arrives. If you are the executor or a contingent beneficiary, send the insurer or plan a written notice of the claim and a copy of any charges or court findings as early as you can.

## An example with real numbers

Here is an invented case. Robert, 61, dies. His will leaves his $400,000 house and $150,000 of savings to his second wife, Lorraine, and names his daughter Ana as the backup. Lorraine is later convicted of voluntary manslaughter in his death.

The court treats Lorraine as having died before Robert. The will's backup clause applies, so Ana takes the house and savings. A life insurance policy of $250,000 named Lorraine, with Ana as contingent beneficiary. The insurer holds the money until it receives the conviction, then pays Ana. A joint checking account of $30,000 was owned by both spouses. Half belonged to Lorraine and half to Robert, and Robert's half joins the estate and passes to Ana.

If Robert had not named Ana as backup, the gift would have fallen into the residuary estate and then to his heirs under intestacy. That would still skip Lorraine, but it would also bring in Robert's own relatives in the order the statute sets. Our article on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) shows that order, and so does the one for [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey).

## What should the family do if this happens?

Move on the practical steps early, because insurers and banks pay out quickly to named people.

1. **Tell the executor in writing.** If the person named as executor is the suspect, ask the court to appoint someone else before any property is touched.
2. **Notify each institution.** Write to the insurer, the bank and any plan administrator that a claim is pending and ask them to hold funds.
3. **Ask the probate court to rule.** An interested person can petition the court to declare that the slayer rule applies. In Illinois that happens in the circuit court handling the estate, and in New Jersey in the Superior Court or the Surrogate's proceeding.
4. **Keep the criminal case records.** A certified copy of the judgment of conviction is the most direct evidence the probate court will see.
5. **Hold off on distributions.** An executor who pays out early can be personally liable if the court later bars the recipient.
6. **Check the deadlines.** A will contest has a short window; in Illinois, [six months after the will is admitted](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline). A slayer claim may have its own timing, so ask an attorney right away.

## Is this the same as contesting the will?

No, and the difference matters. A will contest argues that the document itself is not valid, because of undue influence, lack of capacity or a signing defect. Our guide to [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) covers those grounds. A slayer claim accepts that the will is valid and argues that one person cannot take under it. The will stays in force for everyone else.

One consequence is that a slayer ruling never rewrites the document. The court just applies the will with the killer removed. Whatever the testator wrote for the backup beneficiary is what governs.

## How does the rule interact with beneficiary forms?

The same logic applies, but the forms can lag behind. The law that bars a killer from a will also bars them from a life insurance payout in most states, yet the insurer has no way to know until it is told. Beneficiary forms are a separate topic from wills, and our guide on whether a [beneficiary designation can be challenged](/learn/beneficiary-designations/when-a-beneficiary-designation-can-be-challenged) walks through the slayer rule alongside undue influence and capacity.

## How can you plan around this?

You cannot predict it, and you should not live as if it will happen. What you can do is make sure the document has an answer if any named person cannot take.

- **Name backups everywhere.** Put a contingent beneficiary on each account and a backup gift in each article of the will. See our page on [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).
- **Say what happens to a gift that fails.** A clear residuary clause stops the property from drifting to distant relatives.
- **Review after a family breakdown.** If a relationship has become dangerous or abusive, update the plan with an attorney and make sure protective measures are in place. The plan is not a safety plan, but an outdated one can leave an abuser positioned to inherit.
- **Know how a failed gift works.** The same idea governs beneficiaries who die first, covered in our article on [lapsed gifts and simultaneous death](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death).
