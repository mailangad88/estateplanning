A parent has been diagnosed with dementia, or a spouse has weeks to live, and no one has a will. Or there is a will, signed last spring, and one child is quietly sure that another child leaned on Dad until he signed. These are the situations that end up in court, and they are almost always decided on evidence gathered long before anyone filed a petition.

This page explains the legal tests, what makes a will signed in a hard season hold up, and what to do when the window for a will may have closed. The wider picture of challenges is in the [guide to contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will), and the [wills guide](/learn/wills) places this topic among the others.

## What does a person need to understand to sign a will?

The standard is lower than most people expect. The law asks whether the signer, at the moment of signing, understood four things: that they are making a will and what that means, the general nature and extent of what they own, who their close family members are, and how those pieces fit into a plan for the property. They do not have to be able to balance a checkbook, remember today's date or pass a memory test. This is called [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity).

Courts use a lower bar for a will than for a contract or a power of attorney in most states. A person can lack the capacity to manage money and still be able to say, "I want the house to go to Claire and the rest split between my three children."

The timing matters more than the diagnosis. Capacity is judged at signing. A woman who forgets her neighbor's name by dinner may be clear at 10 a.m. with an attorney across the table.

## Does dementia automatically make a will invalid?

No. A diagnosis is evidence, not a verdict. Judges hear from doctors who say the same thing again and again: dementia is a spectrum, and good hours exist even late in the course of the disease.

What hurts a will is the combination of a documented decline and nothing recorded about the day of signing. If a person was hospitalized for confusion in March and signed a will in April, the challenger has a story. If the attorney wrote, "Mr. Alvarez named his three sons, described the farm and the savings account accurately, and explained why he was leaving the farm to Tom," the story is much harder to tell.

## What about a will signed in a hospital bed or hospice?

Illness, pain and medication do not disqualify anyone. Plenty of valid wills are signed in hospitals. The practical issues are different.

- **Formalities still apply.** Two witnesses, usually disinterested, must be present. A nurse who is also the caregiver is fine; a daughter who inherits is not.
- **Time pressure invites mistakes.** A will drafted overnight from a form is more likely to have blanks, a missing page or an unsworn witness. The [signing defects guide](/learn/wills/will-signing-defects-witnesses-dates-and-missing-pages) lists the common ones.
- **Medication can change the picture.** If the person is sedated in the morning and alert in the afternoon, sign in the afternoon.

If the person is too weak to hold a pen, most states let another adult sign for them, at their direction and in their presence, with the witnesses watching. That solves a physical limit. It does not solve lost capacity, and it should never be a beneficiary doing the signing.

## What is undue influence, and how is it different?

Undue influence is a separate claim. The signer may fully understand the will, but the will reflects another person's wishes rather than their own. Courts look at a cluster of facts:

- The helper has a close or confidential relationship with the signer, such as a caregiver, a child living in the home or someone holding a power of attorney.
- The helper benefits unusually from the new will, particularly if it departs from earlier ones.
- The helper helped arrange the lawyer, drove the signer there, sat in on the meeting or handled the paperwork.
- The signer was isolated, dependent or vulnerable.

A single factor means little. Someone who drives their mother to the lawyer's office is doing a kindness. Someone who picks the lawyer, attends the meeting and takes a larger share raises questions. The [glossary entry on undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) gives the short definition, and the [guide on elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) covers the overlap with exploitation.

## How do you make a will hold up when capacity or influence may be questioned?

These steps work in most states, and none cost much compared with litigating later.

1. **Use an attorney the signer chooses.** Not the beneficiary's lawyer, and not a friend of the beneficiary.
2. **Meet alone.** The attorney should talk to the signer without family present, ask open questions and write down the answers.
3. **Ask the signer to describe the plan in their own words.** "Who are your children? What do you own? Who gets what and why?" Notes on this are worth more than a checklist the signer nodded to.
4. **Record the reasons for any surprise.** If a child receives less, a short, calm explanation in the file or a letter helps.
5. **Get a medical note on the same day if there is a diagnosis.** A short statement that the patient was alert and oriented is enough.
6. **Use a self-proving affidavit.** It does not stop a challenge, but it makes the will easier to admit. See [self-proving affidavits](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained).
7. **Keep the original safe and tell the executor where it is.**

## Example: two timelines, one family

Marcia, 79, had moderate Alzheimer's. Her daughter Dana lived with her and handled her bills. In October, Dana found a will form online, Marcia signed it with two neighbors witnessing, and it left the house to Dana alone. Marcia's son Peter challenged it. The neighbors remembered that Marcia seemed confused and that Dana did most of the talking.

Suppose instead that Marcia's physician, with Dana waiting outside, had asked the questions, and an attorney had met Marcia alone and documented her answers. The same will, with the same result, would have been much harder to attack. The wishes were identical. The paper trail was not.

## What if the person clearly cannot sign a will?

Then the will is not the right tool, and forcing one will cause more harm than leaving it. Options include:

- A [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) if the person still has capacity for that narrower task. If they do not, see [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).
- Beneficiary designations and joint ownership already in place, which pass assets without a will.
- If no valid will exists, the intestacy rules of the state decide who inherits. That outcome is often acceptable for a family that agrees. It is the case that [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains.
