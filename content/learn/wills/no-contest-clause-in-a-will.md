Some people add a sentence to their will aimed at a particular relative. If you challenge this will and lose, you get nothing. It feels like a safeguard. You can picture the son who has threatened to sue, and the clause sitting there like a locked door.

The reality is more mixed. A no-contest clause can be useful, pointless or even harmful depending on who it is aimed at and where you live. This page explains how it works and what to do alongside it.

## What is a no-contest clause?

A [no-contest clause](/glossary/no-contest-clause), sometimes called an in terrorem clause (Latin for "in fear"), is a provision in a will or trust that cuts off any beneficiary who challenges the document in court. A typical version says that if a beneficiary files a legal action to invalidate the will or any part of it, the gift to that person is revoked and goes to the others as if the challenger had died first.

It does not stop anyone from filing. It sets a price on losing.

## How does a no-contest clause work?

Think of it as a bet the challenger must make. Suppose Helen leaves $200,000 to her daughter Joan, and the rest to her son Paul. The will has a no-contest clause. Joan believes Paul pressured Helen into the will.

- If Joan sues and wins, the will is thrown out or reduced, and the clause falls with it.
- If Joan sues and loses, she loses her $200,000.
- If Joan does not sue, she keeps her $200,000.

The clause changes Joan's math. A strong case may still be worth the risk. A weak one may not be worth $200,000.

## Why does the clause fail against some people?

Because the penalty needs something to take away. The clause does nothing to a person who is left out.

If Helen leaves nothing to a third child, Ray, he has no gift to lose, and a challenge costs him only his legal fees. This is the weakness people overlook. The heir most likely to sue is often the one who got the least or nothing, and that is the person the clause cannot discourage. Leaving even a modest gift to a person who might otherwise sue can give the clause something to bite on. It can also look like a bribe, so think it through with an attorney. The related questions of leaving someone out are covered in [how to disinherit someone or leave unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).

## What do courts and states do with these clauses?

States split into three groups, roughly.

| Approach | What it means | Effect on the clause |
|---|---|---|
| Enforce unless probable cause | The clause holds, but a challenger with good-faith, reasonable grounds keeps the gift | Common; follows the approach in the Uniform Probate Code |
| Enforce strictly | Courts apply the clause as written, with few exceptions | Strongest for the testator |
| Refuse to enforce | The clause is treated as against public policy | No effect; Florida is one example |

Even in strict states, courts read the wording narrowly. They ask exactly which actions the clause covers. A clause that says only "contests" may not reach a petition to interpret the will, a claim against the estate or a dispute about who should serve as executor. Courts also tend to excuse beneficiaries who ask for information or who object to how an executor is behaving. Rules differ a lot, so the specifics for your state matter more than any general statement.

## What challenges does the clause reach?

It depends on how broadly it is drafted and how the state reads it. Typical fights fall into these groups:

- **Challenges to validity.** Claims of lack of [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity), [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence), fraud or forgery. These are the core target.
- **Challenges to a specific gift.** For example, arguing a particular bequest was added under pressure.
- **Interpretation requests.** Asking the court what an unclear sentence means. Often protected.
- **Executor disputes.** Asking to remove an executor who is acting badly. Usually separate from a contest. See [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).
- **Creditor-type claims.** A person claiming they were promised property or are owed money. Usually outside the clause.

## Should you include one?

It depends on your family. A rough way to decide:

1. **Identify who might challenge.** Name them privately. Is it one person or several?
2. **Check what each would receive.** If a likely challenger gets nothing, the clause adds little. If they receive a meaningful share, it may matter.
3. **Check your state's rule.** If your state refuses to enforce, skip it and put your effort elsewhere.
4. **Weigh the cost in goodwill.** A clause can read as a threat. In a family that is already tense, it can deepen the damage.
5. **Ask what else protects the plan.** Capacity documentation and a clear explanation do more.
6. **Have an attorney draft the wording** for your state, and match it in your trust if you have one.

For many families the honest answer is that the clause is a minor tool. For a family with a known troublemaker and a sizable gift at stake, it is worth including.

### Another example

Leonard, 80, has three children. His eldest, Greg, handled his banking for years and has been resentful that Leonard's money would be split evenly. Leonard leaves each child about $300,000 and adds a no-contest clause. Greg thinks about challenging the will, claiming Leonard was confused. Greg's lawyer asks for proof. Leonard had signed with a self-proved will, his doctor's note on capacity and a letter explaining his choices. Greg faces losing $300,000 against a thin case. He lets it go. The clause did some of the work. The paperwork did most of it.

## What works better than a clause?

The most effective protections are dull ones.

- **Use an attorney and a proper signing,** with neutral witnesses and a [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained).
- **Document capacity** if there is any question, with a recent doctor's note or an attorney's file memo.
- **Explain surprises** in a signed letter or in the [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction), in a calm tone.
- **Talk to the family** before the plan is final. [Talking to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) has ideas for how to do that.
- **Consider a funded living trust,** which moves assets out of the probate process and into a document less often attacked. See [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust). Trusts can be challenged too, so this is no sure thing.
- **Read the broader picture** in [will contests: who can challenge a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## What should a beneficiary do if they face a clause?

Do not file anything or send an angry letter before speaking with an attorney. The question of whether your planned action counts as a contest, and whether you would be excused for probable cause, is exactly the kind a lawyer reads the clause and the state's cases to answer. Many people can ask questions, request documents and demand an accounting without triggering the clause. Others find that a decision to sue should wait until they know the full picture. Deadlines are short, so act soon.
