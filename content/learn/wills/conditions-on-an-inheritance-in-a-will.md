Raymond, 66, has a grandson who has bounced through three colleges. Raymond wants to leave him $60,000, but only if he finishes a degree. He also has a daughter whose husband he does not trust, and he would like the daughter's share to stay hers if the marriage ends. Can a [will](/learn/wills) do that?

It can do some of it. This page sorts out which conditions hold up, which ones courts throw out, and where a will runs out of tools.

## The short version

- Conditions are allowed if they are lawful, clear and possible to meet.
- Conditions that tie into a specific act the beneficiary controls, such as finishing school or reaching 30, are the easiest to enforce.
- Conditions on marriage, religion or lifestyle are the most litigated and the most likely to be narrowed or struck.
- The will itself cannot police a condition after you die. A trustee can.
- If you want to keep property away from a spouse, a creditor or a poor spender, a trust does more than a clause in a will.

## What kinds of conditions do courts accept?

Courts generally enforce conditions that are lawful, reasonably clear, and tied to something the beneficiary can actually do. A condition that reads like a goal is easier than one that reads like a leash.

| Type of condition | Example | How courts tend to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| Age | "At 25" | Enforced, though a trust is needed to hold the money |
| Education | "After earning a bachelor's degree" | Usually enforced if the degree is defined |
| Survival | "If she outlives me by 30 days" | Enforced, and common |
| Work or career | "Once he has held a job for 2 years" | Often enforced if measurable |
| Marriage, total restraint | "Only if she never marries" | Often struck as against public policy |
| Marriage, partial | "Only if he marries within a given faith" | Varies widely by state and wording |
| Illegal or impossible | "Only if he commits a crime" | Struck |

The point is not that you cannot say something personal. It is that the more a condition depends on judgment ("lives responsibly," "treats my wife well"), the more likely it ends in a lawsuit. Judges do not like deciding what a dead person would have thought was responsible.

## Why is enforcement the real problem?

A will transfers property at death through the executor, and then the executor is done. Think about Raymond's grandson. The will says "$60,000 if he graduates." Who holds the money while he studies? Who decides whether a bachelor's from an online school counts? What if he graduates in six years, at 29? Nothing in an ordinary will answers those questions.

In practice, one of three things happens. The executor holds the estate open for years, which costs money and annoys everyone. The family argues about whether the condition was met. Or the beneficiary receives the money and the condition is never checked.

If a condition matters to you, write down:

1. Exactly what counts as meeting it, including documents that prove it.
2. Who decides, and what happens if that person disagrees with the beneficiary.
3. A deadline, so the gift cannot hang open forever.
4. Where the money goes if the condition is never met.

## When should the condition live in a trust instead?

Whenever the condition lasts longer than the probate process, put the gift in a trust. A trustee can hold the assets, make payments for school as the grandson progresses, and release the balance when he finishes. A trust built inside your will is called a [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust). A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can do the same job and avoids probate for those assets.

For Raymond's daughter, a trust with a [spendthrift clause](/glossary/spendthrift-clause) is the better tool. The daughter's share stays in the trust. A divorcing spouse or a creditor generally cannot reach what the trustee still holds. Our page on [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) shows how that works, and [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) covers staged payouts at 25, 30 and 35.

A third pattern is a gift for something specific, such as tuition or a first-home down payment, paid to the school or seller by the trustee. That removes most of the temptation and most of the argument.

## What about conditions on marriage?

Courts are most skeptical of conditions that push people in or out of marriage. A gift that is lost if the beneficiary marries at all is a restraint on marriage and is often struck. A gift for a widow that ends if she remarries, on the other hand, is often accepted, because it is seen as support rather than punishment. Conditions about marrying someone of a particular faith or race are the hardest to defend, and in some places they will not be enforced at all.

The law differs by state, and judges differ within states. If a marriage condition matters to you, ask an attorney before relying on it. In the meantime, a discretionary trust is a quieter option: the trustee may distribute for a beneficiary's health, education and support, with no stated marriage test at all.

## What happens if the condition is not met, or is struck?

Every conditional gift needs a second sentence: where the property goes if the condition fails. Without it, the gift lapses into the residue, or into intestacy, and the person who gets it may be someone you never had in mind. Say it plainly. "If Evan has not earned a bachelor's degree by his 30th birthday, this gift passes to the Lakeshore Animal Shelter."

Courts also sort conditions into two kinds, and the difference matters. A condition precedent must be met before the gift vests. A condition subsequent takes the gift away after it was given. Judges are slower to take property back than to withhold it, so "once he finishes school" is safer than "but forfeited if he stops working."

If a court strikes the condition as invalid, it asks whether you would still have made the gift. When the condition was the heart of the gift, the gift often falls with it. When it was an add-on, the beneficiary may receive the property free of the condition. A savings clause helps: "if any condition is held invalid, the gift shall pass to X instead," which keeps you in charge of the fallback.

## How should the wording look?

Plain, measurable and dated. Compare these two.

- Weak: "To my grandson Evan, if he straightens out his life."
- Better: "To my trustee, to hold for my grandson Evan. The trustee may pay his tuition directly to an accredited college. When Evan earns a bachelor's degree, the trustee shall distribute the remaining balance to him. If he has not done so by age 30, the balance passes to his sister Hana."

The second version names a decision-maker, a proof point, a deadline and a fallback. It also treats Evan as a person who might need help rather than as a problem to be policed, which tends to survive a hostile reading better than the first.

## What is a conditional will, and should you use one?

A conditional will is different: the entire will depends on an event. "This will is to take effect only if I die during my surgery on March 3" is the classic example. If you survive, a court may find the will never became effective. Many courts will read such language as simply explaining your reason for signing, and admit the will anyway. But that is a fight your family should not have to have.

If you are worried about a specific event, such as a procedure or a long trip, sign a plain will that works in every case and add a [codicil or a new will](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) later. See also [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) for limits that no clause fixes.

## How do conditions interact with a no-contest clause?

They are separate tools, and a condition does not stop a challenge. If a beneficiary thinks a condition is invalid, they can ask a court to strike it. A [no-contest clause](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) may discourage that, but it is not enforced everywhere, and it will not rescue a condition that is against public policy.
