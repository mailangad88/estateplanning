Wen and Li moved from Ohio to Arizona two years ago. Their wills were signed in Ohio and are in a drawer in the new kitchen. Li wonders whether the wills are even valid in Arizona, and whether a lawyer in a different state needs to look at them.

Short answer: the [wills](/learn/wills) are probably valid, and they probably also need a review. This page explains why those two answers can both be true, and what changes if you live abroad or are not a US citizen.

## Is a will valid after you move to a new state?

Usually yes. Most states have a rule that a will is valid if it met the signing requirements where it was made, where you lived at that time, or where you were a citizen. Wen and Li's Ohio signing, with two witnesses, satisfies Arizona too. If the will also has a [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained), most states accept it without calling the witnesses.

That is the reassuring part. The less comfortable part is that valid and well-suited are different. The new state's law decides the things a will relies on at death: what a surviving spouse is entitled to, who qualifies as executor, how long creditors have to make claims, and how homestead and exempt property work. Your old will was written with another state's rules in mind.

## What should you check after moving?

Look at the points where state law tends to differ. This is the list our attorneys walk through with clients who have moved.

1. **Marital property system.** Nine states, including Arizona, Texas and California, use community property. Most others use common law rules with an elective share. A couple who moves between systems can find that "my half" means something different. See [community property](/glossary/community-property) and [elective share](/glossary/elective-share).
2. **Executor requirements.** Some states require a bond, or limit who may serve if they live elsewhere. Your named executor may now live out of state, or you may have moved away from them.
3. **Witness and notary practice.** A will signed with two witnesses is usually safe, but affidavits, notary forms and remote signing differ. See [signing documents electronically](/learn/basics/sign-estate-planning-documents-electronically).
4. **Real estate in the old state.** If you kept a house there, probate may be needed in both states. Our page on [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) and [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains the problem and the usual fix.
5. **State estate or inheritance tax.** Your old state may have taxed estates under a few million dollars while the new one has none, or the reverse.
6. **Other documents.** Powers of attorney and health care directives are separate from the will and have their own state forms. See [do advance directives work in other states](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states).
7. **Wording that points to the old state.** References to old statutes, courts, or county names are not fatal, but they cause confusion.

Our [state guides](/estate-planning/arizona) summarize local rules, and the broader question is covered in [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## Can a US citizen living abroad make a will?

Yes, and the will can be valid in the US if it follows the signing rules of the state you tie it to. Citizens abroad usually keep a home state through their last US address, their voting registration or the place their assets sit. Pick the state that fits, and have the will signed under that state's formalities.

Three problems show up abroad:

- **Foreign property follows foreign law.** A flat in Lisbon or land in Mexico is governed by the law where it sits. Some countries restrict how much you can leave outside the family. Fixed shares for children, sometimes called forced heirship, are common in civil-law systems. A US will might not override that.
- **Two wills can collide.** A foreign will and a US will, each with a general revocation clause, can cancel each other. Local counsel and a US attorney should draft them together, with each limited to its own country's property.
- **Remote signing may not be accepted.** Rules on witnessing by video differ. A US consulate can sometimes provide notary services, though that does not replace valid witnesses.

## Can a non-citizen make a will in the US?

Yes. Any adult of sound mind can make a will, whatever their citizenship or immigration status. You may leave property to anyone. A beneficiary does not need to be a citizen either.

What changes is tax. A US citizen or resident has the federal exemption of $15,000,000 per person in 2026. A non-resident, non-citizen who owns US property, such as a house or US stocks, has a much smaller exemption, currently $60,000 on US-situated assets, though treaties with some countries change this. That can create a tax bill on a modest US vacation home. If you own US property from overseas, this deserves a conversation with an attorney. A non-citizen spouse has a separate issue with the marital deduction, covered in [non-citizen spouse and the QDOT](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot).

## Can a non-citizen or out-of-state person serve as executor?

Often yes, with limits that vary. Many states let a nonresident serve, sometimes if a resident agent is named for legal papers or the executor posts a bond. Some make it harder for a non-citizen who lives abroad. The practical advice is simple: if your first choice lives far away, name a backup who lives near your assets. [Choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) covers the other qualities that matter.

## Which state counts if you live in two places?

The state of your domicile, which is the place you treat as your permanent home, decides most questions at death. Snowbirds who spend winters in Florida and summers in Michigan are a common case. Courts look at where you vote, where your driver's license and car registration are, where you file taxes, where your doctor is and where you say you intend to stay. A few of those facts, pointing one way, matter more than the number of days in each house.

Two states can each claim the same person as a resident for estate tax purposes. That fight is real and expensive for families, and it is much easier to prevent than to win. If you split your year, keep a consistent set of records and have your will say which state you consider home. Real property in the second state is a separate matter: a house in Florida will usually need a Florida proceeding unless it sits in a trust or passes by a beneficiary-style deed where your state allows one, whatever your will says.

## How do you update a will after a move?

For most people a clean new will is better than a codicil, because many details change at once. A new will signed in the new state has fresh witnesses who are local, a self-proving affidavit that fits local form, and wording written for the state's law. The old one is revoked by the new document.

Bring: your current will, a list of assets with the state where each sits, the names of your executor and guardian choices and where they live now, and any foreign property details. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) can help you decide what else needs attention.
