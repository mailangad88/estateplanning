Of all the decisions in a will, choosing the executor is the one that most affects how smoothly your family gets through the first year. The executor is the person who steps in when you die and makes the legal and financial wheels turn. They can make a hard time easier or much harder.

This article walks through what the role involves, how to evaluate candidates, when to pick a professional, and how to handle the awkward cases, like a family where nobody seems right.

## What does an executor actually do?

An [executor](/glossary/executor) is the person named in your will to settle your estate. Some states call the role a [personal representative](/glossary/personal-representative). After a court confirms the appointment, the executor receives [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary), which are the official proof that they can act for you.

The work usually includes:

- Locating the original will and filing it with the probate court.
- Collecting mail, securing the home and protecting valuables.
- Getting death certificates and notifying banks, insurers and Social Security.
- Opening an estate bank account and inventorying assets.
- Notifying creditors, paying valid debts and filing tax returns.
- Selling or distributing property to your beneficiaries.
- Keeping records and reporting to the court and the beneficiaries.

The full process is covered in [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties). The point here is that it is a part-time job that can last from several months to more than a year, and it carries legal responsibility. Executors are [fiduciaries](/glossary/fiduciary), which means the law holds them to a duty of loyalty and care toward the beneficiaries.

## What qualities should you look for?

Think about the work, not the honor. A good executor tends to be:

1. **Organized.** Paperwork, deadlines, statements and receipts are the job.
2. **Honest and fair.** The executor has access to your money and decides how to handle disputes.
3. **Financially responsible.** They do not need to be an accountant, but someone in constant financial trouble is a poor choice.
4. **Willing.** Some people will say yes out of guilt and then drag their feet.
5. **Local, or at least reachable.** Being out of state can work, but selling a house or attending hearings from far away is harder. Some states restrict out-of-state executors or require them to appoint a local agent.
6. **Calm under pressure.** Family members who are grieving can be difficult.
7. **Likely to outlive you.** A spouse who is your age or older may not be able to serve for long. This is the reason backups matter.

### A quick scorecard

Rate each candidate from 1 to 5 on the traits above. A candidate who scores low on organization or honesty should not be your first choice no matter how much you love them. A person with a lower score in availability might still work with a professional co-executor handling the paperwork.

## Who are common choices, and what are the tradeoffs?

| Choice | Strengths | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse | Knows your finances, motivated, often also main heir | May be elderly or grieving, may struggle with complex tasks |
| Adult child | Often available, familiar with family | Sibling jealousy, unequal workload, conflicts of interest |
| Sibling or relative | Close and trusted | Often similar age, may not outlive you |
| Close friend | Neutral between family members | May lack family standing, can face resentment |
| Co-executors | Share work, check each other | Decisions can stall if they disagree, some states require unanimity |
| Bank or trust company | Experienced, continuous, impartial | Fees, minimums, less personal touch |
| Attorney or professional fiduciary | Legal knowledge, impartial | Fees, may be unfamiliar with your family |

Rosa, 64, has three children. Her oldest, Daniel, is a nurse who lives two states away. Her youngest, Elena, lives nearby, runs her own bookkeeping business, and gets along with everyone. Rosa named Elena as executor, Daniel as backup, and told all three children why. The conversation took twenty minutes and removed any suspicion that Elena was being favored.

## Should you name co-executors?

Co-executors can spread the load and keep siblings from feeling excluded, but they also create friction. If two people must sign everything, delays and disagreements multiply. If you name co-executors, say in the will whether one can act alone, how disputes are resolved, and who has the final say. Many attorneys prefer a single executor with a clear backup and a requirement to keep the beneficiaries informed.

## When does a professional executor make sense?

A professional is worth considering when:

- Your family has a history of conflict, or the executor would be one sibling among several heirs who distrust one another.
- The estate is complicated, such as a family business, rental property, assets in several states or a lawsuit.
- The people you trust live far away or are in poor health.
- You are single, with no children, and your closest relatives are distant.
- You are leaving a long-running trust and need a neutral trustee, which is a different role. See [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).

A bank trust department, a licensed professional fiduciary or an attorney can all serve. Fees are usually a percentage of the estate or an hourly rate, and some corporate fiduciaries have minimum estate sizes. Ask for a fee schedule before you decide, and check how your state limits or reviews executor fees. The [cost of probate](/learn/probate/probate-costs) article explains typical charges.

A hybrid often works well: a family member serves as executor and is allowed in the will to hire an attorney, accountant or other help, with fees paid by the estate. This gives you someone who knows the family, paired with someone who knows the process.

## What if there is no obvious choice?

Not everyone has a spouse or grown child who fits. Some practical options:

- **Ask a trusted friend or younger cousin.** Be honest about the workload.
- **Use a professional,** as discussed above.
- **Name an executor and a "special advisor."** The person has no legal authority, but the will asks the executor to consult them.
- **Pair a family member with a professional** as co-executors.
- **Name backups in order.** Three names is not unusual.

If no named person is able or willing to serve, or if you have no will, the court appoints an administrator from the heirs. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for how that works.

## How should you talk to your executor?

Ask before you name them. A written "yes" is not required, but a conversation is. Cover these points:

1. **Where the original will is kept** and who has a copy. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
2. **A list of accounts and advisors.** A simple one-page inventory saves weeks of searching.
3. **Your wishes about funeral and burial,** which are often better placed in a separate letter or documented elsewhere, since the will may not be read in time.
4. **Whether they will be paid,** and what you expect them to do about hiring professionals.
5. **Who to call:** your attorney, accountant and financial advisor.

Do not forget that your executor deals with your family. If you have a child who may be hurt or upset by not being chosen, tell them yourself and explain why. It is better than the news arriving in a will reading.

## Can an executor be removed?

Yes. A court can remove an executor who steals, mismanages the estate, ignores deadlines, or has a serious conflict of interest, and beneficiaries have rights to information. It is not a quick or cheap process. Better planning lowers the odds. Read [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) for the grounds and steps.

Some states also require an executor to post a bond unless the will waives it. A will that waives the bond saves the estate a premium, and is reasonable when you trust the person. If you have doubts about the person, a bond adds protection.
