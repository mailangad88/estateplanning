Few estate planning decisions carry as much emotion as leaving one child less than another, or leaving a child nothing. The reasons can be sound: an estranged relationship, a child who is already well off, a child who has provided years of care, a child with an addiction who cannot be trusted with a lump sum. The law generally gives you wide freedom here. The risk is not that the choice is illegal. It is that a careless document turns a considered decision into a lawsuit.

This article explains what the law usually allows, where it limits you, and how to put an unequal plan in place so it holds. It focuses on the planning side. If you are on the receiving end of a plan you believe is flawed, see [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## Can you disinherit a child?

In most states you can leave an adult child nothing, if you do it clearly. American law generally does not require parents to leave anything to adult children. The notable exception is Louisiana, which has "forced heirship" rules protecting certain children, usually those who are young or have a disability. Other states have laws protecting a child who was left out of a will because the parent seemed to forget or did not know the child existed, often called a pretermitted heir rule. The details vary by state, so confirm yours with an attorney. See [estate planning in your state](/estate-planning/louisiana) for Louisiana as an example of a state with its own rules.

Minor children are different in practice. You can leave a minor child's share to a trust or to a guardian-managed account, but cutting out a minor entirely is rare, and courts look hard at it.

## How do you disinherit someone correctly?

Follow these steps so the decision is clear, consistent and provable.

1. **Say it in a valid will or trust.** An oral statement or a note in a drawer does nothing. The document needs proper signing; see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).
2. **Name the person and state your intent.** "I intentionally make no provision for my son, Daniel, and this is not an oversight." Naming the person and the intent heads off the claim that you simply forgot.
3. **Do not rely on silence.** A will that gives everything to three children and does not mention a fourth invites a claim that the fourth was forgotten.
4. **Coordinate everything else.** Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance, payable-on-death accounts, joint accounts and transfer on death deeds all pass outside the will. If the child you want to exclude is still named on an old form, they receive the asset anyway. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) and [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).
5. **Name backup beneficiaries.** If a child you do want to provide for dies first, the plan should say who takes their share.
6. **Keep the documents current.** A new will replaces an old one. Do not use a handwritten note or a crossed-out line to change a signed will.

## What if you are not disinheriting but leaving unequal shares?

Unequal shares are common and often sensible. Maya, 68, has three children. Her daughter Lena has lived with her for six years and provided daily care. Her son Omar has a successful business. Her son Peter has a disability and receives benefits. Maya might leave Lena a larger share to reflect her care, Omar a smaller share, and Peter a share in a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) so his benefits are not lost. That is not unfair. It is a plan matched to different needs.

What matters is that the plan is deliberate and that the reasoning is clear. For care-related gifts, consider a written caregiver agreement during life, since a gift in a will is usually harder to explain than a documented arrangement. See [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

## What about your spouse?

Disinheriting a spouse is much harder. Nearly every state protects a surviving spouse in some way, though the method differs:

- **Elective share (or forced share).** In many states, a spouse can choose to take a fixed percentage of the estate instead of what the will leaves. The percentage and what counts toward it vary widely. See [elective share](/glossary/elective-share).
- **Community property.** In community property states, a spouse already owns half of what was acquired during the marriage, so you cannot leave away that half. See [community property](/glossary/community-property).
- **Homestead and family allowances.** Many states give a surviving spouse the right to stay in the home or receive a set allowance.

A spouse can waive these rights, usually in a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement signed with full disclosure. See [prenuptial agreements and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans). In a second marriage, you can often protect your children from the first marriage without disinheriting your spouse, for example by leaving the spouse income for life through a trust. See [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage).

## Why do disinheritance plans fail?

The common failure points are practical, not legal.

| Cause | What happens | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|
| Forgotten beneficiary form | The excluded person still collects the account | Review every form after any change |
| Undue influence claim | A challenger argues someone pressured you | Meet with the attorney alone; record independent advice |
| Capacity claim | A challenger argues you did not understand your choices | Sign while clear; keep medical and attorney notes |
| Pretermitted heir | A child left out by mistake receives a share | Name every child, stating your intent |
| Ambiguous wording | Court must guess what you meant | Use clear language, reviewed by a lawyer |
| Poor signing | The will is invalid and intestacy applies | Follow your state's signing formalities |

If a will is thrown out, your estate may pass by the default rules, which treat children equally. That is the opposite of what you wanted. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## What is a no-contest clause?

A no-contest clause, also called an in terrorem clause, says that anyone who challenges the will or trust loses their gift. It can discourage a beneficiary who has something to lose. It does nothing against someone who receives nothing, so it is weak protection against a disinherited child. States differ sharply: some enforce these clauses strictly, some only when the challenger had no good-faith basis, and a few barely at all. See [no-contest clause](/glossary/no-contest-clause).

## What are alternatives to full disinheritance?

Sometimes the real goal is not to cut someone out but to limit risk or reward a different contribution.

- **A smaller share.** It signals disapproval without total exclusion, and it may reduce the motive to sue.
- **A trust with a trustee.** For a child with debt, addiction or a troubled marriage, a trustee can control distributions. See [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) and [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).
- **Conditions or timing.** Staged distributions at set ages or milestones.
- **A token gift.** Not required, but some people leave a small amount. It does not by itself prevent a contest.
- **Lifetime gifts.** Equalizing earlier gifts, such as paying for one child's down payment, can be addressed by a clause treating it as an advance on inheritance. See [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies).

## How should you explain your decision?

Surprise is the main source of conflict. Consider a short letter explaining your reasoning, written calmly, kept with your documents. It does not replace the legal language but gives your family context. A family meeting, either before or after you sign, can also help. See [talking to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) and [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction).

## What happens to the grandchildren?

If you disinherit a child, that child's own children do not automatically take their parent's share. Many wills say "to my children," and a child who is intentionally excluded is simply not a taker. Some people want to provide for the grandchildren directly, for example through a trust so the funds do not pass through the excluded parent. Your documents should state whether grandchildren are included, because default rules for a child who dies before you may otherwise apply. See [per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes).

## How can you reduce the risk of a contest?

- Hire an attorney and meet with them alone.
- Sign when you are in good health and clear-headed; if capacity might be questioned later, consider a doctor's note around the signing.
- Avoid having a person who benefits attend the meeting or select the lawyer.
- Use clear language, and name everyone involved.
- Keep a short record of why you decided as you did.
- Choose a trustee or executor who is neutral and organized. See [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
