Many people assume that if they say something clearly enough, in front of enough relatives, the family will treat it as the law. Maybe it is a note in the desk drawer that says Aunt Joan gets the piano. Maybe it is a dying father's request, repeated in the hospital room, that the farm never be sold. Families often try hard to honor these wishes. The law is less sentimental.

This page covers which kinds of informal wishes can be enforced, which cannot, how funeral wishes fit in, and how to put your wishes where they will hold.

## What makes something binding after you die?

Only a few things, and the [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers the first one in depth. A valid will controls what passes under it. A trust controls what is in it. Beneficiary forms and the way an account is titled control those assets. Nothing else moves property: not a promise, not a conversation, not a sticky note.

That matters because the paperwork gets applied by strangers. A bank, a brokerage, a county recorder and a probate judge each ask for the right document. None of them will release money because three cousins remember what Dad said.

| What you did | Does it bind? | What actually happens |
|---|---|---|
| Signed and witnessed a will | Yes | The executor follows it under court supervision |
| Typed or handwritten note, signed by you only | Usually no | May be offered as evidence of intent |
| Note named inside the will, existing when you signed | Sometimes | Depends on state rules for lists and incorporated papers |
| Letter of instruction | No | Guides the executor, who chooses whether to follow it |
| Spoken wishes to relatives | No | Family may honor them out of loyalty |
| Funeral instructions in a signed directive | Often yes for burial or cremation | Depends on the state's disposition law |

## Do notes attached to a will count?

Sometimes, and the rules are fussy. A common example is a handwritten list of who gets which family items, clipped to the will. Several states let a will point to such a list, provided the list is signed or in your handwriting and fits within a dollar cap, while others do not recognize it. Even in states that allow it, the will has to describe the list well enough that nobody can mistake which paper is meant.

The safe habit is to have the attorney put the gifts in the will, or in a clause that lets the executor follow a written list. If you want the freedom to rearrange the list later without redoing the whole will, ask which approach your state supports. A list taped to the back of a dresser is only a request.

One problem is timing. Say Lena, 71, writes out her gift list in March and signs her will in June. The June will mentions the list and the list is clear. Now say she writes a revised list in September. The new list did not exist when the will was signed, so a court may not honor it. Many families discover this only after the death. The [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) page explains how to write gifts that do not depend on a loose sheet of paper.

## Does a handwritten letter ever work as a will?

In roughly half of states, a will written and signed entirely in your own hand can be valid even without witnesses. So a letter that says "this is my last will" and divides everything up may count in those states. The catch is proof. Someone must show the handwriting is yours, that you meant the letter as a will, and that it is the final version.

Letters that are chatty, mixed with news or unsigned are much weaker. A real example of the problem: a widower writes his son, "I want the boat to go to you, someday it will be yours." Was that a gift now, a hope, or a will? A judge must decide, and the son needs a lawyer to argue it. The [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) page covers the state-by-state differences.

## What about spoken wishes and deathbed promises?

Spoken wishes have no power over property in nearly every state, with narrow exceptions for some military and sailor situations that you can read about in [oral, military, statutory and international wills](/learn/wills/oral-military-statutory-and-international-wills). For everyone else, the conversation in the hospital room is just that.

Courts also treat last-minute statements with care, because they are a favorite ground for claims of pressure. If one child is present for the final bedside promise and another is not, the absent child will wonder. The law gives these claims a name, [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence), and it is a reason to make changes while you are well, with someone neutral in the room. The [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) page explains how those disputes are argued.

## Can a will control your funeral?

It can express your wishes, but a will is a poor tool for it, and the law often hands the decision to someone you did not choose. The will may not be found for days, and the funeral may be over by the time it is read. Many states give authority over burial and cremation to a spouse first, then adult children, then other relatives, unless you have signed a written appointment naming someone.

Illinois has a statute on this subject, covered in [the Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act). The [New Jersey funeral and burial decisions](/learn/new-jersey/who-controls-funeral-and-burial-decisions-in-new-jersey) page does the same for that state. In both, the practical answer is the same: name the person who decides, put your preferences in writing, and make sure they know where the paper is.

For a family that expects a disagreement, the [who decides funeral arrangements when family disagrees](/learn/after-a-death/who-decides-funeral-arrangements-when-family-disagrees) page describes what happens when no appointment exists. You can also plan ahead using [plan your own funeral in advance](/learn/basics/plan-your-own-funeral-in-advance).

## How do you make your wishes stick?

Follow this order, which matches how the law actually ranks these documents.

1. **List what matters most.** Property, a guardian for children, who serves as executor, a family business, keepsakes, pets, and your funeral. Be honest about which ones are likely to cause a fight.
2. **Put every gift of value in the will or trust.** If it matters enough to say aloud, it matters enough to sign. Name the person, name the item, and name a backup.
3. **Handle personal items on purpose.** Ask your attorney whether a signed list incorporated into the will is possible in your state, or whether a short, specific gift clause works better.
4. **Appoint someone for the funeral.** Use the form your state recognizes, and give a copy to that person and to your doctor.
5. **Write a letter of instruction for everything else.** Keep it separate from the will, short and kind. The [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) page shows what to include.
6. **Tell your family the plan in outline.** You do not need to share dollar amounts. Saying "Mark is my executor and the cabin goes to the girls" prevents a surprise, and the [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) page describes the matters that need other documents.
7. **Review it when life changes.** A letter written before a divorce or a new baby can contradict the plan you now have.

## What if the letter and the will disagree?

The will wins. A letter of instruction that says "sell the house and split it three ways" cannot override a will that leaves the house to one child. The executor follows the will and may ignore the letter, which can leave the family feeling betrayed by a document you wrote with love.

To avoid that, write the letter after the will is done and read the two side by side. If the letter reveals a wish the will does not carry out, go back and change the will. The [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) page explains how a revision is signed.
