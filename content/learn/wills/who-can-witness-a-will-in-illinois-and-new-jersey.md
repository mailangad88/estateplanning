You are about to sign your will and someone has to watch. Maybe the attorney's office will supply people, maybe you are doing this at a kitchen table, and either way you want to know whether a cousin, a neighbor or your own daughter is allowed to sign as a witness.

Illinois and New Jersey both want two witnesses. The rules about who counts are looser than most people fear, but one mistake, a witness who is also a beneficiary, can undo part of what you meant to leave. This page covers who to ask, who to avoid, and how to run the signing so the will holds up when it is needed. For the full signing rules, see our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

## What does a witness actually do?

A witness is there to prove, years later, that the will was signed by the person it says was signed by, and that the person seemed to know what they were doing. That is the whole job. The witness does not approve the terms, vouch for fairness or promise to care for anyone.

In Illinois the will must be attested in your presence by two or more credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3). In New Jersey at least two people must sign as witnesses within a reasonable time after seeing you sign or hearing you acknowledge your signature (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2). Our state pages on [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) and [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) cover the full statutes.

Neither state requires a notary to make a will valid. A notary matters for the optional affidavit that lets the court skip calling the witnesses, which we cover below.

## Who is a safe choice and who is a risky one?

The test is simple: does this person gain anything if the will is accepted? If so, ask someone else.

| Person | Safe as a witness? | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Friend or neighbor who inherits nothing | Yes | No stake in the outcome |
| Employee of the law firm that drafted the will | Yes | Routine, and Illinois protects the firm's role as executor or trustee |
| Your adult child who is a beneficiary | No | Gift can be voided in Illinois, and invites a challenge in New Jersey |
| Your spouse, if named in the will | No | Same problem, and in Illinois the spouse's gift is at risk too |
| The person you named executor | Usually fine, but not ideal | Often an heir as well |
| A person with dementia or serious confusion | No | Cannot reliably testify about the signing |
| Someone about to emigrate or very elderly | Poor | Hard to reach when the will is filed |

A bank teller you see every week and a retired colleague half your age are typical good picks. Younger is better. A witness who is 78 when you are 74 may not be available when the will is filed.

## What happens if a beneficiary witnesses the will in Illinois?

Illinois takes a firm line. If a person who witnessed the will, or that person's spouse, receives a gift under it, the gift is void unless the will was also properly attested by enough other witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-6). The beneficiary can still receive up to what they would have inherited without the will.

Here is an example. Walter, a widower in Naperville, leaves $100,000 to his son Marco and splits the rest among two other children. Marco and a neighbor sign as the two witnesses. Only one witness is disinterested, so Marco's gift is void. If Marco's share without the will would have been $40,000, he gets $40,000, not $100,000. Walter's other children may then receive more than he planned. The fix would have cost nothing: a third signature from a coworker at the bank.

This is why attorneys in Illinois often have three witnesses sign when family is in the room, and why we ask beneficiaries to wait outside during the signing.

## What about New Jersey?

New Jersey is gentler on paper. The statute governing interested witnesses says that signing a will as an interested witness does not by itself invalidate the will or any provision of it (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-12). Do not read that as permission.

The problem is practical. If one child witnesses the will and another child contests it, the signing becomes the first thing a lawyer asks about. The witness is also the person least able to testify neutrally. A will that could have been uncontroversial now opens a credibility fight. Ask two outsiders and the issue disappears.

## How should you run the signing?

Signing day is a short procedure. Following it in order is what makes a will boring in court, which is exactly what you want.

1. Gather two witnesses, preferably three if any beneficiary is in the house, plus a notary if you want a self-proving affidavit.
2. Say aloud that the document is your will and that you are signing it freely.
3. Sign in front of the witnesses, or confirm to them that the signature on the page is yours.
4. Each witness signs while you are there, using the name they use for banking, and prints name and address.
5. If a notary is present, you and the witnesses sign the affidavit before the notary at the same sitting. Our article on the [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) explains why this saves trouble.
6. Keep the original in a safe place and tell your executor where it is. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

## Can witnesses sign over video or remotely?

Sometimes. Illinois has an Electronic Wills and Remote Witnesses Act with extra steps, including that each witness be in the United States and the signature pages be joined within 10 business days. Remote signing is easy to get wrong, so we usually recommend paper for a simple will. If an electronic will appeals to you, read [electronic wills](/learn/wills/electronic-wills-and-wills-made-on-a-phone-or-in-an-email) first.

## What mistakes do we see most often?

Three come up again and again. The first is the family signing party, where a daughter, a son-in-law and a grandchild all sign because they happen to be in the room. The second is the witness who signs a day later at home, away from the testator. The third is a will that never had a printed name or address beside each witness signature, so nobody can find the witness when the court asks.

A fourth deserves a mention because it is quiet: a will signed with witnesses who never knew what the document was. They do not need to read it, but they should hear you call it your will. Witnesses who later say, "I just signed where they told me," are not helpful in a hearing.

Good habits are cheap. Write each witness's full name, address and phone on a separate sheet you keep with the will. Ask the same two people to attend any codicil if you can. And store the original with the other papers described in our [wills guide](/learn/wills) so the executor can find it.

## What if you already used a beneficiary as a witness?

Do not tear up the will. Have it reviewed. In Illinois, a simple fix is often to sign a new will, or confirm the existing one with a properly witnessed codicil that uses two new witnesses. In New Jersey, a quick re-execution still removes the question. Our guide on [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) walks through the choices, and the same fix applies when a trust signing is done carelessly, as in [trust signing, notary and witnesses](/learn/trusts/trust-signing-notary-witnesses-and-where-to-keep-the-documents).
