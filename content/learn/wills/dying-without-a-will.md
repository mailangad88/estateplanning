Someone you love may have died without leaving a will, or you may be wondering what would happen to your own family if you did. Either way, the answer is the same: the law steps in and writes a will for you. It is not a will you would have picked.

This page explains how that default system, called intestate succession, works. You will see who inherits in common family situations, who is left out, what happens to your children, and how much of your property the rules actually reach.

## What does it mean to die intestate?

To die intestate means to die without a valid will, or with a will that does not cover everything you owned. Every state has an intestacy statute that fills the gap. The statute lists relatives in a fixed order, and your probate estate is divided among them according to formulas.

Intestacy can be total or partial. If your will is thrown out, or you never signed one that meets your state's [requirements for a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will), the whole estate passes by statute. If your will leaves your house to your daughter but says nothing about the rest, the rest passes by statute. Lawyers call that partial intestacy, and it happens more often than people expect, especially with older do-it-yourself documents.

## Who inherits under intestate succession?

Your [heirs](/glossary/heir) are the relatives the statute names. The exact order differs by state, but the pattern is similar nearly everywhere.

| Your situation at death | Typical result in many states |
|---|---|
| Married, all children are shared with your spouse | Spouse takes everything or nearly everything |
| Married, you have children from another relationship | Spouse takes a set amount plus a share, children take the rest |
| Married, no children | Spouse takes all, or shares with your surviving parents |
| Unmarried, with children | Children share equally |
| Unmarried, no children | Parents, then siblings and their descendants |
| No spouse, children, parents or siblings | Grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins, in a fixed order |
| No living relatives found | The state (escheat) |

These are patterns, not promises. States set their own fractions, dollar thresholds and definitions. In the Uniform Probate Code approach used in several states, for example, a spouse's share depends on whether either of you has children from outside the marriage. Other states split between spouse and children even when all the children are shared. Check the guide for your own state, such as [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) or [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), or ask an attorney, before assuming a result.

### How are children and grandchildren treated?

Children inherit equally, including adopted children. Children born outside marriage usually inherit too once parentage is established. Stepchildren generally do not, unless you legally adopted them.

If one of your children died before you, that child's own children typically step into the parent's place. The statute uses a method called [per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes) or a close variation, so a grandchild splits the deceased parent's share rather than getting an equal share with aunts and uncles. States differ in the details, which matters in large families.

### What about community property states?

In [community property](/glossary/community-property) states, property earned during the marriage is treated as owned half by each spouse. When one spouse dies without a will, the survivor already owns their half and usually inherits the deceased spouse's half too if all children are shared. The rules change if there are children from a prior relationship.

## Who is left out?

The surprise for many families is who receives nothing.

- **An unmarried partner.** Maria and Dev lived together for 22 years and never married. When Dev died without a will, his siblings inherited his half of the house and his savings account. Maria had no statutory claim.
- **Stepchildren.** A stepchild raised by you is not a legal heir without adoption.
- **Friends, caregivers and charities.** Intestacy has no way to reward them.
- **A spouse you were separated from but never divorced.** Until a divorce is final, most states still treat the person as your spouse.
- **A child you meant to treat differently.** Intestacy splits equally. It cannot account for a child who already received help, a child with special needs, or a child you were estranged from.

A will can fix every one of these. Without one, you also cannot leave specific items, such as a ring or a boat, to a specific person.

## Who runs the estate when there is no will?

Without a will there is no named [executor](/glossary/executor). Instead the probate court appoints an [administrator](/glossary/administrator), often called a personal administrator or administrator of the estate. State law ranks who has priority to serve, usually the surviving spouse first, then adult children, then other relatives. The person must apply to the court, take an oath and, in many states, buy a surety bond to protect the estate.

That bond is a cost a will can waive. If family members disagree about who should serve, the dispute can add months. If you would like to read about what the job involves, see [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor), since the same qualities matter in an administrator.

## What happens to minor children?

Intestacy handles money and care separately, and neither is handled well. A court will appoint a [guardian](/glossary/guardian) for a minor child if no surviving parent can care for them, and without your nomination, relatives may disagree and the judge decides. You can read more in [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named).

For the money, a child's inheritance is usually held by a court-supervised conservator until age 18, then paid out in full. Few parents want a teenager to receive a large sum outright on a birthday. A will can create a trust that delays and stages payment. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) for the options.

## Which assets does intestacy actually control?

Only your probate estate. Intestate succession controls property titled in your name alone with no beneficiary or survivorship feature. Common examples are a house, a car, and bank accounts without a payable-on-death designation.

Other assets pass outside the will, and outside intestacy, no matter what the statute says:

- Retirement accounts and life insurance go to the named beneficiary.
- Jointly owned property with right of survivorship goes to the surviving owner.
- Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts go to the named person.
- Assets held in a living trust follow the trust terms.

That means the effect of intestacy depends on how your accounts are set up. For a deeper look at the categories, see [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets), and the companion article [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).

## What does the process look like after an intestate death?

Here is the general sequence. Details and timing vary by state.

1. **Locate any will.** Check the home, the safe, the attorney's office and the court. Even a missing or unsigned document is worth finding. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
2. **File a petition** in the probate court of the county where the person lived, asking to open an intestate estate.
3. **Notify heirs.** The court requires notice to all known relatives who could inherit.
4. **Appoint the administrator,** who receives court papers authorizing them to act, often called letters of administration.
5. **Inventory assets and pay debts.** The administrator collects property, pays final bills and taxes, and handles creditors.
6. **Prove heirship.** In some states a court holds a hearing or an affidavit establishes who the heirs are.
7. **Distribute what remains** according to the statute, then close the estate.

The overall process is described in [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate). Smaller estates may qualify for a simplified procedure that skips some of these steps.

## Is a will worth it if my plan seems simple?

Usually yes. A short will lets you name your own beneficiaries, choose your own executor, pick a guardian for your children, and waive a surety bond. It costs a modest amount, while an intestate estate can cost far more in court time and family friction.

A will is also only one tool. If you own a home and want it to pass without probate, or you have a blended family, a trust-based plan may fit better. The [wills guide](/learn/wills) lays out the options, and [dying without an estate plan](/learn/basics/common-estate-planning-mistakes) is one of the mistakes we see most.

If you are in the position of settling an intestate estate right now, take the steps in order and do not rush distributions. The court process protects heirs and creditors alike.
