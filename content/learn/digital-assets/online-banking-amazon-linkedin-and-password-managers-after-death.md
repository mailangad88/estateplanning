Ask a family what they are worried about after a death and they will mention the big things: the house, the retirement account, the funeral. Then, a week later, they ask smaller questions. Can anyone close Mom's Amazon account? What do we do about her LinkedIn profile? Why is the bank still sending her emails? This page goes through the everyday accounts one at a time. Each section says what happens by default and what the family should do first. For the full list of digital property and how it fits into planning, see our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## How do these accounts compare?

| Account | What happens by default | First move for the family |
|---|---|---|
| Online banking | Money goes to the beneficiary, joint owner or estate. The login stays unused. | Notify the bank, give it the death certificate and letters |
| Amazon | No transfer. Digital media is usually a license. | Close the account, cancel Prime, remove cards |
| LinkedIn | Profile stays until removed | Submit a report of death with proof |
| TikTok | Account stays until reported | Report the death, ask for removal |
| X (Twitter) | Inactive accounts can linger | Representative asks for deactivation |
| Password manager | Locked | Emergency access, or the master password if the family has it |

Policies change, so check each company's current deceased-user page. The pattern, though, has held for years: companies protect the account holder's privacy, and they respond to a death certificate and proof of authority.

## What happens to online banking?

Nothing about the login matters, and everything about the account title does. An online banking profile is just a door. The money behind it belongs to whoever the account paperwork says.

- **Joint account.** The surviving owner usually keeps the money and the account.
- **Payable-on-death account.** The named beneficiary claims it with a death certificate and ID.
- **Account in one name with no beneficiary.** The money is part of the probate estate and the executor claims it with letters. Our article on [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) walks through the steps.

The common mistake is for a family member to log in to the dead person's banking app to pay bills. It feels responsible. It can freeze the account, because the bank may flag activity after a death, and it can create confusion about who authorized a payment. If automatic payments are a worry, take a list of the recurring bills from the statements and tell the executor.

Alicia, 57, found out after her father's death that his rent, his phone and a gym membership came out of his account on the first of each month. She did not log in. She called the bank, gave them the death certificate, learned that the executor would be recognized once appointed, and then contacted the three companies by phone to stop the charges.

## What happens to an Amazon account?

Amazon is an account, not a piece of property that passes to the heirs. The account holds several different things.

- **Physical orders.** These are done, and they belong to the person who received them.
- **Digital purchases.** Kindle books, music and video are typically a license for the account holder. The license usually ends at death, and the family has no right to keep it, as our article on [subscriptions and purchased media after death](/learn/digital-assets/subscriptions-and-purchased-media-after-death) explains. A device that already has a book downloaded may keep it for a while.
- **Gift card balance and stored credit.** These may be real money. The executor can ask the company whether it can be refunded or applied.
- **Saved cards and addresses.** These are a risk. Delete or let the executor close the account.
- **Prime and other subscriptions.** Cancel them. A subscription charge that keeps hitting a credit card is an annoying cost that some families discover months later.

The executor should close the account, not use it. If the family wants a record of order history for tax or insurance purposes, download it first.

## What happens to LinkedIn?

A LinkedIn profile does no harm in its early days, but it keeps showing "congratulate Tom on his work anniversary" to old colleagues and can be used by scammers to impersonate someone. LinkedIn has a process to report a member's death and request removal of the profile. The family typically needs the person's name, a link to the profile, the date of death and proof such as an obituary. The family does not receive access to the account or its messages.

If the person ran a business page or a consulting practice, check who else is an administrator on that page before asking for the profile to be removed. The business may need to keep running.

## What happens to TikTok and X?

Social platforms generally offer a path to report a death and have the account removed or deactivated. Some allow memorial treatment. Almost none give a family member the login. Our article on [social media after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) compares those choices across the main services.

If the account earns money, as when a creator has a following and sponsorships, the income stream may be an asset, and the executor should be told. Our piece on [online businesses after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-business-etsy-shop-or-youtube-channel-after-death) covers that case. A family that wants to keep the account up as a tribute can ask about memorial options on platforms that provide them, and should decide before requesting removal because removal can't be undone.

## What happens to a password manager?

This is the one that matters most. A password manager's vault is a locked box with every other account's login inside. If the family has the master password, they can open it and see everything. If they do not, the vault is typically unrecoverable by design, and the company cannot reset it for them.

Many managers offer emergency access: the owner names a trusted person, who can request access and gets it after a waiting period unless the owner declines. Some also allow a printed recovery sheet. Our article on [passwords, password managers and two-factor authentication](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) explains how to set this up.

The master password and the recovery code deserve a place in the same location as the will, ideally with a lawyer or in a fire-safe box that the executor knows about. A vault that nobody can open is the most common reason a digital estate goes wrong.

## What should the executor do in what order?

1. **Money first.** Contact banks and card issuers with the death certificate and letters. Stop automatic payments.
2. **Mail and email.** Check the mail and the main email account for bills. This tells you which services exist.
3. **Subscriptions.** Cancel recurring charges through the card issuer if the service is hard to reach.
4. **Shopping and media accounts.** Close them after downloading any records you need.
5. **Professional and social accounts.** Report the death and ask for removal or a memorial.
6. **Credit bureaus.** Place a deceased flag so nobody opens new credit.

Our [online accounts checklist](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) turns this into a longer list, and the [who to notify](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) guide covers the offline side.

## How can you make your own accounts easier to handle?

Keep a short list of the accounts that matter, with no passwords on it. Turn on emergency access in your password manager. Name a legacy contact where the service offers one. Tell your executor where the list and the master password are. A simple [digital asset inventory](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use) puts it in one place, and the [plan finder](/plan-finder) can help you decide which legal documents should back it up.
