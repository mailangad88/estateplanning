Some people spend their lives wanting a few things to stay private: old messages, a journal in a notes app, photos from a rough year, a conversation with a friend. Dying does not change that wish. The question is whether the law, and the technology, will honor it.

The honest answer is that the law leans your way and the technology leans against you. This article covers where each applies and what you can do now. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## The short version

- Providers do not give message content to relatives on request. The default protects your privacy.
- That default does nothing against a family member who has your passcode or finds the phone unlocked.
- You can give permission, withhold it, or limit it to certain accounts in your will or other documents.
- Deleting things yourself is stronger than any instruction.
- A private note to your executor, saying what to delete and what to leave alone, removes the guesswork.

## What does the law protect by default?

A federal privacy statute from 1986 generally prevents an email or messaging provider from disclosing the content of a user's communications to others unless the user consented or a court ordered it. After death, most states layer a model law over that. It sorts what a fiduciary can get into tiers. We explain the framework in [what RUFADAA is](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained).

The key distinction is between the catalog and the content. The catalog tells who wrote to you and when. The content is what was said. Your executor can often get the first from a provider with a death certificate and court letters. The second needs your consent or a judge.

Think of Daniel, a widower of 71. He wrote hundreds of emails to an old friend over several difficult years. His son, who is his executor, can ask for the catalog and learn that those emails exist. Unless Daniel consented, his son cannot read them without convincing a probate judge it is necessary. If Daniel wanted his son to read them, he could say so in his will. If he does not, he need do nothing.

## Where does the protection fail?

Not at the law. At the screen.

1. **A shared or known passcode.** If your daughter knows your phone code, nothing stops her from opening it. The provider's rule applies to requests to the provider, not to someone holding the device.
2. **An unlocked or saved login.** A laptop that opens straight to your email, or a browser that auto-fills your passwords, puts everything within reach.
3. **A password manager entry shared in advance.** If you gave a family member emergency access, they can use it. That is its purpose.
4. **Copies in other people's hands.** The friend you messaged has the conversation too. Group chats, shared albums and forwarded emails live outside your control.
5. **Backups.** An old computer or external drive may hold material you thought you deleted.

None of that makes your family nosy. After a death, people reach for the phone because it holds the funeral contacts, the bank apps and the last texts. If you do not want them to look, do not leave the door open.

## Can you stop family from reading your messages? A step list

1. **Decide what you want private.** Be specific. "My journal app and my messages with Ellen" is easier to act on than "personal stuff."
2. **Delete what you are sure about.** Remove old threads, clear the journal, empty the cloud folder. Check backups.
3. **Separate the account family needs from the one they do not.** If your email holds both bills and private mail, consider opening a second address for the private material.
4. **Do not share the main passcode if privacy matters.** Use a manager's emergency access, which can be timed to open only after a waiting period, so your executor reaches finances but not necessarily the journal.
5. **Leave the consent language out, or narrow it.** Ask your attorney to draft your will so your executor has authority over the catalog and the accounts, but none over content, or only content from named services.
6. **Write a private instruction.** A sealed letter to the executor that says "Delete the notes app, close the second email account, and do not look" gives authority to act without opening anything.

If you do nothing, the likely result is delay and frustration for your family rather than a leak. If you take step 1 through 6, your wishes are the plan.

## Should you delete your accounts before you die?

Sometimes, but selectively. Deletion is the only certain way to make material unreadable. It is also permanent, and sometimes that hurts the people left behind.

A rough guide:

| Account type | Delete before death? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Dating apps, old forums, side email accounts | Usually yes | No estate value, privacy value only |
| Cloud photos you do not want seen | Delete those albums, not the account | The account may hold other things |
| Main email | Usually keep | Resets passwords, receives statements |
| Bank, brokerage, crypto | Never close without transferring | Money is attached |
| Social media | Your choice | Consider memorialization or a Legacy Contact instead |
| Business, domain, online store | Do not delete | These are assets; see the business articles |

If you are unsure, set the account to close on its own. Google's Inactive Account Manager can delete data after a period of inactivity, and some services have similar timers. Our guide to [digital legacy contacts](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) lists the main ones.

## Who owns what you post online?

You usually keep the copyright to original photos, writing and videos you upload. The platform gets a license to display them and can keep copies according to its terms. The account itself, meaning the login and the profile, is a contract with the company and generally does not transfer by will.

This matters for privacy because "I own it" does not mean "my family can claim it." A relative asking Instagram for your photos is not asking for a bank balance; the platform treats the request under its death policy. Our article on [social media and email after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) covers what each major service offers. For photos in particular, see [photos, cloud storage and iPhones](/learn/digital-assets/photos-and-iphones-after-death).

## What if you want some people to see more than others?

You can split it. Perhaps your spouse should have the financial accounts and your sister should have the photos, but neither should read the messages. A will can name different people for different assets, and a trust can hold certain digital property for a named person. It gets complicated fast if the sharing instructions conflict with a provider's own tool. If you named a Legacy Contact, for instance, that person gets what the company's tool allows, whatever your will says.

Keep the pieces consistent. Write down who gets what, set the tools to match, and have your attorney check the will language against them.

## What about your agent while you are still alive?

Privacy questions start before death. If you become incapacitated, an agent under your power of attorney may need to deal with your accounts, and the same consent rules apply. Under the model law in most states, an agent cannot read message content unless the power of attorney says so. That is a protection, and also a limit, because someone managing your affairs through a long illness may need to see an email from your insurer.

A sensible middle path is to allow the agent to access the catalog and financial accounts, and to say nothing about content. Our article on [power of attorney and digital accounts](/learn/digital-assets/power-of-attorney-and-digital-accounts) goes through the wording choices. The executor's side of the same question is in [what an executor does](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties), which is a useful read before you decide whom to trust with the key to your accounts.

Whichever way you decide, review it every year or so. Friends change, apps change, and a person you trusted at 50 may not be the person you want holding your inbox at 70. Our guide to [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists the triggers.
