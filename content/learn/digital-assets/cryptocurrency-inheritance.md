Cryptocurrency is unusual among assets. There is no bank to call and no statement in the mail. If you hold Bitcoin or another token yourself and the keys die with you, the coins can be gone for good. Some estimates suggest a meaningful share of all Bitcoin has already been lost this way.

If you own crypto, or you are the heir of someone who did, this article explains how it fits in an estate and how to avoid a loss. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## Who can inherit cryptocurrency?

Anyone you name can, in the same way as any other property. Crypto is personal property and passes under your will, your trust or, if you have neither, under the intestacy rules of your state. The legal question is easy. The practical question, whether the heir can reach it, is what causes trouble.

What matters is where the crypto is held:

| Where it is held | Who controls it | What heirs need |
|---|---|---|
| Exchange or broker (such as a regulated trading platform) | The company, on your behalf | Death certificate, proof of authority (letters from the court), account information |
| Custodial wallet app | The provider | Usually the same as an exchange |
| Hardware wallet | You | The device, its PIN, and the recovery seed phrase |
| Software wallet | You | The device or backup, password and seed phrase |
| Retirement account holding crypto | A custodian | Beneficiary claim as with any IRA |

Exchange accounts can be claimed, though slowly. Self-custody can be recovered only with the keys. Whatever mix you have, your plan should say which is which.

## What is a seed phrase, and how should it be protected?

A seed phrase is a list of 12 or 24 words that can rebuild your wallet on any device. It is the single most valuable piece of information in your crypto holdings. Anyone who gets it can take the funds, and if no one has it, nobody can.

Avoid these approaches:

- **In your will.** Wills are generally filed with the court and may become public, and they are not read until after you die.
- **In a photo or email.** Cloud accounts get hacked, and a lost password can bury the file.
- **In one place only.** A fire, flood or move can destroy the only copy.

Better options include a metal backup plate kept in a home safe or safe deposit box, a sealed letter held by your attorney, and a split arrangement where two trusted people hold parts that must be combined. Be careful with schemes that involve splitting a phrase into pieces, since a mistake can make it unreadable. Test the recovery process with a small amount before relying on it.

## How does the law treat crypto after death?

The executor or trustee is the legal owner of the estate's digital property for administration, and the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, adopted in most states, applies to the extent that crypto sits in an online account. It does not give anyone magical access to a self-custodied wallet. Our overview of [digital assets in an estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan) explains the framework.

Executors must treat crypto like any other asset: identify it, value it, protect it and report it. That includes looking at the person's tax records, bank statements and email for clues to exchanges and wallets, such as 1099 forms from platforms or deposits from exchanges.

Because the price can swing sharply, an executor may need to decide whether to hold or sell. A will or trust that says nothing about it leaves the fiduciary exposed to criticism, so clear instructions help. Some executors sell promptly into dollars once they have control, which reduces risk and simplifies division among heirs.

## What are the tax rules?

Inheriting crypto is not taxable income, and the heir receives a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis). If you inherit coins worth $50,000 on the date of death that the owner bought for $2,000, your basis is $50,000. The estate or trustee should record the date-of-death value, using a reasonable source such as an exchange price on that date, so that heirs can report any later gain or loss correctly.

The estate may owe federal estate tax only if total assets exceed $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and some states have lower thresholds. Our article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) provides more background. Crypto earned or sold after the death can create income that the estate or heir must report. A tax professional familiar with digital assets can be worth the fee.

## How do you plan for your own crypto?

The goal is to give your executor legal power and practical access without exposing the keys while you are alive. Follow these steps:

1. **Make an inventory.** Record each exchange, wallet type, approximate holdings and where recovery information is kept. Do not write the keys on the same sheet.
2. **Pick who will inherit and who will manage.** These may be different people. A technically careful friend can serve as co-fiduciary alongside a family beneficiary.
3. **Add language to your documents.** Your will, [revocable trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) and power of attorney should expressly cover digital assets and cryptocurrency and authorize your fiduciary to access, hold, sell or transfer them.
4. **Create a recovery plan.** Store seeds in two secure locations and make sure your executor knows how to find them, for example through an attorney, a password manager emergency feature or a safe deposit box.
5. **Leave instructions.** A short letter can explain which wallet is which, how to use a hardware device, and a warning about scams.
6. **Test it.** Have your executor or a trusted person walk through the recovery on a small test wallet.

For example, Omar, 45, holds Bitcoin on an exchange and in a hardware wallet. His will names his sister as executor and covers digital assets. His attorney holds a sealed letter with the location of two metal seed backups. His sister has a one-page guide on what to do first and a warning that anyone who asks for the phrase is probably a scammer. His plan is dull, and that is exactly the point.

## What should an heir do if the owner has died?

Act carefully and do not guess. Search paper and digital records for exchange statements and tax forms. Look for hardware devices, handwritten word lists or unusual metal plates. Avoid entering random passwords on exchanges, which can lock the account. Protect what you find and secure the devices. Then work through the exchange's deceased-account process with an attorney's help, since they require court-issued authority and may take weeks. If a hardware wallet is found without the seed, a reputable wallet recovery service may be able to help, but beware of anyone who contacts you offering to recover coins for an upfront fee, which is a common scam.
