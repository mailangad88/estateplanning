For many families the most valuable thing a person leaves is not money. It is twenty thousand photos on a phone, the last texts, the video of a wedding toast. They are also the hardest thing to reach, because a modern phone is built to keep strangers out, and it does not know that the stranger is a grieving daughter.

This article explains where those photos actually live, which doors open and which do not, and what to do in the first weeks. It is a practical companion to our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets), and to the broader checklist for [online accounts after a death](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist).

## Where do a person's photos actually live?

Usually in three places at once, and the answer decides how you proceed. Many people believe a photo is "on the phone" when it was uploaded to a cloud account years ago and the phone merely shows it.

| Location | What it holds | How family can reach it |
|---|---|---|
| The phone itself | Photos, texts, notes, app data | Passcode, or a court-ordered process with the maker |
| The phone company's cloud (such as iCloud) | Synced photos, backups, files | Legacy contact, or a court order |
| A separate photo or storage service (such as Google Photos, Dropbox, OneDrive) | Uploads, shared albums | That company's deceased-user process, or a sharing setting |
| A computer, hard drive or memory card | Older libraries | Physical access and any password |
| Albums shared by friends and family | Copies of the same moments | Ask the people who took them |

The last row is the one families skip. When access fails, the best recovery is often a cousin's shared album or a friend who still has the group chat.

## How does a family get into a locked iPhone?

There are four routes, from easiest to hardest.

1. **The passcode is known.** Some people tell their spouse or adult child. This is the simplest path and, honestly, the one most families use. Keep a note of what you looked at.
2. **A Legacy Contact was named.** Apple lets an owner name someone in advance. After a death, that person requests access with an access key and a death certificate. This opens much of the iCloud account, but Apple says it does not cover everything, such as purchased media and certain stored passwords. Our guide to [setting up a legacy contact](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) shows the steps.
3. **A court order.** If there is no legacy contact, the usual formal route is an order from a court that confirms the requester is the personal representative and the account holder has died, and that directs the company to release data. Companies tend to read these orders narrowly. Expect the order to need specific wording.
4. **Nothing works.** If the phone is locked, the backup is not reachable and no one has the passcode, the device contents may be permanently out of reach. That is by design.

Android and Google accounts follow the same logic. Google's Inactive Account Manager lets an owner name contacts who receive chosen data after a period of inactivity. Without it, a family generally needs to use Google's deceased-user process or get a court order. Policies change, so check each company's current instructions before you start.

## What should you do in the first weeks?

Protect what exists before you try to unlock anything. Time matters less than care here.

1. **Keep the phone charged and safe.** A dead battery can complicate a later unlock. Put it somewhere secure and do not factory reset it.
2. **Do not cancel the cloud plan or the phone line yet.** A cancelled plan can cut off the backup or the two-factor text messages needed to log into other accounts. Our [after-death checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) covers the order of tasks.
3. **Find out who the executor is** and whether letters have been issued. If not, hold off on formal requests. See [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).
4. **Look for a plan.** Check the will file, the safe, the password manager and any letter of instruction for a legacy contact or sharing setup.
5. **Make copies once you have lawful access.** Download photos to a drive, and make two copies.
6. **Tell friends and relatives** you are collecting photos, and ask them to send what they have.

A short story shows why. When Helen died at 79, her son Victor had her passcode but not her Apple account password. He used the phone to find what the estate needed, and he did not touch the settings. The executor, Helen's daughter Priya, got letters from the court and asked for iCloud access. It took some weeks. In the meantime Victor had already pulled the last three years of photos off the phone itself. Between the two routes, the family kept almost everything.

## What if the photos are in Google Photos or another storage service?

The same order applies, with one difference: there is usually no phone to start from, so the account itself is the target. Check first whether the owner turned on a sharing or inactivity setting. If so, a trusted contact may already have a link waiting. If not, look in the email account for storage receipts and "your storage is almost full" notices, which tell you which services were in use and who was billing.

Storage plans are paid monthly or yearly, and an unpaid plan can lead to a service reducing or removing content. That is another reason not to close the credit card used for those charges until the photos are safely copied. Keep the account open, download, confirm the files open on a second device, and only then cancel.

Large video libraries can take days to export, so start early and expect a few failed downloads. Retry rather than assume the file is lost.

## What are the legal limits?

Photos are property, but access is a matter of company terms and state law. Most states have adopted the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, which lets a person control what a fiduciary may see. In general, an executor can get a catalogue of communications but needs specific consent, or a court order, to see content. Our explainer on [RUFADAA](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained) goes into the layers.

Two points surprise people. First, an account's terms of service often say the account is personal and cannot be transferred, so a company can refuse even a polite request. Second, a will cannot override a company's own tool: if you named an Apple Legacy Contact and your will says something different, the company will follow its own tool. Make sure the two agree.

## How does an owner prepare ahead?

You can fix most of this in a single afternoon, and it is some of the kindest planning you can do for your family.

- **Pick a legacy contact** on your phone and your main cloud account, and tell the person.
- **Back up photos to a place family can reach**, such as a shared album, a drive in a fireproof box or a second account both of you can open.
- **Record where it all is** in a letter of instruction, without writing the passwords on the same page.
- **Name someone to manage digital assets** in your will, as described in [what a digital executor does](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor).
- **Tell your executor which photos you want kept, shared or deleted.** This is the one that is most often forgotten and most often wished for.

Plain advice on passwords is covered in [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access).
