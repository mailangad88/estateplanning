Joan, 71, has read on a Kindle for fifteen years. She has about 900 books in her library, a handful of movies bought through a streaming store, and a music collection she built in the early days of digital downloads. Her daughter asks one afternoon whether any of it can be left to her. Joan assumes the answer is yes, because she paid for every title.

For most of it, the answer is no, and the reason is not what people expect. This article covers what a digital library really is, what happens to subscriptions when the account holder dies, how to stop recurring charges, what you can pass on, and what to do with loyalty points.

## The short version

- Most purchased digital media is licensed, not owned.
- Terms of service usually bar transferring an account, and a will does not override them.
- Subscriptions keep billing until someone cancels them.
- Downloaded files without copy protection are yours to leave.
- Points and miles depend on the program, and some can be moved after a death.

## Do you own what you bought?

Usually you own a right to use it. When you press "buy" on an e-book or a movie, the store sells you a license under its terms of service, and those terms commonly describe the content as personal to the account holder. The license generally cannot be sold, lent outside narrow family-sharing settings, or passed on at death.

That is different from a physical book. A paperback or a DVD is property. You can resell it, give it away or leave it in a will. A downloaded MP3 or a PDF with no copy protection sits in between: the file is yours, but whether the license that came with it allows you to hand it on is a question of the original terms. Most families never test this, because the practical question is whether anyone will stop them. The honest advice is to plan around the restriction, not around the chance of being left alone.

### What about music you ripped from CDs?

Files you created yourself from discs you own, photographs you took, and documents you wrote are ordinary property. They pass under your will or trust like any personal belongings. Keep them on a drive, not only on a streaming service, so your family has something to find.

## What happens to subscriptions when someone dies?

A subscription is a contract for continued service, and it ends when billing stops or when the company learns of the death and closes the account. The company does not know unless someone tells it.

| Type of account | What usually happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Streaming video and music | Billing continues until cancelled | Cancel online or by phone, then close the account |
| E-book and audiobook library | Content stays tied to the account until it is closed | Download anything allowed, then close |
| Software subscriptions | Charges continue, access ends when unpaid | Cancel, export documents first |
| Gaming platforms | Library tied to account | Ask support about a deceased member, keep in mind the terms |
| Meal kits, magazines, box clubs | Shipments continue | Cancel and ask for refunds of unused periods |
| Warehouse or retail memberships | Annual fee already paid | Ask about a partial refund |
| Cloud storage | Plan ends when unpaid, data may be deleted | See [photos and iPhones after death](/learn/digital-assets/photos-and-iphones-after-death) before cancelling |

Cloud storage deserves caution. Cancel it too early and you may lose the only copy of photos, and some services delete data after a period of nonpayment.

## How do you find every recurring charge?

Start with money, not memory. Your loved one's accounts leave a trail.

1. **Collect twelve months of statements.** Credit card, debit card and bank. A yearly subscription may show up only once.
2. **Highlight recurring lines.** Look at small amounts that repeat monthly or yearly, and names you do not recognize.
3. **Check payment apps.** PayPal and similar apps often hold stored subscriptions. Our article on [PayPal, Venmo and Cash App after death](/learn/digital-assets/paypal-venmo-and-cash-app-after-death) covers them.
4. **Search the email for "receipt," "renewal" and "your subscription."** If you have lawful access, that finds what statements miss. Whether the executor may read email is covered in [can an executor access email](/learn/digital-assets/can-an-executor-access-email).
5. **Look at the phone's subscription list.** Both major phone platforms show active subscriptions in settings, if you can lawfully open the device.
6. **Cancel in writing where possible**, and keep the confirmation.

The [online accounts checklist](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) places this step in the larger order of tasks. Do not close the credit card until you have caught the recurring charges, or the company will send collection letters to the estate.

## Who has the authority to cancel?

The executor, once appointed, is the person with authority to manage the estate's contracts, including cancelling a subscription. A surviving spouse or family member can often cancel a simple subscription with a death certificate and no court order. For anything the company resists, the executor's letters are the tool. See [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) and [what a digital executor does](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor).

Companies differ in how they treat a death. Some have a form and a department. Others handle it through the ordinary support line. The names change, so check the company's current instructions.

## What about loyalty points, miles and gift cards?

These sit in a gray area. Airline miles are generally a benefit under program rules, not property, and the rules differ widely. Some programs allow a transfer to an heir within a set window and ask for a death certificate. Others close the account and the points disappear. Hotel programs and credit card rewards vary as well.

Gift cards and store credit are usually worth real money and may be redeemable by the family. A balance on an account with a card on file is often easiest to use up while the person is alive. If you are the owner, make a list of the larger balances and where the cards or numbers are kept.

Cash-back balances and credit card points are usually forfeited when the account closes, so redeem them before the final statement, if the executor can do so.

## What can you do now to leave a usable library?

You cannot change the terms, but you can reduce the loss.

- **Keep your own copies of what you can.** Photos, documents and unprotected music files belong on a drive that your family can find.
- **Use family sharing carefully.** Some platforms let you share a library with family members who stay on the plan. Read the terms, since the benefit may end when the organizer dies.
- **Write down the list.** A page that names your main accounts, subscriptions and where the payment comes from saves months of work. The [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets) has an inventory method.
- **Decide what matters.** A library of 900 books may not matter to your children. A handful of family movies might. Spend your effort on the things that carry meaning.
- **Mention digital items in your will in general terms.** A will can give your executor authority to deal with digital property, which helps with cancelling and closing even though it cannot override a license. See [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).

## How state law changes this

Most states have adopted a law about fiduciary access to digital assets, usually a version of a uniform act, which lets an executor deal with certain online accounts. It does not rewrite the license terms. A statute that lets your executor manage an account does not mean the company has to hand over the content or let a child keep using a library. How the law handles company terms differs by state, and the rules for non-probate transfers do not apply to licenses.
