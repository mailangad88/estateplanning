A generation ago, an estate meant a house, bank statements in a drawer and a box of photographs. Today a person's life is spread across phones, cloud services, apps and sometimes digital currency. Much of it has real value, and much of it has meaning that cannot be priced. None of it is reachable unless someone has planned for it.

This guide explains what digital assets are, how the law treats them, and how to build a plan that is simple enough to finish. Four deeper articles follow each topic, and they are linked below.

## What is digital estate planning?

Digital estate planning is the part of an estate plan that covers online accounts, electronic files and digital money. It has two jobs: to give a trusted person legal authority to act, and to give them a practical way in. Most plans fail at the second.

The standard [digital assets](/glossary/digital-assets) fall into a handful of groups:

| Group | Examples | What your family needs |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | Online banking, brokerage, payment apps, cryptocurrency | Account list and authority to claim or manage |
| Personal | Photos, cloud files, email, messages | Access or legacy settings, and consent for content |
| Business | Domain names, websites, online stores, social media pages | Successor and access to admin accounts |
| Licensed or rewards | Streaming, e-books, loyalty points | Usually little; read terms |
| Keys | Password manager, phone, email, authenticator app | Recovery methods |

## What does the law say?

Most states have adopted a uniform law, the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, that sets rules for fiduciary access to electronic accounts. It gives priority first to any online tool the company provides, such as Google's Inactive Account Manager. Then it looks at your will, trust or power of attorney. Finally it falls back on the company's terms of service.

The law distinguishes the catalog of your communications, which a fiduciary can often obtain, from the content of messages, which usually requires your consent. Federal privacy law adds another layer, and the details vary from state to state. The practical takeaway is simple: say what you want, in the company's tool and in your documents. We explain this in [how to include digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).

## Why do passwords and access matter so much?

Legal authority does not unlock a phone. A fiduciary may have every right to your accounts and still be locked out by a passcode, a two-factor code or a missing recovery key. Planning for access means choosing a password manager, setting up emergency access, printing backup codes and recording where each key lives.

Do not put passwords in your will. A will may become public when filed, and no one reads it until after you have died. A sealed letter or an emergency access feature is safer. Our article on [passwords, password managers and two-factor authentication](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) walks through setup step by step, including how to keep access working if you become incapacitated and need an agent under a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## What about social media and email?

Each platform has its own policy. Facebook and Instagram offer memorialization and legacy contacts. Google lets you decide in advance whether to share or delete data after a period of inactivity. Apple has a Legacy Contact system. Without one of these, families often face refusals or long waits, and private messages are generally not released.

Email deserves special attention because it receives password reset links for almost everything else. If your family can reach your email, they can often recover other accounts. We cover provider by provider in [what happens to social media and email accounts when you die](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death), including what families should and should not do in the first weeks. If you are the family member dealing with accounts now, see our [checklist for what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

## What about cryptocurrency?

Cryptocurrency is the digital asset where mistakes cost the most. Coins on a regulated exchange can usually be claimed through the company's process, with a death certificate and court authority. Coins in a self-custody wallet cannot be recovered without the private key or seed phrase, and no court can reset them.

That makes crypto planning a balance. Your heirs need access, but you do not want to expose the keys while you are alive. Good plans use secure backups, split custody or an attorney-held sealed letter, plus clear instructions in your documents. Heirs generally receive a step-up in basis to the date-of-death value. Our guide to [cryptocurrency and estate planning](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance) covers the options, taxes and the steps an heir should take.

## Which legal documents should mention digital assets?

Your core documents should each play a part:

- **Will:** names your executor and gives them authority over digital property after death.
- **Revocable living trust:** can hold digital property that is owned outright, such as domain names, and lets a successor trustee manage it.
- **Durable power of attorney:** gives your agent authority while you are alive, which matters if you are hospitalized or incapacitated. See [durable financial powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
- **Healthcare documents:** these do not cover accounts, but a HIPAA authorization can help someone obtain records held in online patient portals.

To see all the pieces and where each fits, read the [basics of estate planning](/learn/basics).

## How do you get started?

You can make real progress in a single afternoon. Follow this order:

1. **Make an inventory** of accounts, devices and subscriptions. Record locations and purposes, not passwords.
2. **Secure the keys.** Use a password manager, print backup codes and set up emergency access.
3. **Use the legacy tools** at Apple, Google and Meta, and name your contacts.
4. **Back up what matters.** Copy photos and key documents to a drive or service your family knows about.
5. **Choose a digital fiduciary** and an alternate, and tell them.
6. **Update your legal documents** to include digital assets authority and consent language.
7. **Review yearly,** or after changing phones, accounts or providers.

## What if you are dealing with a loved one's accounts now?

If someone you love has died and you are looking at a locked phone or an email full of unknown accounts, slow down. Secure the devices, do not guess passwords, and start with the person's email and financial statements to see what exists. A court-appointed executor has stronger authority than a family member who simply has the password. Our guide to [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) explains the legal path, and the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) outlines the wider timeline.

## What are the most common mistakes?

- Assuming the family can simply log in
- Writing passwords in a will
- Relying on two-factor codes sent to a single phone
- Forgetting the recovery email or phone number
- Leaving cryptocurrency keys in one place, or nowhere
- Never updating the inventory

## How should families think about photos and memories?

Photos and messages are often the most valued digital assets, and they are the easiest to lose. Cloud photo libraries are tied to one account, one payment method and one set of terms. If the card expires and the account closes, years of pictures can disappear. Phones break, and most people have never exported a single album.

A simple habit solves most of this. Once or twice a year, download your photo library and important documents to an external drive or a second service, label it clearly and tell your family where it is. Printing a handful of favorite photos is also a gift to the people who will have to sort through your things. If you use a service that offers a legacy contact, set it up, because it gives your family a legitimate path to the files that no passcode can.

## What about online businesses and income?

If you earn money online, such as an online store, a blog, a channel with advertising revenue, a domain portfolio or freelance platform accounts, the plan needs to go further than family photos. The questions are who can take over, who owns the domain and hosting, who has the administrator login for each platform and where the payments go.

Domain names are property, and they can be held in your name, your company's name or a trust. If the registration email is your personal address and no one else can reach it, the domain can lapse and the business vanish. List renewal dates, registrars and admin contacts. If the business is an LLC or corporation, its operating agreement and succession plan should cover who is authorized to act. Our cluster on [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners) goes deeper on succession and continuity.

## How do digital assets fit with the rest of your plan?

Digital assets are not a separate plan. They sit inside the same documents as everything else, and the same fundamentals apply. Choose people who are trustworthy, name backups, keep the plan current and tell the people involved. If you are still deciding what an estate plan should include, our [wills guide](/learn/wills) and [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) explain the main building blocks, and the [basics guide](/learn/basics) shows how they fit together.

It also helps to think in terms of three time frames. If you are alive and well, your plan is about convenience and security. If you are incapacitated, it is about whether your agent can pay bills, keep accounts open and reach medical portals. After death, it is about who is in charge and what they can reach. A plan that works in all three is better than one that works only in the last.
