After a death, the house and the bank accounts get most of the attention. The phone on the nightstand, the cloud photos and the dozen small accounts that charge a card every month get left for later. They deserve a little attention early, because some cost money while they sit and some can be lost for good.

This article explains what generally happens to digital assets when someone dies, then gives a working checklist for a family and a shorter one for you to prepare ahead. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## The short version

- Digital property is part of the estate when it has value or is owned, and a license or an account may end by the company's terms.
- The company's rules, the person's tools and the will decide access, in that general order. The law behind this is explained in [what RUFADAA is](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained).
- Money-related accounts, such as exchanges, payment apps and online brokerages, are handled like other financial accounts through the executor.
- Photos, email and social media follow each company's deceased-user process.
- Many things are only licensed, not owned: e-books, digital movies and some game purchases usually end with the account.

## What are the first steps in the first week?

1. **Secure devices.** Keep the phone, tablet and computer somewhere safe, powered down if possible. Do not wipe, trade in or give them away. They hold two-factor codes and saved logins.
2. **Keep the phone line on.** Verification texts often arrive there.
3. **Tell the bank and card companies** and stop automatic payments that no longer make sense, but keep utilities running.
4. **Protect identity.** Ask the credit bureaus to flag the file as deceased, and watch statements for charges you do not recognize.
5. **Find the executor's authority.** Companies will ask for proof, so the executor should start the court appointment in the normal timeline described in our [week-by-week checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).
6. **Look for instructions.** A letter of instruction, a note in the will, a password manager with emergency access, or a legacy contact setting might exist.

## How should you sort the accounts?

Grouping accounts by purpose makes the work manageable. Each group has a different priority.

| Group | Examples | What to do first |
|---|---|---|
| Money | Bank apps, payment apps, online brokerage, crypto exchange, PayPal balance | Notify the institution, show letters, follow its estate process |
| Bills and subscriptions | Streaming, software, gym, meal kits, cloud storage fees | Cancel or transfer; check card statements for what recurs |
| Communication | Email, text archives, messaging apps | Preserve first; follow the provider's process, as in [can an executor access email](/learn/digital-assets/can-an-executor-access-email) |
| Memories | Photos, videos, documents in the cloud | Download before closing; use legacy tools if set |
| Social media | Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, X | Memorialize or remove; see [social media after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) |
| Business and income | Domain names, online store, creator payouts, royalties | Transfer or sell; these may be probate assets |
| Rewards | Airline miles, hotel points, gift cards | Ask about transfer rules early |

## How do you find the accounts?

Start with places that list them for you. Credit card and bank statements show recurring charges. The phone's home screen shows the apps the person used. An email search for words like "receipt," "welcome," "statement" and "password reset" turns up accounts, though you should do it only if you have the authority to read the mail. Tax forms, such as a 1099 from a payment platform or exchange, point to income accounts. Paper mail still delivers statements for many of them.

Make a running list as you go, with the company, the account name, what it is for, the date you contacted them and the answer. A plain spreadsheet works. You will refer back to it when you account to the beneficiaries, which is part of the [executor's duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

## What are the next 30 to 90 days?

1. **Contact each financial account** with the death certificate and the court letters. Ask for the balance as of the date of death, because you need it for the estate inventory.
2. **Download what you want to keep** from photo, cloud and email accounts before any account is closed, and keep a second copy.
3. **Memorialize or delete** social media according to the person's wishes and the family's.
4. **Cancel subscriptions** after confirming no one wants them, and ask for refunds of unused prepaid periods.
5. **Transfer assets of value,** such as a domain name or an online store, to the heir or put them up for sale.
6. **Switch the contact details** on bills and accounts you are keeping so reset links no longer go to the old email.
7. **Close the old email and phone last,** when nothing important points to them.

## What mistakes cause the most harm?

- **Wiping a phone too early.** The authenticator app and saved passwords go with it.
- **Closing the email first.** Every reset link then dies, and the other accounts can become unrecoverable.
- **Using the person's login.** It may violate the company's rules, and it can trigger security locks.
- **Ignoring small recurring charges.** A few of them add up, and the estate keeps paying for months.
- **Missing the valuable items.** Crypto, domain names and online income are easy to overlook if no one searches for them.

## What about purchases that seem like property?

Many people assume the music, films and e-books on an account belong to the estate. Usually they do not. A digital purchase is typically a license to use the item, tied to the account holder and not transferable by will. A shared family library or a physical copy is the exception. Games and in-app currency work the same way. Before you pay to keep an account alive for its library, read the terms, or ask the company what happens at death. Some will let a family member keep using an account for a time, and some end the license outright.

## An example

Carlos, 54, lost his mother Ines. She had a bank, a pension, a tablet full of photos, a Facebook page and a streaming plan. Carlos was named executor. In week one he took the tablet and phone, left the phone line active and asked the bank to freeze the account to new activity. His mother had set Apple's Legacy Contact to his name years before, so within a few weeks he got her photos with the access key. The Facebook page he memorialized. By going through three months of card statements, he found a subscription to a magazine and a cloud storage fee, which he canceled for a prorated refund. Nothing was lost, and he spent about as many evenings on it as he might have on a week of ordinary paperwork.

## What should you do now for your own accounts?

A short preparation list:

1. Write an inventory of the accounts that matter, with no passwords in it.
2. Use the legacy tools at Apple, Google and Meta. [This guide](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) shows how.
3. Set up a password manager with emergency access, as in [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access).
4. Name a [digital executor](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) and add digital language to your will.
5. Tell the person where it all is.
6. Check the plan each year.

## Do state rules change any of this?

The framework is similar across the country, because nearly every state adopted a model digital assets law. The details differ in court procedure and in how each state treats small estates, which can speed up the financial accounts. Look at your state's rules through our [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) or ask an attorney.
