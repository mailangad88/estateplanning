The phone is the first thing many families reach for after a death. It has the photos, the last messages, the contacts you need to call and the codes that open half the person's accounts. It is also usually locked, and the longer the family stays shut out, the more urgent it feels.

This article covers what you can do with a locked phone, why the phone number is more valuable than the handset, and what happens to texts, billing and logins. The broader subject is in our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## Who owns the phone and what is on it?

The handset belongs to the estate, like a watch or a laptop. If you are the executor or the heir, you may keep it, give it away or sell it. That ownership does not unlock the contents. The data inside is protected by the passcode, by encryption and by privacy laws that stop companies from disclosing a person's communications without proper authority.

Under the federal Stored Communications Act, companies that hold messages and account content are generally barred from sharing them unless the account holder consented or a court orders it. The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, adopted in most states, lets an executor reach a catalog of an account's activity more readily than its content. We describe both in [RUFADAA explained](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained). In short: the law gives families a path, but it runs through paperwork, not a password reset.

## What can you do if the phone is locked?

Start with the ordinary options before the legal ones.

1. **Check obvious places for the passcode.** A wallet card, a notebook near the desk, a password manager entry, a note given to a lawyer. Many people use a code that also opens a safe or a garage.
2. **Try a trusted device.** A tablet or a computer signed into the same account may already have messages and photos synced.
3. **Look for biometric access, carefully.** Some phones accept a face or fingerprint, but repeated failed tries can lock the phone for hours or erase it. Most people who try this succeed rarely, and each failure risks the data. Do not experiment.
4. **Ask the maker about its process.** Apple and Google both publish steps for families, and both usually require a court order that names the account. They do not break passcodes on request.
5. **Ask a court if the stakes are high.** If the phone holds business records, crypto keys or evidence the estate needs, an attorney can petition for an order directing the provider to cooperate.

Do not pay a stranger who advertises phone unlocking. Some are scams and some will wipe the device. If the phone matters, ask the estate's attorney before anyone tries again. For iPhone-specific steps, such as the Legacy Contact and recovery key, see our article on [photos and iPhones after death](/learn/digital-assets/photos-and-iphones-after-death).

## Why does the phone number matter more than the phone?

The number is a login. Banks, email providers, social networks, brokerage firms and shopping sites send one-time codes by text to confirm that you are you. If the number goes dead, the family can lose the easiest route to those accounts.

So before anyone calls the carrier to cancel:

- List every account that might use that number for codes or recovery. Check the email inbox for "your verification code" messages.
- Use the company's deceased-user process for each important account, not a text code.
- Ask the carrier whether the line can stay active for a few months, or be moved to a plan the executor controls. Some carriers will keep a line open on request, others will not.
- If you let the number go, assume it can be reassigned to a stranger who might receive codes for accounts that still use it. Update recovery details on any account you keep.

An authenticator app is trickier. Apps like Google Authenticator or Authy often live only on the phone. If the phone is gone and no backup was made, the codes are gone too, and the family must go through each company's account recovery. That is a reason to store backup codes with your will papers.

## What happens to text messages?

Texts live in three places: on the phone, in a cloud backup such as iCloud or Google, and in the carrier's records. Carriers generally keep records that a message was sent, with time and number, but they do not keep the words for long. A message app with end-to-end encryption, such as iMessage, WhatsApp or Signal, stores content on the devices only.

So if the phone is lost or wiped and no backup exists, the messages are probably gone. If the phone is unlocked, the family can read them. If a backup exists, it is protected by the account password and the same rules apply. A surviving spouse who wants only the last messages or voicemails should ask first whether a signed-in device exists.

There is also a privacy side that cuts the other way. Some people would be upset if their family read every message. If you feel that way, write it down in your plan; see [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) for the ways to limit access, and tell the person you name as digital executor what to read and what to leave.

## What about the bill, the device plan and shared accounts?

The wireless account is a contract like any other. After the account holder dies:

| Item | What to do |
|---|---|
| Monthly service | Contact the carrier with a death certificate; ask to suspend, transfer or close |
| Device payment plan | Ask for the payoff; the estate may owe it, or a family member can take over the plan |
| Family plan | The surviving members usually keep service; the account holder role must be reassigned |
| Cloud storage fee | Check Apple, Google or Microsoft billing; storage often bills separately |
| Joint Apple ID or shared family group | The organizer role matters; a family member may be able to remove the deceased from the group |
| Subscriptions billed to the phone bill | List and cancel each one |

Most carriers have a bereavement team, and a phone call is faster than a form. The executor should keep a record of every cancellation, since a missed autopay can run for months. Our [checklist for online accounts after a death](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) has a longer list, and [who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) covers the carrier among others.

## An example

Carlos, 63, died suddenly. His daughter Elena, the executor, finds his phone locked with a six-digit code. She does not know it. His email account has a recovery number, and his bank sends codes by text. Elena's first impulse is to cancel the line to stop the bill. Instead she calls the carrier, shows the death certificate and her letters from the court, and asks to keep the line for 90 days on her own plan. She contacts the bank's estate department, which works from the death certificate and not a code. With her lawyer she asks Apple to send her what it can from his account, and learns that a court order will be required. Meanwhile she finds Carlos's passcode on a card in his desk drawer under the safe's instructions.

None of that was glamorous. Keeping the number alive bought time, and the process, not a hack, solved the rest.

## How do you prepare your own phone?

You can fix this for your family in one afternoon.

- Write the passcode and the carrier's account PIN on a card and keep them with your will, or in a password manager with an emergency contact.
- Set up Apple's Legacy Contact or Google's Inactive Account Manager. See [digital legacy contacts](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) for steps.
- Save backup codes for your main accounts in the same place.
- Tell your executor which accounts rely on your number.
- Decide what you want your family to see, and say so in writing.
