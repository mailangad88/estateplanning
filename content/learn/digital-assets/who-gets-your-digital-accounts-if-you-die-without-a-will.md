Rafael, 34, was single, kept about $40,000 in Bitcoin on an exchange, ran a small hobby website, and had thousands of photos in a cloud account. He never wrote a will. When he died, his parents wanted to know three things: who inherits the coins, who gets the photos, and who is allowed to open anything at all. Each has a different answer, and none of them is "whoever knows the password." This page sorts out the rules when there is no will, which is a bigger gap online than most people expect. The wider topic is in our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What does dying without a will mean for digital property?

It means state law writes the will for you. This is called [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession). Each state lists who inherits in order, usually a spouse and children first, then parents, siblings and more distant relatives. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains the idea, and our [who-inherits tool](/tools/who-inherits) shows an example order.

For digital property, intestacy answers one question only: who owns what the estate owns. It does not say who may open the account, who can talk to the company, or who should keep the photos. Those depend on two other things, the company's terms and the state's digital-access law, which most states have adopted in some form. Illinois and New Jersey are among the states that have. Our explainer on [RUFADAA](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained) covers that law.

The third piece is a person with authority. With no will there is no named executor, so a court appoints an [administrator](/glossary/administrator), usually a spouse or adult child. Companies treat the court's letters as proof of who may act for the estate. Until the administrator is appointed, even the legal heirs cannot make claims on the estate's behalf. See [letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) for how that works.

## Which digital assets pass to heirs and which do not?

Think of three groups.

| Type of digital property | Examples | What happens with no will |
|---|---|---|
| Things with money value | Crypto, domain names, a payment-app balance, a website that earns income, in-game items that can be sold | They are estate property and pass to heirs under intestacy |
| Licensed content | Purchased music, e-books, movies, app subscriptions | Often a license that ends with the user, so usually nothing passes |
| Accounts and personal content | Email, social media, cloud photos, messages | Ownership of the content may pass, but access is controlled by the company and by state law |

The first group is the easiest. Cryptocurrency on an exchange is property, so an administrator can claim it with court papers, and the heirs split what is left. Our guides on [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance) and [domain names after death](/learn/digital-assets/domain-names-and-websites-after-death) cover the details.

The second group disappoints people. A music library or a shelf of e-books is often a license from the store. The purchaser agreed that the license ends at death, or cannot be transferred. A family can usually keep using a shared device where the files are downloaded, but no heir inherits the account. See [subscriptions and purchased media after death](/learn/digital-assets/subscriptions-and-purchased-media-after-death).

The third group is where most conflict happens, and the next sections focus on it.

## How does an administrator get into the accounts?

Not by guessing. The administrator starts with the paper trail: bank and credit card statements, the mail, the phone's bill, the email account's recovery address. Those show which services the person used and what they paid for. If the administrator has the phone, the passcode or the password manager's master password, those open many doors, subject to the earlier caution about terms of service.

Where that fails, each company has a process. The administrator sends a request with the death certificate and letters of administration. What comes back varies. A company may close the account, give a list of the account's activity, or require a court order before it releases content. Provider rules are summarized in our article on whether [an executor can access email](/learn/digital-assets/can-an-executor-access-email), and the same rules apply to an administrator.

Under the usual state law, a person with authority over the estate can generally get a catalog of communications (who wrote to whom and when) more easily than the content of those communications. The content normally needs either the owner's earlier permission or a court order. With no will and no online tool used, the owner left no permission. So the court order becomes the way in for email and messages, and it requires a petition that shows why the access is needed.

## Who gets the photos?

Legally, photos the person took are property of the estate, and copyright in them passes with the estate as well. With no will, the heirs in the same class share equally. If a person died with no spouse and two children, each child has a half share of a photo library. That says nothing about which one should keep the original.

In practice, families resolve it informally, and it goes well when one person makes copies for everyone. It goes badly when the person who holds the password quietly deletes pictures, or when an estranged relative demands them. A sibling who gets the cloud account should not delete anything until all of the heirs have a copy. Our article on [photos, cloud storage and iPhones after death](/learn/digital-assets/photos-and-iphones-after-death) explains how the companies handle requests.

Photos that include other people raise privacy questions. A court rarely gets involved, but the administrator should treat private images with discretion. An intimate or embarrassing set of photos is a good reason for an owner to leave instructions, and our piece on [keeping your family out of your private messages](/learn/digital-assets/can-you-keep-your-family-out-of-your-private-messages-after-you-die) covers that.

## What about social media accounts?

An heir does not inherit an Instagram or X account the way they inherit a car. Platforms offer a handful of options: memorialize the profile, close it, or in some cases allow a legacy contact to manage a limited set of features. The decision usually goes to a family member who can prove the death, and without a will it is typically the spouse or a parent who asks. Our article on [social media after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) lists the common choices and their consequences.

If the account earns money, as with a monetized channel or an online shop, the revenue stream is an asset. It passes through the estate and the administrator may need to deal with the platform's business terms. See [online businesses after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-business-etsy-shop-or-youtube-channel-after-death).

## What goes wrong most often?

- **No one has authority.** For weeks or months after death, nobody can speak for the estate. Subscriptions keep billing, and a free cloud account may be flagged as inactive.
- **A secret wallet.** Heirs inherit coins they do not know exist. The coins sit unmoved, and a wallet with no recovery phrase cannot be reopened.
- **Family conflict over a profile or a photo library.** One sibling holds the password, the others suspect something.
- **Identity theft.** Accounts that nobody watches are targets. Notify credit bureaus and close unused accounts.
- **Assumed rights.** A family member posts as the dead person, cancels a plan, or buys something with a saved card. Each of those can breach the terms and create trouble.

## What can you do now so your family is not in Rafael's position?

Make a will, and keep it short and specific where digital assets are concerned. Name an executor, add a clause giving that person authority over digital accounts and content, and name a back-up. Set a legacy contact with Apple and Google, if you use them. List your key accounts and where the access instructions are, ideally in a place your executor can reach without a court. Our [digital asset inventory guide](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use) shows a simple format, and [what a digital executor does](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) helps you decide whom to choose.

If you have crypto, add one more step: write down how a trusted person could reach it and what to do with it. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) will point you toward the right documents.
