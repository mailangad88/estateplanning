Someone you love has died, and their Facebook or Instagram page is still up. Birthday reminders keep arriving. Strangers post. Or the opposite: the page is quiet and you want to be sure it cannot be hacked. You are asking how to memorialize it, or whether to delete it, and what Meta will ask of you.

This page covers the steps for each platform, the proof you will need, and the choice between memorializing and deleting. For the broader picture across Google, Apple and others, see our [social media after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) article, and for the legal setting see the [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What does memorializing a Facebook account actually do?

Memorializing freezes the profile as it was. The word Remembering appears next to the person's name, and nobody can log in. The posts and photos stay visible to the same audience that could see them before. Depending on the privacy setting, friends can share memories on the timeline.

The profile also stops showing up in places that would be painful. It no longer appears in friend suggestions, ads or birthday reminders. That is the practical reason most families choose memorializing: the account stops sending the person's name out into the world.

Two limits are worth knowing. First, memorializing is not a way to read private messages or recover files. Nobody gains access. Second, if the person did not choose a legacy contact, the profile simply sits unchanged.

## How do you memorialize a Facebook account, step by step?

You do not need to be a family member to ask, but Meta will want proof that the person has died.

1. **Open the memorialization request form** from Facebook's help center. Search the help center for "memorialize an account" because the link location changes.
2. **Enter the person's name** and the profile link or the email address on the account.
3. **Provide proof of death.** An obituary link, a memorial card or a news article is usually accepted for a memorialization request. A death certificate is not always required at this stage.
4. **Submit and wait.** Meta reviews the request. You will usually hear back by email. If the request is approved, the profile changes to the Remembering state.
5. **Save what you want first.** Once memorialized, no one can log in to download photos, so copy what matters while that is still possible or while a legacy contact can request a download.

## What can a Facebook legacy contact do?

A legacy contact is someone the account holder named in advance. After memorialization, that person has limited powers. They can pin a tribute post to the top of the timeline, respond to new friend requests, and update the profile picture and cover photo. If the owner turned the option on, they can download a copy of what the owner shared.

They cannot log in as the person, read private messages, remove friends or post as the person. This is by design. The account holder's private conversations are protected, and the law agrees: the federal Stored Communications Act and state fiduciary access laws limit what a company may reveal.

If you are the legacy contact, you can also ask Meta to delete the account. Our walkthrough on [setting up a digital legacy contact](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) covers this from the owner's side.

## How is Instagram different?

Instagram has no legacy contact. Anyone can report a death and request memorialization with proof such as an obituary or news article. After approval, the account is memorialized: nobody can log in, posts stay visible to the same audience, and the account stops appearing in suggestions for others.

Removing an Instagram account is a stricter request. Meta generally asks for proof that you are an immediate family member, such as a birth or marriage certificate, and a death certificate or proof of authority as an executor. Instagram will not hand over the person's private direct messages.

## Should you memorialize or delete?

Memorialize if the profile is a place where friends are grieving. Delete if the person was private, or if the account is a risk to the family. The choice is about the person, not just the platform.

| Question | Points to memorialize | Points to delete |
|---|---|---|
| Did the person leave wishes? | They wanted to be remembered online | They asked for the account to be removed |
| What is on the profile? | Photos, tributes from friends | Private content, arguments, sensitive details |
| Does the family agree? | Yes | Disagreement; memorialize first, delete later |
| Is there a business page? | Transfer admin rights first | Not a reason to delete before the page is handled |

An example. Marcus, 61, died in Joliet. His daughter Tanya saw that his Facebook page had more than 400 friends, many of them from his union local, and that people kept posting. She chose memorialization. Marcus had never named a legacy contact, so the profile stayed exactly as he left it,. Her aunt had asked her to delete the page, but Tanya explained that deleting cannot be undone. They agreed to revisit in a year.

## What happens to a Page, a group or a business account?

A personal profile and a Page are separate things. If the person was the only administrator of a business Page or a group, memorializing the profile can leave that Page with no one who can manage it. Before you submit anything, check who else has admin rights and add someone while the owner's login still exists, if that is possible. If the Page earns money or sells products, treat it as a business asset, which is the subject of our article on [online businesses after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-business-etsy-shop-or-youtube-channel-after-death).

## What if the person did not leave a legacy contact?

The family can still act. They need proof of death for memorialization and proof of relationship for deletion. An executor appointed under Illinois or New Jersey law has the clearest standing. The state laws that give an executor access to digital assets are described in [Illinois digital assets law for executors](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) and [New Jersey digital assets law](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law). They give a fiduciary limited access to a catalog of the person's communications, not the content, unless the user consented or a court orders it.

If the family cannot agree, take the middle path: memorialize first, since it can be reversed.

## What should you do before requesting anything?

A short checklist keeps you from losing something you cannot get back.

- Look for photos and posts the family wants and save them from other devices or from friends who tagged the person.
- Check whether the person ran a Page or a group, and who else has admin rights.
- Check the person's email for Meta notices, which show whether a legacy contact exists.
- Gather the death certificate and your letters of authority if you are the executor. See [how to ask a tech company for a deceased person's account](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-ask-a-tech-company-for-a-deceased-persons-account) for how requests are usually phrased.
- Decide who in the family should submit the request, so Meta receives only one.

## How can you plan this for yourself?

Open your Facebook settings, find the memorialization settings and pick a legacy contact or choose deletion on death. Tell the person you chose. Add an entry in your digital inventory so your executor knows the account exists. Our guide to [creating a digital asset inventory](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use) shows how, and your will can state your wishes, though it is read too late to be the only place.
