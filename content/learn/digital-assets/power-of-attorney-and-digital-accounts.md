Helen, 72, handles everything online. Her pension deposit, her utility bills, her pharmacy refills and her photo library are all behind passwords that live in her head. Her son Marcus has a power of attorney for her. After a fall and three weeks in rehab, the electric company called him about a missed payment. He showed them the document. They said he needed to speak with Helen, or log in as her.

That is the gap this page is about. A power of attorney is a legal document written for a world of paper, and online accounts run on logins and company policies. Here is how the two fit together, what your agent can and cannot do, and how to set things up before anything goes wrong. Our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets) covers the wider topic of what happens to your online life after death.

## Does a power of attorney cover online accounts?

Yes, in the sense that an agent under a general financial power of attorney can manage your money and property, and your online banking, investment portal and bill-pay accounts are simply the modern way those assets are held. The agent steps into your shoes for financial decisions, just as they would at a branch counter. Our article on [durable financial powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) explains the basics, including why "durable" matters when you are incapacitated.

What the document does not do is make a company's website recognize the agent. Companies set their own verification rules. A bank may want the document and a visit to a branch. A utility may accept a faxed copy. A social media company may not have any process for agents at all. The legal authority exists, but making it work in practice requires some effort, and the agent should expect it.

## What about emails, texts and private messages?

This is where the law draws a line. Most states have adopted a model law, explained in our article on [RUFADAA](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained), that separates two things:

- **The catalog.** A list of who you wrote to and when, plus other record information, but not the words.
- **The content.** The text of emails, messages and other communications.

A custodian may give an agent the catalog if the power of attorney gives general authority over your property. The content is protected more strongly. The agent needs express authority in the document to ask for it, and the company may still require a court order or may offer a limited release.

So if you want your agent to be able to read your email to find a bill or a doctor's appointment, say so in the document. If you do not want anyone reading your messages, leave that out and say you prefer privacy. Either choice is legitimate. What is not wise is saying nothing and hoping the agent will cope.

## Which access method fits which account?

There are four common ways to let someone in. Each suits different accounts.

| Method | Best for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Power of attorney on file with the institution | Banks, brokerages, retirement plans, insurers | Each institution has its own form or review process |
| Built-in delegate or legacy contact feature | Email, phone and cloud accounts | Settings must be turned on while you are well, and they can limit what is shared |
| Password manager with emergency access | Everything with a login | The waiting period and who can request access |
| A sealed list of logins | Accounts with no built-in feature | The list can go stale and may breach terms of service |

Sharing a password directly is the simplest approach and the riskiest one. Many terms of service forbid it, two-step verification codes go to your phone, and a change of device can lock the agent out in a moment. Where a platform gives you a proper delegate feature, use it. Our article on [digital legacy contacts](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) lists the major ones, and [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) explains how to choose a manager.

## What should the document say?

Ask your attorney to include a digital assets paragraph in your power of attorney. It should cover these points:

1. **A definition** that includes online accounts, devices, cloud storage, cryptocurrency and any account you open in the future.
2. **Express authority over the content** of electronic communications, or an express statement that you do not grant it. Choose deliberately.
3. **Permission to use your credentials** or to act through a delegate feature, so the agent is not accused of unauthorized access.
4. **Authority to close, change or pay** for accounts, including recurring subscriptions.
5. **Authority to work with a service** that cannot be reached except by an online login, including a technician or password manager company.
6. **A successor agent,** in case the first one is unavailable.

If your documents are older than 2015 or so, they probably say nothing about digital assets. The agent can still act on your bank accounts, but the content of your email would be out of reach. An update is simple, and a short amendment may be enough.

## How do you prepare your digital life for incapacity?

The preparation takes less than an afternoon, and it has the biggest payoff of anything in digital planning.

1. **List your accounts.** Use a spreadsheet or a password manager's notes. Include banks, utilities, insurers, health portals, email, phone carrier, cloud storage and anything that bills you automatically.
2. **Mark what is time-sensitive.** Bills, medications and anything that expires. Those are the ones an agent will need first.
3. **Set up delegate or emergency features** on the accounts that offer them.
4. **Choose where the master list lives.** A password manager with emergency access works for many people. A fireproof box with a sealed copy is the traditional option.
5. **Tell your agent where to look,** not what the passwords are.
6. **Sign the document** and give copies to your agent and your financial institutions. Keep it where your agent can find it quickly.
7. **Revisit once a year.** Accounts multiply, and a list from three years ago will miss half of them.

The same steps help in the more usual short-term situations, such as surgery or a long hospital stay. The article on [what happens if you become incapacitated without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney) shows what the alternative looks like: a family that has to ask a court for authority before it can pay a single bill.

## When does the agent's authority stop?

At your death, immediately. The agent has no authority over your accounts the next morning, and the executor named in your will takes over once the court appoints them. If the same person holds both roles, they will still need to show the proper document for each stage. A company will treat a power of attorney presented after death with suspicion, and acting under it can cause real problems.

This is why a complete plan works in three layers: a power of attorney for the time you are alive but unable, a will or trust with digital language for after death, and a way to find and use the accounts in both cases. If you have named a [digital executor](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor), make sure that person and your agent can find each other. If a bank balks at the document while you are alive, read what to do when a [bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).

## What mistakes do people make?

- **Assuming "all my property" covers email.** It covers a lot, but the content of communications needs an express grant.
- **Using the agent's own login or the principal's login without a plan.** It can trigger account locks and breach terms.
- **Leaving the master password only in one person's head.** That is a single point of failure.
- **Naming an agent who is not comfortable with technology and giving them no help.** Pair the agent with a helper if needed.
- **Never testing the setup.** Ask your agent to try the delegate or emergency feature once, with your permission, so the first time is not an emergency.
