Two people with the same amount of Bitcoin can leave their families in opposite positions. One holds it on a large exchange with a named beneficiary on file for the estate. The other keeps it on a hardware device in a drawer, with the recovery words in the owner's head. The coins are identical. The first family files some paperwork and waits. The second family may never see the money.

This article compares the three common ways to hold crypto, exchange, self-custody and multisig, from your heirs' point of view. For the basics on inheriting coins, see our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What is the difference between custody types?

The central question is who controls the keys, the secret numbers that move the coins.

| | Exchange (custodial) | Self-custody (single key) | Multisig |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who controls the keys | The company | You | Several key holders |
| What you own | A claim against the company | The coins themselves | The coins themselves |
| Heir's path | Legal process with the company | Find the device, words and PIN | Gather enough key holders |
| Main risk | Company failure, hacks, frozen accounts | Lost or hidden keys, theft | Setup mistakes, a lost key holder |
| Tech skill needed | Low | Medium | High |
| Usual best fit | Smaller amounts, new owners | Long-term holders who can document | Large holdings, families with a plan |

Notice that each choice trades one risk for another. Nothing makes the problem disappear.

## What happens to crypto on an exchange?

An exchange holds the coins in its own wallets and credits your account with a balance. When you die, your family cannot log in as you, and the company cannot hand over a password. Most large exchanges have a process for deceased customers. It usually asks for a death certificate and proof that the person asking has legal authority, such as letters from the probate court. Some will transfer the assets to a wallet the executor controls; others sell and pay out cash. Rules differ by company and change often, so the executor should read the current policy on the company's site.

Two things help your family. First, keep the email address that receives exchange messages in your inventory, because the company's contact comes through it. Second, list the exchange in your will or letter of instructions so the executor knows to look. Our article on [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust) explains how to name who gets what.

Do not assume an exchange offers a beneficiary form. A few financial platforms allow transfer-on-death designations on some accounts, but most crypto exchanges do not. If you cannot name a beneficiary, the account passes through the estate, which usually means probate. Our [online accounts checklist](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) shows how to list each one.

## What happens to crypto in self-custody?

With self-custody, you hold a seed phrase, usually 12 or 24 words, that can recreate the wallet anywhere. Whoever has those words has the money. Nobody else, not a court, not a lawyer, not the wallet maker, can recover it for you.

That is both the appeal and the hazard. The coins cannot be frozen by a company, and they cannot be inherited by anyone who does not have the words. We cover the details in [what to do when heirs cannot find the seed phrase](/learn/digital-assets/lost-seed-phrase-or-crypto-heirs-cannot-find) and in [hardware wallets and NFTs after death](/learn/digital-assets/hardware-wallets-and-nfts-after-death).

Practical steps for a self-custody holder:

1. Write the seed phrase on paper or stamp it on metal. Do not store it in a photo, a note app or an email.
2. Keep two copies in two secured places, such as a home safe and a safe deposit box. Check the box rules in your state, because some banks restrict access after a death until an executor produces papers.
3. Write a separate letter that says what the assets are, which wallet app, which device and where the PIN is.
4. Tell your executor and your attorney where the letter is, not what is in it.
5. Test the plan. Have a trusted person restore a small wallet from your instructions.

## How does multisig change things?

A multisig wallet is set up so that spending needs a minimum number of approvals, for example two of three. The keys sit on separate devices, often held by different people or in different places. A thief with one key gets nothing. A family that loses one key still has the other two.

For inheritance, a "2-of-3" setup might look like this: you hold key one, your spouse holds key two, and your attorney or a trusted adult child holds key three. If you die, your spouse and the third holder can move the funds together. If your spouse loses a device, you and the third holder still can.

The drawbacks are real. Setup is technical, and a mistake can lock everyone out. Backup files for the wallet's structure matter as much as the keys. Key holders must be people you trust to cooperate and who understand what they hold. And the plan needs to say what happens when a key holder dies or moves away. Plenty of families that like the idea give up at the paperwork stage, and that is a fair reason to start with a simpler design.

## What about inheritance services and dead man's switches?

A growing number of companies sell crypto inheritance plans. The structures vary: some split a key into fragments held by different parties, some email a letter of instruction after you stop responding, and some use a legal agreement with a named beneficiary. They can fill a real gap for people who will never write a good letter.

Before you rely on one, ask:

- Does the company ever have the ability to move your coins alone?
- What happens to your plan if it is sold, hacked or closed?
- How does it confirm that you have died, and who can trigger release?
- Does its design survive your state's rules for the estate, or could it conflict with your will?
- What will it cost each year, and what happens if you stop paying?

A dead man's switch is the simplest form: if you do not check in, a message goes out. It is only as dependable as the service, and a missed check-in during an illness could release your keys while you are still alive. For most people, a sealed letter with an attorney and a reminder every year is dull and safer.

## How should an executor handle it?

An executor who finds crypto should do the following in order:

1. Do not move anything yet. Secure the device and the papers.
2. Record the value on the date of death for each coin, using a documented price source.
3. Confirm legal authority with letters from the court.
4. Contact the exchange, or restore the wallet using the documented method.
5. Move the assets into an estate account the executor controls, then follow the will.

If the executor does not understand crypto, hire someone who does, and do it before touching anything. A mistaken transaction cannot be reversed. The tax side, including the date-of-death basis, is covered in [how inherited crypto is taxed](/learn/digital-assets/how-inherited-crypto-is-taxed). Choosing an executor who can work with advisers matters here; see [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).

## An example

Hannah, 54, holds a small amount of Ethereum on an exchange and a larger amount of Bitcoin on a hardware wallet. She worries that her husband, Ben, would not know what to do. With her attorney she signs a will that names Ben as executor and a letter of instruction that lists the exchange, the email address and the wallet. She buys a second hardware device, sets up a 2-of-3 multisig wallet with Ben and her brother holding keys, and moves the Bitcoin into it. Her attorney keeps the sealed letter. Once a year she opens a test wallet with Ben to make sure the instructions still work.

That is more effort than most people put in, and it is a reasonable template. The important parts are the written plan and the rehearsal. You can adopt them without the multisig.
