Plain bitcoin sitting in a wallet is hard enough for an executor. Add staking, airdropped tokens or a loan on a decentralized finance platform, and the estate has assets that move on their own, lock themselves, or can be wiped out by a price swing while the family is still choosing a casket.

This page explains what happens to staked crypto, airdrops and DeFi positions when the owner dies, what the tax rules say, and what to write down while you are alive. For the basics, start with our guide to [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance) and the [digital assets pillar](/learn/digital-assets).

## What happens to staked crypto when the owner dies?

The coins are still part of the estate. Staking means you have committed coins to help run a network in exchange for rewards, and the coins usually stay yours throughout. What changes at death is access.

Many networks impose an unbonding period before staked coins can be withdrawn. Depending on the network, that can be a few days or several weeks, and some networks add a queue on top. The clock does not start until someone with access asks to unstake. If the owner staked through an exchange, the exchange handles it and the executor will deal with the exchange's estate process. If the owner staked from a personal wallet, the executor needs the wallet access and then waits out the lock.

Value can move during the wait. A coin worth $3,000 on the date of death can be worth more or less when it finally lands in an account the executor controls. The tax basis, however, is fixed at the date of death value (see below). Our article on [crypto custody for heirs](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-custody-exchange-self-custody-and-multisig-for-heirs) explains how exchange and self-custody access differs.

## What about airdrops that show up after death?

An airdrop is a free distribution of tokens to wallet holders. They are sent to an address, and the owner may never have asked for them.

Tokens that arrive after death are generally estate property, because they landed at an address the deceased controlled. Two practical problems follow. First, some airdrops must be claimed by connecting the wallet and signing a transaction before a deadline, and nobody can do that without the key. Second, some airdropped tokens are worthless or even harmful: scam tokens can show up uninvited, and interacting with them can expose a wallet. An executor should not click on unfamiliar tokens. It is better to record what is visible, ask a qualified tax preparer how to report it, and move on.

On tax, the IRS has said that airdrops are ordinary income when the recipient has control of them. Staking rewards are treated the same way, generally income when received. For the deceased's final income tax return, rewards received before death are the owner's; rewards after death belong to the estate or the person who takes control. See [how inherited crypto is taxed](/learn/digital-assets/how-inherited-crypto-is-taxed) for the rest.

## What is the danger with DeFi loans and pools?

DeFi, short for decentralized finance, lets people lend, borrow and earn on crypto through software instead of a bank. Three situations matter for an estate.

| Position | What it is | What can go wrong after death |
|---|---|---|
| Crypto-backed loan | You deposited coins as collateral and borrowed against them | If the price falls, the protocol sells the collateral automatically (liquidation) |
| Liquidity pool | You supplied two tokens to a trading pool and earn fees | The value can drift, and exiting needs a signed transaction |
| Yield or lending deposit | You deposited tokens to earn interest | No customer service; if the key is missing, nobody can withdraw |

A bank can pause a loan when someone dies. A DeFi protocol cannot, because it does not know. A loan is watched by a computer program, and the collateral ratio is its only concern. In a falling market a position can be liquidated within hours, long before letters of office are issued. That is a risk the owner can reduce while alive, by keeping borrowing conservative and keeping a clear note of how to close each position.

## How does an executor find these positions?

A wallet address is public, and anyone can look it up on a block explorer. That lets an executor see the balance, past transactions and many tokens without having the key. It does not let them move anything.

The usual search starts with the owner's records: exchange emails, tax forms, bank transfers to exchanges, a hardware wallet in a drawer, a note in a password manager. Our checklist for [online accounts after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) describes where to look. If the keys are missing, see [lost seed phrase or crypto heirs cannot find](/learn/digital-assets/lost-seed-phrase-or-crypto-heirs-cannot-find).

Executors can also see the history of a wallet and notice a smart contract interaction, which is the sign of DeFi. That is the cue to slow down and get help before touching anything.

## What authority does the executor have?

The executor has authority over the digital assets of the estate once the court issues letters, but that authority is only as useful as the access they hold. Under both Illinois and New Jersey law, an executor may request records from online service providers, which helps with exchanges and custodial platforms. It does nothing for a self-custody wallet, since no company holds those keys. Our pages on [Illinois digital assets law](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) and [New Jersey digital assets law](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law) explain the request process.

## What should you write down now?

A page like this for each position is enough. Keep it separate from the keys.

1. The platform or protocol name and the website address.
2. The wallet address and which wallet holds it.
3. What the position is: staked, borrowed against, pooled, or lent out.
4. What happens on a price drop, such as the collateral ratio that triggers liquidation.
5. The steps to close it, and who should do them.
6. Where the key or seed phrase is kept, without writing it on the same page.
7. Whether the executor should unwind everything or hold.

### An example

Marcus, 54, holds some Ethereum staked through an exchange and borrows against another batch on a DeFi lending platform to pay a tax bill. His will names his sister Nadia as executor. Nothing says what the loan is or where the wallet is.

If Marcus dies in a falling market, Nadia has two problems. The exchange will move the staked coins only after she presents letters and a death certificate, and then the unbonding period applies. The loan sits in a wallet she cannot open, and the price keeps dropping. If Marcus had left Nadia a one-page list with the platform, the wallet's location and instructions to repay the loan first, she could act within days of getting letters. As it is, the collateral may be sold before she knows it exists.

## What does estate tax have to do with it?

For federal estate tax, crypto is valued at its fair market value on the date of death. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026, so most estates owe nothing federally. Illinois taxes estates over $4 million, and New Jersey has an inheritance tax rather than an estate tax. Loans against crypto reduce the net value only if the debt is real and documented.

For income tax, inherited coins generally get a stepped-up basis to the date-of-death value, so selling soon after death usually produces little gain. That step-up does not apply to gifts made during life.
