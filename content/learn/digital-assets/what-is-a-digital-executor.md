Your daughter manages your calendar, your photos and half your bills on a phone you can barely work. When you die, your lawyer's choice of executor is your brother, who still prints his emails. Who deals with the cloud accounts? Some people solve this by naming a digital executor, and the idea is simple enough that most families can use it.

This article explains what the role is, what it can and cannot do, and how to write it into your plan. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What does a digital executor do?

A digital executor finds, secures, copies, transfers or closes your online property according to your wishes. The job is practical and often tedious. It usually involves:

- Locating your accounts using your inventory, your email, bank statements and your phone.
- Sending death certificates and proof of authority to companies that require them.
- Downloading photos, documents and records you wanted preserved.
- Closing or memorializing social media and subscriptions.
- Transferring or selling items with value, such as a domain name, an online store or a cryptocurrency balance.
- Stopping automatic payments so the estate does not keep paying for apps and memberships.

The person works alongside the regular [executor](/glossary/executor), who handles the court, the debts and the distribution. If you name only one person, the one person does both.

## Is "digital executor" a legal title?

Usually not. Nearly every state has adopted the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, which we describe in [what RUFADAA is](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained). It speaks of fiduciaries such as the executor, the agent and the trustee. It does not create a separate office. So a "digital executor" is generally a person you name in your will, in a trust or in a memo, and the law treats that person's authority according to the document.

Two ways to make the role work:

1. **Name your executor and add digital powers.** The will states that the executor may access, manage and close your digital assets and consents to disclosure of the content of communications.
2. **Name a separate person for the technical tasks.** The will either appoints that person as a special fiduciary for digital assets, or authorizes the executor to delegate to that person. Delegation is the more flexible approach, because the executor stays in charge and the helper works under the executor's authority.

Wording and rules differ by state, so an attorney should confirm which approach your state favors. Where your documents are silent, a helper without formal authority can still sit beside the executor and act as a guide, but companies will deal only with the person holding court-issued [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary).

## Who should you choose?

Think about temperament before you think about skills. Software is the easier part to learn. What matters is whether the person will return calls, keep records and make decisions in a stressful month.

| Option | Strengths | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Your regular executor | One person, one chain of authority | May be overwhelmed or unfamiliar with tech |
| A tech-comfortable child or relative as helper | Knows your devices and habits | Family friction if not chosen as executor |
| A friend you trust | Neutral and often patient | May lack standing with companies |
| A professional fiduciary | Organized, experienced with paperwork | Cost, and less personal knowledge of you |

Avoid naming someone who is likely to die or move away before you do, and avoid anyone you have not asked. If you have crypto, picking someone who understands self-custody is a safety issue, because a careless move can lose funds permanently. Our article on [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust) explains the stakes.

## How do you name a digital executor?

1. **Ask first.** A short conversation and a "yes" matter more than any clause.
2. **List your assets.** Use a simple inventory of accounts, devices and anything of value, as our [digital asset planning guide](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan) describes.
3. **Choose what happens to each.** Keep, delete, memorialize, sell, give to a person.
4. **Put the authority in your documents.** A clause in your will, plus matching language in your trust and your [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) so someone can act if you are incapacitated.
5. **Arrange practical access.** Use the company tools in [our guide to digital legacy contacts](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) and a password manager with emergency access.
6. **Store the instructions where the person can find them,** not in the will, which may be public. A [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) works well.
7. **Revisit every year,** especially after a new phone, a new job, or a change in family.

## What should the clause cover?

The clause does not need to be long. A useful one answers a few questions in plain words: who has authority, what counts as a digital asset, whether the person can read the content of messages, whether the person may use the accounts to administer the estate, and what happens to personal items like photos. It should also allow the person to hire help, such as a data recovery firm, and pay reasonable costs from the estate. If you want certain things erased unread, say it.

## How is this different from a legacy contact?

A legacy contact is named inside one company's settings and reaches only that company's data. A digital executor is named in your legal documents and reaches everything your estate owns. They are meant to work together. The contact might hand over your photo library in an afternoon, while the digital executor handles the ten accounts that have no tool at all, such as your domain name, your online brokerage login and the loyalty points you forgot about. Our guide to [legacy contacts](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) shows the settings. Many families name the same person for both roles, which keeps the paperwork simple.

## Should the role be shared?

Sometimes. Two siblings can serve as co-fiduciaries, and some people like the check it provides. The cost is friction: every request needs two signatures, and companies are slow enough already. A cleaner pattern is one decision-maker and one helper. If you do name two people with equal authority, say whether either can act alone for small matters like canceling a streaming subscription.

## What can go wrong?

Most problems come from the same four gaps:

- **Authority with no access.** The person has the right paper and no passwords, so nothing opens.
- **Access with no authority.** The person knows the passcode and uses it to log in as you. That can violate a company's terms and, for some accounts, the law. The proper route goes through the estate.
- **No instructions.** The person can do many things and does not know which ones you wanted.
- **Conflicting roles.** Two people think they are in charge. Say in writing who decides.

## An example

Hal, 70, names his niece Priya as executor and his neighbor's son, Devon, as his "digital assistant," since Devon set up his router and his photo backup. The will lets the executor delegate digital tasks to Devon and consents to disclosure of account content. Hal's sealed letter lists his password manager, tells Priya where the emergency kit is, and says to keep his photographs, close his social accounts and cash out his small online store.

When Hal dies, Priya gets letters from the court, signs the requests, and Devon does the technical steps. It takes a few weeks, not a few months, because neither of them has to guess.

## Does the digital executor owe duties?

Yes. Anyone who acts for an estate is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary) and must act in the estate's interest, keep records and avoid self-dealing. That is true for the helper as well as the executor. For a fuller picture of what falls on the regular executor, see [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) and [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
