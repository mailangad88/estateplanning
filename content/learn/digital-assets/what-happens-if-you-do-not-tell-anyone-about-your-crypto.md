Plenty of crypto owners keep it quiet on purpose. They do not want relatives asking for loans, and they do not want anyone to know how much they hold. That is a reasonable instinct while you are alive. After you die, the same silence can turn a valuable asset into a locked file that nobody knows exists.

This page explains what actually happens if you tell no one, and how to hand on Bitcoin or other coins to your children without exposing your keys. It builds on our overview of [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance) and belongs to our [digital assets series](/learn/digital-assets).

## What happens to Bitcoin if the owner tells no one?

It depends on where the coins are held.

If they sit in an account at an exchange, the company has your name, your identity documents and often your email. Your executor, once appointed, can usually contact the exchange, show a death certificate and court letters, and ask to transfer or close the account. The family still has to know the account exists. Tax forms, bank deposits to the exchange and emails are the usual clues, as our article on [crypto heirs cannot find](/learn/digital-assets/lost-seed-phrase-or-crypto-heirs-cannot-find) describes.

If the coins are in a wallet you control, such as a hardware device or a phone app, the access is a recovery phrase or private key. Nobody can reset it. If the phrase is lost, the coins remain on the blockchain, visible but unreachable. Estate planners often hear the same story: the heirs found the device in a drawer and had no idea what it was.

So the answer is not always a loss. It is a loss when three things are missing together: no one knows crypto exists, no one knows where it is held, and no one can get in.

## What does an heir need to know?

Three things, and each one can be solved separately.

| What the heir needs | Where it can be recorded | Risk if missing |
|---|---|---|
| That crypto exists | A letter to the executor, your attorney's file, a line in your asset inventory | Nobody searches for it |
| Where it is held | Exchange name and login email, hardware device location, wallet app name | Family finds the device but not the account, or the reverse |
| How to get access | Recovery phrase or key, stored offline, with instructions | Coins are permanently out of reach |

The first two are not secrets that can hurt you. The third is. That is why it should be stored apart from the others.

## Should the seed phrase go in my will?

No. A will tells the court and your family who gets what. In both Illinois and New Jersey, a will is filed with the court after death, and filings are generally open to anyone who asks. Anyone who sees the seed phrase could move the funds before your executor does. Our article on [whether probate is public](/learn/probate/is-probate-public) explains what becomes visible.

What the will or trust should say is who owns the crypto, in general terms. For example: "I give all my digital assets, including cryptocurrency, to my daughter, Anita." Our guide to [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust) covers the wording and when a trust is a better home for it.

## How do I store the seed phrase so heirs can use it?

Write it on paper or stamp it on metal, and keep it offline. Do not photograph it, store it in a notes app, email it or save it in cloud storage that a hacker or a sync error could expose. Then decide where the one copy, or the few copies, will sit.

| Option | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Home safe | Easy access for the family | Fire, theft or a relative who finds it too early |
| Safe deposit box | Protected from the house | May be sealed until an executor is appointed; someone must know it exists and be authorized |
| Attorney's vault or file | Professional custody and a record of the instruction | Needs a signed agreement and the firm to still exist |
| Two or more locations, each holding part | Resists theft of one location | Easy to botch; heirs may not realize they need every part |

A word on splitting. Cutting a twelve-word phrase in half and hiding each half is something many owners do on their own. It can work, but it also makes the phrase useless if either half is lost, and a half of a phrase is easier to guess than many people think. If you want a split arrangement, use a method designed for it, and write down the exact steps. Multisignature wallets, which require more than one key to approve a payment, spread control in a more structured way. They also add more steps for heirs, as covered in our piece on [crypto custody for heirs](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-custody-exchange-self-custody-and-multisig-for-heirs).

## What goes in the instruction letter?

A short letter, kept separate from the phrase, that tells your executor where to start. It does not need to contain any secret. A useful one covers:

1. The names of the exchanges and wallet apps you use, and the email address tied to each.
2. Where each hardware device is kept, and what it is.
3. Where the recovery phrase is stored, in general terms, such as "the gray envelope in the safe deposit box at First Bank."
4. Who knows the letter exists.
5. Any wallet passphrase hint, but not the passphrase.
6. A note that some devices wipe themselves after repeated wrong PIN attempts, so the executor should not guess.

Our guide to a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) shows a format. Put a copy with your attorney and tell the executor where to find it.

### Example: Marcus and the gray envelope

Marcus, 52, of Cherry Hill, held Bitcoin on an exchange and a smaller amount on a hardware wallet. He told no one but his wife. He then changed his plan. He wrote the exchange name and login email in a letter to his brother, who is his executor, and kept the letter with his lawyer. He wrote his recovery phrase on a steel plate and put it in a safe deposit box in his own name and his wife's. His will left his digital assets to his wife, then to his two children. His brother knew the letter existed and where the box was. When the time comes, none of the secrets are in a public file, and the people who need them know where to start.

## How do I test the plan?

Once a year, or after any change of device or exchange, check it. Hand the letter to a trusted person and ask what they would do first. Better, do a test with a small amount: set up a second wallet from the written recovery steps, send a small amount from the first one, and make sure the instructions are clear. If the exercise confuses the helper, the real thing will be harder. Update the letter if you move coins or buy a new device.

## What about taxes and the executor's duties?

Crypto in the estate gets a new tax basis at death in most cases, which is explained in our article on [how inherited crypto is taxed](/learn/digital-assets/how-inherited-crypto-is-taxed). Your executor must also list it as an estate asset and may need to value it on the date of death. An executor who does not know it exists cannot do either. In Illinois and New Jersey, the digital assets statutes help executors deal with online platforms, but they do not open a self-custody wallet. See [Illinois digital assets law](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) and [New Jersey's digital assets law](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law).
