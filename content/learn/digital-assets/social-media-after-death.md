After a death, a person's accounts keep existing. Birthday reminders still appear, messages arrive, and a profile may sit online for years. For grieving families, that can be painful, or it can be a comfort. Either way, there are practical questions: who can close an account, who can see the messages and what the person would have wanted.

This article describes how the main providers handle accounts after a death and how to set up your own wishes. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## Who gets to decide what happens to an online account?

The company, the account owner's earlier choices and the law all play a part. In most states, a law called the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act sets this order: first any online tool the owner used, then the owner's will or other documents, and last the provider's terms of service. Privacy laws also limit what companies may disclose.

In practice, that means the strongest thing you can do is use the platform's own setting. Without it, your family must work through the company's request process, which usually needs a death certificate and sometimes proof that the requester is the executor.

## How do the main services treat a deceased user?

Policies change, so check the company's current help page. The following summary shows the usual pattern.

| Service | Options for the owner in advance | Options for family afterward |
|---|---|---|
| Facebook and Instagram | Name a legacy contact or choose deletion | Request memorialization or removal with proof of death |
| Google (Gmail, Drive, Photos, YouTube) | Inactive Account Manager: share with chosen people or delete after a set period | Request through Google's process; content may be released only in limited cases |
| Apple (iCloud) | Legacy Contact, who receives an access key | Legacy Contact submits the key and death certificate for access |
| Microsoft | Limited tools | Request through Microsoft's process; content access is restricted |
| X, LinkedIn and others | Usually none | Request deactivation with proof of death |

### Facebook and Instagram

Facebook lets you pick a legacy contact who can manage a memorialized profile, including pinned tributes and profile updates, but cannot read private messages. You can also tell Facebook you want your account deleted when you die. Without either, family members can ask for memorialization or removal and provide proof of death.

### Google

Google's Inactive Account Manager lets you set a period of inactivity, such as 3, 6, 12 or 18 months, after which Google notifies people you choose and optionally shares selected data, or deletes the account. If you set nothing, the family can request closure, and Google may provide content only in limited circumstances, usually after review and sometimes a court order.

### Apple

A Legacy Contact receives an access key. After you die, they submit the key and a death certificate, and Apple gives them access to much of the iCloud data, such as photos, notes and messages stored in iCloud. Without a Legacy Contact, a family generally needs a court order.

### Other services

Most other social and professional networks will deactivate or remove an account for a family member who provides proof of death. They usually will not turn over private messages.

## Should an account be memorialized or deleted?

Memorializing preserves a profile as a place to remember someone, while deleting removes it. Neither is better. A memorialized Facebook profile lets friends post memories, and it can be a comfort. It can also be painful when it shows up in suggestions or reminders. Ask who in the family wants what and whether the person left any wishes.

Consider these points:

- If the person had a business page, decide who will manage it before closing the personal profile.
- Download photos and posts first, since deletion is permanent.
- If you are not sure, memorialize now and delete later. Memorializing is usually reversible in a way that deletion is not.

## What should family do in the first weeks?

You do not have to act immediately. Accounts cannot harm anyone while they sit, but you should deal with a few things reasonably soon.

1. **Secure the devices.** Keep the phone and computer safe. Do not wipe or sell them yet, since they may be needed to receive verification codes.
2. **Watch the email.** Bills, bank notices and account recovery messages come to the email address. Check it only if you have the authority and the means, and consider forwarding. Do not reply to messages from the person's account.
3. **Fight identity theft.** Dead people's accounts are targets. Notify the credit bureaus and watch for fraud. Report the death to banks and card issuers.
4. **Save what matters.** Download photos, contact lists and documents while access still exists.
5. **Request closure or memorialization** from each platform once you are ready.

Our [week-by-week checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) shows where these fit in a broader timeline.

Be careful about logging in as the deceased person. Even with family intent, using someone else's password can violate a service's terms and, in some cases, laws about unauthorized access. If the person gave you the password during life for a specific reason, such as a shared streaming account, that is different, but ask an attorney if you need to go further. An executor with court authority has stronger footing, and the steps are in [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

## How can you set this up for yourself?

Use each platform's legacy tool and document your wishes. A sensible set of steps:

1. Choose a trusted person for each platform's legacy or inactive-account option.
2. Add digital assets language to your will and durable power of attorney. See [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).
3. Give your legacy contacts a note that explains what you want, such as keep, memorialize or delete.
4. Back up photos and files to a drive or service that your family knows about.
5. Store passwords in a password manager with an emergency access feature, as described in [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access).
6. Review your choices once a year. Companies change their tools.

For example, Joanne, 67, names her son as Apple Legacy Contact, sets Google to share her photos with him after 6 months of inactivity, and picks Facebook's delete-on-death option because she does not want a memorial page. Her will and power of attorney grant her son authority over her digital accounts, which lets him handle email and bills if she is ever incapacitated.

A power of attorney matters here too, because it operates while you are alive. Read our overview of the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

## Can you keep a loved one's messages and photos?

Often you can keep what you can already see. Photos that were shared with you, group chat histories on your own phone and posts you were tagged in remain yours. What is harder to reach is the private content stored in the person's own account. Ask friends to send you pictures and videos they have. If the person used a cloud photo service, check whether they set up a legacy contact, and if not, speak with an attorney about requesting access through the executor.
