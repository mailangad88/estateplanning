A spouse or parent has died, and the email account probably holds the clues to everything else: where the bank statements go, which cards exist, what is autopaying. Wanting to get in is natural. The law and the providers are cautious about it, and the right route is slower than typing in a password.

This article explains what an executor can request, what the provider will want to see, and what to do in the meantime. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## The short version

- An executor can usually request the account's catalog (names, dates, subject lines in some cases) without proof that the person consented.
- Reading the content of messages requires the person's consent in a will, trust or the provider's own tool, or a court order.
- Providers want a death certificate and certified letters from the court naming the executor.
- Logging in with a password you found is the risky path. Use the process.
- You can learn most of what you need from paper mail, statements and a credit report while you wait.

## When the answer is yes

An executor gets into the account in one of three ways.

**The person set up a tool.** Google's Inactive Account Manager and Apple's Legacy Contact are examples. If the person named someone, the company follows that choice. See [how to set up a digital legacy contact](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts). The named contact may or may not be the executor, and the executor cannot override it.

**The will gave consent.** Under the model law that nearly every state has adopted, described in [what RUFADAA is](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained), a will, trust or power of attorney that expressly allows disclosure of the content of communications counts as consent. The provider will want a certified copy of the document.

**A judge ordered it.** If there was no instruction, the executor can ask the probate court for an order. The court must usually find that the account belonged to the person, that disclosure is reasonably necessary to settle the estate, and that the request is narrow enough to be fair.

Take Louisa, executor for her father Gene, who died with no digital clause. She can send Gene's email provider the death certificate and her letters and ask for the catalog. The provider may release a log of senders and dates but not the messages. To read them, she needs the court order.

## When the answer is no

Several situations block access:

- **No authority.** A sibling who is not the executor has no standing, even if the family agrees.
- **Prohibition.** If the person chose to have the account deleted at death, or wrote that content must stay private, the provider follows that.
- **Insufficient proof.** The provider cannot verify the account belongs to the person, or the request is too broad, such as "everything from 2005 on."
- **Policy limits.** Some providers offer only a download, a closed account or a refusal, whatever the court says, because of how their systems or terms work.

Do not read a refusal as the end. A narrower request, a court order and patience often work.

## What does each type of provider usually do?

Policies change, so treat this as a pattern and check the company's current page.

| Provider type | Typical path | What you may get |
|---|---|---|
| Google | Inactive Account Manager contact, or a request with death certificate and proof of authority | Sometimes content with consent or court order; often only closure |
| Apple | Legacy Contact with access key, or a court order | Most iCloud data for a contact; a court order for others |
| Microsoft (Outlook) | Next-of-kin or court order process | Often a court order for content |
| Yahoo and smaller providers | Death certificate and letters | Frequently closure, not content |
| Employer email | Governed by employer policy | Often none; business records stay with the company |

## What should you do now?

1. **Do not log in** with the person's password, and do not reset it. If the account detects odd activity it can lock, and a lockout can cost you the very evidence you want.
2. **Get certified copies** of the death certificate. Plan for more than you expect, because each company keeps one.
3. **Get the court appointment** if you have not. The order of events is in our guide to [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties), and the process is described in [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate).
4. **Ask the provider for its deceased-user process,** and send exactly what it lists. A form letter with extra requests slows things down.
5. **Search paper and financial records.** Statements, tax forms, loan and credit card paperwork and a credit report show the accounts that matter.
6. **Update the mailing address** on accounts you identify, so statements and notices come to the executor.
7. **Forward or monitor mail,** where permitted, for time-sensitive items such as bills and renewals.
8. **Keep a log** of each request, date and answer. It protects you if a beneficiary asks questions later.

## How state law changes this

Nearly every state has a version of the digital assets act, but the court procedure, the standard of proof and the time limits differ. Some states are fast and simple for small requests. Others need a formal petition. An estate planning attorney in the person's state can tell you which applies, and our [state guides](/estate-planning/illinois) show how the details can play out. If the person lived in one state and the provider is in another, the provider's terms may add a layer.

## What if the email is the only way into other accounts?

Often it is, because password resets for banks, brokers and shopping sites go to the inbox. That is why the provider's process matters, and why you should work from the other end too. Contact the bank directly, show your letters, and ask for statements and account control. Banks have well-worn procedures for deceased customers, and they do not need the email to act.

## What about the phone, where email is already open?

Often the email app on a phone is already signed in, and families wonder whether reading from it is fair game. The legal risk is the same as typing in a password: the company's terms and privacy law care about how the access happens, not whether the screen was unlocked. A more defensible approach is to use the phone to gather facts you could get elsewhere, like the names of banks in the notifications or the list of installed apps, and to hold off on reading personal correspondence until the executor has the authority. Do not delete anything, and do not wipe the device, since it may hold the authenticator app that every other account depends on. If the phone is locked and no one knows the code, do not guess repeatedly, because some devices erase after too many tries.

## What if there is a dispute about who gets to read it?

Email can hold private letters, medical details and things a family member would rather the others never see. If heirs disagree about whether to open it, a judge can set limits: appoint a neutral person to review, restrict the search to certain senders or dates, or allow the executor to take only what the estate needs. Courts generally respect a dead person's privacy where the paperwork is silent. If you are the executor, keep your requests narrow and written, and avoid sharing what you see beyond what administration requires. That is part of your duty as a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary).

## How can you make this easier for your own family?

Put the consent in your documents, name a contact in your email provider's tool, and give your executor the path to your password manager. Our article on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) lays it out, and the [social media article](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) covers accounts that are not email.
