You may have hundreds of online accounts, and you probably know only a handful of the passwords. A password manager remembers the rest. That is good for security, and it creates a problem for your family: if you cannot unlock the vault, no one can read your bills, tax files or photos.

This article shows how to make access possible for the right person, at the right time, without making you easier to hack. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## Why do passwords matter in an estate plan?

Without access, a fiduciary can know that an account exists and still be unable to use it. Legal authority and technical access are two separate things. A will or power of attorney can say "my executor may manage my accounts," but a company will not necessarily open them, and a locked phone cannot be argued open.

Passwords also matter while you are alive. If you have a stroke or develop dementia, your agent under a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) may need to pay bills online, reach statements and cancel services before there is any death. A plan that works only after you die leaves a gap.

## What are the safest ways to share access?

The best methods let a trusted person get in when needed without giving them standing access today. In order of preference:

| Method | How it works | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Password manager emergency access | A named contact requests access and gets it after a waiting period | Contact must be set up and kept current |
| Family or shared vault | Key information is stored in a shared vault | Others can see it now |
| Sealed instructions with an attorney or in a safe | A paper record with the master password and recovery details | Can become outdated or be lost |
| Legacy tools at Apple, Google and Meta | The company grants access to named contacts | Limited to that company |
| Telling someone the password | Simple | Weak security, easy to forget to update |

Many popular managers, including 1Password, Bitwarden, Dashlane and LastPass, offer some form of emergency access, recovery kit or shared vault. Features and names change, so check your own provider's current options.

## What is the minimum set of keys to preserve?

Four items unlock most of the rest, so protect them first:

1. **The password manager's master password** and any secret key or recovery kit.
2. **The phone passcode,** because verification codes and authenticator apps live there.
3. **The primary email account,** because it receives password reset links for nearly everything.
4. **Recovery codes** for your most important accounts, such as email, banking and cloud storage.

If your family has these four, they can usually recover the rest. If they lack them, even routine accounts become very hard.

## How does two-factor authentication affect your family?

Two-factor authentication improves your security and can lock out your family if you are not careful. If a bank sends a text code to your phone, the code goes to a phone your executor may not be able to unlock. An authenticator app on that phone may be gone if the phone is lost or wiped.

Steps to make 2FA survivable:

- Print or write the backup codes the service offers and store them with your access plan.
- Use an authenticator that supports encrypted backups, and keep the recovery information safe.
- Avoid relying on a single device. Register a second device or hardware key where possible.
- Note which accounts use 2FA in your inventory.
- Do not remove 2FA to make things easier. A better plan is to document how to recover.

## What about phones and computers?

A device passcode is a key, and many families are stopped here. If the phone is locked and the owner cannot give the code, Apple and Google will not usually bypass it without court orders, and even then access may not be possible. Add the passcode to your sealed instructions or set up the legacy tools. Use a legacy contact on your phone account so the company can help.

Keep the devices safe after a death. Do not wipe, sell or give away the phone or laptop until you have collected account information, verification apps and photos. Our [week-by-week checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) reminds families to hold the devices for a while.

## What should you never do?

Avoid a few common errors:

- **Never put passwords or seed phrases in your will,** because wills are typically filed with a court and can become public. See [cryptocurrency and estate planning](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance) for why this matters even more for crypto.
- **Never store the master password in the same place as the vault,** such as a document in your own cloud storage.
- **Never email or text passwords** to family members.
- **Never rely on memory,** because it does not survive an emergency.
- **Never assume a family member may sign in as you.** Using another person's login can breach a company's terms, and a fiduciary with proper authority has a better path. Read [what happens to social media and email accounts](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death).

## How do you set up an access plan in an afternoon?

1. **Pick a password manager** if you do not already use one, and move your important logins into it.
2. **Name an emergency contact** inside the manager, or create a printed recovery kit.
3. **Print backup codes** for email, banking, cloud storage and your phone account.
4. **Write a one-page letter** listing where each key is, such as safe, binder or attorney. It should contain locations, not the passwords themselves.
5. **Tell your executor and agent** where the letter is. A conversation is easier than you might think, and our article on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) can help.
6. **Add digital assets language** to your will and power of attorney, as explained in [how to include digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).
7. **Review once a year,** and after buying a new phone or changing your master password.

For example, Walter, 72, uses a password manager and names his daughter as his emergency contact with a 7-day waiting period. He keeps printed backup codes and his phone passcode in a sealed envelope in his safe. His attorney knows the safe's location. If he is ever hospitalized, his daughter can get into the bills in a week. If he dies, she can reach his email, bank and photos.

## What if you are the family member who has been locked out?

Start with what is legally in your hands. If you hold the person's phone and know the passcode, you can often reach their email, which lets you see what accounts exist. Then, rather than trying to sign in everywhere, use each company's deceased-user process, which requires a death certificate and, for content, often a court order. An executor with letters testamentary has the strongest footing. Make a list of every account you find, and note recovery codes in drawers, notebooks and address books, because many people write them down in obvious places. Do not pay anyone who offers to crack a device or recover an account for an upfront fee.

## How do you decide who should hold access?

Choose someone who is both trustworthy and likely to be reachable, and consider a backup. Some people split roles: one person gets emergency access to the password manager, and another, perhaps your attorney, holds the sealed letter.
