Your father kept everything on a desktop in the spare room and a second drive he called "the backup." He died last week. The computer asks for a password nobody knows, and somewhere in there are his tax returns, the deed to the lake property and thirty years of photographs. You want in, but you do not want to make things worse.

This page, part of our [digital assets planning guide](/learn/digital-assets), covers who has the right to open a deceased person's computer, drives and cloud storage, what to try in what order, and how to avoid the mistakes that cost families files.

## Who has the legal right to access a deceased person's computer?

The executor or administrator does. A computer, external drive, USB stick or tablet is tangible personal property, and it belongs to the estate. Once a court issues letters testamentary or letters of administration, the personal representative can take it into custody and open it. If the estate has not been opened yet, a close family member can usually secure the device, but should not start searching accounts on their own.

Cloud storage is different because the files sit on a company's servers under a contract. Both Illinois and New Jersey follow a version of the uniform law on fiduciary access to digital assets. Under it, a personal representative can generally obtain a catalog of the account and, with the right authority, the contents. The provider may still require a court order or its own online tool. Our guides to [Illinois digital assets law](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) and [New Jersey digital assets law](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law) explain the state rules.

## What should I do first?

Secure, then search. Here is the order our attorneys suggest:

1. **Do not turn things off or reset them.** Leave a locked laptop locked. Keep a phone charged and, if it has a SIM, leave it in.
2. **Collect all devices.** Check desks, drawers, cars, the safe deposit box, and any home server or network storage box.
3. **Look for written passwords.** A notebook by the desk, a password manager, a printed recovery key, or a sealed envelope from the attorney.
4. **Check the will and estate documents.** Some include digital instructions or name a person with access.
5. **Try the known password once or twice, then stop.** Some systems lock after a few failures.
6. **Record what you do.** A short log protects the executor if anyone later asks what was accessed.

## What if the computer is locked and nobody knows the password?

You have four realistic routes, from easiest to hardest:

| Route | How it works | Works when |
|---|---|---|
| Saved password | A password manager or browser has the login, or a family member knows it | The manager can be opened by someone authorized |
| Recovery key | Windows or Apple ID account stores a recovery code online or on paper | You can reach that account or find the paper |
| Account reset | The online account tied to the computer sends a reset to an email you can open | You control the email and the account allows it |
| Professional recovery | A data recovery service tries to read the drive | The drive is not fully encrypted, or you hold the key |

If a drive is encrypted and no key exists, the files are gone for practical purposes. That is not a failure of effort. It is how modern encryption is meant to work. This is why our attorneys tell clients to store recovery keys with the will.

A caution: do not hand the machine to a stranger offering to "crack it" for cash. You are giving someone private tax, medical and financial records. Ask a reputable repair shop what they will access, and keep a family member present.

## How do I get files out of Dropbox, OneDrive or iCloud Drive?

Each service has a process for the families of deceased users, and none of them is instant. In general you need:

- A copy of the death certificate
- Proof that you are the executor or otherwise entitled (letters from the court)
- The account email address
- A written request, often through a web form

Expect a few weeks. Providers differ on whether they give a full download or only close the account, and some will not release content without a court order. For Google, we have a separate guide: [Gmail, Drive and Google Photos after death](/learn/digital-assets/gmail-drive-and-google-photos-after-death-inactivity-timers-and-how-long-google-keeps-data). For Apple, see [photos and iPhones after death](/learn/digital-assets/photos-and-iphones-after-death). The general approach for any company is in [how to ask a tech company for a deceased person's account](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-ask-a-tech-company-for-a-deceased-persons-account).

If the family is already signed in on the loved one's computer, the sync folder may hold copies of the files. Copying that folder to a drive of your own is often the fastest route, as long as the sync client is not set to delete files remotely when removed locally. Pause the sync first.

## An example: Priya and the NAS box

Priya's mother, Anita, kept family documents on a small network storage box in her closet, plus a laptop she used for banking. Priya, the executor, found the laptop password in a recipe notebook, but the storage box needed a separate admin login. She did not guess. She photographed the label, found the model, and used the manufacturer's account recovery tied to Anita's email, which Priya could open with the laptop. Two days later she had the files. The key steps were simple: stop, look for the recovery path, and use an account she was authorized to open.

## What files should I look for first?

Sort by deadline, not by sentiment. Photos matter, but tax returns do not wait.

- **Money documents:** last three years of tax returns, brokerage and bank statements, pension papers, a list of accounts.
- **Legal documents:** the will, trust, deed, insurance policies, powers of attorney.
- **Business records:** invoices, customer lists, anything with a due date.
- **Personal records:** photos, letters, family history, recipes.

Once you have them, copy to a new drive and keep the original untouched. See [how to find a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) for what to do with the information.

## When can I wipe or sell the old computer?

Not until the files are copied, the executor confirms nothing else is needed, and the estate is settled enough that no one will ask for records. Tax authorities can ask about a final return for several years, and a surviving spouse or business partner may need the same files. Once you are sure, do a proper factory reset before donating or selling, and remove any saved logins first. If the machine held client or medical information from the person's work, shred the drive instead of selling it.

## How can I make this easier for my own family?

Write a short note that says where each device is, which are important, and where the recovery keys are. Put it with your will, not in it, because a will usually becomes public. Our guide to [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) covers password managers and emergency access, and the [digital asset inventory guide](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use) gives a form you can fill in. Back up the important files to a second place that your executor can reach, and tell someone where it is.
