Most families discover how little a company will say only after they have called support. A grieving daughter explains that her father has died, gets a polite reply that nobody can discuss another person's account, and is sent to a form she cannot find. The process is not secret, but it rewards preparation. This page explains what to put in the packet, what each type of request can realistically get you, how a court order works when a company says no, and how to keep a trail so the next step is easier. It sits within our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What exactly are you asking for?

Be specific, because companies handle these requests in different tracks.

| What you want | How companies tend to treat it | Usually needed |
|---|---|---|
| Close the account and stop billing | The easiest request | Death certificate, proof of relationship or authority |
| A list of what exists | Often possible for the estate's representative | Letters from the court |
| A copy of content (emails, photos, files) | Often refused without a court order or earlier permission from the owner | Letters, and usually a court order |
| Full login access | Rarely granted | Almost never available, apart from a legacy contact |
| Memorialize a social profile | Possible on proof of death | Death certificate or an obituary link |

Think about which row you need. A son who only wants his mother's monthly cloud storage charge to stop is in the first row. A widow who needs two years of emails to prove a business deal is in the third. The more you ask for, the more documents you should expect to produce.

Also ask whether you need the account at all. Many things families want from email or a phone, such as bills and bank statements, are available from the original sources. Our checklist of [online accounts after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) shows how to rebuild the list another way.

## What goes in the packet?

Assemble it once and reuse it for every company.

1. **Certified death certificate.** Order extra copies. Our guide to [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need) helps you decide.
2. **Proof of your authority.** For an executor, letters testamentary. For an administrator, letters of administration. For other relatives, the company's own relationship form.
3. **Your photo ID.** A clear copy of a government-issued ID.
4. **The account identifier.** The exact email address, username, phone number or Apple ID. A request that names the wrong address goes nowhere.
5. **The will page that names you, if the company asks.** Often the letters are enough.
6. **A short, specific statement of purpose.** One or two sentences, such as "to close the account and download photos for the estate."
7. **A court order, if you have one.** More on that below.

Keep a log: the date, the company, the person you spoke to, the ticket number and the reply. That record helps if you later need to show a court that you tried the company's own process first.

## What do the big companies tend to do?

Company policies change, so check the current deceased-user page before you start. In general, as of 2026:

- **Apple** offers a Legacy Contact tool for people who set it up in advance. Without it, Apple generally asks for a court order naming the account, the owner and the requester before it will release data.
- **Google** has an Inactive Account Manager that the owner can set, and a deceased-user request process. Content release is limited, and Google commonly asks for a court order or other documents.
- **Meta** lets the family memorialize or remove a profile, and a pre-set legacy contact can manage a memorialized Facebook account in limited ways. Meta does not give out login details.
- **Microsoft** has a next-of-kin process, and content access commonly needs a court order.

The pattern is the same everywhere. Without prior permission from the owner, the company is protective of the content. Our articles on [legacy contacts](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) and on [social media after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) explain what each company's setting does.

## When do you need a court order?

When a company will not release content on letters alone, and the content matters. State law shapes the order. Most states, including Illinois and New Jersey, have adopted some version of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act. The act gives a personal representative a path to the catalog of records more easily than to the content, and lets the owner give permission ahead of time through a company tool or a will. The details are in our [RUFADAA explainer](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained). For Illinois specifically, see our article on [Illinois digital assets law for executors](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors).

A petition for an order is usually filed in the probate case the executor already has open. It asks the court to direct the company to disclose records or content. The petition often has to show the following:

- The person died and you are the personal representative.
- The account is identified, with the owner's name and address.
- The owner either consented or the disclosure is reasonably needed to administer the estate.
- The request is limited in scope, such as one account and a date range.

A narrow request is easier for a judge to sign and for a company's legal team to accept. "All emails, ever" invites pushback. "Emails between June and December 2025 with the contractor, needed to settle a disputed invoice" does not.

## How long does it take?

It varies a lot. A closure request sometimes completes in days. Content releases involve legal review and can take months. A court order adds the time of filing, a hearing if one is set and service on the company, which may have a registered agent in another state. The practical advice is to start the paperwork early, run requests in parallel, and not let one slow company hold up the estate.

## What can you do while you wait?

- **Preserve what you can.** If the phone is open, back it up. If you know the cloud password, download photos and documents now. Our piece on the [locked phone after death](/learn/digital-assets/locked-phone-after-death-texts-two-factor-codes-and-the-phone-number) covers the pitfalls.
- **Do not log in to someone else's account in a way that changes things.** Reading to find bills is one thing. Deleting, posting or changing settings is another.
- **Stop money leaks.** Cancel subscriptions through the card issuer or the original bank if a service is hard to reach.
- **Watch for fraud.** Place a deceased flag with the credit bureaus and check for new accounts.
- **Tell your attorney early.** If the digital material matters to the estate, as when a business depends on it, plan for a court order from the start.

## What if the company refuses?

Ask what the refusal rests on. Sometimes the problem is a missing document. Sometimes the company points to its terms of service or a federal privacy law. In the second case, a court order that finds the disclosure lawful is the usual answer. Appeals within the company are slow, but a clear, narrow petition with a strong reason is hard to ignore. Keep your tone factual. If the account holds nothing of financial value and the family only wants memories, an alternative may be to reconstruct them from other sources, such as photos on other family members' devices.

## How can you make this easier on your own family?

Use the tools while you are alive. Name a legacy contact, turn on emergency access in your password manager, and add a sentence to your will or power of attorney giving your fiduciary permission to access your digital accounts and content.
