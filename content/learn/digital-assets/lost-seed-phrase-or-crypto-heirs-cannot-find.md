Tom, 61, told his daughter Nadia a few years ago that he had "put a little into Bitcoin." After he died of a heart attack, she found no mention of it in his will. There were no exchange statements in his files. In a desk drawer sat a small black device that asked for a PIN. Tom's laptop had a browser extension she did not recognize. She could not tell whether there was ten dollars at stake or ten thousand times that.

This is the part of any [digital estate](/learn/digital-assets) that no legal document can fully fix after the fact. This article explains how to look for crypto, what each type of loss really means, what exchanges will and will not do, and where an executor's responsibility ends. If you want the broader planning picture, start with our overview of [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance).

## First question: is it on an exchange or in a wallet?

Almost every answer in this area turns on one distinction.

| | Held on an exchange or broker | Held in a self-custody wallet |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls the coins | The company, on the owner's behalf | Only whoever holds the keys |
| Can a password be reset | Yes, after identity checks | Not for the wallet itself |
| Can an executor recover them | Yes, through the deceased-user process | Only with the seed phrase or device and PIN |
| Can a court order help | Yes, the company will obey | Rarely, there is no one to order |
| Typical loss risk | Hack, freeze, bankruptcy | Lost keys, destroyed paper, theft |

An exchange account is a customer relationship. The company knows who its customers are, has their identification, and will deal with a representative who brings a death certificate and letters from the court. A self-custody wallet is different. The blockchain records which address holds the coins, but nothing links the address to a name, and nothing lets anyone move the coins without the key.

## How do you find out whether crypto exists?

Do this slowly and write down what you find. Most of the clues are ordinary financial paper.

1. **Bank and credit card statements.** Look for transfers to well-known exchanges, payment apps with a crypto feature, or ATM-type purchases. Go back at least three years.
2. **Tax returns.** The individual income tax return asks whether you received or sold digital assets during the year. A "yes" is a lead. Exchanges also send tax forms after sales.
3. **Email.** Search the inbox for the names of exchanges, "verify your identity", "withdrawal", "wallet", "recovery". Note that the executor's right to read email is limited, and our article on [whether an executor can access email](/learn/digital-assets/can-an-executor-access-email) explains the limits.
4. **The phone and computer.** Look for wallet apps and browser extensions. Do not delete or "clean up" anything. Do not open a wallet app and send coins.
5. **Physical clues.** Small devices that look like USB sticks, steel plates with engraved words, notebooks with 12 or 24 lines of words, a metal or paper card in a safe.
6. **People.** Ask the family and close friends. People who owned crypto often told someone, even if they never wrote it down.

If you find evidence of a wallet but no keys, you are in a different situation from finding an exchange account. Our article on [hardware wallets and NFTs after death](/learn/digital-assets/hardware-wallets-and-nfts-after-death) explains what to do with the device and where recovery words are usually kept.

## What does a lost seed phrase really mean?

A seed phrase is the master backup for a wallet. If you have it, you can rebuild the wallet on any compatible device. If you do not, and the original device is gone or locked beyond recovery, the coins stay on the blockchain forever and no one can spend them.

That sounds harsh, and it is. A few points soften it.

- **The device is not the wallet.** A broken or lost hardware wallet is not a loss if the words exist. Many people store the words in a different place from the device.
- **The password may not matter.** Some wallet apps use a password on one computer. With the seed phrase, you can restore the wallet without it.
- **A forgotten PIN on a device can sometimes be bypassed by the seed phrase.** The device can be reset and restored with the words.
- **Paid "recovery services" are mostly a risk.** Be wary of anyone who offers to crack a wallet for a share. Scams in this area are common, and they target grieving families. A legitimate attempt to recover an old password the family partly remembers is a narrow exception, and an attorney can help you judge it.

If the seed phrase is truly gone and cannot be found after a diligent search, the executor should record the facts, list the asset in the inventory as "reported, unrecovered," and explain it to the heirs. Do not guess a value.

## What can an exchange do for the family?

Most large exchanges publish a procedure for deceased customers. It usually involves a death certificate, identification for the person asking, proof of authority such as [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary), and sometimes a form with the account details. The company freezes the account, verifies the documents, and then either pays out in cash, transfers the assets to an account the estate controls, or closes the account.

Two practical points. First, do not try to log in, even to look. Some exchanges lock an account when unusual access occurs, and you may make the process harder. Second, an exchange can refuse a request if the paperwork is incomplete or if two people claim to be the representative, so a clean court appointment matters.

If an exchange will not cooperate after a full submission, an attorney can write to its legal department or, if necessary, ask the probate court for an order. Most delays are paperwork, not refusal.

## What if the exchange itself fails?

When a crypto exchange goes into bankruptcy, customers' claims are handled by the bankruptcy court. Whether customer coins are treated as belonging to the customer or as part of the company's assets depends on the exchange's terms and the facts, and courts have not reached a uniform answer. Typically, you file a claim as a customer and wait. You might receive a payment in dollars based on the value at the filing date, which can be very different from today's price.

For the executor, this means the claim is an asset of the estate and has to be listed and followed. It also means that leaving all of your holdings on a single platform is a risk you should weigh when you plan. Keeping some holdings at an insured bank or broker and some in self-custody under a clear plan spreads that risk.

## Is the executor responsible if coins are lost?

An executor is a fiduciary and must act with care, but care is measured by what a prudent person would do with the information available. The law does not require the impossible.

A careful executor does five things: searches for the assets, secures any devices and papers, contacts custodians promptly, keeps a log of every step, and tells the heirs what was and was not found. If those boxes are checked, the loss of an unreachable wallet is not a breach. An executor who cannot handle technology should say so early and consider hiring a technical consultant, paid from the estate, which most courts accept as a reasonable expense. This is also where a named [digital executor](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) can help.

Executors should not sell, swap or move coins in a hurry, and should not take them for themselves. Our article on [what an executor can and cannot take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate) explains the limits.

## What should you do now so your family is not in Nadia's position?

A single sheet of paper, kept in a safe place, solves most of this. It should name each exchange and wallet, say where the recovery words are stored, and say who is allowed to get them. Do not put the words in your will, because a will can become public. Our article on [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust) walks through the legal choices, and [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) covers safe ways to share the keys.
