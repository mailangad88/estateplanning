Weeks after the funeral, the mail keeps coming. Credit card offers addressed to your father. Catalogs for your mother. Emails from stores that think she is still a customer. Each piece is a small reminder, and some of it is a risk. This page explains how to stop it and how to use it.

The rest of this article explains who can make each request, what to expect, and why some of this mail is worth reading before you throw it out. For the wider set of tasks on the digital side, see our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What stops junk mail for someone who has died?

Three actions do most of the work, and none of them needs a lawyer. Do them together so the effect builds.

1. **Register on the Deceased Do Not Contact list.** The DMAchoice program, run by a national marketing association, keeps a list of people who have died. Mailers that follow industry practice remove those names from their lists. Anyone close to the person can submit the name, address and date of death.
2. **Opt out of pre-screened credit and insurance offers.** The credit bureaus run a joint opt-out site, optoutprescreen.com. For a deceased person you can opt out online or by mail. This stops the pre-approved card offers that carry personal information and can be stolen from a mailbox.
3. **Notify the three credit bureaus of the death.** Each bureau will flag the file as deceased, which blocks new credit in the person's name. Send a copy of the death certificate and, if you are the executor, a copy of your letters. Our article on [who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) lists the others to call.

Give it about three months. Mailers prepare their lists weeks ahead, so the first wave arrives anyway. After that the volume drops. Mail that does not stop is usually from a company with a real account relationship, which needs a direct call.

## Who has the authority to do this?

For opt-out lists, almost anyone. A request to put a name on a suppression list does not give you control of anything, so the programs generally accept it from family members. For anything involving accounts, authority matters. The executor, appointed by the probate court, is the person with legal standing. A power of attorney does not help, because it ends at death. If you hold one and the person has died, our blog post on [what happens to a power of attorney at death](/blog/what-happens-to-a-power-of-attorney-when-you-die) explains why you should stop signing.

If no executor has been appointed yet, a close family member can still take the protective steps, such as the opt-outs and the credit flag, while the family decides who will petition the court. You do not need to wait for letters to protect against identity theft.

## How do you sort the mail?

Not all of it is junk. A pile of mail is the closest thing to a map of the person's financial life, and families who toss it early often regret it. Sort it into these piles before you act.

| Type of mail | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Catalogs and charity appeals | Marketing lists | Opt out through the Deceased Do Not Contact list |
| Pre-approved credit or insurance offers | Credit bureau lists | Use the prescreen opt-out and flag the credit files |
| Statements from a bank, brokerage or insurer you did not know about | A real account | Keep it; notify the institution and list it as an asset |
| Bills and past-due notices | A debt or a subscription | Do not pay yet; see the creditor claim steps for your state |
| Notices from the state about unclaimed property | Money the state is holding | Check the state's unclaimed property database |
| Tax forms such as a 1099 | Income that must be reported | Give to the person preparing the final return |

A true example. Beth, 44, was sorting her late father's mail in Evanston, Illinois, and found a yearly statement from a mutual fund she had never heard of. It held about $11,000. Her father's bank accounts had been the whole list in his files. The statement was the only evidence of the fund, and without it the executor would have closed the estate and missed it. Our guide to [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) uses the same approach.

## What about the post office?

The post office will let a court-appointed executor or administrator file a change of address for the estate. You will usually need your letters of authority and the death certificate, and the request is made in person at a post office. Forwarded first-class mail is then delivered to you. This is useful for the first few months, when bills and bank statements still go to the old address.

Without that authority, do not fill out the form yourself in the person's name. A family member can write "Deceased, return to sender" on junk mail, and many carriers will stop delivering some of it. For a house that is vacant, mail piling up is a signal to burglars, which is covered in our article on [vacant inherited houses](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters).

## What do you do about spam email?

Treat the inbox as sensitive. Do not reply to messages or click links in them, and do not unsubscribe from anything you do not recognize. A scam sender who gets a click knows the address is live. Read the list of senders instead. Subscriptions you can cancel, receipts from online stores and notices from banks tell you which accounts exist.

Whether you may open the email at all depends on authority, and the rules differ from account to account. We explain this in [can an executor access email](/learn/digital-assets/can-an-executor-access-email). In both Illinois and New Jersey, a fiduciary has a statutory path to a deceased person's digital records. See the state articles on [Illinois digital assets law for executors](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) and [New Jersey digital assets law](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law).

Set up a rule to forward or label financial senders so you do not miss a bill. If you are unsure which recurring charges to stop, check the person's card statements and use our checklist on [subscriptions after death](/learn/digital-assets/subscriptions-and-purchased-media-after-death).

## Can the unclaimed property mail help?

Yes, it is one of the more useful kinds. States hold forgotten bank balances, uncashed checks and insurance payouts. Illinois and New Jersey both keep searchable databases, and an executor can claim on behalf of the estate. The steps are in [finding unclaimed property for an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/finding-unclaimed-property-for-an-illinois-estate) and [for a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/finding-unclaimed-property-for-a-new-jersey-estate). Beware of letters that charge a fee to find property for you. The state search is free.

## When should you worry about fraud?

Pay attention to anything that mentions the death, because some scammers read obituaries. Warning signs include a caller who says the person owed money and demands payment today, a letter that asks for a Social Security number to release a refund, and a request for fees to open a safe deposit box. Real creditors send written notices and wait. Do not pay a debt from your own money, since you are not responsible for it unless you co-signed.
