A hardware wallet looks like a small USB stick or a key fob. It might be in a desk drawer next to the spare batteries. Your family may not know what it is, and if it holds six figures of crypto, that matters a great deal.

This article covers the two assets that cause the most confusion for heirs: the physical device and the NFT. If you want the general picture of how crypto passes at death, start with our guide to [cryptocurrency and estate planning](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance). Here we go one level deeper, into what a person standing in front of the device actually has to do. It is part of the [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What does a hardware wallet actually hold?

It holds the private keys that sign transactions, not the coins. The coins are entries on a public blockchain. The device is a locked box for the key, built so the key never leaves it.

That design has a consequence people miss. If the device is lost, broken or thrown out, nothing is gone. Anyone who has the recovery words, usually 12 or 24 of them, can load the same wallet into a new device and see everything. The reverse is also true. A person holding the device with the PIN, but not the words, has an incomplete picture, and a person holding the words but no device is fine.

So an heir needs to understand three separate things:

| Item | What it does | If it is missing |
|---|---|---|
| The device | Signs transactions after you enter the PIN | Replaceable if you have the words |
| The PIN | Unlocks the device for everyday use | Several wrong guesses can wipe the device, which is fine if the words exist |
| The recovery words | Rebuild the wallet on any compatible device | Nothing else can substitute. This is the asset |

Some owners also add a passphrase, sometimes called the "25th word," and some use multi-signature setups where more than one key is needed to move funds. Each of these is a legitimate security choice that doubles as a way to lock out the people who inherit.

## What goes wrong when the owner dies?

The most common failure is not theft. It is a family that cannot tell what they have, so they do the wrong thing. A few real patterns:

- A relative clears out the house and donates the box of old electronics.
- Someone guesses the PIN repeatedly, the device wipes, and the only copy of the words turns out to be on a sticky note that went in the shredder.
- The words are found and typed into a website that turns out to be a phishing page, and the funds are drained in minutes.
- The owner used a passphrase, so the restored wallet looks empty and the family stops looking.

Scams are worth taking seriously. After a death, anyone who offers to "recover" the wallet for an upfront fee, or who asks for the words over the phone, is a thief. A legitimate adviser never needs you to read the words aloud.

## What should an heir do first?

Slow down. The coins are not going anywhere in the next week, and every mistake is permanent.

1. **Secure the device and anything near it.** Leave it as found. Do not turn it on and do not try PINs.
2. **Photograph where things were found**, such as a metal plate in a safe or a card in a book. You will want the record later.
3. **Look for a guide.** A letter, a note in the will file or something held by the owner's attorney may explain the setup.
4. **Get legal authority.** The executor or trustee should be formally appointed before moving anything, so there is no later argument about who took what.
5. **Do the recovery on a fresh, genuine device** bought directly from the manufacturer. Test with a small transfer first.
6. **Record the date-of-death value** of each holding for the estate and for the heirs' tax basis.

The executor's work is the same as with any asset: identify it, protect it, value it and distribute it. Our overview of [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) walks through that order. Crypto simply adds a technical gate in the middle.

## How are NFTs different from coins?

An NFT is a unique token recorded on a blockchain, and it sits in a wallet like any other. If you control the wallet, you control the NFT. That is the whole practical story for inheritance. The legal story has a twist.

What you own is often smaller than it seems. Many NFT sales give the buyer the token and a limited license to display the artwork, not the copyright. The terms came from the original seller's website or smart contract. A person who inherits the token inherits whatever rights the buyer had, which may be little. This is why we suggest saving the sale terms along with the wallet details.

NFTs also bring a few extras that coins do not:

- **Marketplace accounts.** Some NFTs are listed or stored through a marketplace login. That login is an online account, so the rules in our guide to [what an executor can access online](/learn/digital-assets/can-an-executor-access-email) and the state law explained in [RUFADAA](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained) apply to it.
- **Linked accounts and names.** A token may give access to a community, a game or a domain-style name. Those benefits may be tied to the person and not transfer.
- **Odd values.** One token may be worth almost nothing, another a great deal, and a quoted floor price may mean nothing for your specific piece.
- **Gas fees.** Moving a token costs a network fee, which the estate pays.

## How is the tax treatment handled?

Inheriting a token is not income. For federal tax purposes it is treated as property, and heirs generally receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) to the value on the date of death. If Rachel inherits an NFT her father bought for $1,500 that was worth $9,000 when he died, her basis is $9,000 and only later growth is taxed when she sells.

The hard part is the number. A widely traded coin has a price on a given date. A one-of-a-kind NFT may not, so the executor should keep the evidence: comparable sales, marketplace listings and, for anything meaningful, a written appraisal. The same evidence supports the estate tax return if the estate is large enough to need one. The federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most estates owe no federal estate tax, though some states tax estates at lower levels. Our page on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains the rule in more detail, and a tax adviser who has handled digital assets is worth paying.

## What should you leave behind if you own these assets?

Treat this like leaving instructions for a pilot who has never flown your plane. You are writing for one person, on one bad day.

1. **List what you have**, by type: which device, which blockchain, which marketplace. Do not list the words on the same page.
2. **Say where the words are** and who can reach them. Two metal backups in two places, one of them a safe deposit box or your attorney's vault, is a sensible pattern. Our guide on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) covers the principles.
3. **State whether you use a passphrase or multi-signature** and where those pieces are.
4. **Write the legal side down**: name an executor or trustee and authorize them in your will or trust to handle digital assets. See [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust) for the wording choices.
5. **Add a letter of instruction** to your documents. A [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) is where the plain-English guide belongs.
6. **Test it once.** Ask the person you chose to walk through a restore with a nearly empty wallet.

Take Marcus, 52, who owns a modest amount of Bitcoin on a hardware wallet and a few NFTs bought during a hobby phase. His brother Dev is his executor. Marcus keeps the words on two steel plates, one at home and one with his attorney. A two-page letter explains the wallet, says there is no passphrase, and says the NFTs matter mostly as keepsakes. Dev did a practice restore last spring. If Marcus died tomorrow, Dev would know what to do and what not to touch.

## Should the wallet be held by a trust?

A device cannot be retitled, so the trust works through authority and instructions, not a change of ownership. A common approach is to give your successor trustee the power to take control of digital assets, and to leave the physical backups where the trustee can reach them. Some families prefer a custody company for part of the holdings because it removes the single point of failure. That choice involves trade-offs in cost and control that an attorney can help you weigh.

If you have a large holding, think about whether a single heir should be handed it. A trustee who can release funds gradually is sometimes safer than a lump sum to someone who has never held a seed phrase.
