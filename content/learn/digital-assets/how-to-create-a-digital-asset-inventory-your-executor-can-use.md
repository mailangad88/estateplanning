Nobody wakes up wanting to catalog their online life. Yet when a person dies, the first question family asks is almost always the same: what did they have, and where is it? A good inventory answers that in one sitting. A missing one can cost months.

This article shows how to build one that an executor can use without a technical background. For the wider legal picture, see our [guide to including digital assets in your plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan) and the [digital assets hub](/learn/digital-assets).

## What goes in a digital asset inventory?

Anything online or on a device that has money, memories, access or obligations attached. Sort it into five groups.

| Group | Examples | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Gatekeepers | Main email, mobile number, password manager, Apple or Google account | They unlock everything else through password resets |
| Money | Online banking, brokerage, PayPal, Venmo, crypto exchange, wallets | Balances and tax reporting |
| Business and income | Domain, website, online store, ad or channel revenue | Ongoing value and deadlines |
| Memories | Cloud photos, videos, writing, journals | Cannot be replaced |
| Recurring | Subscriptions, memberships, utilities with autopay, loyalty accounts | Charges continue after death |

Add devices as a sixth group: phones, tablets, laptops, external drives, hardware wallets, smart speakers. Note the model and the lock method (not the code). The phone deserves special attention because it often receives the two-factor codes for the other accounts. Our article on [locked phones after death](/learn/digital-assets/locked-phone-after-death-texts-two-factor-codes-and-the-phone-number) explains why.

## How do you build the list, step by step?

You can do it in an hour or two. Most people stall because they try to remember everything. Start from evidence instead.

1. **Open your email and search for "welcome," "receipt," "your account" and "statement."** This turns up the accounts you forgot you had.
2. **Check the phone's app screen and settings.** Look at the subscriptions list in the phone's account settings; it shows recurring charges.
3. **Scan three months of bank and card statements.** Recurring merchants are accounts.
4. **Look at the browser's saved passwords.** Each saved login is a candidate.
5. **List every device,** including the old ones in a drawer that still have photos on them.
6. **Write one line per item:** what it is, where it lives, who should deal with it, and what to do (keep, transfer, close, or delete).
7. **Add a pointer to the keys.** For example, "Logins in the password manager; the master password is in the envelope with my attorney."
8. **Save the list and tell your executor where it is.**

Take Ruth, 64, a retired teacher. After step one she finds an old photo-printing account, a genealogy subscription she forgot about, and a brokerage login she opened in 2015 and never used. The last one had a few thousand dollars in it. No list she made from memory would have caught that.

## What should you leave out?

Leave out the secrets. The inventory should never contain:

- Passwords or PINs
- The master password to the password manager
- A seed phrase or private key
- Answers to security questions

If the document is lost or photographed, nothing in it should let a stranger in. Think of it as a directory in a building lobby: it tells you which floor, not how to open the door.

The keys need their own plan. For logins, a password manager with an emergency access feature is the usual answer, and we cover the options in [passwords, password managers and two-factor authentication](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access). For crypto, see [what happens when the seed phrase is missing](/learn/digital-assets/lost-seed-phrase-or-crypto-heirs-cannot-find).

## Where should you keep it?

Somewhere your executor can reach quickly, and somewhere safe from fire and casual snooping. Options differ in tradeoffs.

| Place | Good | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| With your estate documents at home | Easy to find, familiar | Fire or theft; keep it in a fireproof box |
| Inside a password manager as a secure note | Updated in one place | The executor needs access to the manager |
| With your attorney | Safe, and retrievable at death | Updating means sending a new copy |
| Safe deposit box | Very secure | Banks often seal the box at death until court papers arrive |
| Cloud folder shared with the executor | Instant access | Shared folders can be found by others, and cloud accounts are themselves part of the problem |

A common arrangement is two copies: one with your estate planning documents, and a second inside the password manager. Our guide on [organizing your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents) covers the paper side.

A safe deposit box deserves one more caution. If the only copy of your inventory, or your crypto recovery phrase, sits in a box that the bank will not open without letters from the court, your executor will wait weeks for the very information needed to apply for those letters. Use a box if you like, but also give the executor written authority and a second route to the facts.

## How do you protect photos and files for your heirs?

Photos are the item families miss most, and they are the easiest to protect.

- Keep a full copy somewhere other than your phone. Phones fail, are lost, and become locked.
- Keep a second copy on a different medium, such as an external drive stored at a relative's house.
- Organize by year or event so the person who inherits the folder can find what they want.
- Decide who gets what. If one child cares most about the family-trip photos and another about the baby pictures, say so in your letter of instruction.
- Once a year, plug in the drive and open a file. Drives that sit untouched can fail without warning.

For how a family actually opens a cloud library after a death, see [photos, cloud storage and iPhones after death](/learn/digital-assets/photos-and-iphones-after-death).

## What if you are the executor and there is no inventory?

Then you build one backwards, and the same evidence works. Collect the mail for several months, since statements and renewal notices keep arriving. Ask the bank for a list of recurring payments. Pull a credit report for the person, which shows loans and cards. Look through the phone's subscription settings if you are legally able to open it. Our guide to [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) walks through that search.

It works, but it is slow, and it is the work an inventory exists to save. Ten minutes of your own effort now removes a weekend of detective work for someone who is grieving.

## How often should you update it?

Once a year is enough for most people, and the review should take ten minutes. Set it to the same date you check your smoke alarms or file taxes. Update it sooner after a new phone, a new bank, a business launch or any crypto purchase. Our broader article on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) covers the wider life events.

If maintaining a detailed list feels like too much, keep a short version: the gatekeepers, the money, and the pointer to the keys. A short list that is current is worth far more than a thorough one that is five years old.
