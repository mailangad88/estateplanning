You have an email account, a phone full of photos, maybe some cryptocurrency, and a vague sense that someone should be able to get at all of it if you die. The question is how much of that you can handle on your own and where a lawyer actually earns the fee.

This page sorts the work into two piles: the parts anyone can do, and the parts where the wording matters. It sits inside our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets), which covers the individual account types in more detail.

## The short version

- You do not need a lawyer to make an inventory, set up a password manager, or name a Legacy Contact or Inactive Account Manager.
- You do need accurate legal language if you want your executor or agent to read your messages or open your cloud files.
- A digital plan is normally part of a will, trust and power of attorney, so it is rarely priced alone.
- The cost of a regular plan depends mostly on whether you need a will or a trust, and on your state. See [what an estate plan costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost).
- A free template is better than nothing. It is not better than a short, correct paragraph from someone who knows your state's law.

## What is the difference between a digital estate plan and a regular one?

A regular estate plan answers who gets your property, who is in charge, who speaks for you if you cannot. A digital plan answers a different set of questions: who can open the account, who may read what is inside, and what should happen to things with no dollar value at all.

Consider Priya, 52. Her will leaves everything to her two sons and names her sister as executor. That is a fine regular plan. But Priya's mother's last voicemail is on her phone, her photos are in a cloud account tied to a two-factor code sent to that same phone, and her small bookkeeping business takes payments through an app. Nothing in her will touches any of that. The sister can be appointed executor and still stand in front of a locked screen.

So the digital layer has four parts:

1. An inventory of what exists and where.
2. A way for the right person to get in (keys, tools, instructions).
3. Legal permission, written into your documents, for that person to access content.
4. Your wishes about what to keep, hand over or delete.

Parts one, two and four are personal organization. Part three is law.

## What can you safely do yourself?

Quite a lot, and you should. A lawyer cannot make an inventory for you because you are the only one who knows what is on the phone.

| Task | Do it yourself? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| List accounts, devices and subscriptions | Yes | Start with email, phone, bank, cloud storage and anything with a balance. |
| Set up a password manager | Yes | Use its emergency access feature if it has one. |
| Name an Apple Legacy Contact | Yes | Done in your device settings. |
| Set up Google Inactive Account Manager | Yes | Done in your Google account settings. |
| Choose Facebook or Instagram memorialization | Yes | Settings on each platform. |
| Write a letter of instruction | Yes | Not legally binding, but useful. See our [letter of instruction guide](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction). |
| Draft consent language for message content | Better with a lawyer | Must match state law and your other documents. |
| Coordinate who is executor, agent and trustee | Better with a lawyer | Gaps between roles cause most of the trouble. |

The company tools in particular work independently of your will. If you name a Legacy Contact, the company follows that choice, whatever the will says. That is a reason to set them up carefully and to make sure they match what your documents say. We explain each in [setting up a digital legacy contact](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts).

## Where does a lawyer actually matter?

In three places.

**The consent language.** Federal privacy law generally bars a provider from handing over the content of electronic communications unless the user consented. Most states have adopted a model act, covered in [what RUFADAA is](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained), that lets you give that consent in a will, trust or power of attorney. The provider decides whether your wording is clear enough. A vague line such as "my executor may access my digital property" may not satisfy a compliance team. A precise clause that names content of communications, catalogs, and the specific authority to download or close accounts usually does.

**The overlap between documents.** Your will operates after death. Your power of attorney operates during life. A revocable trust, if you have one, may own some assets. The digital permissions have to appear in the right documents or there will be a gap. A will with careful language and a power of attorney with none means your agent cannot help you during a long illness, which is when many people most need someone to deal with a locked account.

**Assets with real money attached.** Cryptocurrency, an online store, a domain name that earns revenue, a monetized channel. These are property, and who owns them, who runs them and how they are valued are legal questions. Our articles on [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust) and [online businesses after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-business-etsy-shop-or-youtube-channel-after-death) go through the choices.

## How much does a digital estate plan cost?

There is no standard price, because it is usually not sold as its own item. For most people it is a handful of added paragraphs and a schedule in documents they are already signing. What moves the price is the rest of the plan: a simple will costs less than a trust package, a couple costs more than one person, and some states add notarization or witness rules.

Where it can add real time is when the digital side is substantial. A person with an online business, significant crypto or several devices full of irreplaceable material may need a bespoke schedule, a trust to hold certain assets, and a conversation about custody. That is a drafting and planning cost, not a software one.

Ask any attorney you consult two questions: is the digital language included in the quoted fee, and will it appear in the power of attorney as well as the will? The answers tell you whether it is handled or merely mentioned. Our article on [making an estate plan without a lawyer](/learn/basics/making-an-estate-plan-without-a-lawyer) discusses where the do-it-yourself route works and where it breaks.

## Is an online will or a template good enough for digital assets?

For a simple situation, it can be a reasonable start. A single person with an email account, a phone and a bank app can use a template, set up the company tools, and keep a good inventory. The risk is in what a template cannot see.

A template does not know that you also hold a hardware wallet. It does not ask who will pay the domain renewal next March. It will often use the same digital paragraph for every user, which means it cannot reflect that you want your daughter to inherit the photos but your brother to deal with the business accounts. And if it was written before your state adopted the model act, the wording may be outdated.

If you use one, read the digital section out loud. If you cannot tell what it permits, neither will the provider.

## When should you create a digital estate plan, and how often should you update it?

Start when you first own something you would be upset to lose or something your family would struggle to reach. For most adults that is already true. Do not wait for the full estate plan to be finished; the inventory and company tools take an afternoon.

After that, a short annual review is enough, plus an update after any of these:

1. You change phones or switch between Apple and Android.
2. You open or close a bank, brokerage or crypto account.
3. You change password managers or reset the master password.
4. Someone named in the plan dies, divorces you or moves away.
5. You move to another state and sign new documents there.

The legal language is more stable than the practical details. The pointer to where the keys are kept is the item that most often goes wrong, so check that one first. Our [guidance on when to update your plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) covers the wider triggers.
