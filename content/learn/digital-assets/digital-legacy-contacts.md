When someone dies, their photos, email, cloud files and social profiles do not disappear, and they are not automatically available to family either. Each company decides who can get in, and most of them say no without a court order or a choice the account owner made earlier. A legacy contact is that earlier choice.

This article walks through the main tools, what each one gives your contact, and how to fit them into the rest of your plan. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets). Company settings and wording change, so treat the steps below as a map and confirm them in each company's current help pages when you set up.

## What is a legacy contact, and what is it not?

A legacy contact is a person you name inside a particular company's account settings. After your death, that person can ask the company for a defined level of access, or manage a memorial version of your account. The right is created by the platform's own rules, not by your will.

That has three consequences:

- It covers only that platform. A contact at Apple has no say over your Google account.
- It does not make the person your executor. Your [executor](/glossary/executor) handles the estate, the debts and the legal side.
- It operates alongside state and federal law. Most states have adopted a version of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, which generally respects an owner's choice in an online tool first, then a will or other document, and then the company's terms. A federal privacy law, the Stored Communications Act, also limits when companies may disclose the content of communications.

## How does Apple Legacy Contact work?

Apple lets you name one or more legacy contacts in your Apple Account settings, on an iPhone, iPad or Mac running a recent software version. Find it under your account name, then Sign-In & Security, then Legacy Contact.

1. Open the setting and choose Add Legacy Contact.
2. Pick a person from your family sharing group or your contacts.
3. Apple creates an access key, which you share with the person by message or print and give to them. Without it, they cannot request access.
4. After your death, the contact submits the access key and a copy of your death certificate to Apple.
5. If Apple approves, the contact can access much of what is stored in iCloud, such as photos, messages, notes, files and device backups, for a limited period.

There are limits. Apple states that certain items, including purchased media and some secure data like keychain information, are not available to a legacy contact. The contact also needs the key itself, so storing a copy somewhere findable matters. Review who you named whenever your relationships change.

## How does Google Inactive Account Manager work?

Google's tool, called Inactive Account Manager, works on a timer instead of a death certificate. You set a period of inactivity, and when your account passes it, Google contacts the people you chose.

1. Go to your Google Account, open Data & Privacy, and look for Make a plan for your digital legacy.
2. Choose how long the account must be inactive before the plan starts, from 3 to 18 months.
3. Add up to 10 trusted contacts and their phone numbers or emails. Google may verify them.
4. Decide which data each contact can download, for example Gmail, Drive or Photos, and write an optional message.
5. Decide whether the account should be deleted after contacts are notified.

The contacts are notified only after the inactivity period ends, and you are warned beforehand by message to your phone or backup email. If you do nothing, Google says it may delete accounts that have been inactive for two years, so a missed setup can cost you your photos and email history. Because it triggers on inactivity and not a death, the tool also works if you become seriously ill and stop using the account.

## What about Facebook, Instagram and other social media?

Meta, which owns Facebook and Instagram, offers a legacy contact for Facebook. You choose a friend or family member in your account settings. After your account is memorialized, that person can do limited things such as pin a post, respond to new friend requests or update a profile photo. They cannot log in as you or read your private messages. You can also tell Facebook you want your account deleted when you die. Instagram offers memorialization and removal on request, but its legacy features differ.

Other services vary widely. LinkedIn, X and many others let family request removal or deactivation with proof of death, with no advance tool. Our article on [social media after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) compares the main services in detail.

| Platform | Tool | Setup | What the contact can reach | Main limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apple | Legacy Contact | Account settings; share access key | Photos, messages, notes, files, backups | Needs key and death certificate; purchased media and some secure data excluded |
| Google | Inactive Account Manager | Account settings; 3 to 18 months; up to 10 contacts | Data types you choose | Triggers on inactivity; Google may delete long-inactive accounts if no plan |
| Facebook | Legacy contact | Facebook settings | Memorialized profile tools | Cannot read messages or log in |
| Instagram | Memorialize or remove | Request by family | None from a contact | No active legacy contact like Facebook |
| Password managers | Emergency access | Vendor settings | Vault, after a waiting period | Depends on vendor and plan |

## What about password managers and everything else?

Most major password managers offer an emergency access or family feature. You name a trusted person, who can request access after a waiting period that you set. This can be the most powerful tool in the list, because a vault may hold the logins to everything else. Our article on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) explains how to use one safely and why two-factor authentication needs a plan of its own.

Then there are the accounts without any legacy tool. These need a note in your inventory, and your executor will need to follow each company's process:

- Email accounts outside Google, bank and brokerage portals, and utilities
- Cloud storage and photo services
- Domain names and website hosting
- Cryptocurrency exchanges and wallets, covered in [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance)
- Subscriptions, loyalty points and software licenses

## How does your will fit in?

The platform tools handle access. Your will, or your [financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) while you are alive, grants legal authority. A good clause names a digital executor or authorizes your executor to access and manage your accounts, and expressly allows disclosure of content where the law permits. Our article on [including digital assets in your plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan) covers sample clauses.

Do not put passwords in the will itself. Wills become public documents in [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate), and passwords change. Keep the instructions in a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) and a password manager, and tell your executor where to find them.

## What is a 20-minute setup checklist?

Here is a realistic sequence for one sitting.

1. Choose one or two people you trust, with a backup, and ask whether they are willing.
2. Set up Apple Legacy Contact if you use an iPhone or Mac.
3. Set up Google Inactive Account Manager if you use Gmail, Drive or Photos.
4. Set a Facebook legacy contact, or choose deletion, if you use it.
5. Turn on emergency access in your password manager.
6. Write a short list of your important accounts and where the instructions are, in a place your executor can find.
7. Add or update a digital assets clause in your will, and add a calendar reminder to revisit all of this each year.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **A locked phone and tied-up two-factor codes.** If codes go to a phone that no one can unlock, the accounts behind it may be unreachable.
- **A contact who has died or fallen out of touch.** Review the names yearly.
- **Assuming a family sharing group solves everything.** It shares purchases, not legal access.
- **Logging in as the deceased person.** Using someone's login can violate company terms and, in some cases, the law. Use the legal process instead.
