Priya inherited her father's crypto after he died last spring. It was Bitcoin and a smaller amount of Ether on an exchange. He had paid very little for it years ago. She sold half in the fall to cover a roof repair, and in January her tax preparer asked for two things: the price on the day her father died and the date she took control. She had neither.

That is how crypto tax problems usually start, not with a rule that is hard, but with a record that was never kept. This article explains the basis reset, who owes what on a sale, how the executor values coins that trade around the clock, how staking and other income is handled, and the points where state tax and estate tax enter.

## The short version

- Inheriting crypto is not income.
- The heir's basis is the date-of-death value in most cases.
- Sell later and you are taxed on the change since that date.
- Income earned after death is taxed as income.
- The executor should record the value, source and wallet details on day one.

## Is inherited crypto taxable income?

No. The federal income tax does not treat an inheritance as income to the person who receives it. Crypto is property in the eyes of the IRS, and property received at death follows the same rules as stock or a house. The tax questions come later, when the heir sells, spends or otherwise disposes of the coins.

An inheritance tax is different. A handful of states tax what an heir receives, often at lower rates for close relatives. That is separate from the federal system, and our article on [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) lists how it works. The federal estate tax applies only to estates above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and a married couple can shelter $30,000,000 with portability. See [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

## How does the basis reset work?

Property inherited from a person who died generally gets a new tax basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death. That is the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), which is a step-down when the asset lost value. Basis is the starting number used to compute gain or loss when you sell.

| Scenario | Original cost | Date-of-death value | Sale price | Taxable gain or loss to the heir |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rises after death | $4,000 | $60,000 | $75,000 | $15,000 gain |
| Sold soon after | $4,000 | $60,000 | $60,500 | $500 gain |
| Falls after death | $4,000 | $60,000 | $52,000 | $8,000 loss |
| Value fell before death | $90,000 | $30,000 | $30,000 | None, and the earlier loss is gone |

Inherited property is also generally treated as held for more than one year, so any gain is long-term, even if the owner bought it a few months before dying. Long-term rates are lower than ordinary rates for most people.

Priya's father paid $3,000 for his Bitcoin. On the date he died it was worth $48,000. Her basis is $48,000. If she sells at $50,000, her gain is $2,000, not $47,000. That one fact changes the decision about whether to keep or sell.

## How does the executor value crypto on the date of death?

There is no single government price table for coins, and prices differ across exchanges and move every second. The task is to choose a fair, defensible method, apply it consistently and write it down.

1. **List every holding.** Wallet addresses, exchange accounts, tokens and amounts at the moment of death.
2. **Choose a source.** A major U.S. exchange or a widely used price aggregator is a reasonable choice.
3. **Choose a method.** Many executors use the average of the high and low for the day, or a quote at a consistent time. Use the same method for every asset.
4. **Capture evidence.** Save a screenshot or a CSV export that shows the date, the time zone and the source.
5. **Record who received what and when.** For basis purposes, the date the heir takes control matters too.
6. **Give the numbers to the heirs and the tax preparer.** Each heir needs a basis for the coins they receive.

A large estate that files a federal estate tax return can elect to value everything six months after death, but only if that lowers both the estate's value and the tax. That election does not help an estate below the filing threshold. The [estate tax return](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) article explains the filing triggers.

Hardware wallets and self-custody holdings need extra care because the coins cannot be viewed through an exchange. The executor needs the public address, which can be looked up on a public blockchain explorer, not the private key. See [hardware wallets and NFTs after death](/learn/digital-assets/hardware-wallets-and-nfts-after-death).

## What about staking rewards, airdrops and interest?

Income that arrives after death is taxed as income, not as part of the stepped-up basis. If the estate still holds the coins when a staking reward is paid, the reward is income to the estate and the estate may need to file its own income tax return. After the coins are distributed to an heir, the heir reports later rewards on their own return. In either case, the value of the reward when received becomes the basis of that new coin.

Rewards earned before death were the decedent's income and belong on the final return. The executor should check the last year of exchange statements for staking income that was never reported, because exchanges report some of it and not all. Our article on [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) explains what to file.

Airdrops, forks and similar events are fact-specific. If you are not sure how to treat a particular token that appeared after the death, ask a preparer who handles crypto, and have the transaction history in hand.

## What happens when an heir sells?

The heir reports the sale on their own return. The calculation is proceeds minus basis. Exchanges and brokers are phasing in new reporting forms for crypto sales, which means the exchange may report your proceeds to the IRS but may not know your inherited basis. If the form shows a basis of zero or the original owner's cost, you correct it with your own records. This is where the date-of-death number pays for itself.

If the coins stay on an exchange in the decedent's name until the executor transfers them, the transfer itself is not a sale. A transfer from the estate to the heir is a distribution. A sale by the estate before distribution creates a gain or loss on the estate's return. Which one is better depends on who is in the lower bracket.

## What if the owner gave coins away before dying?

A gift made during life does not get the reset. The recipient takes the giver's original cost, called carryover basis, and pays tax on the full gain when they sell. The giver may also need to file a gift tax return if the gift to one person is over $19,000 in 2026. For someone with large unrealized gains, keeping the coins until death often leaves the heirs with a better tax result than a lifetime gift. That has to be weighed against Medicaid, creditor and family concerns. See the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) article for how annual exclusion gifts work.

## What mistakes cost families money?

- **No date-of-death record.** Without a basis, the IRS may treat the heir's basis as zero or the original cost.
- **Selling from the dead person's account.** Only the executor can sell or move estate assets.
- **Forgetting the estate's own return.** Income earned in the estate's first year can require a return.
- **Missing the state angle.** Some states tax smaller estates or tax inheritances.
- **Assuming a trust changes the tax.** A revocable trust passes the same stepped-up basis as a will. See [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust).
- **Waiting to find the coins.** If you do not know where the wallets are, tax is the least of the problems. See [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance).

## How state law changes this

Federal income tax rules on basis are the same in every state. State differences come from state estate taxes with exemptions below the federal level, state inheritance taxes that depend on who the heir is, and state income tax on gains from a sale. Residents of community property states may see a step-up on both halves of a couple's property, which is a different calculation than in other states.
