Your brother died last month. He mentioned owning some Bitcoin, and you found a Coinbase email in his inbox. You know his email password. The question you are asking is whether you can just sign in and move the money.

The short answer is no, and the better route is not much harder. This page walks through how to access a deceased person's Coinbase or other crypto exchange account properly, what papers to prepare, and what to expect on taxes. For crypto kept outside an exchange, see [crypto custody for heirs](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-custody-exchange-self-custody-and-multisig-for-heirs). This article is part of our [digital assets](/learn/digital-assets) series.

## Can I log in to the account myself?

You should not, for three reasons. First, the exchange's user agreement ties the account to one person, and entering it as someone else is a breach. Second, security systems notice new devices, locations and withdrawal requests, and the account may be frozen at exactly the moment you need it. Third, most accounts use two-factor authentication, with codes sent to a phone that may be locked. Our guide to [a locked phone after death](/learn/digital-assets/locked-phone-after-death-texts-two-factor-codes-and-the-phone-number) covers that obstacle.

There is also a legal reason. A fiduciary should be able to show a clean record of what they did. If you log in and a transfer goes wrong, you will be explaining it to the other heirs. The cleaner path is the exchange's own deceased-account process, and you will need to follow it anyway to close the account.

## What does an exchange account actually hold?

When you buy crypto on an exchange, the exchange typically keeps the coins for you. What you own is a balance on the company's books and a right to ask for it. That is different from a hardware wallet, where you hold the keys.

Two consequences follow. The balance is part of the owner's estate and normally passes by will or intestacy, because exchanges seldom offer a way to name a beneficiary on the account. Check the account settings and the exchange's current policy, since features change. And it can be recovered through a company process, which a lost seed phrase for a self-custody wallet cannot.

## What are the steps?

1. **Get the death certificate.** Order several certified copies. The [Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph) and [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-death-certificates-who-can-order-and-how-many-you-need) guides explain who can request them.
2. **Find out who has authority.** That is the executor named in the will, once the court has issued letters, or an administrator if there is no will. If the estate is small, some exchanges accept a small estate affidavit instead. In Illinois, the affidavit route is for personal property up to $150,000, not counting registered vehicles (see the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit)). In New Jersey, the figures are lower and depend on who is claiming (see the [New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit)).
3. **Find the exchange's deceased-account instructions.** Large exchanges publish them on their support pages. Use the official site, typed in by hand, not a link in an email. Scammers target people looking for a dead relative's crypto.
4. **Submit the documents.** Typical items are the death certificate, the letters or affidavit, your government ID, the account holder's name and email, and sometimes a notarized form.
5. **Decide what you want done.** The exchange may sell the holdings and send cash to the estate's bank account, or transfer the coins to a wallet or exchange account in the estate's name. Ask what is allowed. Selling is simpler. Transferring keeps the position but requires you to set up an estate account.
6. **Record the date-of-death balance.** Ask for a statement showing the value on the date of death. You will need it for the estate tax return, if one is due, and for the heir's basis.
7. **Close the account** after the transfer so that no fees, staking or recurring buys continue.

## How do I find the account if I do not know which exchange?

Search is detective work, and the trail is usually paper and email. Look for:

- Emails from exchanges, including password resets, "verify your identity" notices and monthly summaries.
- Bank and credit card statements showing transfers to an exchange.
- A tax form from the exchange, such as a 1099, and the income tax return's digital asset question.
- Apps on the phone or computer, and bookmarks in the browser.
- A password manager entry or a note in a safe.

Our guide on [how to find a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) covers the wider search, and the [online accounts checklist](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) lists the digital places to check. You can also write to the company itself. See [how to ask a tech company for a deceased person's account](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-ask-a-tech-company-for-a-deceased-persons-account).

If the exchange can't be found and the person also kept coins in a personal wallet, you are in a different situation. See [lost seed phrase or crypto heirs cannot find](/learn/digital-assets/lost-seed-phrase-or-crypto-heirs-cannot-find).

## What do the laws say about an executor's rights?

Both Illinois and New Jersey have adopted the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act. It lets an executor ask a custodian for records, and, with the right authority, for the account's contents. The details differ by the type of record, and a company's own online tool can override it. Read the [Illinois digital assets law](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) or the [New Jersey version](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law) before relying on it.

For an exchange the practical authority comes from the court papers. The company wants to know it is paying the right person, and letters are the proof.

## How is the account taxed?

Receiving the crypto is not income. For federal estate tax, the account is valued at fair market value on the date of death. The exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026, so most families owe no federal estate tax. Illinois taxes estates over $4 million. New Jersey has an inheritance tax, from which a spouse or child is exempt.

For income tax, the heir's basis is generally the date-of-death value. An example makes this concrete.

Eli, 61, dies owning Bitcoin worth $90,000 on his date of death. He paid $20,000 for it years ago. His sister Rena inherits and the estate sells at $95,000 six months later. Her taxable gain is $5,000, not $75,000, because of the step-up. Held more than a year or not, inherited property is generally treated as long-term. The tax details are in [how inherited crypto is taxed](/learn/digital-assets/how-inherited-crypto-is-taxed).

## What should you do now so your family does not face this?

Keep a list of your exchange accounts with the email used, and tell your executor where it is. Keep the password in a password manager with a plan for access, not in the same note as the seed phrase. Review whether the exchange offers a way to name a beneficiary or a legacy contact, and set it up if so. Name your executor in a will, and put exchange accounts on your asset inventory. See [how to create a digital asset inventory your executor can use](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use).
