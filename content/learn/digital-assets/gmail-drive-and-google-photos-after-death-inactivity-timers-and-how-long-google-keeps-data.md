When Walter died in March, his daughter Joan assumed his Gmail was safe where it was. It held thirty years of messages, the tax preparer's attachments, the scanned deed to a lake lot and most of the family photos. Nobody opened it for two years. Then she tried, and the account no longer existed.

That story is the reason this page exists. Google is generous with storage and strict about access, and its quiet housekeeping can erase what a family wanted. Here is what Google keeps, for how long, how to get data out, and what to do first. It builds on our broader guide to [digital estate planning](/learn/digital-assets) and the [checklist for online accounts after a death](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist).

## What exactly is in a Google account?

One login covers a lot more than email, and each piece behaves a little differently.

| Part of the account | What families usually want from it | Access note |
|---|---|---|
| Gmail | Bills, tax documents, contacts, the trail to other accounts | Message content is the hardest to get |
| Google Drive and Docs | Scans, spreadsheets, a draft of the will | Files can be shared copies that others already hold |
| Google Photos | Family albums and phone backups | Shared albums can live in other people's accounts |
| Contacts and Calendar | Names and phone numbers for the funeral notice | Easy to copy if you can sign in |
| YouTube and AdSense | A channel or ad income | Can be a business asset |
| Google Play and Google One | Purchased apps, storage subscription | Licenses; the subscription keeps billing |
| Google Voice and Authenticator | A number and one-time codes for other accounts | Easy to overlook, and a single point of failure |

Walter's Gmail address was also the sign-in for his bank, his brokerage portal and the county tax site. Whoever gets into the mailbox can often reset those, which is exactly why Google guards it.

## Does Google keep an account after the owner dies?

It keeps it until something removes it. Google has said it may delete personal accounts that have been inactive for about two years, and that deletion can include the content stored in Gmail, Drive and Photos. Policies like this change, so check Google's current published terms, but plan as if the clock exists.

Two points matter in practice. First, inactivity is measured by the account, and a death simply stops the activity. Second, a family that has no password and does no outreach has quietly chosen to let the clock run. A grieving household often needs months before anyone has the energy to deal with email. Write the date of death on a calendar, add eighteen months, and treat that as your real deadline for deciding what to keep.

## How does Inactive Account Manager change the outcome?

It turns the inactivity clock into a delivery system. The owner opens the Google account settings, finds the digital legacy plan, and chooses a waiting period of 3 to 18 months of inactivity. They name up to 10 trusted contacts and decide which data types each contact may receive. When the timer runs out, Google contacts those people and sends a download link for the chosen data. The owner can also tell Google to delete the account afterward.

This is the only route where the owner's wishes are written down in Google's own system, and the statutes in both of our states give an online tool like this priority over a conflicting will. Our page on [digital legacy contacts](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) shows the setup steps. If Walter had spent ten minutes on it, Joan would have received an email instead of an error message.

If the owner did set it up, check the contact's own email, including spam, since Google's notice comes from an automated address.

## What can you do if there was no setup?

You make a request to Google's process for deceased users. Expect to provide the following:

1. The full name and, if you know it, the Google email address of the person who died.
2. A copy of the death certificate.
3. Proof of who you are, and your relationship or authority, such as letters of office from the court.
4. A short statement of what you want, such as closing the account or obtaining specific content.

Google has said it may close an account or, in certain cases, provide content. What it gives depends on its policy and the law at the time. Stored message content is the area where it is most protective, so a request for Walter's email may meet a request for a court order. Our guide to [asking a tech company for a deceased person's account](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-ask-a-tech-company-for-a-deceased-persons-account) explains the court order route and what the order needs to say.

The executor's job is to ask for the least that solves the problem. A family that needs one tax document is better served by narrow, well-supported requests than by a demand for everything.

## How do state laws affect your request?

Illinois and New Jersey both follow the uniform fiduciary access approach. In Illinois the law is 755 ILCS 70, and in New Jersey it is N.J.S.A. 3B:14-61.1 and the sections after it. In each, an online tool beats a will, a will beats the terms of service, and the content of communications generally needs consent or a court direction. A list of the accounts (without the messages) is easier to get. Our state pages on [Illinois digital assets law](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) and [New Jersey digital assets law](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law) give the specifics.

Whether the account holder lived in one of those states matters, since the law that applies generally follows where the user lived.

## What should an executor do in the first month?

Keep the account intact and do not guess your way into it. A short list:

1. Ask family whether the owner used Inactive Account Manager, and check whether any contact received an email.
2. Do not delete the account, the phone number or the recovery email yet.
3. Ask relatives and friends for photos and files that were shared with them. Many shared albums survive in several accounts.
4. Look at other places the data may live, such as a computer's synced folder, a backup drive or a printed statement.
5. List the accounts that use the Gmail address, using the mailbox's own bill notices where you have legal access, or mail and credit reports where you do not. Our page on [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) covers the paper trail.
6. Send Google your request with the certified documents, and keep a copy of everything.
7. Cancel the paid subscriptions, such as Google One storage, after the data is safe.

## What about photos and business files?

Photos are the thing families most regret losing, and the one with the most workarounds. Google Photos can hold the original files, the phone backup and shared albums. Friends who were tagged in a trip may have copies, and an old laptop may have the same pictures. If the account includes a YouTube channel or an online store login, treat it as a business asset and read our page on [online businesses after a death](/learn/digital-assets/online-business-etsy-shop-or-youtube-channel-after-death), since revenue and payouts add a second set of questions.

## How can you prevent this for your own family?

Do three things this week. Set Inactive Account Manager to a period you can live with, name two contacts instead of one, and choose the data types on purpose. Then add the account to a one-page inventory, and put the recovery email and phone number on it. Finally, name your executor's power over digital accounts in your will or trust, so there is written consent for message content if it is ever needed.
