Sam and Dolores shared everything for forty years, including one Apple ID. It sat on both iPhones, the iPad in the kitchen and the old laptop. Photos from every holiday were in one iCloud library, and the monthly storage bill went to Dolores's debit card. When Dolores died, Sam kept using the phone as always. Two weeks later it asked for a code that went to a number nobody was answering.

That small lockout is the usual way this problem shows up. This page covers the parts a surviving spouse tends to meet in order: what a shared Apple ID really is, what keeps working, what breaks, and how to move to your own account without losing the photos. It fits with our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets) and the broader piece on [photos and iPhones after death](/learn/digital-assets/photos-and-iphones-after-death).

## Whose account is it when two people share one Apple ID?

Legally it belongs to the person who set it up, even if both of you used it every day. Apple's terms currently describe an account as personal, non-transferable, and, unless the law requires something different, ending its rights when the holder dies. There is no such thing as a joint Apple ID, whatever the household did in practice.

That does not mean the account vanishes the day of the death. It means nothing protects your access. The account stays live until Apple learns of the death, someone closes it, or a security system decides the pattern looks wrong. Dolores's old devices, her email address and the phone number for her codes are all hanging by that thread.

The estate's executor is the person with real authority over the account's value, not the spouse as such. If you are both the spouse and the executor, you hold two hats. Our page on [what a digital executor is](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) explains how that authority is written down.

## What keeps working, and what tends to break?

Most day-to-day features keep working for a while. The things that break are tied to a second factor or to a payment.

| Item | Usually what happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Photos synced to iCloud | Stay visible while the account is active and paid | Download or copy them now |
| Two-factor codes | Go to a trusted device or number that may be unreachable | Keep the old phone and its SIM active until you are done |
| iCloud storage plan | Keeps billing the old card until it fails | Copy data, then cancel |
| Family Sharing | Depends on the organizer's account | Check the group and rebuild it |
| Purchased apps, music, movies | Tied to the buyer's account | Keep using on the same account, or repurchase in your own |
| Apple Pay cards | Remain until the bank closes the card | Executor closes each card account |
| Find My and Activation Lock | Still lock devices to the old ID | Remove devices from the account before any sale |

Notice how many rows say "keep the old phone." That is the single most useful habit. If the dead person's phone number is cancelled right away, the codes that prove who they are stop arriving, and a recoverable account becomes a locked one. Our article on [locked phones, texts and two-factor codes](/learn/digital-assets/locked-phone-after-death-texts-two-factor-codes-and-the-phone-number) goes through that in detail.

## What happens to Family Sharing after a death?

The family group runs through its organizer, the adult who set it up and who pays. If the organizer is the person who died, the others may keep seeing shared purchases for a time, but shared subscriptions, family storage and purchase sharing can stop without much warning. If the person who died was only a member, the group usually keeps going and only needs a member removed.

Here is the order that tends to work:

1. Open Family Sharing settings on a device that is still signed in and write down who the organizer is and what is shared. Take screenshots.
2. If you are not the organizer, create or confirm your own Apple ID before anything changes.
3. Download anything stored only in a shared album or shared storage plan.
4. Set up a new family group under a living adult and re-subscribe to anything the household still wants.
5. Cancel the old subscriptions after you have confirmed the new ones work.

A note on children. If there are minors on the account, their screen time settings and purchase approvals may route to the organizer. A teenager can end up unable to download an app because the person who approves it is gone. Fix that before you do anything else on their devices.

## How do I move my own life off the shared account?

Treat it like moving house: pack first, close the old place last. Do the copying while the account is still open.

- **Photos and videos.** Download the library from iCloud to a computer or an external drive, or use Apple's data export. It can take hours for a large library. Keep two copies.
- **Contacts, calendars and notes.** Export or copy them to your own account. Notes shared by invitation can be re-shared.
- **Messages.** These are the delicate part. A spouse has usually seen them anyway, but they are legally the dead person's private communications, and the executor's authority over message content is narrower than over other files. See [whether an executor can access email](/learn/digital-assets/can-an-executor-access-email) for the same reasoning applied to mail.
- **Passwords.** If the Keychain held your joint passwords, copy the ones you need to a password manager now. Legacy access does not usually reach them.
- **Purchased media.** Apps, books and films are licenses, not objects. Some stay usable on the same account and some cannot be passed on. Our page on [subscriptions and purchased media](/learn/digital-assets/subscriptions-and-purchased-media-after-death) lays out which is which.

Dolores's iCloud library held about fifteen years of photos. Sam's daughter, Rosa, exported them to an external drive over a weekend, then they cancelled the storage plan. Nothing was lost because the order was right.

## How do I close the account, and when?

Close it last, and only after you have copied what you need. Closing is permanent, and Apple generally cannot restore data afterwards.

If your spouse named a Legacy Contact in advance, that person can request access with an access key and a death certificate. Apple's feature currently opens much of the account's data, but not everything, and it is not the same as the authority to close the account. If no one was named, Apple usually asks for a court order before releasing data. The executor can often request that the account be closed without getting the content, which is the common choice when the family has already copied what it wants. Our step-by-step guide to [asking a tech company for a deceased person's account](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-ask-a-tech-company-for-a-deceased-persons-account) lists the documents.

What helps in a request:

1. A certified death certificate.
2. Proof of your authority, such as letters of office or a small estate affidavit where the state allows one.
3. The Apple ID email address and, if you know it, the device model.
4. A short, specific statement of what you want: closure, a data copy, or removal of a device lock.

Expect processing to take some time, and do not rely on a particular timeline. Policies change, so read Apple's current instructions on the day you start.

## What about the iPhone or iPad that is still locked to her account?

A device signed in with the dead person's Apple ID can stay locked to it even after you reset the screen. That matters if you want to give the tablet to a grandchild or sell the laptop. If you still have the passcode and the account password, the cleanest fix is to sign out of the account from the device before the account closes. If you do not, ask Apple support what proof of death and of ownership it will accept. Do not take a device to a repair shop and hope. They cannot remove an account lock without the same proof.

## How state law fits in

Both of our states have adopted a version of the uniform fiduciary access law. Illinois's version is in 755 ILCS 70, and New Jersey's is at N.J.S.A. 3B:14-61.1 and following. In both, the account holder's own choices come first, then the will, trust or power of attorney, then the company's terms. Content of communications generally requires the holder's consent or a court order, while a list of what accounts exist is easier to obtain. Our state pages, [Illinois digital assets law for executors](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) and [New Jersey's digital assets law](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law), set out the paperwork each state expects.

## How can you make this easier for the next spouse?

Give each person their own Apple ID, even if you share a family group. Name a Legacy Contact on each. Keep a note of the organizer and what is shared with the rest of your [digital asset inventory](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use). That takes about twenty minutes and spares a grieving partner the lockout Sam hit.
