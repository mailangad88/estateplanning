A domain name looks like a small thing. It is a line of text and a yearly bill. But for a photographer, a writer or the owner of a three-person shop, that address may be where customers find them, where their email lives and the only home of ten years of work. Lose the domain and you can lose all three at once.

This article covers how domain names and websites pass at death, what an executor needs to do in the first month, and how to set things up so the address survives. It sits within our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## Do you own a domain name?

Not exactly. You hold the right to use a name for a set period, usually a year or more at a time, by registering it with a company called a registrar. When the term ends, the right ends unless renewed. That is why a domain behaves differently from a bank account. A bank account stays put when no one is looking. A domain keeps counting down.

Courts and statutes treat the right to a domain as a form of intangible personal property. It can be sold, given and inherited. It goes through your estate like any other asset, under your will, your trust or your state's intestacy rules if you have neither. The legal question is easy. Everything that comes after is where families run into trouble.

## What does a website actually consist of?

Most sites are a stack of separate services, often bought at different times by different people. Heirs need to find each layer.

| Layer | What it does | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| Domain registration | The address itself | Registrar emails, card statements. |
| Hosting | Stores the site files | Host billing emails. |
| Admin login | Edits pages and posts | Password manager, web developer. |
| Domain email | Mail at the business name | Separate email service. |

The hosting and admin rows trip people up. Beyond the table, many sites also rely on a payment processor for orders and on ad or analytics accounts, each with its own login. If the site was built by a freelancer, the freelancer may hold the admin login. If the site is run on a store platform, the "website" may really be an account on someone else's service, and the platform's terms control whether it can move.

## What happens in the first month?

The goal is not to fix anything. It is to stop things from expiring.

1. **Find the renewal date.** Look at the registrar email or run a public lookup, which usually shows the expiry date. Many people set auto-renew, which only works as long as the card on file is valid and the account is open.
2. **Keep the card active.** Closing the owner's credit card can cause the next renewal to fail. Ask the executor to switch billing to an estate account when one exists.
3. **Look for the login** in a password manager, a letter of instruction or a legacy tool. Our guide to [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) explains how those work.
4. **Contact the registrar** if you have no login. Expect to send a death certificate and, for a transfer, court-issued letters showing the executor's authority. Policies differ, so read the company's current instructions.
5. **Turn on a registrar lock and extend the term**, once you have access. Renewing for several years at once reduces the risk of a second scare.
6. **Do not change DNS or delete files** until you understand what depends on them. Email may run through the same settings.

If you cannot reach anyone at the registrar before the date, renew the domain by paying as a guest if the registrar allows it. That doesn't give you ownership, but it buys time.

## How does an heir take over?

By transfer, with the paperwork the registrar asks for. The usual path is that the executor, with letters from the court, asks the registrar to move the account or the domain into the heir's name. Some registrars have a formal deceased-owner process. Others handle it through support tickets, which can be slow.

If the domain is held through an LLC or a trust, things get simpler. The entity owns the domain, so the successor manager or trustee simply logs in. That is a good reason to think about whether a site that earns money should be owned by a business entity and not by you personally. See our guide to [what happens to a sole proprietorship at death](/learn/business-owners/what-happens-to-a-sole-proprietorship-at-death) for how a one-person business is treated.

Here is a realistic case. Jorge, 61, ran a small online shop and registered the domain in his own name. His wife Alma was executor. She found the renewal notice in his inbox three weeks before it lapsed. Without letters, the registrar would not move the account, but it did let her pay the renewal. Once she had letters, she transferred the domain into the name of a new LLC she formed to take over the shop. The address, the email and the customers stayed put.

## What if the site lives on a platform, not your own hosting?

Many small owners do not have a domain and host at all. They have a shop on a store platform, a page on a creator site or a channel on a video service. These are accounts under the platform's terms, and the terms usually say the account is personal and non-transferable. A family can often close it or download content, but may not be able to keep it running under a new name.

The practical fix is to keep your own copies of the things that matter: product lists, writing and images. If a channel or shop earns real money, ask the platform what its deceased-account process allows and think about moving the audience to a domain you own.

## What about the money and the taxes?

A website that earns money is part of the estate's value. For a modest blog the value is small and may be little more than a year of ad income. For an established site with steady profit, it may be a real asset that needs a valuation, using methods similar to those described in how [a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).

For income tax, income earned before death goes on the final return and income afterward goes to the estate or to whoever owns the site. Heirs generally get a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) in the site's value at death, which matters if they later sell. A domain name that was resold at a high price can be worth far more than its yearly fee, so a one-line check by a broker or marketplace may be useful. The federal estate tax applies only above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most estates owe none at the federal level.

## How do you plan so the address survives?

Make the site boring to inherit. A few small steps do most of the work.

- **Register the domain in your own name, not a freelancer's**, and use an email address you control.
- **Turn on auto-renew** and keep a second card or a calendar reminder.
- **Keep one page that lists** the registrar, the host, the site platform, the admin contact and where the logins are kept.
- **Name someone** in your will or trust and give them authority over digital assets. Our piece on [what a digital executor does](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) explains the role.
- **Say what you want**: keep the site, sell the domain, hand it to a charity, or let it expire.
- **Consider an entity** for a site that earns real income.
