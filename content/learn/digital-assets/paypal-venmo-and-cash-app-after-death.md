Marcus, 34, died suddenly. His sister Elena was the one to go through his phone, and she found three payment apps. One had a few hundred dollars in it. Another had been used to split rent with a roommate. The third held a small amount of bitcoin that Marcus bought out of curiosity. She had his passcode. She wondered if she could just move the money to his mother and be done with it.

That instinct is common and understandable, and it is the wrong first move. This article explains who owns the balance, how to claim it, what to check before closing the account, and what to do about app-held crypto or stock, scheduled payments and unclaimed money.

## The short version

- The balance belongs to the estate, not to whoever knows the password.
- Claim it through the company's process, using the executor's authority.
- Before closing anything, list linked cards, banks and recurring payments.
- Check for stock or crypto held inside the app.
- Do not use the account for any new transaction.

## Who owns the money in a payment app?

When you hold a balance in PayPal, Venmo or Cash App, you hold a claim against the company for that amount, much like a deposit at a bank. At death, that claim is a personal asset of the estate. It passes under the will, or by intestacy if there is no will, after the executor collects it and pays the debts.

That matters for two reasons. First, the heirs are not entitled to the balance directly, and the person who happens to hold the phone has no special right to it. Second, the company will not release funds to a relative who writes in without authority, because it risks paying the wrong person. Our article on [what happens to a bank account at death](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) explains the same logic for a bank.

### Why not use the login?

Logging in as a dead person is the shortcut everyone considers, and it carries three risks. The terms of service generally forbid anyone but the account holder from using the account. State and federal computer access laws can apply to unauthorized access, though prosecutions of grieving relatives are very rare. And if there are other heirs, a transfer you make on your own can look like taking more than your share.

If you have already done it, stop, write down what you did and tell the executor.

## How do you claim the balance?

Each company has its own form, but the shape is the same.

1. **Get certified copies of the death certificate.** Order several. You will use them for the apps, the bank and the phone carrier.
2. **Confirm who the executor is.** If a will names one, the court may need to issue letters before a company will act. See [letters testamentary](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration).
3. **Find the company's deceased account page.** Support sites typically describe the documents needed. Policies change, so go to the current instructions rather than a forum post.
4. **Gather the documents.** Usually a death certificate, proof of authority such as letters, the executor's photo ID and the account email or phone number.
5. **Submit and keep copies.** Send by the method the company prefers and write down the reference number.
6. **Provide payout details.** The company will usually send a check or transfer to the estate account, not to an individual.
7. **Confirm closure in writing**, after you have exported the transaction history.

Small balances are sometimes handled with less. Some companies release a modest balance to a relative on a simple form, and state law may allow a [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) to substitute for letters. The dollar limits for that are set by state law.

## What should you check before closing the account?

Do not close it the first day. A payment app is usually the hub of several other things.

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Linked bank accounts and cards | The account may be pulling money for bills or subscriptions |
| Recurring payments | Rent shares, subscriptions, loans, donations |
| Pending transfers | Money in transit belongs to the estate |
| Transaction history | Useful for tax returns and for finding other accounts |
| Stock or bitcoin holdings | A separate asset that needs a separate process |
| Business or seller profile | Revenue, tax forms and customer funds |
| Linked email and phone | Needed for verification codes while you finish other work |

A history of transactions can also help you find accounts the family did not know about. Regular payments to a utility, an insurance company or a subscription tell you which bills to stop. The same list may reveal a loan or a friend's payment that needs a response. The [online accounts checklist](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) places this inside the wider order of tasks.

## What if the app holds stock or crypto?

Some apps let users buy fractional stock or bitcoin inside the same app. Those holdings are separate assets, and the company often has a different department and different paperwork for them. The executor needs to identify the date of death value for each holding, because it feeds the estate's records and the heir's later tax basis. Some companies will sell the holdings and pay cash to the estate, some will transfer them, and some will do either at the executor's choice.

Crypto in an app is held by the company on the user's behalf, so there is no seed phrase to find. That is easier in one way and more limited in another. Read [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance) for how the broader picture works, and [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust) for how to leave it.

## What about scams after a death?

Payment apps attract fraud, and a death notice can bring it out. Typical patterns include a stranger who sends a payment "by mistake" and asks for a refund, a message that claims the deceased owed money, or a caller who says they can help recover the balance for a fee. Do not respond from the account, do not pay anyone to "release" the money, and do not give the company's verification code to anyone. If someone sends money to the account after death, leave it alone and report it to the executor.

Also guard the linked phone. If the number is cancelled too early, the app may send verification codes to someone else after the number is reassigned. Keep the line until the accounts are closed or updated. For a phone bill that keeps coming, the [online accounts checklist](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) covers the cell account.

## What happens to unclaimed balances?

If nobody claims a balance, the company may eventually be required to report it to the state as unclaimed property. Each state has a dormancy period and a process. The money is not lost, and most states keep a searchable database where an executor or heir can file a claim. For a small balance, the cost of claiming may be worth it only if you are already there for other reasons.

## How can you plan ahead?

- **Keep balances low.** Payment apps are for moving money, not storing it. Move savings to a bank account that can have a pay-on-death beneficiary.
- **Write down the apps you use.** The list can sit with your other digital information. See [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).
- **Tell your executor.** The person should know the apps exist and how to find the registered email.
- **Name a digital executor or give your executor express digital authority** in your will or trust. See [what is a digital executor](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor).
- **Review once a year.** People open and abandon apps quickly.

## How state law changes this

Most states have adopted a fiduciary access law that governs who can manage a dead person's online accounts. It tends to favor the executor with proper documents over a relative who simply has the password. Small estate thresholds and procedures vary widely, as do the time before a dormant balance is turned over as unclaimed property.
