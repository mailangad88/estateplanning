People who set up a living trust are told to retitle the house, the bank accounts and the brokerage. Then they ask about the rest of their life, the part that lives on a phone. Can the trust own the Bitcoin? What about the family website, the photos, the Instagram account? The honest answer is that some of it can, some of it cannot, and the part that cannot still needs a line in the trust.

This page sorts digital property into those groups and shows how to word the trust so your trustee has real authority. It sits beside our guide to [funding a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust), our main [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets) and the page on [crypto in a will or a trust](/learn/digital-assets/crypto-in-a-will-or-a-trust), which goes deeper on coins.

## Which digital assets can a trust actually own?

A trust can own anything you own outright and can transfer. The test is whether you hold a property right or only a license to use a service.

| Asset | Can the trust own it? | How |
|---|---|---|
| Cryptocurrency in self-custody | Yes | Assignment, and keys held under a plan the trustee can reach |
| Crypto at an exchange | Sometimes | Many platforms will not title a retail account to a trust, so ask about a trust account |
| Domain names | Yes | Change the registrant at the registrar |
| A website, blog or app you built | Yes | Assign the content and the hosting contract |
| Original photos, writing, music, video | Yes (copyright) | Written assignment of the rights |
| Online business, store or channel | Often | Assign the business interest; platform rules vary |
| Royalty or ad income streams | Often | Assignment plus a payee change |
| Gmail, iCloud, Facebook, Instagram | No | License; terms say non-transferable |
| Purchased movies, music, e-books, games | No | Licenses tied to your account |
| Frequent-flyer and loyalty points | Usually not | Terms usually bar transfer |

Notice the pattern. Things you made or bought outright move. Things you merely signed up for do not. A domain name is a good example, because a registrar will change the owner on request, and a trust can hold it like a boat. Our page on [domain names and websites after death](/learn/digital-assets/domain-names-and-websites-after-death) explains why that matters.

## Why can't I put my Apple or Google account in the trust?

Because the terms you clicked say the account is personal and cannot be passed on. These companies treat the account as a service agreement with you, not a piece of property that exists apart from you. A trustee who takes over cannot step into your contract.

That does not leave the trustee empty-handed. Under the uniform fiduciary access laws, a trustee who is not the original account holder can ask a company for a catalogue of electronic communications and other digital assets, and can reach content if the trust consents. In both of our states that is the rule. Illinois's version is in 755 ILCS 70, and New Jersey's is at N.J.S.A. 3B:14-61.1 and following. Our page on [what RUFADAA does](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained) gives the national picture.

So the practical move is not to assign these accounts. It is to put your consent in the trust.

## What should the trust say about digital assets?

Four short provisions do most of the work. Your attorney will word them for your state, but this is the shape.

1. **A definition.** Say what you mean by digital assets in plain terms: accounts, files, domain names, crypto, online businesses and the credentials that open them.
2. **Authority for the trustee.** Let the trustee access, manage, copy, transfer, close or sell digital assets, and hire a technical helper.
3. **Express consent.** State that you consent to a company disclosing both a catalogue and the content of your communications to the trustee, which is the sentence companies most often ask to see.
4. **Instructions.** Tell the trustee whether to preserve, memorialize, delete or distribute things, and who gets the photos and the business.

Pair the trust with a general assignment, a short signed document that transfers your interest in all digital property you own to the trust, listed or not. It does for the digital world what the assignment of tangible personal property does for furniture. Our page on [trust funding](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) shows where that fits in the funding steps.

Last, add a pour-over will so that anything you forgot lands in the trust at death. It still has to go through probate, but it gets to the right place.

## What goes in a separate letter, not the trust?

Keys and passwords. A trust is private compared with a will, but it is shared with advisors, a bank, sometimes a court, and a trustee's family. A seed phrase typed into a trust is a seed phrase in a dozen inboxes. Put the instructions for finding credentials in a sealed letter or a password manager's emergency access, and have the trust refer to it. Our article on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) lays out safe storage, and the piece on a [digital asset inventory](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use) shows what to list.

Think of Marcus, 54, who holds about half his savings in Bitcoin at a hardware wallet. His trust names his sister as successor trustee and gives her authority over digital assets. The seed phrase is on a steel plate in a safe deposit box, and a one-page letter says where. If he dies, his sister walks into the box, restores the wallet and acts at once. If he had put the phrase in the trust, it would have been copied into a lawyer's file for ten years.

## How do I retitle crypto into a trust?

The steps depend on where the coins sit.

1. **Self-custody wallet.** You do not retitle anything on the blockchain. The trust owns the coins by assignment, and you keep the keys. Make sure the trustee can reach the keys when you die or become unable to act.
2. **Exchange account.** Ask whether the platform allows a trust account. Some require an entity or trust application, and some will not open one for individuals. Do not move coins to a new address until you have confirmed the process.
3. **Retirement accounts.** Crypto held inside an IRA cannot be retitled to a trust, because the IRA owns it. Use a beneficiary designation. See our page on [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).
4. **Records.** Keep the cost basis and acquisition dates. Retitling to your own revocable trust is generally not a sale for income tax, but the paper trail helps whoever files your final return.

## Is a trust better than a will for digital assets?

For most people with real digital value, yes, though not because of any special tech feature. A trust avoids the delay and publicity of probate, and a successor trustee can act without waiting for a court. For someone with only photos and a few accounts, a will plus a digital clause and a legacy contact may be enough. Our page on [will versus trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) can help you decide, and the [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) shows where your plan is thin.
