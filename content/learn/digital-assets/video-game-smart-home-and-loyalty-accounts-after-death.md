Estate plans are built around houses, accounts and heirlooms. Then someone dies and the family finds a gaming console with fifteen years of purchases, a doorbell camera that will not stop sending alerts, and 400,000 airline miles that nobody knows how to claim. None of it is huge in dollar terms, and all of it causes headaches.

This article covers the small, odd digital items that do not fit the usual categories: game libraries, smart home devices, loyalty points and similar accounts. For the major ones, such as email, photos and crypto, start with our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## Why are these accounts so awkward?

Most of these items are licenses, not property. When you buy a game on a console or an app store, you generally pay for a right to use it under the platform's terms. Those terms usually make the account personal and forbid selling or giving away logins. A will can name who should receive your property, but it cannot rewrite a contract you accepted when you created the account. This is the same limit described in our article on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).

In real life, enforcement is uneven. Families keep using a parent's account for years, and companies rarely chase them. But you cannot count on that, and a closed account can erase a library at once. The point of planning is to remove the risk where you can and to avoid asking your executor to do something that breaks a contract.

## What happens to video game accounts?

A game account usually bundles three things: a library of purchased titles, saved progress, and sometimes stored money in a wallet or digital currency. Rules vary by platform, but the pattern is consistent.

- The library is a license tied to the account holder.
- Saved progress may live on the console, in the cloud, or both.
- Wallet balances and in-game currency typically cannot be cashed out and often disappear on closure.
- Valuable accounts, such as those with rare items, can be sold on gray markets, which also violates the terms and carries scam risk.

The tangible parts pass normally. The console, the controller and any physical discs belong to the estate and go to whoever the will names. If you want your teenager to keep playing, tell your executor to leave the account open and add the child as a family member, or give them access under a family plan. For the larger question of purchased movies, music and books, see [subscriptions and purchased media after death](/learn/digital-assets/subscriptions-and-purchased-media-after-death).

A realistic note on value: a few accounts are worth real money to collectors, but most are worth sentimental value only. A child who wants dad's save file will care more about the console than the paperwork.

## What happens to smart home devices?

Smart devices are physical objects with a software account behind them. The thermostat, doorbell, locks, speakers, cameras and hub are property that passes with the house or by your will. The account that controls them belongs to you.

Problems start when the account holder dies and the family needs to enter the house, change a code or turn off a camera. Questions to settle in advance:

1. Who has administrator access to the app? If it is only you, nobody else can add a user.
2. Where are the codes and backup methods for locks and alarm panels?
3. Are there cameras that record guests or neighbors, with video stored in the cloud under a paid plan?
4. Does the system send alerts to your phone, which will be silent or in the estate's hands?
5. Are there accounts tied to your credit card?

If the house is being sold, the executor should reset the devices to factory settings and remove them from your account, so the buyer does not inherit your camera feeds. Doing it after the sale can cause real disputes. When the house stays in the family, the new owner can create their own account. For security, change door codes and Wi-Fi passwords early. A deceased person's login can remain active for a long time and may be a risk if it is on a shared computer.

## What about airline miles and reward points?

Loyalty programs are contracts and they differ widely.

| Program type | Typical rule at death | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Airline miles | Some allow transfer to a family member with a death certificate; others cancel the account | Read the program terms; ask while the account is active |
| Hotel points | Often transferable to an heir, sometimes for a fee, or they expire | Contact customer service early |
| Credit card rewards | Usually lost when the card is closed, unless redeemed first | Redeem or transfer before closing the card |
| Store or app credits | Normally not transferable | Use them or let them go |
| Gift card balances | Treated like property; may be claimed | Check issuer's rules |

Points can look valuable when the balance is large, but companies often put a cap on transfers and may charge a fee. Some programs say outright that the miles have no cash value and belong to the company. The practical point is to ask while you are alive, and to note in your plan which programs have meaningful balances. Our [checklist for online accounts after a death](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) is a good place to track them.

## Do accounts expire if you stop using them?

Often, yes. Providers set inactivity periods after which they can close an account and delete its content. Email and cloud services commonly choose a period of a year or two. Reward points may lapse after 18 or 24 months with no activity. The terms differ and they change, so treat any number as something to confirm, not rely on.

This matters in two ways. After a death, your family should log in or contact the company before the account lapses, if there is anything worth keeping. And while you are alive, you can use the provider's own tools to name a contact who may download your data or close the account. Our article on [digital legacy contacts](/learn/digital-assets/digital-legacy-contacts) describes the options.

## How do you leave these items to different people?

Digital items rarely split cleanly, and heirs often want different things. A short written plan avoids arguments.

1. Make a list of the accounts that matter: what it is, where it lives, who should get access, and what should happen to it (keep, transfer, close).
2. Name a [digital executor](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) or your executor to carry it out, and make clear what authority they have.
3. Put the allocation in your will or in a signed memorandum that the will refers to, where your state allows it. Ask an attorney, because rules differ.
4. Store login information securely, in a password manager with emergency access or in a sealed envelope.
5. Review once a year, since accounts change.

A simple example: Walt, 68, has a Steam library of about 300 games, a smart home system he set up himself, and 220,000 airline miles. He has two children. His daughter Maya lives in his house after his death and keeps the smart home. His son Evan plays on Walt's console every Christmas. Walt's memorandum says: console and games to Evan, who may use the account; house systems to Maya, with a note that she should reset them; miles to whichever child asks first, if the airline permits a transfer, otherwise use them for a family trip before the account lapses. None of this is legally dramatic, and all of it saves the executor from guessing.

## What should your executor avoid?

- Do not log in as the deceased on a platform whose terms forbid it, if the account matters and a legal path exists. It may risk the account being locked.
- Do not leave subscriptions running. Cancel anything billed to a card in the estate.
- Do not sell an account without checking the terms and the buyer.
- Do not assume anything is worthless before reading the terms; a few items, such as domain names or a monetized channel, are valuable. See [online businesses after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-business-etsy-shop-or-youtube-channel-after-death).
