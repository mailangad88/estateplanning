Owen, 49, bought bitcoin years ago and now holds a mix of exchange balances and a hardware wallet in his desk. He has asked his lawyer a question that comes up a lot: should the crypto go in his will, or in a trust? The honest answer is that the document matters less than three other things, which are who is named, whether the asset can be reached, and whether the paperwork matches how it is held.

This article compares the two tools for crypto and other digital assets. For the basics of how crypto is held, start with [cryptocurrency and estate planning](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance). Everything here is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What is the difference between a will and a trust for crypto?

A will is a document that directs your executor after death, and it takes effect only through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate). A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is an arrangement you create now, in which a trustee holds assets for your benefit and then for your beneficiaries. You can serve as the trustee while you are alive.

For crypto, the practical differences show up in four places.

| Question | Will | Living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Does it go through probate? | Yes, if the asset is in your name | Not if the asset is held by the trust |
| Is it public? | The will is generally filed with the court | The trust usually stays private |
| Who can act if you become incapacitated? | Nobody under the will; you need a power of attorney | The successor trustee, if the asset is in the trust |
| What is the cost and effort? | Lower to set up | Higher to set up and to fund |
| Does it solve access? | No | No |

The last row is the one people miss. Both are legal documents, and the blockchain does not read legal documents. Only a key moves coins.

## How does a will handle crypto?

A will names who inherits and an executor to carry it out. Crypto owned in your own name is part of the probate estate, so your executor needs court letters, then collects the assets. For an exchange account, the executor sends a death certificate and the letters, and the exchange follows its process. For self-custody, the executor needs the keys.

Things to put in a will:

- A gift of your "digital assets, including cryptocurrency" to the people or trust you choose.
- Authority for your executor to access, manage, sell or transfer them.
- A short statement that your executor may hire a technical professional and pay reasonable fees from the estate.

Wills have two real drawbacks for crypto. First, probate takes months, and the price of crypto does not wait. An executor generally has a duty to protect assets, and that creates a decision about selling or holding that many families dread. Second, the will is public in many places, so anyone can learn that you owned crypto, and sometimes how much. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for the broader picture.

## How does a trust handle crypto?

If you put your exchange account into a revocable trust, a successor trustee can take over without probate. The trustee needs the trust document, a certification of trust and a death certificate. Because the account was already in the trust's name, the exchange does not have to wait for a court. Many people also value privacy, since a trust is not a public filing.

A trust also protects you during life. If you have a stroke, your successor trustee can manage the account, where a will gives nobody authority until you die. A [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) covers the same gap for assets outside the trust.

The catch is funding. A trust controls only what it owns. You must retitle exchange accounts (if the platform allows it), assign your wallets in writing, and keep the instructions current. A trust that was signed and never funded accomplishes little, which is a common failing described in [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust). A [pour-over will](/glossary/pour-over-will) catches what you missed, but anything it catches still goes through probate.

## What about self-custody wallets?

You cannot retitle a hardware wallet. It has no name on it. What you can do is:

1. State in the trust that the wallet and everything on the blockchain accessible through it belong to the trust.
2. Name the successor trustee as the person to hold the device and the recovery phrase.
3. Store the phrase securely, in a way the successor can reach, such as a metal plate in a safe deposit box or a sealed envelope with your attorney.
4. Keep the location out of the trust document and the will, since both may be seen by more people than you intend.
5. Test the recovery with a small amount.

Our article on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) covers storage in more depth.

## What about other digital assets in a trust?

The same logic applies to a domain name, a website or an online store. You can assign the domain to the trust, usually by changing the registrant name with the registrar. Email, photo libraries and social media accounts are different. These are governed by the company's terms and the digital assets law, and most are not transferable property you can retitle. For these, the trust or will provides authority and consent, and the company's legacy tool does the rest. Explained in [what RUFADAA is](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained), the law lets a trustee access an account the trust owns without any further consent, which is one reason some people put domain names in the trust.

## Which should you choose?

Think of it as a short decision.

1. **Mostly exchange accounts, modest value, simple family?** A will with a good digital clause, plus beneficiary designations where the platform offers them, may be enough.
2. **Large holdings, privacy matters, or you want continuity if you are incapacitated?** A living trust is worth the added effort.
3. **Mostly self-custody?** The deciding factor is the key plan. Either document works only if the heir can get in. Our [will versus trust comparison](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) can help you weigh the rest of your estate.
4. **Real estate in your name, minor children, or a blended family?** You may want a trust anyway, and the crypto simply goes along.

## What do heirs owe in taxes?

Crypto is property for federal tax purposes. Heirs generally receive a step-up in basis to the value at the date of death, so selling shortly afterward often produces little taxable gain. The federal estate tax applies only to estates above the $15,000,000 per person exclusion in 2026. These rules are the same whether the asset passes by will or trust. Keep records of the date of death value, which an executor needs. An accountant who understands crypto reporting is worth consulting.

## An example

Owen decides on a revocable living trust. He moves his exchange account into the trust's name, assigns his hardware wallet to the trust in a schedule, and leaves a sealed envelope with his attorney that tells his sister Kelly, the successor trustee, where the recovery phrase is stored. His pour-over will catches anything he forgets. If Owen dies, Kelly shows the trust and a death certificate to the exchange and collects the wallet from the safe. She does not need to wait for a court. His will, filed later, mentions nothing about crypto at all.
