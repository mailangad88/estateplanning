Think about what lives on your phone and in the cloud: family photos, tax documents, bank apps, a business email address, years of messages, maybe a bit of cryptocurrency. If something happened tomorrow, could the person you trust find these things and get into them? For many people the answer is no, and the result is locked accounts, lost photos and unpaid bills.

This article explains how digital assets fit into an estate plan, what the law allows, and how to prepare. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What counts as a digital asset?

A [digital asset](/glossary/digital-assets) is any electronic account, file or right you own or control. They fall into four groups that are treated differently:

| Type | Examples | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Money and financial accounts | Online banking, brokerage, PayPal, Venmo, cryptocurrency | Direct financial value and bills to pay |
| Personal content | Photos, videos, documents, cloud storage, email, text messages | Sentimental value and privacy concerns |
| Business and online property | Domain names, websites, online stores, social media business pages, ad accounts | Income and continuity |
| Licenses and rewards | Streaming services, e-books, game accounts, airline miles, loyalty points | Often cannot be inherited under the terms of service |

The distinction matters because owning something and licensing it are not the same. A domain name is yours. Your Kindle library is generally a license that ends with you, though some airlines and hotel chains allow points to transfer.

## What does the law say about access after death?

Nearly every state has adopted the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, usually called RUFADAA. It creates a three-level order of authority, from strongest to weakest:

1. **The online tool.** If a company gives you a tool to name someone, such as Google's Inactive Account Manager or a Facebook legacy contact, that choice controls.
2. **Your legal documents.** If you did not use a tool, your will, trust or power of attorney can grant access.
3. **The terms of service.** Without either, the company's default policy applies.

The law also distinguishes the catalog of communications (such as who emailed you and when) from the content of those messages. A fiduciary usually can obtain the catalog with a court order and a death certificate. To see the content, the law generally requires your consent, either through a tool or in your documents. Because of privacy protections, companies are cautious about releasing the content of private communications, so a missing instruction usually means a slow request or a refusal.

State variations exist, and federal law on privacy and stored communications still applies in the background, so a clear written consent from you is the strongest protection. Ask an attorney to confirm how your state treats it.

## How do you build a digital asset plan?

A good plan has five parts, and you can finish a first version in an afternoon.

### 1. Make an inventory

List each account by name and purpose, but do not write passwords. Include the email account used for recovery, since it unlocks the rest. Cover:

- Email addresses and phone number accounts
- Banks, brokerages, payment apps and cryptocurrency exchanges
- Cloud storage and photo services
- Social media and messaging accounts
- Subscriptions and autopay bills
- Domain names, websites and online businesses
- Devices, including phones, tablets and laptops, with their lock method

### 2. Decide what should happen to each

For each item, choose: keep, transfer, memorialize, download or delete. Many people want family photos preserved, business accounts handed to a successor and old social accounts closed. Write short instructions.

### 3. Choose a digital executor

You can name someone to manage digital property, who may be the same executor as for your estate or someone more at ease with technology. Name an alternate. In your will, trust or [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), include a clause that gives your fiduciary authority over your digital assets and consent to disclose content where you want that.

### 4. Use each company's tools

Set up the legacy options that exist, for instance Apple's Legacy Contact, Google's Inactive Account Manager and Facebook's legacy contact. These take effect even if your documents are silent, and they have the highest priority under the law. See [social media and email after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death).

### 5. Plan access safely

Store account access in a password manager and arrange emergency access for a trusted person, or keep a sealed written copy in a safe place. Our article on [passwords, password managers and two-factor authentication](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) goes through how to do this without creating new risks.

## What should go in your documents?

Your documents should name who may act, define digital assets broadly and state your preferences. Common language includes the authority to access, manage, copy, transfer and close accounts and devices, and an explicit statement that you consent to the disclosure of the content of electronic communications to your fiduciary.

Place these clauses in the right documents:

- **Will,** which operates after death.
- **Revocable trust,** which can hold domain names or business accounts and lets a successor trustee manage them.
- **Durable power of attorney,** which operates while you are alive, if you are incapacitated. Without this, family members may be unable to manage your online bills or accounts if you have a stroke or injury.

Compare these tools in our [estate planning documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist).

## What are the common mistakes?

Most problems come from missing information rather than complicated law.

- **Writing passwords in the will,** which can expose them publicly.
- **Forgetting the recovery email or phone,** which locks everyone out.
- **Relying on two-factor codes that go only to your phone.**
- **Assuming a beneficiary can simply log in,** which may breach a service's terms and the law.
- **Leaving cryptocurrency without recovery information,** which can mean it is lost forever. See [cryptocurrency and estate planning](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance).
- **Never updating the list** after changing banks, phones or providers.

For example, Elaine, 61, keeps all her tax records in cloud storage under a Gmail address. She names her daughter as her agent and executor and adds a digital assets clause to both documents. She sets up Google's Inactive Account Manager to share her Drive with her daughter after 6 months of inactivity, and she adds a one-page inventory to her estate planning binder. Her daughter will not have to guess where anything is.

## How often should you update the plan?

Review the inventory every year and after any major change, such as a new phone, a divorce, a new business or a major purchase of cryptocurrency. It takes ten minutes. Our guide to [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists other triggers.

## Who pays for the work of managing digital assets?

The estate does. A fiduciary can use estate funds to pay for reasonable costs, such as a locksmith for a device, a data recovery service or a technician. Keep receipts.
