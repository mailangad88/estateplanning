You are an executor, and the person who died left a Gmail account, an iCloud library and a Facebook page. You write to each company and get three different answers. One asks for a court order. One says it cannot discuss the account. One sends a form. That patchwork is exactly what a law called RUFADAA was written to tame.

This article explains what the act says, how it ranks competing instructions, and what you can do now so your family does not have to argue the point later. It is part of our [digital estate planning guide](/learn/digital-assets).

## What does RUFADAA stand for?

RUFADAA stands for the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act. The Uniform Law Commission, a group of lawyers and judges that drafts model statutes, published it in 2015. Nearly every state has since passed its own version. The "revised" matters: an earlier draft gave fiduciaries broad access by default, and technology companies objected on privacy grounds. The revision is a compromise that protects the account holder's privacy while giving a clear path to access.

The act reaches four kinds of fiduciary: an [executor or personal representative](/glossary/personal-representative) of a deceased person's estate, an [agent](/glossary/agent) under a power of attorney, a [trustee](/glossary/trustee) of a trust, and a court-appointed conservator or guardian. It applies to a "custodian," meaning the company that holds or carries the data, such as an email provider or social network.

## Why was a special law needed?

Because the old rules did not fit. Before the act, an executor had a clear right to a dead person's filing cabinet. An email account looked similar, but a federal law from 1986, the Stored Communications Act, restricts companies from disclosing the content of private communications. Companies also hid behind terms of service that said an account was personal and could not be transferred.

Families paid for the gap. A mother's photos sat behind a login that no one could use. A widow could not close her husband's subscription. Courts reached different results on nearly identical facts. RUFADAA gave everyone a shared order of operations.

## How does the three-level order work?

The act looks at what the account holder chose and ranks the sources. The first level that applies wins.

| Level | Source | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | An online tool offered by the company (Google's Inactive Account Manager, a Facebook legacy contact) | Controls over everything below, even a conflicting will |
| 2 | A will, trust, power of attorney or other written record | Applies if no tool was used, or the tool was left unset |
| 3 | The company's terms of service | Applies if the holder gave no instruction at all |

The point of level one is that the account holder's most recent, most specific choice should win. If you told Google to delete everything after 12 months of inactivity, your will's blanket permission will not override that. If you did not use the tool, your will speaks. If neither exists, the company's rules do.

## What is the difference between the catalog and the content?

The catalog is the record of an account's activity: the list of people you wrote to, the dates, the subject lines or the log of logins. The content is the substance itself, such as the text of messages, the files, the photographs. The act treats them differently.

A fiduciary can usually ask for the catalog unless the account holder prohibited disclosure or a court says no. Getting the content requires more. The holder must have consented, in a will, trust, power of attorney or the company's tool, or a court must order disclosure. That consent also satisfies the federal privacy law's exception for "lawful consent."

For a family, that matters in a practical way. Knowing who someone corresponded with can reveal which banks, brokers and bills exist. It is often the first useful step, and it needs less proof than reading mail. We cover the email side in detail in [can an executor access email](/learn/digital-assets/can-an-executor-access-email).

## What does a fiduciary need to send?

Each company sets its own process, but the model act lets a custodian ask for specific documents. Plan on gathering:

1. A certified copy of the death certificate, for an estate.
2. A certified copy of the [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary) or letters of administration showing the court appointed the executor.
3. A copy of the will, trust or power of attorney if it contains the consent that unlocks content.
4. Where the company asks, the account's email address or username and proof it belongs to the person who died.
5. Where needed, a court order finding that the account belonged to the person, that disclosure is reasonably necessary to administer the estate, and sometimes that the fiduciary has lawful consent.

The company may answer with limited access, such as a download of the data, instead of a login. It does not have to let anyone sign in as the account holder. Under the model act it generally has 60 days to comply with a complete request, and the fiduciary can go to court if it does not. After that, [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) continue as usual, including collecting what the estate owns.

## What does the law not do?

People expect more than RUFADAA delivers. It does not:

- Give your family passwords, recovery codes or access to a locked phone.
- Cover property you hold without a company, such as a hardware crypto wallet.
- Override a court order or a contract with a bank that has its own rules for deceased customers.
- Force a company to keep data forever. Some services delete inactive accounts under their own policies.
- Answer questions about taxes or who inherits an account's value. Those follow your will, trust or state law.

The act is a right to ask with leverage behind it. The practical work is still on you, which is why [passwords and two-factor planning](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) belongs in the same plan.

## What should your documents say?

You do not need pages of technical language. A good clause does four things:

- Names who may act: your executor, trustee and agent, or a specific [digital executor](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) you trust with technology.
- Gives that person authority to access, manage, copy, transfer or close your digital assets and accounts.
- States your consent, in plain words, to the disclosure of the content of your electronic communications.
- Says what you want, for instance "keep my photographs, close my social accounts," or leaves it to the fiduciary's judgment.

A clause in a will works after death. An agent under your [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) needs the same language to act while you are alive but unable, and a trust needs it so the trustee can manage anything transferred to the trust. Many people have the clause in one document and forget the others.

## An example of the order in action

Dina, 66, keeps 15 years of family photos in Google Photos. She sets Google's tool to share her photo library with her sister Marguerite after 6 months of inactivity, and her will gives her executor authority over "all digital assets." Her email, though, holds sensitive medical correspondence, and she wrote nothing about it.

When Dina dies, level one applies to the photos: Google follows her tool, and Marguerite downloads them with no court involved. For the email, the will's general clause is level two. Her executor submits the death certificate, the letters and the will, and Google can release the catalog and, with that consent, the content. If the will had said nothing at all about communications, the executor would need a court order for the content, which takes months and a filing fee.

## Do state laws differ?

Yes, in details. States have changed notice periods, court procedures, who counts as a fiduciary and how a company may verify a request. A few have kept different frameworks. If you move, ask whether your digital clause should be refreshed with the rest of your documents, and see our general guidance on [updating a plan after a move](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).
