Priya made handmade candles and sold them through an online shop for six years. She also ran a small cooking channel with about 40,000 subscribers, which paid her a few hundred dollars most months. When she died at 47, her husband Rahul found that both were still active. Orders were coming in, a wholesale customer wanted a quote, and a platform email said a payout was pending. He had her laptop, but not her logins.

Online businesses sit in an odd place within any [digital estate plan](/learn/digital-assets). They have real value, and they keep running without you, but the account that makes them work belongs to a company with its own rules. This article explains what passes to your family, what stops, and what to set up now so the business does not die when you do.

## What exactly passes to the family?

Think of an online business as several separate things bundled together. Some can be inherited. Some cannot.

| Piece of the business | Does it pass to heirs? | How |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory, equipment, supplies | Yes | Part of the estate, like any other property |
| Your original photos, videos, product designs | Yes | You own the copyright, and it passes by will or trust |
| Customer list and email list you built | Usually yes | Check the platform's terms on who owns customer data |
| Unpaid earnings and pending payouts | Yes | A debt owed to the estate |
| Brand name, logo, domain name | Yes | See the article on [domain names and websites](/learn/digital-assets/domain-names-and-websites-after-death) |
| The platform account and login | Usually no | Terms of service tie it to one person |
| Followers, ratings and reviews | No | They belong to the account, not to you |

That last row is what surprises families. Priya's 40,000 subscribers belong to the channel, and the channel belongs to an account that nobody else may lawfully use. Her videos are hers and will pass to Rahul under her will. The audience does not come with them.

## What do the terms of service actually say?

Almost every major marketplace and video platform says the account is personal, non-transferable and licensed to you rather than owned by you. A few have a written process for the death of a user: a family member or executor sends a death certificate, and the company closes the account, memorializes it, or releases certain data. Very few promise to hand the account to someone else.

So when someone asks whether they can leave a channel to their children, the honest answer is in two halves. You can leave the content and the money. You cannot rely on leaving the account. Where a platform does allow a transfer, it generally has to happen while the owner is alive, through a feature the platform offers, and often only to a business account.

Policies change, and they differ between companies, so read the current terms for each platform you use. If you find a conflict, the platform's rules control what happens to the account, but your will still controls who receives the money and the content.

## What should the family do in the first week?

Speed matters more than perfection here. Customers do not know anyone has died.

1. **Find the account email and the payout bank account.** Platforms pay out to a bank account on file, and those statements tell you which platforms exist even if you cannot log in.
2. **Pause new orders if you can legally do so.** A representative can often request vacation mode or a pause. If nobody can log in, contact the platform with the death certificate and ask what it can do.
3. **Handle open orders.** Orders already paid for are obligations of the estate. Refund them through the platform's process, or have the executor fulfill them if the business can still ship.
4. **Ask the executor to request the pending payout.** The executor needs letters of authority from the court, usually called [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary). Without them the platform will not pay.
5. **Post a short notice if the audience matters.** A single pinned message beats silence, and it should not be written from the dead person's account unless the platform gives permission.

Do not log in with the dead person's password to keep things running. It breaks the terms of service, and an account that gets flagged for suspicious access can be frozen with the money inside it.

## How is online business income taxed after death?

Income taxes follow the date the money was earned or received, not the date the platform lists.

- **Before death.** Income through the date of death is reported on the person's final individual return. A sole proprietor files the business schedule with it. Our article on [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) explains who signs and when.
- **After death.** Payments that arrive later, such as a payout for sales made in the last month, are income to the estate if the estate collects them, and the executor reports it on the estate's own return. This kind of income does not get a stepped-up basis, because it was earned by the owner before death.
- **Assets of the business.** Equipment, inventory and a domain name generally take a new tax basis equal to value at death, which is the [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis). That can wipe out most of the taxable gain if an heir sells them soon after.
- **Sales tax and other filings.** Marketplace sellers sometimes owe sales tax in several states. The executor should check for returns still due, because those are debts of the estate.

A good tax preparer can separate the income into the right buckets in an afternoon. An executor who guesses can end up reporting the same dollars twice.

## Is it better to run the business through an LLC?

Often, yes. If Priya had set up a limited liability company and registered the shop and channel under it, the business would have a legal life of its own. Her ownership interest would pass by will or trust. Rahul, as the new owner, could name himself manager the same week, update the platform's business information and keep operating.

An LLC does not solve every problem. Some platforms still require an individual to hold the account, and a one-person LLC does not remove the need for a [digital executor](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) or a clear instruction for who may act. But it fixes the largest one, which is who owns the bank account, the contracts and the brand. For bigger operations, [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) goes further, and an owner working alone should read what happens to a [sole proprietorship at death](/learn/business-owners/what-happens-to-a-sole-proprietorship-at-death).

## What should you put in place now?

A short list does most of the work.

- **A written inventory** of every shop, channel, store and payment processor, with the email address each one uses and the bank account that receives payouts.
- **A named person** who knows the business, listed in your will as your executor or digital executor, with authority to deal with online accounts. The [RUFADAA](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained) language in your documents matters because platforms look for it.
- **Instructions on what you want to happen.** Wind down, sell, or keep going. Even two sentences help.
- **A secure way to share access** for the business accounts, such as a password manager with emergency access. Our article on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) covers the options.
- **A beneficiary for the business assets,** whether that is a person under your will or a trust you have funded.

If your online income is large enough that it matters to your family, mention it at your next planning meeting, so the documents describe it accurately. Most older wills do not mention it at all.
