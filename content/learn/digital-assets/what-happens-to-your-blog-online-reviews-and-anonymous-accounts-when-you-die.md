Dana writes a food blog with about nine years of recipes, runs an anonymous account where she posts about her health, and has left four hundred reviews of restaurants and contractors. If she dies tomorrow, who owns all that writing, and does it stay on the internet? Most people never ask until a relative finds a blog still posting from a scheduled queue three months after the funeral.

This page, part of our [digital assets planning guide](/learn/digital-assets), covers what happens to the words you publish: blogs, reviews, comments and anonymous accounts. For the big social platforms, see [social media after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death).

## What happens to my blog when I die?

The blog stays online until someone stops paying for it, deletes it, or the host closes it. Whether your family can control it depends on where it lives.

| Where the blog lives | Who controls it after death | What the family must do |
|---|---|---|
| Self-hosted site (you pay a web host) | Whoever controls the hosting login and domain | Executor logs in or contacts host, pays or cancels |
| Free blogging platform | The platform | Send a deceased-user request with a death certificate and proof of authority |
| Newsletter or subscription platform | The platform, plus your payment account | Request closure, and handle subscriber payouts through the estate |
| Blog run by your employer or a publisher | The company | Check your contract; you may not own it |

The domain name is a separate asset with its own rules. If you own `yourname.com`, it renews on a schedule and can lapse if nobody pays. The [domain names and websites guide](/learn/digital-assets/domain-names-and-websites-after-death) explains how an executor handles it. A lapsed domain can be bought by someone else, who then controls what appears at your old address, so decide whether you want it kept, redirected or released.

## Who owns the copyright to my posts?

You do, unless you gave it up. Copyright is a form of property that exists the moment you write something, and it passes by will or by intestacy like furniture or a bank account. For works created after 1977, the term generally runs for the author's life plus 70 years. Works published anonymously or under a pseudonym are treated differently and can have a fixed term measured from publication, so ask an attorney if your writing has real commercial value.

Two things get confused here. Owning the copyright does not mean you own the platform's account. And granting a platform a license to display your posts, which nearly every terms of service requires, does not transfer ownership to it.

For a writer, photographer or video creator with real income, this is one of the more valuable digital assets. Name who gets it. A will clause might read: "I leave the copyright in my writing and online posts to my sister Priya. She may preserve, republish or delete them as she chooses." If you do nothing, the copyright falls into the residue and is shared among your heirs, which means a book deal or republication needs everyone's signature. The [freelancer estate plan guide](/learn/business-owners/freelancer-estate-plan-royalties-copyrights-and-client-work) goes deeper on royalties.

## Do online reviews and comments stay up after I die?

Mostly yes. A review lives on the platform, tied to your account. It remains visible unless the platform removes it, you delete it, or the account is closed and the closure takes content down with it.

That has a practical side. If you were a contractor reviewing competitors, a landlord responding to tenants, or someone with a public dispute, your family may be asked to take things down. They can ask the platform, but only the account holder or an authorized person can delete reviews directly. If you want certain posts removed, say so in writing and leave the login to the person you trust.

On the other hand, many families treat old reviews and comments as a record of a person's voice. A line like "do not delete my blog or reviews" in your digital instructions saves your executor from guessing.

## What if the account is anonymous or pseudonymous?

Family often has no way to know it exists. If you post as "NightOwl_Dana" using a separate email address, the platform has no record of your real name, and neither does anyone else. Your executor will not find it by looking at your phone unless it is saved there.

The privacy you wanted in life can become a problem after death, in two ways:

- **No one can close it.** Platforms generally need proof that the requester is the account holder's personal representative. With a pseudonym, proving the link is hard.
- **No one knows it matters.** If the account holds income, a following or private writing you wanted preserved, that value is lost.

If you want the account to stay private, you can still leave instructions. Write a sealed note for your executor listing the handle, platform, login method and what to do: delete it, memorialize it, or hand its contents to a named friend. A [password manager's emergency access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) is another way to hand over the key without revealing the contents to everyone.

State law also shapes how much your executor can see. Both Illinois and New Jersey have adopted a version of the uniform law on fiduciary access to digital assets. In general, it lets an executor see a catalog of your communications, such as the sender and date, but not the content of private messages unless you consented in your will or through an online tool. See [Illinois digital assets law for executors](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) and [New Jersey digital assets law](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-digital-assets-law), and for more on the uniform law, [RUFADAA explained](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained).

## How do I plan for my writing and posts?

Use this list. It takes about an hour.

1. **List every place you publish**: blog, newsletter, review sites, forums, anonymous accounts. Include the email address each uses.
2. **Mark each as keep, delete or hand over.** Be specific about who gets it.
3. **Leave logins somewhere safe**, such as a password manager with an emergency contact, not in your will, which becomes a public court record in most probate cases.
4. **Add a copyright clause** to your will or trust naming who owns your written and creative works.
5. **Renew domains for several years** and name the person responsible for renewing them.
6. **Review once a year** when you update the rest of your [digital inventory](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use).

A common mistake is putting passwords in the will itself. A will is usually filed with the probate court and can become part of the public record. Keep credentials in a separate, secure place and refer to them in the will.

## What about private writing, like journals and drafts?

Private drafts, journals and unpublished manuscripts are just as much your property, and your executor has authority over them. If you do not want family to read them, say so and name a person who may delete them unread. The [private messages guide](/learn/digital-assets/can-you-keep-your-family-out-of-your-private-messages-after-you-die) explains how far that wish can be enforced.
