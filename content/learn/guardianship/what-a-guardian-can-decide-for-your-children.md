You have picked someone to raise your children if you cannot. Now the practical worries start. Will she keep them in the same school? Will he take them to church, or stop taking them? Could they pack up and move to another coast? The answers depend on what a guardian is allowed to decide, and where the law steps back and the court steps in.

This page lays out what a guardian can decide on their own, what they cannot, and how you can shape those choices ahead of time without pretending the law will enforce every wish.

## The short version

- A guardian of the person acts as the parent for daily life and health decisions.
- The guardian answers to the court, not to your written preferences, though judges read those preferences closely.
- Money and property for the child are usually someone else's job, or the guardian's job under court supervision.
- A move out of state, a sale of the child's property, or a change of the arrangement can all trigger court involvement.

## What does a guardian of the person actually decide?

A guardian of the person decides the same things a parent would. That covers where the child sleeps, what school they attend, which doctors they see, what activities they join, and the thousand small calls in between. The guardian signs report cards, consents to routine care, and can authorize emergency treatment.

Think of Dana and Luis, who name Dana's brother Marcus as guardian for their two children, ages 6 and 9. If both parents die, Marcus does not receive a rulebook. He makes the call about the pediatrician, about whether the 9-year-old stays on her soccer team, and about whether the family keeps the same summer routine. Most of those choices never reach a judge.

What he does not automatically get is control of the children's inheritance. That is the second job, covered in [guardian of the person versus conservator of the estate](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate).

## Can a guardian make medical decisions?

Yes, for the child's health care, including routine visits, vaccinations, surgery and mental health treatment, within the limits of state law. Some states require extra steps for particular decisions, such as psychiatric admission or sterilization-type procedures, which for a minor are rarely at issue.

A practical point: the paperwork has to catch up. Hospitals and schools want to see the court order, often called letters of guardianship, before they accept a new adult's signature. That is why many families set up a short-term medical consent for the period before a court acts, as described in [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians).

## Can a guardian change my children's school or religion?

A guardian can change the school, and in most places the law does not tie a guardian to your faith. Courts tend to leave religious upbringing to the person raising the child, with limited exceptions where a parent's wishes were clearly expressed and are easy to follow.

Your instructions in a will or a separate letter carry real weight, because the judge and the guardian will read them. They are guidance, not a command. If your family has strong feelings, such as a faith community, a school for a child with learning differences, or a language you want the children to keep, say so plainly and explain why. A reasoned request is more likely to be honored than a bare demand.

Two things help the most.

1. Choose a guardian who already shares your core values, so the instructions are mostly a confirmation.
2. Put the reasons in writing, with specifics: the name of the school, the therapist the child already sees, the grandparent they must keep visiting.

The [letter of intent for your children's guardian](/learn/guardianship/letter-of-intent-for-your-childrens-guardian) is the right place for all of this.

## Can a guardian move out of state with my children?

It depends on the state and on the court's order. In some places a guardian may relocate with the child freely once appointed. In others the guardian must give notice to the court or get permission first, especially when the move would disrupt contact with family or affect school and medical care.

Here is how that tends to play out.

| Situation | What usually happens |
|---|---|
| Move across town or within the same county | Guardian decides; may file a change of address with the court |
| Move to another part of the same state | Often allowed; some courts want notice |
| Move to another state | Many courts require notice or permission; the case may have to be transferred |
| Move abroad | Court approval is commonly required |

If you name a guardian who lives in another state from the start, the question becomes whether the children will move to the guardian or the guardian will move to the children. Think that through with the person, because it is the single most disruptive part of any plan. The blog on [naming a guardian who lives out of state](/blog/can-you-name-a-guardian-who-lives-in-another-state) walks through it in more detail.

## Who handles the money, and can a guardian sell the child's house?

The guardian of the person usually does not hold the child's property. In many states a second role, called a conservator or guardian of the estate, handles it, and may be the same person or someone else. That role is typically supervised: the court may require a bond, an inventory and yearly accountings.

If a child inherits a house, for example, the conservator generally cannot just sell it. Many states require a court order, a fair price and a showing that the sale serves the child. A trust avoids that friction. The trustee follows your terms, pays the bills, and may be able to hold or sell the property on a schedule you choose. See [guardian versus trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) for who should hold which role, and [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) for the options.

### Can the guardian be paid?

Reimbursement for the real costs of raising the children is normal and usually comes out of the child's funds, with court approval in many states. Paying a guardian for the work itself is less common and more tightly controlled. If the guardian is a relative taking in three kids on a tight budget, do not leave it to chance. A trust that pays the guardian a housing allowance, or helps with a larger car or a home addition, answers the question directly.

## How is the guardian supervised?

A guardianship for a minor is not a private arrangement. The court that appointed the guardian keeps authority over the case. Many states require periodic reports on the child's living situation, school and health, and a conservator files financial accountings. A relative who sees a problem can bring it to the court.

Courts can remove a guardian who neglects the child, misuses funds or stops being able to serve. The path is laid out in [whether a guardian can be removed or refuse to serve](/learn/guardianship/can-a-guardian-be-removed-or-refuse-to-serve).

## How do I shape these decisions in advance?

Use three layers, in order of strength.

1. **Name the guardian and a backup in your will.** This is the nomination the court looks at first. See [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian).
2. **Create a trust for the children's money** with a trustee and clear spending rules, so the guardian is not the only one holding the checkbook.
3. **Write a letter of wishes** covering school, faith, health, family contact, and the house rules that matter to you.

Not every state treats the letter the same way, and none treat it as binding. It is still the clearest way to tell the person raising your children what you would have wanted, and it helps a judge see your intent.

Parents with a child who has a disability should plan this more carefully, because adult guardianship questions and public benefits come into play. A [guardianship overview](/learn/guardianship) is the place to start, and an attorney can tailor the documents.
