Most parents think of a guardian as one person. The court sees two jobs. One is looking after a child. The other is looking after the child's property. When the assets are small, a single guardian handles both without anyone noticing. When the child receives real money, the second job becomes its own formal role with its own rules.

This article explains the difference, what the court expects of each person, and how parents can plan so that their children are not pulled into a conservatorship at all.

## What does a guardian of the person do?

A guardian of the person makes the decisions a parent would. The guardian decides where the child lives, picks the school, consents to medical treatment, and handles daily life. In most states the guardian is the person named in your will, confirmed by the court after your death. The standard is the child's best interests.

Authority over the child does not include control of the child's money. A guardian who takes in a niece and wants to use her inheritance for the mortgage cannot do it without proper authority. Our guide on [how to make guardianship legal](/learn/guardianship/how-to-make-guardianship-legal) explains how the court process works, and [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) covers the selection.

## What does a conservator or guardian of the estate do?

A conservator manages the child's property. They collect money owed to the child, invest it prudently, pay legitimate expenses, and file reports with the court. They owe [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary) duties to the child. Some states call this role "guardian of the estate" or "conservator of the estate." The word "conservatorship" is also used for adults who cannot manage their affairs, as our glossary entry on [conservatorship](/glossary/conservatorship) shows.

The court usually restricts the account. Withdrawals may require permission, and a bond may be required. The conservator reports on a regular schedule until the child is an adult.

## How do the two roles compare?

| | Guardian of the person | Conservator of the estate |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | The child's care and upbringing | The child's money and property |
| Typical decisions | Home, school, doctors, activities | Investing, paying expenses, selling property |
| Named in | Parents' will, then court confirmation | Court appointment, sometimes nominated in a will |
| Court oversight | Light in most states, sometimes annual reports | Heavy: inventory, bond, annual accounting |
| Needed when | Both parents are gone or unable to care for the child | The child owns significant property outside a trust or custodial account |
| Ends | At adulthood or earlier by court order | At adulthood, after final accounting |

## When is a conservator actually needed?

Only when a minor owns assets in their own name and no one else has legal authority over them. A typical trigger is a life insurance payout made directly to a child, an inheritance with no trust, or a personal injury settlement. Many states allow an adult to manage small amounts without court appointment, but the threshold varies and is often modest.

Consider this example. Lena and Marcus have two children, ages 5 and 8. They name Lena's sister as guardian in their will but never name a beneficiary other than the children on their life insurance. If both parents die, the insurer will not pay a child. The aunt must ask the court to create a conservatorship. She posts a bond, files an inventory and reports every year. The children turn 18, and the account turns over whatever is left. A trust would have avoided all of it.

That scenario is covered in more detail in [what if a minor inherits without a trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust).

## How can parents avoid a conservatorship?

Plan the money so that no child owns it directly. Options:

1. **A trust,** either [living or testamentary](/learn/guardianship/testamentary-trust-for-minor-children), with a trustee you pick.
2. **A UTMA custodian** for smaller amounts, as compared in our guide to [UTMA vs. trust](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child).
3. **Correct beneficiary forms** on life insurance and retirement accounts, naming the trust or custodian.
4. **A pour-over will** to catch anything left out of a living trust.

These approaches also let you split the jobs: one person raises the children and another controls the money, a design we describe in [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee).

## What does the court look at when choosing a conservator?

The judge wants a person who is honest, able to handle money, and free of conflicts. A parent's nomination carries weight, but the court checks. It may look at credit history, criminal history and whether the nominee has a personal interest in the funds. If the nominated person lives far away, the judge may ask how they will handle the work.

Conflicts matter more than people expect. Suppose the guardian who raises the child is also a beneficiary of the child's estate if the child dies. The court may prefer a separate conservator, or require extra safeguards, to remove the temptation to treat the child's account as the household's.

### What does a conservator report?

Typical reports include an initial inventory of the child's assets, usually due within a set number of days after appointment, and an annual account showing income, expenses and the current balance. Receipts must support spending. Spending on a child's actual needs is allowed, while ordinary parental duties, such as basic food and clothing, are often supposed to come from the guardian's own funds unless the court says otherwise. The details vary by state, so ask the clerk of the court or an attorney in your county.

### What happens if the conservator misuses the money?

The court can remove the conservator, order repayment from the conservator or the bond, and in serious cases refer the case for criminal charges. The child, or another interested person, can raise the problem. Once the child is an adult, they can usually demand a full accounting and sue for losses. The process resembles the one described in [can a guardian be removed or refuse to serve](/learn/guardianship/can-a-guardian-be-removed-or-refuse-to-serve).

## Does the same terminology apply to adults?

The words are the same but the situation is not. For an adult who cannot manage their affairs, a guardian handles personal decisions and a conservator handles money, in states that use both terms. Some states, such as those following the Uniform Guardianship and Protective Proceedings Act, use "guardian" for personal decisions and "conservator" for property. Others use "guardian" for both. If you are caring for an aging parent, a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) may let you avoid court. Our comparison on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) covers when it will and will not.

## What should I put in my will?

Your will can do three useful things:

- **Name a guardian and an alternate,** so the court has your preference.
- **Name a trustee or custodian** for any property left to the children.
- **Say whether one person should handle both,** and ask the court to waive a bond if you trust them.

None of that binds the court completely, but courts generally follow a parent's reasonable choice. The question of whether a nomination survives a challenge is in [does a guardian named in a will hold up in court](/learn/guardianship/does-a-guardian-named-in-a-will-hold-up-in-court).
