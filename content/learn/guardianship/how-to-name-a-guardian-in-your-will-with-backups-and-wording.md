If you have a child under 18, naming a guardian is the most important sentence in your will. It tells a court who you want to raise your children if you cannot. Writing it takes ten minutes. Getting it right takes a few decisions that most parents have not yet made.

This page shows how to name a guardian, what the clause looks like, how to line up backups, and what belongs in the will versus a separate letter. If you are still deciding who, start with [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) and come back. The overview lives in our [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship).

## How do you name a guardian in a will?

You write a clause that appoints a person by full name to care for your minor children if you die and no other parent is able to act. The clause sits in the body of your will, usually in a section on children. It does not need legal jargon, but it does need precision.

Here is the shape of a typical clause. It is an illustration only, so do not copy it without an attorney adapting it to your state.

> If at my death any child of mine is under age 18 and no other parent is living and able to act, I appoint my sister, Renata Okafor, of Naperville, Illinois, as guardian of the person of each such child. If Renata Okafor cannot or will not serve, I appoint my friend, Daniel Reyes, as guardian of the person. If neither can serve, I appoint my brother, Tomas Reyes.

Notice what the clause does. It states the condition, so it does not collide with a living parent's rights. It uses full names and a place so that nobody confuses two cousins both called Dan. It lists the backups in a fixed order. And it says "will not serve," because refusal is as common as death.

## How many backup guardians should you name?

Two backups is a good number. Three is better if you can find three people you would trust.

The first choice is the one most likely to fall through. People move, divorce, fall ill and lose jobs. A couple you pick together can split up, and then the clause names two homes that no longer exist. A good test is to ask who you would choose if your first choice said no today. If you hesitate, your backup list is not finished.

Beyond that, a few practical points:

1. **Spread the geography.** Do not name three relatives who all live on the same street if you would rather the children stay in their school.
2. **Ask each person.** Say it plainly, and say what you would hope for. Some will tell you they cannot.
3. **Name individuals, not couples.** If you name "my brother and his wife" and they divorce, the court has to pick one. Name the person you trust most and note that the spouse is welcome.
4. **Check age and health.** A grandparent who is 74 is a loving choice and a short-term one. Our article on [grandparents as guardians](/learn/guardianship/can-grandparents-be-named-guardian) covers the trade-offs.

## What is the difference between a guardian of the person and the estate?

A guardian of the person raises the child: housing, schooling, doctors, daily life. A guardian of the estate manages money the child owns. You can name one person for both, or split the work.

Many parents name a relative to raise the children and a different person, or a bank, to manage money through a trust. That prevents the guardian from having to report to a court for each tuition payment. Our article on [guardian versus trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) shows when each fits. If you do nothing, a court may appoint a guardian of the estate and require reports and a bond.

## What does Illinois or New Jersey require?

Both states let a parent nominate a guardian in a will, and neither treats the nomination as an order.

In Illinois, a parent can designate a guardian for a minor in writing, including in a will, and the designation needs at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/11-5). A minor who is 14 or older may nominate a guardian too. The court still decides what is in the child's best interest. Read the details in [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship).

In New Jersey, either parent may appoint a guardian of the person and a guardian of the estate by will for a child who is under 18 and unmarried at the parent's death (N.J.S.A. 3B:12-13), and a surviving parent can also make the appointment (N.J.S.A. 3B:12-15). Our [New Jersey guardianship article](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) has the full steps.

In both states, sign the will with the witnesses the state requires. A self-proving affidavit lets the court accept the will without calling the witnesses. Our guide to [making a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) explains the signing.

## Can you leave instructions for how the children are raised?

Yes, but carefully. A will is a public document once it is filed, and courts do not enforce parenting directions like a contract. So put legal appointments in the will and put everything else in a letter.

What goes in the will:

- The names of the guardian and the backups.
- A request that the guardian serve without bond, if your state allows it.
- A line saying the clause is your considered choice, so a later challenger sees your intent.

What goes in the letter:

- Schools, doctors, medications, allergies and routines.
- Your views on religion, activities and family contact.
- People who matter to the children: grandparents, close friends, a godparent.
- Where the money is and who holds it.

Our guide to the [letter of intent for your children's guardian](/learn/guardianship/letter-of-intent-for-your-childrens-guardian) gives a full outline. Update it each year, usually at a birthday.

## What mistakes do parents make when naming a guardian?

These are the ones our attorneys see again and again.

| Mistake | What goes wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Naming no backup | The court chooses if the first cannot serve | Name two or three in order |
| Using a nickname or role only | "My brother" could mean two people after a remarriage | Full legal name and place |
| Never asking the person | The nominee refuses at the worst moment | Ask, and write down the answer |
| Forgetting the money | Guardian must petition for every expense | Trust or guardian of the estate |
| Leaving an old will in place | The named person has moved, divorced or died | Review every few years |
| Two wills that disagree | Court sees conflicting nominations | Match the two parents' clauses |

One more issue deserves a plain statement. If the other parent is alive and fit, that parent keeps custody, whatever your will says. Your nomination only operates when no parent is available. If you are divorced and worried, our article on [what happens when you die and the other parent survives](/learn/guardianship/does-the-surviving-parent-get-custody-if-you-die) explains the limits.

### A worked example

Priya and Sam, both 36, have a daughter aged 3 and a son aged 6. They choose Priya's sister Anjali in Schaumburg as first guardian, Sam's college friend Ben as second, and Sam's mother as third. They ask each person. They name Anjali as guardian of the person only, and a trust handles the money, with Priya's cousin as trustee. Their letter lists the children's school, the pediatrician, and the sentence "please keep the Sunday call with Grandpa." They file all of this with their will, and mark the calendar to look again after the next child or the next move.
