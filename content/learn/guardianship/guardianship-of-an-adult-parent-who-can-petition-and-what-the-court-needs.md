Your mother has dementia. She has stopped paying bills, she has been taken in by a phone scam, and she will not discuss moving. She never signed a power of attorney. A hospital social worker, a bank manager, or an attorney friend uses the word guardianship, and now you are trying to learn what it means before you decide.

This page is about guardianship of an adult, not of a child (for children, see our [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship)). It covers who can file, what a judge looks for, what a person loses, how to contest it, and what documents prevent it. Our article on [guardianship versus power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) compares the two tools directly.

## The short version

- Guardianship is a last resort. A court takes decision-making away from a living person, so the law requires proof.
- It is expensive, slow, public and stressful. Families usually regret not signing documents earlier.
- A judge needs medical evidence, notice to the person, and a hearing where they may object.
- If the person still understands what they are signing, a power of attorney is cheaper and keeps them in charge.
- If it is too late for documents, guardianship is how families protect someone who cannot protect themselves.

## What is guardianship of an adult?

It is a court order that gives another person legal authority over some or all of an incapacitated adult's decisions. The person is usually called the ward or the protected person. The appointed person is the guardian, or in some states the conservator, and the words differ. Many states use guardian for personal and medical decisions and conservator for money, as our page on [guardian of the person and conservator of the estate](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate) describes. New York calls its process Article 81, and other states use different names for similar steps.

The idea behind all of it is the same. A competent adult has the right to make bad decisions. A court steps in only when a person cannot make or communicate decisions about their own safety or property, and when less drastic help will not work.

## Who can petition for guardianship of an adult?

Most states let a broad list of people file: a spouse, an adult child, a sibling, another relative, a friend, a nursing home or hospital, or a government agency. Some states let the person file for themselves. The petitioner does not have to be the person who ends up as guardian.

The petition usually asks the court to name a specific person. When several relatives want the job, judges often prefer a spouse or an adult child, then other relatives, then a neutral professional. If a power of attorney or healthcare directive named someone, most courts give that choice real weight, even though the document does not end the guardianship case.

## What does the court need to see?

A petition alone is not enough. Judges generally want:

- **Medical evidence.** A physician's report or evaluation describing the person's condition and how it affects daily functioning. Many states require a specific form or a court-appointed examiner.
- **Notice to the person.** The adult has the right to be told the case is happening, in plain language, and to attend the hearing.
- **A lawyer for the person.** Many states appoint one, or a guardian ad litem, to represent the person's interests and report to the judge.
- **A showing that less restrictive options will not do.** The court asks whether a power of attorney, supported decision-making, home care or a representative payee could meet the need.
- **A proposed plan.** Where the person will live, who will pay for care, and who will manage the money.

Think of Hector, 71, who has advanced Alzheimer's. His daughter, Lucia, files after he leaves the stove on twice and a bank flags a large wire to a stranger. His physician submits an evaluation. A court investigator interviews Hector and visits his home. At the hearing, Hector's son, who lives out of state, supports Lucia. The judge appoints Lucia guardian of the person and of the estate, with a requirement to file annual reports.

## What rights does a person lose?

That depends on the order. Under a full guardianship, the person may lose the right to decide where to live, consent to medical care, manage money, sign contracts, or make a will. Some states reserve the right to vote, marry or make a will unless the court rules otherwise. Under a limited guardianship, the court gives the guardian only what the person can no longer handle and leaves the rest alone. Many states require the court to consider limited guardianship first.

| Decision | Full guardianship | Limited guardianship |
|---|---|---|
| Where the person lives | Guardian decides | Guardian decides only if the order says so |
| Medical treatment | Guardian consents | Guardian consents to listed treatments |
| Managing money | Guardian or conservator | Only the accounts the court lists |
| Signing contracts | Usually barred | Allowed except as limited |
| Making a will | Varies by state | Usually remains the person's right |

## Can a person contest a guardianship?

Yes. Anyone told they might be put under guardianship can object, ask for a lawyer, and present evidence. A relative can contest too, as can the person after the order is entered. Common grounds are that the medical evidence is weak, the person has more capacity than reported, a less restrictive option exists, or the proposed guardian is a poor choice or has a conflict.

If you are the person facing it, or a relative who disagrees, move quickly. Deadlines to object can be short. Ask for the medical report, and consider asking for an independent evaluation. A person who was put under guardianship can usually ask the court to modify or end it later if their condition improves.

## What does a guardian have to do?

A guardian of the person looks after housing, health care and daily needs. A guardian of the estate manages money, pays bills and protects property. Neither can treat the person's money as their own. Most states require:

1. A bond or insurance on the estate's value, in many cases.
2. An initial inventory of the person's assets.
3. Periodic reports to the court, often yearly, and accountings of every dollar.
4. Court approval for major moves, such as selling a house or placing the person in a facility.

Guardians can be paid reasonable fees in many states, but the court usually reviews them. A professional guardian, sometimes called a public guardian when run by a county or state agency, is appointed when no suitable family member can serve.

## How can you avoid guardianship for a parent?

The most reliable way is to act while the person still has capacity. If your parent can understand what they are signing, even in the early stages of memory loss, the following usually keep a court out of it:

1. **A durable financial power of attorney.** Our page on [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) explains what it covers.
2. **A healthcare power of attorney and HIPAA release.** See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
3. **A revocable living trust,** if there is a house or sizable accounts, so a successor trustee can manage them.
4. **A conversation about their wishes,** written down. Our article on [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) suggests how to start it.

If there are no documents and the capacity question is close, ask an elder law attorney whether the person can still sign. Capacity is task-specific, and a person with memory loss can often sign a power of attorney on a good day. Our article on [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) walks through that situation.

## What about emergencies and sibling disputes?

Emergency or temporary guardianship exists for situations where waiting weeks would cause harm, such as a hospital discharge with no safe plan. The court acts quickly on sworn facts, and the order is short-lived. It is followed by a full hearing.

Sibling disagreements are the other common trigger. One child controls the money, another suspects abuse, and a petition follows. If that is your situation, gather records, ask for a neutral evaluation, and consider whether a professional guardian or an independent fiduciary takes the pressure off the family. Our guide on [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) lists warning signs that judges take seriously.
