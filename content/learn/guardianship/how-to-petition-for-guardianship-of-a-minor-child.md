Most guardianship cases are not planned. A mother is hospitalized for months. A father is deported or incarcerated. A grandparent has had the child at home for a year and the school wants a court order before it will talk to anyone but a parent. Whatever brought you here, the practical question is the same: what do I file, and what will the judge want to see?

This guide covers the court process for a minor child, not the planning side where a parent names a guardian in a will. For that, begin with the [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship). Court names and forms vary by state, so treat the steps below as a map and check your local court's website for exact papers.

## When do you need a court-ordered guardianship?

You need one when an adult who is not the child's parent must make lasting decisions and no parent can sign. Signing school forms, consenting to surgery, applying for benefits or moving a child between states are the usual triggers.

A shorter fix may do if the gap is brief. Parents can sometimes sign a limited authorization letting a relative handle school and medical matters for a defined time. Several states offer a formal caregiver authorization or affidavit for this purpose. Our page on [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians) covers those tools. If the arrangement will last more than a year, or the parents cannot be reached, court appointment gives you the authority outsiders respect.

Be clear on what you are asking for. [Guardianship, custody and adoption](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-vs-custody-vs-adoption) are different requests, and the judge will want to know which you want.

## Who can ask the court to appoint a guardian?

In most states any interested adult can file. That includes a grandparent, aunt or uncle, adult sibling, family friend, or the person a parent named in a will. A few states limit who may file or give relatives first claim. In many states a teenager also gets to say whom they prefer, and the judge listens.

A guardian named in a will starts with an advantage. Courts generally follow a parent's nomination unless something shows the person is unfit or the choice would harm the child. See [whether a named guardian holds up in court](/learn/guardianship/does-a-guardian-named-in-a-will-hold-up-in-court).

## What are the steps?

The details vary, but the path looks like this.

1. Confirm the right court. It is usually in the county where the child lives, and it may be probate, family, surrogate or juvenile court.
2. Get the forms. Most courts post a petition, a notice, a proposed order and an information sheet online or at a clerk's office.
3. Complete the petition. It lists the child, the parents, the people with whom the child has lived, why a guardian is needed and who you are.
4. File and pay the fee, or ask for a fee waiver if you cannot afford it. Fees vary a good deal by court.
5. Give notice. Parents, and often grandparents or others close to the child, must be formally notified, usually by personal delivery or mail with proof. A child over a certain age gets notice too.
6. Get consents or responses. A parent can sign a consent form, which usually simplifies everything. An objection moves the case toward a contested hearing.
7. Complete required checks. Courts commonly require a criminal background check and sometimes a child-protection record check.
8. Attend the hearing. The judge asks questions, hears from the parents and decides.
9. Collect the order and letters. After approval the clerk issues a document, often called letters of guardianship, which you show to schools, doctors and agencies.

## What does a judge look for?

The standard is the child's best interests, applied against the strong legal presumption that a fit parent should raise their own child. A court will not hand a child to a relative because the relative has a nicer house. It will do so when the parent cannot or will not provide care, or consents.

Judges commonly weigh these points:

- The child's safety and any history of abuse or neglect in the household.
- How stable the proposed home is, including housing, income and who else lives there.
- The existing bond between the child and the petitioner, and whether the child already lives with them.
- The child's wishes, given weight as the child gets older.
- The parents' views and whether they consent.
- The petitioner's ability to meet medical, school and emotional needs.
- Whether siblings can stay together.

When the facts are contested, the judge may appoint a guardian ad litem or order a home study. These add weeks but also give you a chance to show the court how the child lives day to day. Be honest about your own history. A court can forgive an old arrest, but it rarely forgives one that the petitioner hid.

## Do you need a lawyer?

If the parents agree and no one objects, many people file themselves, with the help of the court's self-help desk or a legal aid clinic. Paperwork errors, particularly in notice, are the main thing that delays a case. A defective notice can force you to start the step over.

Hire an attorney when a parent contests, when the child has property or an expected inheritance, when a government agency is already involved, or when the child or a parent lives in another state or country. Cases that touch several states raise questions about which court has power to decide, and those are easy to get wrong.

Aaliyah, 29, wanted guardianship of her 12-year-old brother Dominic after their mother's long hospitalization. Their father lived overseas and could not be reached. Aaliyah filed alone, but the court would not proceed until she tried to serve her father by the methods the rules allow. After a few weeks of documented efforts, the judge allowed notice by another method, held a short hearing and granted the order. A lawyer would have told her that on day one.

## What if the child needs help right away?

Most states have an emergency or temporary guardianship that can be granted within days on a showing of immediate risk, such as a parent unreachable or a child left without a caregiver. The order is short and is followed by a full hearing. Ask the clerk about an emergency petition if the child cannot safely wait. Illinois families can look at [Illinois standby and short-term guardians](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children) for one state's version.

## What happens after you are appointed?

You take on duties. A guardian of the person decides where the child lives, school and care. If the child has money, a guardian of the estate or conservator manages it under court supervision. Many courts require an annual report on the child's wellbeing. Our page on [what a guardian can decide](/learn/guardianship/what-a-guardian-can-decide-for-your-children) sets out the limits, and [removal and resignation](/learn/guardianship/can-a-guardian-be-removed-or-refuse-to-serve) covers what follows if things change.

The order is not permanent. Parents can ask to end it when their circumstances improve. For the planning side, parents who want to avoid this process altogether can name a guardian in advance. Try the [guardian picker](/tools/guardian-picker) if you are deciding between candidates.
