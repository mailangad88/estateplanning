People searching for how to make a guardianship legal are usually in one of two places. Some are parents with a baby and a blank will, wondering how to be sure someone they trust gets their kids. Others are aunts, grandparents or family friends who have a child living with them and no paper that proves they can sign a school form.

The two situations use different tools. This page walks through both: how a parent sets up a guardianship in advance, and how a caregiver asks a court for one today. Neither route is complicated, but each has steps you cannot skip.

## The short version

- Only a court can make someone a guardian. A will nominates, and a court appoints.
- Parents make the future guardianship legal by signing a valid will that names the guardian and backups.
- A caregiver who needs authority now files a petition with the court that handles minors in their county.
- Expect notice to the parents, a hearing, and sometimes a home visit or background check.
- Until the order is signed, short-term documents can fill the gap.

## Route one: parents set it up in advance

If you are planning ahead, there is no filing with a court today. The guardianship legally begins only if you die or cannot act, but you create the foundation now.

1. **Pick the guardian and backups.** Our guide to [choosing a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) covers how.
2. **Ask each person.** Nobody can be forced to serve.
3. **Put the nomination in a will.** The will has to follow your state's signing rules, which usually means the testator's signature plus two witnesses. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).
4. **Add a self-proving affidavit if your state offers one.** It lets the court accept the will without tracking down witnesses.
5. **Tell the guardian where the will is.** Give them a copy and your attorney's contact.
6. **Plan the money separately.** A trust or properly named life insurance keeps the guardian from needing a court-run account. See [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).
7. **Review every few years.**

After both parents die, the nominated guardian files a petition, shows the court the will, and asks to be confirmed. How that plays out when the nominee is challenged is in [does a guardian named in a will hold up in court](/learn/guardianship/does-a-guardian-named-in-a-will-hold-up-in-court).

## Route two: a caregiver petitions the court now

This route fits situations where the parents are alive but cannot or will not parent, or where a parent has died and no nomination exists. A grandmother, an uncle or a family friend files a petition for guardianship of the minor.

### Which court handles it?

The name varies. It might be probate court, family court, juvenile court, or a surrogate's or orphans' court. Start with the clerk of the court in the county where the child lives. Most clerks can tell you which form to use, though they cannot give legal advice.

### What are the steps?

| Step | What happens | Typical papers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Petition | You explain who you are, who the child is, why a guardian is needed | Petition form, child's birth certificate |
| 2. Notice | Parents, certain relatives and sometimes the child must be told | Proof of service |
| 3. Consent or objection | A parent may sign consent, or contest | Consent form or response |
| 4. Review | Some courts require a background check or home study | Fingerprints, references, income information |
| 5. Hearing | A judge asks questions and decides on the child's best interests | Your testimony and any witnesses |
| 6. Order and letters | The judge signs an order and the clerk issues letters | Certified copies |

If a parent agrees, the case is often quick, and some courts decide it on paper. If a parent objects, expect a contested hearing, and consider a lawyer.

### What will the judge look at?

The test is the child's best interests. The judge asks whether the child is safe where they live, whether you can provide stable housing and schooling, whether the parents consent or have been unreachable, and what the child wants if they are old enough. A judge who sees a caregiver already providing steady care, with a clear explanation, tends to see the case as simple.

Here is a typical situation. Denise, 52, has had her 10-year-old niece Amari for eight months while Amari's mother recovers from a long illness. The school keeps asking for a parent's signature and the doctor will not schedule a procedure. Amari's mother signs a consent. Denise files the petition, the court sets a hearing three weeks out, and she receives letters the same day. The mother later asks to end the guardianship once she is well, which the judge grants after a review.

## Can you get something faster than a full guardianship?

Yes. Courts and states offer quicker options.

- **A temporary guardianship order.** A judge can grant short-term authority when delay would hurt the child. Our article on [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians) explains how it works.
- **A parent's written authorization.** Many states let a parent sign a notarized form giving another adult authority for school and medical decisions for a set time. It requires no court, and the parent can revoke it.
- **A medical consent form.** It covers treatment only, and hospitals usually accept it in non-emergency situations.

These do not replace a full order if the arrangement lasts, but they keep life moving.

## What if the child or the parents live in another state?

Venue is the usual stumbling block. A petition normally goes to the court where the child lives, but a court that already handled a custody case for the family may keep control. If the parents are in another state, notice has to reach them there, which adds time. When a child has recently moved in with a relative, ask the clerk which court has jurisdiction before filing, because a petition filed in the wrong place gets dismissed and the clock starts over.

Parents who are planning ahead face a smaller version of this. A will signed in one state is generally honored in another, but the guardianship petition after a death is filed where the children live then. If you move, have the will checked against your new state's rules. The overall plan is described in our [guide for parents on naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship).

## Is guardianship of a child the same as guardianship of an adult?

No. This page is about minors. Guardianship of an incapacitated adult is a separate court process with different standards, more oversight and its own consequences for the person's rights. If that is your situation, our article on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains the adult side and how planning can avoid it.

## What should you do after the order is signed?

1. Get several certified copies of the letters.
2. Give copies to the school, the pediatrician and the child's health insurer.
3. Ask the clerk whether annual reports or check-ins are required. Some courts require a short report on the child's wellbeing.
4. Keep receipts if you manage any of the child's money, and ask whether a separate conservator of the estate is needed.
5. Calendar the end date or review date if there is one.

## How state law changes this

States differ in the name of the court, the forms, the notice rules, whether a home study is required, and how long a temporary order lasts. Some states have statutes that let parents sign a standby designation. Check your state court's self-help page, and read our state guide for [Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) or [California](/estate-planning/california) for how the process looks locally.
