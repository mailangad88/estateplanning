Parents with young children usually want one thing from their estate plan: that the kids are cared for and the money goes where it should. The law gets in the way in a simple but surprising manner. A child under 18 cannot legally manage property. If you leave an inheritance to a minor with no further instructions, the money often lands in a court-supervised account.

This page compares the main ways to leave money to minors, shows where each works well and where it does not, and explains why your beneficiary forms matter as much as your will.

## What happens when a minor inherits directly?

When a minor is named as beneficiary of a will, bank account, retirement account or insurance policy, the institution cannot hand the money to a child. A court typically appoints a guardian or conservator of the estate. That person manages the money under court rules, posts a bond, files periodic accounting reports, and asks permission for larger expenses.

The costs are real. Court filings, attorney fees, bond premiums and annual reports take money from the child's inheritance. Many states then require the account to be turned over at 18, when most young people are not ready for a lump sum. A court-supervised account is better than nothing, but it is the default you want to avoid.

## What are your main options?

| Option | Who manages the money | Child receives it | Cost and complexity | Control |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trust (living or testamentary) | Trustee you choose | At ages you set, or lifetime management | Higher to set up, ongoing admin | Highest |
| UTMA custodial account | Custodian you name | Automatically at 18 to 25 (state law) | Low | Low |
| 529 plan | Account owner | Used for education | Low | Medium, education only |
| Court conservatorship | Court-appointed person | Usually at 18 | High over time | Court's rules, not yours |
| Life insurance naming a trust or custodian | Trustee or custodian | Per the trust or UTMA rules | Low to moderate | Depends on the vehicle |

### How does a trust for minor children work?

A trust is a legal arrangement in which a trustee holds assets for a beneficiary under written rules. For children, a trust usually says the trustee may spend money for health, education, maintenance and support, and then the trust pays out in stages. A common pattern is one-third at 25, one-third at 30 and the rest at 35, but families choose anything from a single payout to lifetime protection.

You can set up a [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) now and fund it, or write a [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust) inside your will that is created at your death. The second goes through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) but costs less to create. Our guide to [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) explains staggered distributions, incentive terms and how to treat children of different ages. Adding a [spendthrift clause](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) keeps a child's creditors, or a future ex-spouse, from reaching trust assets.

The person who runs the trust is the [trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee). Choose a person and a backup with care.

### How does a UTMA account work?

A UTMA account lets someone give money or securities to a minor through a custodian. The gift is irrevocable. The custodian invests and spends for the child's benefit, and when the child reaches the age set by state law, the account is theirs with no strings. State ages range from 18 to 25, and some states let the donor pick within a range. Your own state's rules decide, so ask an attorney.

Reasons parents use UTMA accounts: they cost almost nothing to open, they work for modest sums such as a grandparent's gift, and they require no lawyer. Reasons to be cautious:

- At the age of transfer, the child controls everything, including a large amount you may not have intended.
- Custodial assets can count against the student in financial aid formulas.
- Investment income is taxed to the child, and the "kiddie tax" rules can tax unearned income above a threshold at the parent's rates.
- A custodian who dies or is unfit may be replaced only through a simple filing or a court, depending on state.

You can name a custodian in your will or beneficiary designation. Many states let you do so, but the nomination only works if state law allows custodianship for that type of asset, so check.

### What about 529 education plans?

A 529 plan is useful for education savings, and it keeps control with the account owner rather than the child. If you die, a successor owner you named takes over. Because the money is limited to education uses without penalties, a 529 plan is a supplement, not a substitute, for a trust.

### What is a court conservatorship, and why is it a last resort?

A conservatorship (called a guardianship of the estate in some states) is a court-run arrangement. The person managing your child's money must follow rules for investments, report to the court, and seek permission for many expenses. It protects the child, but it is public, takes time, and gives you no voice. A trust lets you design the rules yourself.

## How should you handle life insurance for minors?

Life insurance is often the biggest asset a young family leaves, so the beneficiary designation matters a great deal. Never name a minor child directly. Options are:

1. Name a trust as beneficiary, with the trustee under the terms you wrote.
2. Name an adult as "custodian for [child] under the [state] Uniform Transfers to Minors Act," which works for smaller sums.
3. Name your spouse first, then a trust as contingent beneficiary.

Review every form, because forms override your will. Our guide to [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) shows how to coordinate them, and [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) explains why a backup matters. The same applies to retirement accounts, where naming a trust takes extra care; see [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

### Example: Dana and Marcus

Dana and Marcus, both 36, have a 4-year-old and a 7-year-old. They carry $1 million in term life insurance each, a house with a mortgage, and about $180,000 in retirement savings. They pick Marcus's sister as guardian and her husband's cousin, an accountant, as trustee. They create a living trust for the children, name the trust as contingent beneficiary on their policies and 401(k)s, and set distributions at 25, 30 and 35. If both die, the guardian raises the children, the trustee pays for housing, school and health, and the children get lump sums as adults. No court account is needed.

## What should the trust say about spending?

Good instructions answer practical questions ahead of time.

- What counts as support and education, and does it include private school or college?
- Can the trustee help the guardian with housing?
- How are the children treated if one has a greater need, such as medical costs?
- When does the trust end, and what happens to a child who dies before getting their share?
- What if a child has special needs and relies on public benefits? A standard trust could put those benefits at risk; see [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).

## How do you keep this up to date?

Review beneficiary forms and trust terms after any birth, death, divorce, move or big change in assets. A common mistake is a policy bought years ago that still names an ex-spouse, a deceased parent, or "my children" with no trust at all.
