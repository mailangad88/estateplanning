A child has lost a parent, and the money that was meant to carry them through school is now in someone else's hands. Months later, you notice that the guardian has a new truck, the child has the same shoes, and nobody will answer questions about the account. You want to know what the law gives you to work with.

This page covers how a court treats a guardian who handles a minor's money, how to ask for an accounting, what the court can do, and how parents can plan so that no one has to ask. It is part of our [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship).

## What is a guardian allowed to do with a child's money?

Use it for the child, keep it separate and be able to prove it. A guardian of the estate (called a guardian of the property in New Jersey) is a fiduciary, which means the law requires loyalty to the child, not to themselves. The money should sit in an account titled for the child's benefit. Our page on [guardian of the person versus conservator of the estate](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate) explains the two jobs.

| Allowed | Not allowed |
|---|---|
| Paying school costs, medical bills and activities | Buying a car titled in the guardian's name |
| Reasonable repayment of documented expenses for the child | Lending the money to the guardian or family |
| Court-approved fees | Paying the guardian a salary the court never approved |
| Investing in line with court rules | Speculative investments, or investing in the guardian's business |
| Setting aside savings for the child's adulthood | Mixing the child's money with the guardian's accounts |

A guardian who is also a relative often feels the line is blurry. A grandmother raising her grandson may spend a share of her electric bill and food on him, and that is reasonable. What she cannot do is treat his $200,000 as a family fund. A good habit is to write a short note beside each large expense that says how it helped the child.

## What are the warning signs?

Usually it is a pattern, not one event. Here are the signs families report.

- The guardian refuses to say where the money is held.
- Account statements stop, or arrive in the guardian's name.
- The child's life does not match the funds: old clothes, missed activities, no savings for college.
- The guardian makes a large purchase soon after taking over.
- Court filings that were due are late or missing.
- A guardian suddenly asks about changing the investments or cashing out an annuity.

None of these proves anything. All of them justify a question.

## How do you ask for an accounting?

1. **Write down what you know.** Dates, amounts, names, and where you saw it.
2. **Find the case.** Call the clerk of the court that appointed the guardian. In Illinois that is the circuit court clerk of the county, and in New Jersey, the county Surrogate or the Superior Court, depending on the type of case. Ask what has been filed.
3. **Read the filings.** An inventory and periodic accounts should be in the file. Missing ones are themselves a reason for a motion.
4. **Ask the guardian first, in writing,** if the relationship allows. A short, polite letter asking for a copy of the latest account sometimes ends the matter.
5. **File a petition or motion.** A relative, the child's other parent, a guardian ad litem or an attorney can ask the court to compel an accounting. A lawyer helps because courts expect specific requests, not general suspicion.
6. **Attend the hearing.** The guardian will have to explain gaps. A judge can order the missing records, appoint someone to review them and set deadlines.

If the money is in a trust, not under a guardianship, the path is different. The trustee has a duty to inform and account to beneficiaries. For a child, the notices go to the child's legal representative. Our page on [a trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) covers the rights beneficiaries have.

## What can the court do about it?

Judges have a wide range of tools, and they usually start with the mildest one that works.

1. Order an accounting and a deadline.
2. Limit the guardian's authority, for example by requiring court approval before each withdrawal (a blocked account).
3. Require or increase a bond.
4. Appoint a guardian ad litem or an accountant to review the books.
5. Remove the guardian and appoint a successor. See [can a guardian be removed](/learn/guardianship/can-a-guardian-be-removed-or-refuse-to-serve).
6. Order repayment with interest, sometimes called a surcharge.
7. Send the matter to prosecutors if funds were taken.

A bond is insurance for the child's money. If a court orders one, a surety company stands behind the guardian up to a stated amount, and the child's estate pays the premium. The company then pursues the guardian for what it has to pay. It is no cure-all: the bond amount may be lower than the loss, and getting repayment takes time. In New Jersey, our page on [guardian of the property for a minor](/learn/new-jersey/minor-inherits-money-in-new-jersey-guardian-of-the-property) explains how it works there.

## A worked example

Example only. Noor, 8, inherits $240,000 in life insurance when her mother dies. Her aunt Carla, named guardian in the will, becomes both guardian of Noor and guardian of the money, since the will did not name a trustee. Two years later, Noor's grandfather notices that Carla has put in a new kitchen. He asks for the accounts. The court file shows an inventory but no accounts since the first year.

He files a motion. The judge orders Carla to file an accounting in 30 days. It shows $38,000 spent on "household improvements" and $12,000 on a vehicle. Carla says it all helped Noor. The judge allows a share for Noor's housing, disallows the rest, orders repayment and replaces Carla as guardian of the money with a bank, while Carla stays as Noor's guardian of the person. Noor's care continues. Her money is now protected.

The better ending is one where Noor's mother, before she died, had named a trustee. That is covered next.

## How can a parent prevent this?

Do not give a guardian control of the money by default. Three structures work well.

- **Separate the roles.** Name a guardian for the child and a different trustee for the money. Our page on [guardian versus trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) shows how.
- **Use a trust inside your will or a living trust.** A [testamentary trust for minor children](/learn/guardianship/testamentary-trust-for-minor-children) puts the funds under a trustee's rules until the child reaches the ages you pick.
- **Add safeguards.** Require annual statements to a second person, such as a relative or the guardian, allow payments only to providers for education and medical costs, and give the trustee a written way to pay the guardian a monthly housing amount for the child.

If you cannot afford a trust, a UTMA account still has a custodian who answers for the money, but with fewer controls. See [UTMA versus a trust](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child).
