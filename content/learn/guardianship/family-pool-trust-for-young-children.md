Parents of two or three young children often hit the same snag when they plan. One child is seven, another is four, a third is on the way. The money left for them has to cover diapers and braces now, and college and a first apartment later. Splitting it into three equal buckets on day one feels tidy, but it does not match how kids actually cost money.

A family pool trust is built for that mismatch. This page explains how it works, where it helps, and where it gets in the way.

## What is a family pool trust?

It is a single trust that holds the inheritance for all of your minor children together. The trustee uses the fund for whichever child needs it, guided by a standard you write, which is usually the child's health, education, maintenance and support. Nobody keeps a running tally that has to come out even.

The pool ends at a trigger you choose. The most common trigger is the youngest child reaching a particular age. At that point the remaining money is divided into equal shares, and each share is then managed under the age rules you set for individual children.

Some lawyers call it a pot trust, because everything goes into one pot. Our guide to [testamentary trusts for minor children](/learn/guardianship/testamentary-trust-for-minor-children) shows the setup from the will's side, and the [guardianship overview](/learn/guardianship) shows how it fits with the person who raises the children.

## How does the money move through a pool?

Picture Priya and Ben, who have a son, 8, and a daughter, 3. Neither has a lot of savings, but a life insurance policy would pay a substantial amount if both die. They set up a family pool trust with Ben's sister as trustee.

In the first year after their deaths the trustee pays the guardian a housing contribution, covers the son's tutoring, and buys a bigger car. Then the son needs orthodontic work that costs more than anything the daughter needs. The trustee pays for it without matching it. Years later, when the daughter turns 25, the trustee divides what is left in two and each child's share is paid under the schedule in the trust.

The sequence in plain steps:

1. You die, and the insurance, accounts or estate property flow into the trust.
2. The trustee invests the fund and pays bills for the children as they come up.
3. The guardian reports needs, and the trustee decides what to pay.
4. When the trigger arrives, the trustee splits what remains into separate shares.
5. Each share is distributed at the ages you chose, or held longer if the trustee has discretion.

## Pool or separate trusts? A comparison

| Feature | Family pool trust | Separate trust per child |
|---|---|---|
| Spending | By need, any child | Only from that child's share |
| Fairness | Unequal spending is expected | Equal shares from day one |
| Age gaps | Older child may feel shortchanged when the pool lasts for the younger | Each child's timing is independent |
| Administration | One account and one set of tax returns | More accounts and filings |
| Special needs child | Poor fit without extra planning | Easier to protect benefits |
| Blended family | Can create friction between half-siblings | Cleaner lines |

Neither is better in the abstract. The pool favors flexibility and the separate trusts favor predictability. Siblings who are 2 years apart usually do fine in a pool. A 16-year-old and a 3-year-old rarely do, because the older child ages out of the pool's purpose long before the younger one does.

## What age should the pool end?

Pick an age that matches the youngest child's needs, not your own sense of what is fair. Many families choose a point when the youngest is finished with school, often somewhere between 21 and 25, though that is a choice and not a rule. If the oldest child is already an adult when the pool starts, you may want to pay that child out directly rather than hold the money while the younger ones grow up.

Some people use two stages. The pool runs until the youngest hits a first age, and then each share continues in its own trust with distributions at staggered ages. This is the "stagger my child's inheritance" approach that [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) discusses in more detail. A staggered schedule gives a young adult a chance to handle part of the money, learn from a mistake, and still have a cushion.

## Who should be the trustee?

The trustee is the most important decision in the document. The pool gives this person real discretion, and the discretion is only as good as their judgment. You want someone who can say no to a relative, keep records, and talk to a child about money without judgment.

You can name the guardian as trustee, but many families do not. Splitting the roles gives the children a check on the person who raises them. [Guardian versus trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) covers that choice. Where nobody in the family is suited to the job, a professional or corporate trustee is an option, with fees you should understand before you sign.

## What goes wrong with a family pool trust?

A few things, and they are mostly avoidable.

- **Silent resentment.** A child who sees the trust pay for a sibling's expensive treatment may feel cheated. Tell the trustee to explain decisions and tell the guardian to avoid scorekeeping.
- **Vague spending language.** "For their benefit" invites arguments. Say what counts: school, health care, reasonable housing, a car, a first-apartment deposit.
- **Underfunding.** A pool that pays everyone's costs and divides what remains only works if there is enough money. Life insurance often fills the gap. See [how much life insurance you need](/tools/life-insurance-needs) for a way to size it.
- **Mixing with an UTMA account.** If a child also holds assets in a custodial account, those pass to the child at the age fixed by state law, usually 18 to 25. Compare the two in [UTMA versus a trust](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child).
- **Wrong asset in the wrong place.** A trust named in a will does not receive retirement accounts or life insurance unless the beneficiary form names it. Naming the trust, or a trust created in your will, on the form is a separate step. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

## Where do 529 plans and grandparents fit?

A 529 plan is useful for the education piece, and the trust can cover everything else. The owner of a 529 controls it, so name a successor owner. Grandparents who want to help can fund a 529 or add to the trust, or they can create their own trust for a grandchild. With the federal estate and gift exclusion at $15,000,000 per person in 2026, most families will not face the generation-skipping tax, but large gifts deserve a look.

## How do I set one up?

Start by deciding who raises the children, who manages the money, and how much you can leave. Then an attorney drafts the trust, either inside a will or in a living trust, and you coordinate your beneficiary forms so the assets reach it.
