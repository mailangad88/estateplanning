You wrote your will, you named your sister as the person who would raise your kids, and now a quiet worry has crept in. What if your mother disagrees? What if a judge simply picks someone else? Parents ask this more often than almost anything else about guardianship.

The honest answer is that your choice carries real weight and no certainty. This page explains what a court does with a nomination, the specific things that make one fail, and how to write yours so that it is very hard to knock down.

## The short version

- The nomination is a request. A judge has to approve the appointment and must do what is best for the child.
- When the will is valid and the nominee is willing and fit, judges follow it in the large majority of cases.
- A living, fit legal parent normally has custody before anyone you name gets a say.
- Most failures are paperwork or timing problems: an unsigned will, a nominee who moved, a choice made ten years ago.
- Your best protection is a current will, a named backup, and a conversation with your family before you sign.

## Why a nomination is not binding

A guardian, in the legal sense, is someone with court-given authority over a minor. Only a court can give that authority. Your will cannot hand it over by itself, which is why the document is described as a "nomination" and not an appointment.

The reason is simple. A court's job is to protect the child, and the law does not let a parent's wishes from years earlier override what a judge sees at the hearing. Suppose Priya names her brother Dev in 2019. By 2026 Dev has lost his job, moved overseas and been hospitalized twice. A judge who simply rubber-stamped the will would be placing a child in a home nobody has checked.

So the court reads your nomination as strong evidence of what is best for the child. It treats you as the person who knew the child best. Many state statutes say so directly, giving a parent's nomination priority unless there is a good reason to depart from it. The details vary, so check your state.

## When the answer is yes

A judge nearly always follows a nomination when four things line up.

1. The will was validly signed and witnessed, and the court can accept it without a dispute.
2. Both parents have died or the surviving parent cannot act.
3. The person named is alive, willing, and has no obvious red flags such as a serious criminal history or a risky home.
4. Nobody else files a credible competing petition.

Take Hannah and Luis, both 38, with a 5-year-old and an 8-year-old. Their will names Hannah's older cousin Renee, with Luis's brother as backup. Both parents die in an accident. Renee files a short petition with a copy of the will and a statement that she is willing. Relatives are notified, no one objects, and the judge approves the appointment after a brief hearing. The whole matter takes a few weeks, and the children are not left in limbo.

That is the ordinary result. The surprise cases get discussed, but they are the exception.

## When the answer is no

There are five common ways a nomination fails or gets challenged.

| Problem | What happens | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|
| The will is not validly signed | The court may give the nomination little or no weight | Sign with the witnesses or notary your state requires |
| The nominee refuses or cannot serve | The court moves to the backup, or to competing petitions | Name successors in order |
| A fit surviving parent exists | That parent keeps custody, and the nomination is not reached | Plan for the other-parent scenario separately |
| The nominee is unfit by the time of the hearing | Judge declines to appoint | Review the choice every few years |
| Relatives challenge the choice | Contested hearing, costs and delay | Explain your reasons in advance and in writing |

### The surviving parent comes first

This surprises people most. If you and your ex shared custody and you die, the other parent generally has the right to the child, even if you wrote that you never want them to have custody. A will nomination does not remove a legal parent's rights. To keep a child from a parent, someone has to prove unfitness in court, which is a separate and difficult case. If that is your situation, see an attorney about it now; a clause in a will is not enough. Our guide to [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) covers the related updates.

### A challenge by family

A grandparent who thinks the nominee is a poor fit can ask the court to appoint someone else. Challengers sometimes argue the will itself is invalid, for example that the parent was pressured. Claims of [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) are uncommon where the parent's choice made sense and a lawyer supervised the signing. The process for attacking a will looks the same as any other, covered in our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## How do you make your nomination hard to challenge?

A few habits matter more than clever drafting.

1. **Put it in a valid will.** The formalities differ by state, and a [self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) lets the court accept the will without calling the witnesses. Our guide to [making a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) has the steps.
2. **Name at least two backups.** Courts are far more comfortable when the parents obviously thought it through.
3. **Ask each person first.** An unwilling guardian is the most common reason plans unravel. Our article on [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) has a script.
4. **Write a short statement of reasons.** One page saying why you chose this person, and why you did not choose others, gives a judge something to hold onto. Keep it separate from the will so it is not read aloud at a hearing.
5. **Tell the family in your own words.** Surprise creates challenges. A calm conversation does not promise peace, but it removes the shock.
6. **Keep the will findable.** If nobody can locate it, the nomination does not exist. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
7. **Revisit it.** The choice that made sense for a toddler may not suit a teenager. Look again every few years.

## What if the other parent is alive?

If the other legal parent is alive and fit, the court generally does not appoint a guardian at all. The nomination matters if that parent dies later, or if both of you die together. In that case it is smart to have both parents sign wills that name the same guardian, so the documents never conflict. When parents live apart and disagree about the choice, each parent can still nominate, but the court is not obliged to prefer one over the other if both parents die.

## How state law changes this

The principle is the same in nearly every state, but several details differ.

- **How much weight the nomination gets.** Some states say the court "shall" appoint the nominee unless it would harm the child. Others say the court "may" consider the nomination.
- **Who gets notice.** Many states require notice to relatives of a certain degree, which gives them a chance to object.
- **Handwritten and out-of-state wills.** Rules on whether they count differ widely.
- **Child's voice.** Many courts ask an older child's preference, often from around 12 to 14.
