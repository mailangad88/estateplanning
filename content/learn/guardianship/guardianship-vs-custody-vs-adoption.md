When a child needs a caretaker other than a parent, three words come up in the first phone call: custody, guardianship and adoption. People use them as if they meant the same thing. They do not, and picking the wrong one can leave a child without health insurance, an heir without an inheritance, or a parent without rights they thought they had.

This page sets the three side by side in plain terms, then covers inheritance, the question families raise last and most often. For planning that names who raises your children if you cannot, start at the [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship).

## What is custody?

Custody is the legal right and duty to care for a child, and it usually belongs to the parents. Courts decide it in divorce, separation or paternity cases. It has two parts. Legal custody is the power to make major decisions about school, medical care and religion. Physical custody is where the child lives day to day. Parents can share either one.

Custody orders can be changed if circumstances change, and they stay in family court. A parent with custody does not need a guardian. A parent without custody still has rights in most states, such as visits, unless a judge limits them.

When a parent dies, custody does not simply move to a person named in a will. The other living parent normally continues or takes over, which our page on [whether the surviving parent gets custody](/learn/guardianship/does-the-surviving-parent-get-custody-if-you-die) explains in detail.

## What is guardianship of a child?

Guardianship is a court appointment that lets an adult who is not the parent make decisions for a child. A grandparent raising a grandchild, an aunt caring for a nephew, or a family friend named in a parent's will might all become guardians. A judge signs an order, and that order is the proof schools, doctors and banks ask for.

Most states divide guardianship into two jobs, though the names vary:

- Guardian of the person: decides where the child lives, school, medical care and daily life.
- Guardian of the estate, called a conservator in some states: manages the child's money and property.

One person can hold both roles, or two people can split them. See [the difference between guardian of the person and conservator of the estate](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate). A guardian usually reports to the court, files accounts if there is money, and may need court permission for big decisions. And the order ends when the child turns 18 in most states, or earlier if the court ends it, or if the child dies or is adopted.

The important legal fact is that a guardianship does not remove the birth parents. Their rights are limited while the order lasts, but they are not erased. A parent who recovers from illness, leaves a prison sentence or finishes treatment can often ask the court to end the guardianship.

## What is adoption?

Adoption permanently makes someone the child's legal parent. The court ends the birth parents' rights, unless the adopter is a stepparent who steps into the role alongside one birth parent, and issues an adoption decree. A new birth certificate is typically issued. There is no ongoing court supervision and no end date at 18.

Adoption needs consent from the birth parents or a finding that their rights should be ended, and it follows a formal process with home studies in many cases. Once final, it is very hard to reverse. Think of it as a different category from guardianship, not a stronger version of it.

## How do the three compare?

| Feature | Custody | Guardianship | Adoption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who holds it | A parent | A non-parent | A new legal parent |
| Who decides | Family court between parents | Probate or family court on petition or a will | Court after a formal process |
| Birth parents' rights | Remain | Limited, not ended | Ended |
| Duration | Until 18, changeable | Until 18 or until the court ends it | Permanent |
| Court supervision | Usually none after the order | Often reports and accounts | None |
| Inheritance | Child inherits from parents | Child does not inherit from a guardian by that fact | Child inherits like a birth child |

## What about inheritance?

Inheritance is where the three diverge most sharply. Intestacy laws, the rules that apply when someone dies without a will, give property to a spouse, children and other blood relatives. They treat adopted children as children. They do not treat a child under guardianship as a child of the guardian.

So a niece raised by her aunt for fifteen years under a guardianship order has no automatic claim on the aunt's estate. The same is true for stepchildren in most states, which our guide to [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) covers. The remedy is simple: name the child in a will or trust. If you do not, read what the law says in [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

Going the other way, a child under guardianship still inherits from the birth parents, and may receive Social Security survivor benefits through them. A child who is later adopted generally stops inheriting from the birth parents under intestacy, although some states preserve rights in stepparent and relative adoptions. Check your state before relying on either rule.

## What about health insurance, school and benefits?

A guardianship order is usually enough to enroll a child in school and consent to treatment, but it does not by itself add the child to your health plan. Employer plans differ on whether they cover a child in your guardianship, so ask the plan administrator before you assume. Public benefits for children raised by relatives, such as cash help for kinship caregivers, also vary by state and often turn on the child's living arrangement and the parents' income. Ask the court clerk or a legal aid office what your state offers before you pick a route.

Adoption changes this picture. An adoptive parent can generally add the child to their own plan as any parent would, and some states offer adoption subsidies for certain children. These are practical differences families feel in the first month, long before inheritance matters.

## Which one fits which situation?

Match the tool to the situation and to how long it needs to last.

1. A parent is deployed, in the hospital or in treatment for a few months: a power of attorney for the child's care, a delegation or a temporary guardianship. See [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians).
2. A parent has died and named a guardian in a will: the named person petitions the court, and a judge confirms. Grandparents often fill this role; read [can grandparents be named guardian](/learn/guardianship/can-grandparents-be-named-guardian).
3. A grandparent has been raising a grandchild for years with the parent absent: guardianship, and later perhaps adoption if the parent consents.
4. A stepparent wants a permanent bond: stepparent adoption, with the other legal parent's consent or a court finding.
5. Parents separate: custody orders in the family court.

Maria, 61, took in her grandson Theo, 7, when his mother entered residential treatment. She got a guardianship so she could enroll him in school and consent to his dental work. When Theo's mother completed treatment 14 months later, the court ended the guardianship and returned him to her. Maria had never needed to adopt, and the order matched what the family intended. If the mother had stayed away for good, adoption would have been the next conversation.

## What if you are planning ahead as a parent?

You cannot grant custody in your will, but you can name a guardian and an alternate, and you can say who should manage money for the child. Most parents of young children do both. A guardian handles the child's care, while a trust or custodial account holds the money. Our page on [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) walks through that decision, and the [guardian picker](/tools/guardian-picker) helps you compare candidates.
