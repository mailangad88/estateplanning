Parents usually imagine the worst case as a single event: both of them gone. The more common emergency is smaller and more ordinary. A parent is in a coma for two weeks. A single mom is hospitalized after surgery with complications. Both parents are on a flight and delayed overseas. The children are alive and need someone with authority to act, and the will in the drawer does not help.

This guide explains the documents that fill that gap, how they differ, how to set them up, and which problems they do not solve.

## Why doesn't a will cover an emergency?

A will takes effect only at death. The guardian nominated in your will has no legal authority while you are alive, even if you are in intensive care. That means your children's caregiver may be unable to sign a school form, approve a non-emergency medical treatment, or take the children out of the state.

Courts can appoint a temporary guardian, but it takes time, a lawyer, and a hearing. Parents who have signed the right papers in advance remove that scramble. For the death scenario, see [what happens if you die without naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named).

## What tools cover a short-term gap?

There are four main tools, and most families use more than one.

| Tool | What it does | Court needed? | Typical limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delegation of parental authority (power of attorney for a minor) | Gives an adult authority over school, medical and daily decisions | No | A set period in many states, such as six months to a year |
| Standby guardianship designation | Names a guardian who steps in on a trigger such as incapacity | Often a later confirmation | Varies by state |
| Temporary guardianship | A court appoints a short-term guardian | Yes | A limited time, renewable |
| Medical consent and school authorization | Lets a caregiver approve treatment and enroll or pick up | No | Until revoked or expired |

### What is a delegation of parental authority?

It is a signed document, often notarized, that lets another adult make everyday decisions for your child for a limited time. In many states it is called a power of attorney for a minor child or a caregiver authorization. It is not a transfer of custody. You remain the parent and can revoke it at any time.

States differ on whether it exists, how long it lasts, and whether it includes medical decisions. Some states provide a statutory form. Other states recognize it only through general [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) law. Because rules differ, we recommend a form that fits your state rather than a template from another state.

### What is a standby guardianship?

A standby guardian is a person you appoint now who takes over when a defined event happens. Historically, these statutes were created for parents with serious illness who wanted to line up care before they became too sick to do so. Many states now cover any parent who may become incapacitated, and some extend to events like military deployment or immigration detention.

The usual steps are:

1. The parent signs a written designation naming the standby guardian and the triggering event, such as incapacity or death.
2. A physician's statement or other proof confirms the trigger when it occurs.
3. The standby guardian files with a court, sometimes within a set number of days, to have the authority confirmed.

If your state has this process, it is a step above a simple delegation, because the guardian has recognized legal status. Ask a local attorney whether your state has a statute and how it works.

### What is a temporary guardianship?

A temporary or emergency guardianship is something a court grants after someone files a petition. It is usually fast when the facts are clear, but it requires a filing, notice to the parents or other interested parties when possible, and often a hearing. Courts use it when no prior paperwork exists. It is the expensive version of what the documents above accomplish ahead of time.

## What is a medical consent form for a caregiver?

It is a short signed document that tells a doctor, hospital or school that your child's caregiver may consent to treatment on your behalf. It should include:

- Your child's full name and date of birth
- The caregiver's name, phone and relationship to the child
- Consent for routine and emergency care, and any limits you want
- Insurance information and allergies
- Your own signature, date, and a notary acknowledgment if your state wants it

Pair this with a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so the caregiver can talk to your child's doctors, and with your own [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) so someone can speak for you.

## What if you are the one who is incapacitated?

If you are hurt, the people who need authority are your agents. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets someone pay your bills and mortgage. A healthcare agent speaks for you in the hospital. A separate delegation for the children covers the third piece, who has authority over the kids. A complete package includes all three. See how these differ from court appointments in [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## Who should be your temporary caregiver?

Often it is the same person as your named guardian, but not always. A grandparent who lives five minutes away may be the better emergency caregiver, even if your long-term guardian lives across the country. Name both on separate documents so each person understands their role.

Questions that help:

1. Who can be at my house within two hours?
2. Who do the kids already know and trust?
3. Who can take time off work?
4. Who would a school or doctor's office accept as a legitimate adult?

## How do you set this up step by step?

1. Choose a primary and a backup emergency caregiver.
2. Ask both. Share your children's schedules, medications, allergies and doctors.
3. Have an attorney prepare a delegation or standby designation that fits your state, plus medical consent and school authorization forms.
4. Sign and notarize where required. Give copies to the caregivers, and keep originals where your family can find them.
5. Store digital copies in a shared folder and print a one-page "if something happens to us" sheet for the fridge or a family binder.
6. Review at least once a year, because temporary delegations expire.

### Example: Priya

Priya, a single mother of two, learns she needs surgery that may involve a long recovery. She signs a six-month delegation naming her sister, a medical consent form, a school pickup authorization and a HIPAA release for her kids' pediatrician. While she is in the hospital, her sister signs a field trip form, takes her son to urgent care, and talks with the school counselor without any court involvement.

## What these documents do not do

They do not give the caregiver access to your money, they do not replace a guardian nomination in your will, and they do not decide custody if the other parent disputes. They also may not be recognized in every state, especially in a different state from where you signed. If you travel with your children or have relatives in another state, mention it to your attorney.
