Grandparents wanting to leave $30,000 to a toddler, or parents buying a life insurance policy for their kids, usually hear the same two options. Open a custodial account, or set up a trust. One costs an afternoon, the other costs a lawyer. So why would anyone pay for the second?

The short answer is a single clause: the age at which the child takes over. This guide compares the two options side by side, using realistic examples, so you can see where the cheaper route is fine and where it is a mistake. For the wider picture of leaving money to children, start with our guide to [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

## How does a UTMA account work?

A UTMA account, under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, is an account in a child's name managed by an adult custodian. The adult gives money or investments, and from then on the property belongs to the child. The custodian invests it, may spend it for the child's benefit, and must turn over what remains at the age your state sets. You can read the definition in our glossary entry on the [UTMA account](/glossary/utma).

Key features:

- **Irrevocable.** A donor cannot take the gift back.
- **One name, one custodian.** You pick the custodian when you open the account. A will or a beneficiary form can name a custodian for gifts made at death.
- **No court supervision** in normal cases, and no tax return of its own. Income is reported under the child's Social Security number.
- **Fixed termination.** The child gets everything at the age in the statute, whether that is wise or not.

## How does a trust for a child work?

A [trust](/glossary/trust) is a written agreement in which a [trustee](/glossary/trustee) holds property for a beneficiary under rules you set. For children you can decide when distributions happen, what they can be used for, and what the trustee can do in the meantime. Details are in our guide to [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).

A trust can be part of a revocable living trust, a will-based arrangement, or a standalone document. It can last until the child is 25, 35 or for life.

## UTMA vs. trust: how do they compare?

| Feature | UTMA custodial account | Trust for a child |
|---|---|---|
| Setup cost | Low, often free at a brokerage | Legal fees for drafting |
| Time to set up | Minutes to days | Days to weeks |
| Age child gets control | Fixed by state, commonly 18 to 21, sometimes 25 | Any age or ages you choose |
| Who controls it | Custodian, until the child takes over | Trustee, under written rules |
| Can you set purposes? | Only "for the benefit of the child" | Yes, as specific as you like |
| Staged payouts | No | Yes |
| Can last past early adulthood | No | Yes |
| Protection from child's creditors or divorce | Very little after turnover | Can be strong with a spendthrift clause |
| Financial aid impact | Counted as the child's asset | Depends on how it is drafted |
| Court supervision | Usually none | Usually none for living trusts |

## When is a UTMA enough?

When the amount is small, the child is likely to handle it sensibly, and you want to avoid paperwork. Take Omar, who wants to give his niece $5,000 for her eighth birthday. A UTMA at a brokerage takes ten minutes. By 18 the account has grown, she buys a used car or pays a semester of tuition, and nobody is hurt.

It also works as a stopgap. Parents who have just had a baby, and have not yet visited an attorney, might name a UTMA custodian on a small life insurance policy rather than leave the child named directly.

## When does a UTMA backfire?

When the sum is large enough that handing it to an 18-year-old is unwise. Imagine Tessa's parents leaving her $400,000 through a UTMA after an accident. At 18, with no say from anyone, she receives all of it. Good outcomes happen, but so do spent inheritances, bad relationships and creditors. The parents who set it up had no way to change the terms.

Other problems:

1. **Divorce and creditors.** Once the child owns the money outright, a spouse or a creditor can reach it.
2. **Financial aid.** Colleges treat a child's assets as available for tuition at a higher rate than a parent's. A big UTMA can reduce aid.
3. **Taxes on unearned income.** Investment income of a child can be taxed at higher rates through the kiddie tax rules. Ask a tax professional about the current thresholds.
4. **Custodian problems.** If the custodian dies or becomes incapacitated without a successor named, a court may need to appoint a replacement.
5. **Public benefits.** A child who may need Medicaid or SSI later should not own assets outright. A [special needs trust](/glossary/special-needs-trust) is the usual answer.

## Why do larger gifts usually call for a trust?

Because control matters more as the number grows. A trust lets you say that your daughter gets a third at 25, a third at 30 and the rest at 35, with the trustee able to pay for school and medical care in the meantime. If she runs into trouble, the trustee can hold back. If she thrives, distributions can be generous.

A trust also gives you a place to put instructions that a UTMA cannot hold. Maybe you want the money used for education and a first home but not a luxury car. Maybe you want your children treated differently because one has a health condition.

## Can I use both?

Yes, and many families do. A common layout is a trust for the main inheritance and a small UTMA for gifts that grandparents want to make without legal work. You can also pair the choices with a [guardian](/glossary/guardian) chosen separately from the money manager, as we explain in [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee).

## How do I name either one correctly?

The paperwork matters as much as the choice.

1. **For a UTMA:** the form should read something like "Jordan Lee, as custodian for Ava Lee under the [State] Uniform Transfers to Minors Act." Name a successor custodian if the form allows.
2. **For a trust:** name the trustee of the trust as beneficiary, for example "The Lee Family Trust dated March 3, 2026," not the child.
3. **Check retirement accounts.** A trust named as IRA beneficiary needs careful drafting, because the 10-year payout rule applies to most non-spouse beneficiaries.
4. **Review every beneficiary form.** An old form naming the child directly will override your new plan. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) can help you list them.
5. **Tell the custodian or trustee.** They should hear about the job from you, not from a lawyer after you are gone.
