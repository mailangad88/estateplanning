Most parents assume that if something happens to them, the right relative will step in without a fuss. Sometimes that is exactly what happens. Often it is not so simple, because the law needs someone to hold legal authority over a child, and a relative's willingness is not the same as a court order.

This page walks through what happens to minor children when both parents die and no guardian has been named, how courts decide, what it costs families, and the simplest steps that prevent the worst outcomes.

## When does the guardian question even arise?

It arises when no legal parent is available to care for a minor child. If one parent dies and the other is alive, capable and legally recognized, the surviving parent usually continues as sole custodian and no guardianship is needed. The question becomes urgent in three situations:

- Both parents die, for example in a car accident
- The surviving parent is incapacitated, incarcerated, or otherwise unable to parent
- A single parent dies and the other parent has no legal rights or cannot be found

If you are a single parent or share custody with an ex who is not a safe choice, naming a guardian matters even more. Our guide to [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) covers the other documents that belong in the same plan.

## What happens in the first few days?

In the first hours, the children go wherever someone safe takes them. A neighbor, a school, or the police may call a relative, or a child protective agency may be contacted if no adult steps forward. Nobody has legal authority to consent to medical care, enroll the children in school, or access their funds.

That gap is the real problem. A relative can usually provide emergency care for a few days, but doctors, schools and banks will ask for papers. If the family cannot show a document, someone must go to court. Many courts can issue an emergency or temporary guardianship order within a day or several days, but it still requires a lawyer or a hurried filing by someone in grief.

## How does a court choose a guardian?

A judge applies the "best interests of the child" standard. This is a broad test, and it means the judge looks at who can provide a safe, stable and loving home. The factors vary by state, but they usually include:

- The child's relationship with each person who petitions
- The petitioner's age, health, income and criminal history
- Stability of the household and other children in it
- The child's own wishes, if old enough (often around 12 to 14, depending on state)
- Whether siblings could stay together
- Any wishes the parents expressed, in writing or otherwise
- Continuity in school, community and faith

The last point matters. Without a will, the judge may hear witnesses say what you "would have wanted." Witnesses often disagree. A written nomination replaces that debate with your own words.

### Do relatives have a legal priority?

In most states, there is no fixed ranking of relatives for guardianship of minors. Some states list preferences, such as grandparents or adult siblings, but those lists are guidelines. Many courts also appoint a non-relative, such as a close friend or godparent, if that is best for the child. If you want someone outside the family, you really need to say so in a will.

## What if relatives disagree?

A contested guardianship case can run for months and cost each side tens of thousands of dollars in attorney fees, some of which can be charged to your children's estate. Hearings are often public. Children may be interviewed by a court investigator or a guardian ad litem, an attorney or social worker appointed to speak for the child.

Real families disagree for understandable reasons. One set of grandparents may live nearby and the other three states away. A sister may be more nurturing while a brother is more stable. A judge cannot know the history behind each opinion. If you have strong feelings, write them down.

## Can children end up in foster care?

It is possible, especially if no relative can be reached immediately or if the court worries about a placement. Temporary foster care or kinship care under a child welfare agency is a common stopgap, and it usually ends when a guardian is appointed. It can be distressing for a child who has just lost both parents.

You cannot eliminate this risk entirely, but you reduce it by naming at least two backup guardians who know they are named, and by giving them a copy of your documents. A named person can show up at the hospital or the agency and say, "I am the nominated guardian, here is the will." That does not substitute for a court order, but it can sway the immediate placement and speeds the appointment.

### Example: the Okafor family

Chidi and Amara Okafor, both in their late thirties, die in a highway accident, leaving a 5-year-old and an 11-year-old. They never signed a will. Chidi's mother, who lives two states away, and Amara's brother, who lives ten minutes from the children's school, each file for guardianship. The judge appoints a temporary guardian, the brother, and sets a hearing for several months later. Both families hire lawyers, and a court investigator interviews the older child. Had the parents named a guardian in a will, the hearing would likely have been a short confirmation.

## What about the children's money?

Without a plan, money left to a minor child usually goes to a court-supervised account or "conservatorship" managed by a guardian of the estate, who may or may not be the same person as the guardian of the person. The court requires reports and often a bond, and the money is typically released outright at age 18 in many states. We explain the alternatives in [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) and [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee).

Dying without any will also means your property passes under state [intestacy](/glossary/intestate) rules, which generally send everything to your spouse and children in set shares. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for what that means in practice.

## How do you prevent this?

Preventing the problem takes a few documents and one honest conversation.

1. Choose a guardian and at least one backup. Our guide on [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) offers a way to compare candidates.
2. Name them in a valid [will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will). A nomination in a document that is not properly signed may carry little weight.
3. Create a trust or other plan for the money so the guardian is not forced into court for every expense.
4. Prepare standby or temporary authority for emergencies when you are alive but unable to act. See [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians).
5. Tell the people you named, and keep your documents where they can be found.
6. Review everything after a move, a birth, or a change in your family.

### What if you wait?

It is natural to wait until finances are in better shape or children are older. The cost of waiting is a risk of a court choosing for you. Many parents write a simple will with a guardian nomination first and add trusts later as their assets grow.
