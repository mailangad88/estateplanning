Marcus and Dana are flying to Lisbon for their anniversary, their first trip without the kids in five years. Dana's mother will stay at the house with an eight-year-old and a twelve-year-old. On the way to the airport Dana realizes the school has never heard of her mother, the pediatrician's records list only her and Marcus, and the will that names a guardian does nothing unless both of them die.

If you are leaving your children for a trip, a surgery or a deployment, you need a short list of papers that tell schools and doctors who is in charge. This page covers who can pick up your child from school, what a medical consent can and cannot do, and what to do if you are in the military. It belongs with the other pieces in our [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship). This is general education, not legal advice.

## Who can pick up my child from school if I cannot?

Only a person the school has on file, in nearly every case. Front offices release children to a parent with custody and to adults listed on the emergency card, and they are right to refuse anyone else, because they cannot verify who a stranger is. Many families discover this when a grandmother arrives with a copy of her ID and a story and is turned away.

Fix this in order:

1. Ask the school office for the current emergency contact and pickup authorization form. Some schools have a portal.
2. List at least three adults, in the order you want them called, with their cell numbers.
3. Mark who may take the child from school and who may only be called.
4. Tell each person they are on the list, and tell them to carry photo ID.
5. Do the same for after-school care, a sports program and any camp.

Add the caregiver's name to the form even if the trip is two weeks out. Some schools take a few days to process changes. If your child's other parent has no right to pick up the child, ask the school how that limit appears in its records.

## What papers do you leave with the caregiver?

Leave a folder, not a loose note. A good folder has these items:

| Item | Why the caregiver needs it |
|---|---|
| Signed medical consent for the child | Lets the caregiver authorize treatment while you are away |
| Copies of both insurance cards | Hospitals ask for them at the front desk |
| Medication list, allergies, doctor and pharmacy numbers | Prevents a dangerous guess in an emergency room |
| Birth certificate copy | Proves the child's name and parents |
| Travel permission letter, if the child will travel | Airlines and border agents ask for one |
| Your itinerary and a way to reach you | The caregiver needs to find you |
| Your guardian's name and number | The caregiver needs to know whom to call if you cannot be reached |

## What does a medical consent actually do?

A medical consent lets the person you name approve treatment for your child when you cannot be reached. It is a simple signed paper that says who the child is, who the caregiver is, what dates it covers and what kind of treatment is allowed. Ask your doctor's office whether the form should be notarized.

Be realistic about its limits. Hospitals will treat a child in a true emergency without anyone's consent. For routine or scheduled care, such as a cast change, a vaccination or a follow-up visit, a clinic may demand either the parent's phone approval or a paper it recognizes. The paper is a tool, not a promise. A copy sent by email to the doctor's office ahead of time makes the difference.

For a parent who is going into surgery or a hospital stay, [estate planning before surgery or a hospital stay](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-before-surgery-or-a-hospital-stay) shows how to pair this with a power of attorney and health care directive for yourself. A [HIPAA release](/glossary/hipaa-authorization) for the caregiver can also help them talk to your child's doctor.

## When do you need a short-term guardian instead?

When the absence is long or the stakes are higher. A signed medical consent is enough for a week in Lisbon. It may not be enough for a six-month recovery from surgery or a deployment, when schools, insurers and landlords all want someone with real authority.

Illinois has a specific tool for that. A parent can sign a short-term guardian appointment, in front of two adult witnesses who are not the appointee, with no court filing. It lasts 365 days by default, and military parents can set a longer term. If the child's other parent is alive and involved, that parent usually must consent. Our page on [Illinois standby and short-term guardians](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children) walks through the form. Families elsewhere can read the general overview of [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians).

New Jersey has a standby guardianship process for parents who are seriously ill, and a court-based option for longer gaps. Because the details and forms differ from Illinois, ask an attorney which paper New Jersey schools and hospitals expect. Our [New Jersey guardianship article](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) gives the overview.

## What if I am deploying with the military?

Service members who are single parents, or who are married to another service member, are generally required to maintain a family care plan, and your command will tell you what it needs. A family care plan names a short-term caregiver and a long-term caregiver and covers money, housing and medical authority. It is separate from your will.

Pair the military paperwork with civilian documents. The family care plan tells your command who is responsible; a state short-term guardian form or a notarized medical consent tells the school and the pediatrician. Without both, a grandmother with a military form may still hear no from a front desk. The Judge Advocate General's office on base can help with powers of attorney and wills for service members at little or no cost to eligible service members.

## How is this different from naming a guardian in your will?

A will names who raises your children if you die. It does not help while you are alive. Many parents assume the will covers an accident or an illness, then discover that a person named in a will has no authority to sign a permission slip today. The pieces fit together like this:

| Situation | The tool that covers it |
|---|---|
| A weekend away | Caregiver letter, medical consent, school pickup list |
| Surgery or long recovery | Short-term guardian or standby guardian paper |
| Deployment | Family care plan plus a short-term guardian form |
| Both parents die | Guardian named in a will, confirmed by a court |
| No paper at all | A judge decides, after a delay |

The page on [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) describes the last row. The pickup list and medical consent are the cheapest protection against it, and many families skip them because the trip is "only a week."

## A checklist before you leave

- Emergency card at school and camp updated with the caregiver's name.
- Signed medical consent, dated, with trip dates, copied to the pediatrician.
- Insurance cards and medication list in a folder.
- Travel letter if the child will fly or cross a border with someone else.
- Written backup: who the caregiver calls if you cannot be reached.
- A current will with a named guardian and a backup.
- Your guardian knows and has agreed.
