If you are a parent, there is one question you have probably tried not to think about for long: if something happened to both of us, who would raise our kids? The question is hard because it cannot be answered with a spreadsheet. It involves love, trust, and the realization that someone else would carry out your hopes.

This guide is the overview. It explains what a guardian is, how to choose one, what happens when you do not, how to protect your children's money, and what to do about emergencies that fall short of death. Each section links to a detailed article on the topic. You do not need to read everything at once. Many parents start with the choice of a guardian, then deal with the money, then fill in the emergency documents.

## What does it mean to name a guardian?

Naming a guardian means writing down, in a valid will, who you want to raise your minor children if both parents die or cannot act. The person you name is a [guardian](/glossary/guardian) of the person. They take over the daily work of parenting: where the children live, where they go to school, medical care, religion and discipline.

The nomination is not self-executing. A court has to appoint the guardian. Judges usually honor a parent's nomination unless something is clearly wrong, so a clear, recent nomination makes a big difference. You can read more on how courts treat it in our guide to [what happens to your children if you die without naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named).

You also name backups. Life changes, and your first choice may be unable or unwilling when the time comes. A good will names a first guardian, a successor, and sometimes a second successor.

## How do you choose the right person?

The right guardian is someone who shares your values, has the health and energy to raise children to adulthood, is willing, and fits into your children's lives. Closeness of blood is less important than how well the adult would parent.

A short comparison helps. List two or three people, and for each, think about:

- Values around education, faith, honesty and discipline
- Health, age and energy
- Home, location and stability, including whether your kids would need to move
- Existing relationship with your children
- Willingness, and the willingness of a spouse or partner
- Whether they could handle the role with the financial help you plan to leave

The detailed process, including a comparison table, how to talk to your spouse when you disagree, and how to ask the candidate, is in [how to choose a guardian for your children](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian). The key advice is to ask first. A person who learns they were named only after you die is in a very hard position, and may decline.

## What happens if you do not name one?

If both parents die without naming a guardian, the court decides. Relatives file petitions, the judge applies a "best interests of the child" test, and the outcome may differ from what you would have chosen. Cases can be slow, cost money and create family conflict. During the gap, children may stay with a relative or in temporary foster care.

A surviving parent with legal rights usually keeps custody, so the guardian question comes up mostly when both parents die or when the surviving parent cannot care for the children. If you are a single parent, or if the other parent is not a safe choice, a nomination matters even more.

The full picture, including how judges weigh factors and how to reduce the risk, is in [what happens if you die without naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named). Also see [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for what happens to your property under intestacy rules.

## Why separate the guardian from the person who manages the money?

The person who is the best parent is not always the best money manager, and one person controlling both the child and the funds can create conflicts. Many parents therefore name a guardian for the children and a trustee for their inheritance, and ask them to cooperate.

| Role | Guardian | Trustee |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | The child's daily life | The child's money |
| Named in | Will | Trust (or will-based trust) |
| Typical end | Age 18 | Ages chosen by parents, often 25 to 35 |

The detailed article, [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee), explains how the two work together, how a trustee pays for the guardian's costs, when one person can do both, and how to choose each. For help choosing a trustee in particular, see [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).

## How do you protect the money your children inherit?

Children under 18 cannot manage property. If you leave money to them outright, a court usually appoints someone to hold it, and the child often gets everything at 18. There are better tools.

1. A trust, which lets you choose a trustee, set spending rules, and stagger payments across ages.
2. A UTMA custodial account, which is simple and cheap but ends at a fixed age between 18 and 25 depending on state law.
3. Life insurance that names a trust or a custodian as beneficiary rather than the child.
4. A 529 plan, which holds education savings under an owner you name.

Life insurance is often the largest asset young parents leave, so the beneficiary form is as important as the will. Our article on [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) compares the tools, shows an example, and covers pitfalls such as naming a minor directly. See also [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) for staggered distribution designs and [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

If one of your children has a disability and may receive means-tested benefits like SSI or Medicaid, a standard inheritance can put those benefits at risk. In that case, read our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs) before you finalize a trust.

## What if something happens but you are still alive?

A will only operates after death. If you are hospitalized, in a coma, or unreachable for a period of time, your will's guardian nomination does nothing. Without paperwork, a caregiver has no legal authority to approve medical care or sign school forms.

There are tools for this gap:

- A delegation of parental authority, a signed document giving another adult temporary authority
- A standby guardianship designation, where your state allows it
- Medical consent and school authorization forms
- Your own powers of attorney and healthcare directive, so your agents can act for you

[Temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians) explains the options and how to put them in place. Your own documents matter too. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets someone pay the mortgage and bills, and a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) lets someone speak for you with doctors. Our guide on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains how those relate to court-appointed roles.

## What documents make up a complete parent's plan?

A complete plan for parents of minor children usually includes:

1. A will with a guardian nomination and backups, signed with the formalities your state requires. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).
2. A trust, or a will-based trust, for the children's inheritance, with a trustee and successor.
3. Beneficiary designations on life insurance, retirement accounts and bank accounts that name the trust or a custodian, not a minor.
4. A durable financial power of attorney for each parent.
5. A healthcare power of attorney and living will for each parent.
6. A temporary or standby authorization for emergency caregivers.
7. A short letter of instruction describing your children's routines, health, and what you hope for them.

The checklist overlaps with our guide to [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents), which also covers budgeting for insurance and timing.

## What mistakes do parents make most often?

- Not naming a guardian at all, usually because the choice is uncomfortable
- Naming someone without asking
- Forgetting a backup guardian
- Leaving everything to a minor child outright
- Never updating beneficiary forms after the baby was born
- Choosing co-guardians who would disagree
- Assuming a will covers a hospital emergency

Our guides to [wills](/learn/wills), [trusts](/learn/trusts) and [powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) cover each document in more depth, and our overview of [common estate planning mistakes](/learn/basics/common-estate-planning-mistakes) lists more across the whole plan.

## How do you talk to family about it?

Tell the people you chose before you sign, and tell family members who might feel left out in your own words, kindly and briefly. Most conflict comes from surprise. Explain that you picked based on your children's needs, not on who you love most. If you want, put your reasons in a letter that stays with your documents. It can help a judge as well as your family. For more on these conversations, see [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan).

## How often should you update your plan?

Revisit your guardian and trustee choices every three to five years and any time your life changes. Triggers include another child, a move, a divorce, a death, a change in a guardian's health or marriage, or a significant change in your assets. Children grow, and teenagers have preferences that a toddler does not. Our guide on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) has a longer list.

## What special situations need extra care?

Some families need more than the standard plan, and it is worth knowing which category you are in.

- **Single parents.** If you are the only legal parent, a nomination is the only voice you have. Name two backups, and consider standby authority because no co-parent can step in during an emergency.
- **Divorced or separated parents.** The other parent usually has priority if you die. If you believe they are unsafe, say so in your plan and talk with an attorney about how your state handles that dispute. Do not assume a will can override a parent's rights.
- **Blended families.** Stepchildren have no automatic legal relationship to a stepparent. If you want a stepparent to continue raising your children, name them explicitly, and see our [blended families guidance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) for the inheritance side.
- **Unmarried parents.** Legal parentage may depend on paperwork such as an acknowledgment of paternity, so check that it is in place before you rely on a plan.
- **Families with a child who has a disability.** The guardian's role and the structure of the money both change. Planning should protect benefits and may need to continue after the child turns 18, which is covered in [turning 18 with a disability](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children).
- **Parents with children in more than one state or country.** Rules on recognition of out-of-state documents vary, so document where the children will most likely live and plan for that.

### Example: a plan that works

Elena and Tom, both 34, have a 3-year-old and an infant. They choose Elena's brother and his wife as guardians, Tom's parents as the first successors, and a family friend who is a bookkeeper as trustee. They buy term life insurance for 20 years, name their trust as beneficiary, and sign a will, powers of attorney and healthcare directives. They also sign a six-month delegation form naming Tom's sister, who lives nearby, as emergency caregiver. The whole plan took about two weeks to put together, and they review it every four years.

## Where should you start?

If you have nothing in place, start with a simple order:

1. Decide on a guardian and backups, and ask them.
2. Sign a will naming them.
3. Check life insurance and retirement beneficiary forms.
4. Add a trust for the children's money.
5. Prepare emergency documents.

You can do the first two this month and the rest over time. A good first step is better than a perfect plan that never gets signed.
