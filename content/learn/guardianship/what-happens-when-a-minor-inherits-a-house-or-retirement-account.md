A parent dies, and the will, the deed or the IRA beneficiary form names a child under 18. The family expects the child to simply move into the house or get the account. It does not work that way. A minor owns the asset on paper, but the law does not let a minor act on it.

This page explains what happens to a house and to a retirement account, who decides what, and what a parent can sign now to keep the child's inheritance out of a courtroom. The broader money rules for children are in our [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship).

## What happens if a minor inherits a house?

The child owns the share, and someone else has to act for the child. Title passes to the minor through the will or the intestacy rules, but a minor's signature on a deed or a contract is not valid. If the house has to be sold, refinanced or even insured in a new name, the family needs an adult with legal authority.

That adult is usually a court-appointed manager of the child's property. Illinois calls the role a guardian of the estate, and New Jersey calls it a guardian of the property. Our state pages, [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) and [minor inherits money in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/minor-inherits-money-in-new-jersey-guardian-of-the-property), walk through the appointment. The key points for a house are the same in both:

- The guardian reports to the court, and may need a bond.
- A sale generally needs the judge's approval, a fair price and a reason that benefits the child.
- Money from a sale stays under court supervision until the child turns 18.

Take Ben, 9. His father dies without a will and without remarrying. Ben's mother is deceased. The $360,000 house, free of debt, goes to Ben as the only child. His aunt Teresa moves in to care for him. She cannot sell the house, refinance it or change the insurance without a guardian of the estate. Someone has to petition for that role, which takes weeks and costs attorney fees. The house sits in limbo during that time, with property tax bills still due.

## What if the surviving parent also owns part of the house?

Then the parent and child are co-owners, and the parent cannot sell the whole house alone. The parent can live there and keep it up, but a sale or a new mortgage touches the child's share and needs court approval. Spouses who own a home jointly with survivorship avoid the problem, because the survivor takes the whole house automatically. Problems arise when one spouse owns the home alone and leaves it by will to the children.

If you are a parent reading this, check how your deed reads. A deed that says "tenants by the entirety" or "joint tenants with right of survivorship" behaves very differently from a house in your name alone. Our guides to [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) and [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/joint-property-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety-in-new-jersey) explain each form.

## What happens if a minor inherits a retirement account?

A custodian opens an inherited account for the child. The plan or IRA custodian will not pay a check to a minor, and it will not move the account without a person who has legal authority, which can again mean a guardian. The account keeps its tax deferral, and the child's rules depend on who died.

The IRS rule has two tracks. If the child is the account owner's own minor child, the child is an eligible designated beneficiary and can take yearly withdrawals over life expectancy until reaching the age of majority, which the IRS treats as 21. After that, the 10-year rule applies and the account has to be empty by the end of the tenth year. If the child is a grandchild, niece or any other relative, there is no minor exception. The 10-year clock starts at the owner's death, and annual withdrawals may also be required if the owner was already taking required distributions. See the [inherited IRA 10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) for the year-by-year pattern.

The tax consequence is the other half. Withdrawals are generally taxable income to the child, and a 10-year payout beginning at 19 can land large distributions in the first years of adulthood, when the child may not be ready for them. Naming a trust to receive the account lets a trustee decide timing, but the trust has to be written for these rules or it can lose the stretch. Our article on [leaving an IRA or 401(k) to children or grandchildren](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-an-ira-or-401k-to-children-or-grandchildren) covers that.

## What are the options to avoid a court guardianship?

| Option | How it works | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revocable or testamentary trust | Trustee holds the house or account for the child | Large assets, houses | Must be signed and funded in advance |
| UTMA custodian named on a form | Custodian manages until 21 (both states) | Smaller accounts and cash | Child gets everything at 21 |
| Life estate or occupancy right for a parent | Parent lives in the house, child owns the remainder | A house the surviving parent needs | Poorly drafted ones cause disputes |
| Transfer-on-death deed to a trust | House passes to the trust at death | Houses in states that allow it | Trust must exist first |
| Joint ownership with survivorship | House goes straight to the survivor | Spouses | Does not help when both parents die |

A trust is usually the better route for a house. The trustee can let the guardian or a relative live there, pay the taxes, and sell the property when the time is right, without asking a judge each time. A UTMA works for cash and securities, but real estate usually does not fit it well. See [UTMA versus a trust for a minor child](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child) and [guardian versus trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) for how to split the roles.

One more cost people overlook: the house keeps getting more expensive while the court case runs. Taxes, insurance, utilities and repairs do not pause. If the estate has cash, the executor can often pay them as estate expenses. If it does not, whoever is living there pays out of pocket and has to ask the court to be repaid. Keep every receipt.

## What should a family do right now if a minor has just inherited?

1. Get a certified copy of the death certificate and locate the will, deed and beneficiary forms.
2. Ask an attorney whether a guardian of the estate or property is needed, and who should petition.
3. Keep the house insured and the taxes paid. Tell the insurer if no one lives there.
4. Do not move money out of the child's name or into your own account.
5. Do not sell, rent or refinance the house until you know what the court requires.
6. If an IRA is involved, ask the plan for a beneficiary claim packet promptly. Some payout choices have to be made by December 31 of the year after the death.

For cash and other accounts, see [what happens if a minor inherits money with no trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust).
