A court that needs a guardian for an adult or a child and finds no relative who can do the job has three choices: a friend, a paid professional, or a public office. Most people never think about the second and third until a hospital discharge planner raises them. This page explains what a professional guardian and a public guardian are, who pays them, what changes for the person being protected, and what you can do now so the court does not have to choose a stranger for you. It sits in our [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship). This is general education, not legal advice.

## What is a professional guardian?

A professional guardian is a paid guardian who takes court appointments for a living. Some are individuals, such as a social worker, nurse or attorney. Others are companies or nonprofits with staff who visit clients and manage accounts. They have the same duties as any guardian. They answer to the judge, file reports and have to act in the protected person's interest.

The main difference is the source of the fee. A family member often serves for free or for modest reimbursement. A professional bills hourly or on a schedule, and the bill is paid out of the protected person's money after the court approves it. That is one reason a long guardianship with a professional can use up savings faster than families expect.

Courts usually reach for a professional when:

- No relative is willing or able to serve.
- Relatives are in conflict, and the judge wants someone neutral.
- The person's money or situation is complex, with a business, rental properties or a history of abuse.
- A relative has a conflict of interest, such as owing the person money.

Marguerite, 84, has dementia, no spouse and one son who lives overseas and does not want the job. Her niece in Chicago visits but is raising three children. The judge appoints a professional guardian of the person and a bank as guardian of the estate. Marguerite's savings pay both.

## What is a public guardian?

A public guardian is a government office that serves as guardian when nobody else can or will. Names and reach vary by state and sometimes by county. In Illinois, the Office of State Guardian, part of the Guardianship and Advocacy Commission, serves adults with disabilities who have no one to act for them, and some counties such as Cook have a public guardian's office with its own role. New Jersey has an Office of the Public Guardian for Elderly Adults, which serves older adults who have been found incapacitated and have no one else to act. Confirm the current scope with the court, because eligibility rules and caseloads change.

Public guardians generally have large caseloads and limited staff. They can protect someone from harm, consent to care and manage benefits, but they cannot offer the individual attention a family member can. In many places they accept a case only after a court order and only when no private guardian is available.

| Type | Who serves | Who pays | When used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family or friend | A person the court knows | Usually no fee, or modest reimbursement | The first choice for most courts |
| Professional guardian | Paid individual or company | The protected person's estate, with court approval | No willing family, conflict, complex finances |
| Public guardian | Government office | Public funds, in whole or part | Last resort, often when funds are low |

## What happens for a child with no family to serve?

For a child, the picture differs because the court is trying to place a person in a home. If a will names a guardian and that person can serve, the court usually starts there. If there is no named guardian, or the person named cannot serve, the judge looks at relatives, family friends and any other adult connected to the child. When none is available, the child can end up in the care of the state's child welfare agency, and a foster family may be used while the court decides.

That outcome is rare for a family with a will, a named guardian and a backup. It is much likelier when parents die without having named anyone, as described in [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named). A professional guardian of a child's estate is more common than a professional guardian of a child's person: a bank or a trust company can serve as the guardian of a minor's money while a relative raises the child. Our guide to [guardian of the person versus conservator of the estate](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate) explains the split.

## How do you vet a professional guardian?

If you are the family member helping choose, or you are the person planning ahead, ask these questions.

1. **Credentials.** Are they licensed or certified where your state requires it, and what do they do to keep up?
2. **Caseload.** How many people does the guardian look after, and who visits when the guardian is away?
3. **Fees.** What is the hourly rate, which services are billed and how often? Get it in writing.
4. **Visits.** How often do they see the person, and who do they report to?
5. **Conflicts.** Do they or their company own a facility, a home care agency or a billing relationship with one?
6. **Complaint history.** Has any court or board criticized the guardian? Ask the clerk.

The National Guardianship Association publishes standards of practice, and good professionals can explain how they follow them. Our article on [care managers, money managers and fiduciaries](/learn/elder-care/geriatric-care-managers-money-managers-and-fiduciaries) lists related roles and how they differ.

## Can a guardian place someone in a nursing home against their will?

Often a guardian can choose where the person lives, but the power has limits. Many states require a court hearing, a finding or approval before a guardian moves someone to a locked unit or a more restrictive setting. Courts expect the guardian to consider what the person wants and to try the least restrictive setting that is safe. A guardian who places a person without proper authority can be reported or replaced.

If you worry about a placement, start with the order itself, which describes the guardian's powers. Then raise it with the guardian, the court clerk or the person's lawyer. Our article on [guardianship of an adult](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-of-an-adult-parent-who-can-petition-and-what-the-court-needs) covers how to object, and [removal and refusal](/learn/guardianship/can-a-guardian-be-removed-or-refuse-to-serve) covers the next steps.

## How can you keep a stranger from being chosen for you?

Most of this is choosing in advance. Here is what works.

- **Sign a power of attorney and a health care directive.** An agent you picked usually makes a court guardianship unnecessary. See [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).
- **Nominate a guardian in writing.** Many states let you name who you want as guardian if one is ever needed, including in a power of attorney, and courts generally give that real weight.
- **Name backups.** A first choice who is willing today may be sick or far away in ten years. List a second and third.
- **Talk to the people you picked.** Ask them. Tell them where documents are.
- **Choose a professional on purpose.** If you have no family, you can name a trusted professional, a bank or a trust company in your documents so that the person who steps in is someone you chose. The question of paying for care with no family is covered in [what happens if you need long-term care and have no money or no family](/learn/elder-care/what-happens-if-you-need-long-term-care-and-have-no-money-or-no-family).
- **For your children,** name a guardian and an alternate in your will, and update both after any move or family change. The [guardian picker](/tools/guardian-picker) helps compare candidates.

None of this costs much compared with a contested guardianship. Court supervision, attorney fees and a professional guardian's hourly charges can run for years.

## A common mistake

Our attorneys often see people name one child as their only agent and one guardian and never revisit it. When that person dies, moves away or becomes ill, there is no document for the next step, and the court is back to picking a stranger. Review who is named every few years.
