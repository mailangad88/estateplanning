For many divorced and single parents, the nightmare is not only dying. It is the thought of an ex who has been unreliable, unkind or absent suddenly raising the children. So they write a guardian into their will and assume that settles it.

It usually does not, and finding that out after you sign is better than finding it out through your family. This page explains who gets custody when one parent dies, what a guardian nomination can and cannot do against a living parent, and the steps that give your child real protection.

## The short version

- The surviving parent, if legally recognized and fit, almost always has the first claim to the child.
- A guardian named in a will matters most when there is no surviving parent able to serve.
- To keep a child from a living parent, you need facts a judge can verify, not just a preference in a will.
- The money is easier to control than the custody. Put it in a trust with a trustee you choose.
- Name a guardian anyway. The backup is the one the court needs if your ex cannot serve.

## Does the surviving parent automatically get custody?

Yes, in almost every state. A legal parent has a strong right to raise their own child. When the custodial parent dies, the other parent does not have to go through a guardianship petition. Custody generally passes to them, and a judge becomes involved only if someone challenges it.

This surprises people most when the parents were divorced. Priya, 44, has had primary custody of her 10-year-old daughter, Mira, since the divorce. Her ex sees Mira every other weekend. Priya's will names her brother as guardian. When Priya dies, her brother does not take Mira. The ex has the legal claim and can ask for the girl to come to live with him. Her brother can file to contest, but he would be the one who has to prove why the ex should not have custody.

The same is true when you were never married, as long as the other parent's legal parentage is established. A father or mother whose name is on the birth certificate or who has signed an acknowledgment of paternity is treated as a parent. A biological father whose paternity was never established is in a more uncertain position, and the rules vary a great deal by state.

## When does a guardian named in a will take effect?

The nomination does real work in four situations:

1. **The other parent is deceased.** Both parents have died, perhaps together, and the will controls who raises the child. See our article on [whether a named guardian holds up in court](/learn/guardianship/does-a-guardian-named-in-a-will-hold-up-in-court).
2. **The other parent's rights have been terminated.** A court has already ended them, through adoption or a termination order.
3. **The other parent cannot be found or is unable to serve.** Serious illness, incarceration, or long absence can all count.
4. **A court finds the other parent unfit.** This is the one parents worry about, and it is the hardest to prove.

In the third and fourth cases the judge asks what is best for the child. A nomination you made years ago, with reasons, is persuasive evidence. It is not binding.

## What if the other parent is unfit?

Unfit has a specific meaning in court. It does not mean difficult, irresponsible with money, or someone you cannot stand. Courts look for things like ongoing substance abuse, a history of neglect or abuse, a criminal record that bears on the child's safety, or a long absence with no effort to be involved. Disagreements about parenting style almost never reach the bar.

If you think your ex is unfit, the most useful thing you can do while alive is to build a record.

1. **Keep documents.** Police reports, child protective services findings, court orders, and school or medical records that show a pattern.
2. **Seek a court order now if the problem is current.** A custody order that limits or supervises the other parent's contact is much stronger than a note in a will. A family law attorney handles this, and it is separate from your estate plan.
3. **Write your concerns down for the judge.** A dated letter naming specific events carries more weight than a general statement. Keep it with your will and tell your guardian where it is.
4. **Name a guardian and a backup,** so the court has a ready alternative that you chose.

Our attorneys cannot promise a result in this situation, and nobody honestly can. What they can do is make sure the record exists if a hearing happens.

## Can I exclude a parent from guardianship in my will?

You can say you do not want them to serve. You cannot make that statement stick on its own. A will is not binding on a parent who has independent legal rights, and judges treat a line like "my ex shall not have custody" as an expression of preference, not an order. It still has value. It tells the court you thought about it, and it supports your sister or friend if they petition.

What binds is a court order made while you are alive, or an actual finding of unfitness after your death.

## What can I control if I cannot control custody?

A good deal, as it turns out. Custody and money are separate, and you can protect the money even when you cannot choose who raises the child.

| What you want | What works | What does not |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the ex from controlling your child's inheritance | A trust with a trustee you pick | Leaving money outright to the child |
| Make sure a guardian can pay for the child | A trust that pays the guardian's household costs | Hoping the parent shares support |
| Protect the child from a parent with money problems | A trustee who is not a parent | Naming the surviving parent as custodian |
| Keep insurance proceeds away from the ex | Naming the trust as beneficiary | Naming the child, or naming the ex |

If your child is a minor and you leave assets directly, a court-supervised conservatorship can be set up, and the surviving parent often gets the job. A trust avoids that. Our page on [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) compares the options, and [UTMA vs. a trust](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child) explains why a custodial account may hand control to the very person you worry about.

Social Security survivor benefits may be payable for a minor child, and they belong to the child while the person raising the child manages them. Our page on [what Social Security pays after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) explains how that works.

## What should single and divorced parents do?

Do all of the following, in this order.

1. **Name a guardian and a backup in your will,** and ask each of them first.
2. **Create a trust for the child's inheritance and life insurance,** with a trustee who is not the other parent.
3. **Use a standby or temporary guardian document** if you are the main caregiver and there is a risk you will be incapacitated. Read about [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians).
4. **Update beneficiary forms,** because an ex who is still listed on life insurance or a 401(k) will receive that money. Divorce settlements often require the ex to name the child, and that is another reason to check.
5. **Tell your guardian where everything is,** including the facts you want a judge to see.

For more on what a divorce changes in your plan, see [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce). The broader view is in our [guide to naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship).

## How state law changes this

States differ on how they treat unmarried fathers, how much weight a parent's written nomination carries, and the standard for finding a parent unfit. Some states allow a parent to name a guardian only when the other parent is dead or has no custody rights. Others let the court consider a nomination even if the other parent is alive. A lawyer in your state can tell you which applies. Our [state guides](/estate-planning) are a starting point.
