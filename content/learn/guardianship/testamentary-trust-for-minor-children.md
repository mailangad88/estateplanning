When a couple with a three-year-old and a one-year-old sit down to make a first will, the attorney usually asks the same thing: if you both died tomorrow, who gets the money, and at what age? Answering that question inside the will is what creates a testamentary trust.

This article explains what the trust is, when it makes sense for parents of minor children, how it differs from a living trust, and where parents most often trip. For the bigger picture of protecting children, see our [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship).

## What is a testamentary trust?

A [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust) is a trust whose terms are written into your will. It comes into being only after you die, once the court admits the will to probate. Until then it is just words on paper.

The will names a [trustee](/glossary/trustee), tells the trustee what to do with the property for each child, and says when the trust ends. If both parents die while the children are young, the trustee holds the inheritance, pays for the children's needs, and distributes the rest at the ages you chose.

It is different from a [living trust](/glossary/living-trust), which you sign now and fund now. Our comparison of [will vs. trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) covers that decision at length.

## How does it work, step by step?

1. **You sign a will** with a section creating a trust for any child under a stated age.
2. **You die.** The will goes to probate court and the executor is appointed.
3. **The estate is gathered and cleaned up.** Debts, taxes and costs are paid. See [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate).
4. **The trustee receives the remaining property** for the children and begins managing it.
5. **The trustee pays** for health, education and support as the will allows.
6. **Distributions begin** at the ages you chose, and the trust ends.

Because step 2 to step 4 can take months, the children's guardian may need a short-term source of support. Life insurance with a named beneficiary can fill that gap, as we explain below.

## What are the advantages?

- **Cost.** A will with a trust clause usually costs less to prepare than a full funded living trust.
- **Control.** You pick the ages and the standards, instead of leaving the child to receive everything at 18 or 21.
- **Flexibility.** You can rewrite it as often as you like while you are alive.
- **No funding step.** There is no retitling of accounts or deeds, so there is nothing to forget.

For a young family with a modest home, some savings and term life insurance, that is often the right first plan.

## What are the drawbacks?

| Issue | What it means |
|---|---|
| Probate | The will must be probated before the trust gets anything, which takes time and costs money |
| Public record | The will, and often the trust terms, become public when filed |
| No help during incapacity | It does nothing if you are alive but unable to manage your affairs |
| Possible court supervision | Some states keep testamentary trusts under continuing court oversight, with reports and fees |
| Not a magnet for non-probate assets | Retirement accounts, life insurance and joint property pass by their own rules |

The last point is a common trap. Say Helena names her husband as beneficiary of her life insurance and he is the contingent beneficiary too. If they die together, the proceeds may go to her estate, to her minor children directly, or to a court-supervised account unless a trust or custodian is named. A testamentary trust can receive the money only if the policy names it, or the estate, as beneficiary.

## How do life insurance and retirement accounts connect?

Name the trust, not the child. For life insurance, many attorneys suggest naming your spouse first and "the trustee of the testamentary trust established under my will" as contingent. Some insurers handle this poorly, so check their forms. For retirement accounts, a trust as beneficiary needs to be written so it qualifies for favorable payout rules, because most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years. Our article on [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) walks through the options, and our [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) can estimate how much coverage your children would need.

## What does a realistic plan look like?

Picture Andre and Keisha, both 34, with a daughter aged four and a son aged one. They own a house with a mortgage, have $60,000 in retirement accounts, and carry $750,000 of term life insurance each. Their goals are simple. If something happens, the children live with Keisha's brother and his wife, the money lasts, and the kids do not get a large sum at 18.

Their will does the following. It names the brother and his wife as guardians, with Andre's cousin as alternate. It creates one trust for both children, with the cousin's wife, a retired bank officer, as trustee. The trustee pays for education, health care and support. When the younger child reaches 25, the remainder splits equally, and each child receives one-third of their share at 25, 30 and 35. Each life insurance policy names the surviving spouse first, then the trustee as contingent beneficiary.

It is not elaborate. It also took an afternoon to prepare, and it reflects decisions the couple made on purpose rather than leaving to a judge.

## Does a court watch over a testamentary trust?

In some states, yes. Certain states keep testamentary trusts under the supervision of the probate court, with annual accounts filed and fees paid. Other states let the trustee act without ongoing court involvement unless a beneficiary asks. This is one of the places where your state matters a great deal, and it is a reason to ask your attorney whether a living trust would spare your family the extra layer. State-specific points are covered in our [state estate planning guides](/estate-planning).

## When should a family move to a living trust?

When assets grow, when the family owns property in more than one state, when privacy matters, or when you want the trust to help in case of incapacity. A living trust avoids probate for assets placed in it and allows a successor trustee to act immediately. Many families start with a testamentary trust in a first will, then upgrade as the house appreciates or a business grows. A [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) then catches anything left outside the living trust.

## What should the trust language say?

The strongest testamentary trusts are specific. Cover these:

- **Trustee and backups.** At least two successors, and a way to replace a trustee who is not doing the job.
- **Ages.** One distribution date, or staged ones such as thirds at 25, 30 and 35.
- **Standard for spending.** "Health, education, maintenance and support" is the usual phrase.
- **Guardian housing.** Whether the trustee may help the guardian with a bigger home.
- **Pot or separate shares.** One fund for all children, or one per child.
- **Special cases.** A child with a disability should have a [special needs trust](/glossary/special-needs-trust) so benefits are not lost.
- **Early death of a child.** Where that child's share goes.

## Who should be trustee, and how is that different from guardian?

The trustee manages money. The guardian raises the children. The same person can do both, but many parents split the roles to create a check. Our article on [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) discusses how, and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) goes through the qualities to look for.
