A will can name a guardian in one line. It cannot tell that person that your daughter will not sleep without the hall light on, that your son hides when he is anxious, or that the pediatrician with the kind voice is on Maple Street. A letter of intent can.

This page explains what the letter is, why it matters to the people who step in, what to put in it, and the mistakes that make one useless. It takes an afternoon to start, and it is one of the kindest things a parent can leave.

## What is a letter of intent for a guardian?

It is a plain, personal letter addressed to your guardian, your backup, and often your trustee. It describes your children and your hopes. The words are yours, not a lawyer's. Because it is not a legal instrument, it does not need witnesses or a notary, though signing and dating it is wise.

It differs from a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction), which mostly deals with accounts, bills and funeral wishes, and from the [letter of intent used in special needs planning](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent), which has its own detailed format. A guardian's letter sits between the two. It is about the people, with enough logistics to be useful.

## The short version

- Write it as a letter to a real person, not a legal form.
- Cover who each child is, their daily routine, health, school and the people they love.
- Say what you hope for and what you would accept, so the guardian has room to parent.
- List where documents and accounts are.
- Review it each year, and keep copies with the will, the guardian and the trustee.

## Why does the guardian need it?

Consider what a guardian faces in the first week. The children are grieving. The guardian is also grieving, is possibly changing jobs, moving rooms around, and talking to lawyers. Nobody has time to learn the children's lives from scratch. A letter gives them a working map.

It also helps in a quieter way. A guardian worries about doing it "wrong." Reading your own words about what matters to you tells them where the guardrails are, and where they are free to decide. Judges, too, may find it persuasive if relatives dispute the arrangement, because it shows a parent's actual thinking. How much weight it gets differs by court and is never binding, as we explain in [does a guardian named in a will hold up in court](/learn/guardianship/does-a-guardian-named-in-a-will-hold-up-in-court).

## What should you put in it?

Organize by topic so the guardian can find things fast.

### The children as people

Write a paragraph about each child. What are they like when they are tired, sick, or happy? What frightens them? What do they like to eat, read, play? Which friends matter? Mia, 6, might need a stuffed rabbit and a specific bedtime song. Jonah, 14, might need his coach and his drum lessons. Mention milestones you were looking forward to, like a first communion or a driver's license.

### Daily life and health

- School, teachers, bus routes, after-school care
- Pediatrician, dentist, any medical providers and therapists
- Allergies, medications and anything about each child's health history
- Health insurance details and where the cards are
- Activities and who drives to them

### Values and traditions

Say what you hope for in faith, holidays, discipline, screen time and education. Be honest about priorities and flexible about details. "I hope they keep going to Sunday school, but I will understand if it changes" works better than a rule the guardian will break within a month.

### People and relationships

List grandparents, cousins, godparents and family friends the children should keep seeing. If there are tensions, say how you want the guardian to handle them. If you have chosen a guardian over a relative who may be hurt, explain your reasons kindly.

### Money and paperwork

Describe your hopes for the money without writing legal terms. Explain who the trustee is, how the guardian asks for funds, and what you hope the money will pay for, such as tutoring, therapy, college or a house deposit. Our guide to [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) explains the split. List where to find:

1. The will, trust and the attorney's contact details
2. Life insurance policies and retirement accounts
3. Bank, credit card and mortgage information
4. Passwords or the location of a password manager
5. Birth certificates, passports and Social Security numbers
6. Social media and email accounts, which you may want preserved for the children

### Guidance on bigger decisions

Some parents record their wishes on tough subjects: a child with learning differences, a change of school, whether the guardian should move closer to extended family, or how to tell the children about your death. Give reasons, not just orders.

## What does a short sample look like?

Here is the opening of a letter from a father named Carlos to his daughters' guardian, his sister Rosa:

"Rosa, if you are reading this, I am so sorry, and thank you. Lucia is 9 and will tell you she is fine when she is not. Give her an hour alone, then sit near her without asking. Sofia is 5 and wakes at 5:30 most days. She will only eat the green cereal. Dr. Patel on Maple Street knows both girls and has their records. I would like them to stay at Brookside Elementary through fifth grade if you can manage it. If you cannot, I trust you."

The tone is warm and specific, it gives the guardian permission to decide, and it takes under a minute to read. Nothing in it needs a lawyer.

## How do you write it without freezing up?

Many parents stall because it feels like writing a goodbye. Try this instead.

1. **Start with a list**, not a letter. Write the doctors, routines and contact numbers first.
2. **Add one small story per child.** A memory makes the facts real.
3. **Write in your normal voice.** If you say "kiddo," say it.
4. **Skip perfection.** A rough letter on paper beats a polished one that never gets written.
5. **Date and sign it.** Note the year so readers know how current it is.
6. **Revisit on a birthday.** A yearly habit keeps it accurate.

## What are the common mistakes?

- Treating the letter as a legal document and putting rules in it that belong in a trust
- Naming a guardian only in the letter, with no valid will
- Writing so many rules that the guardian feels watched
- Leaving out the unflattering facts, such as a child's temper or learning difficulty, that a guardian most needs to know
- Putting the only copy in a safe deposit box nobody can open quickly
- Forgetting to update it after a move, a diagnosis or a change in guardian

## Who should get a copy?

Give a copy to the guardian and the backup guardian. Give another to the trustee, if different. Keep one with your [will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) and tell your attorney. Parents sometimes hesitate to share it for fear of upsetting the person who was not chosen. A short, kind conversation first helps, and our guide to [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) can help you frame it.

## How state law changes this

Nothing about the letter itself depends on state law. What changes is how a court treats it, and what the legal documents next to it must say. Some states allow a parent's written statement to be attached to a guardianship petition as evidence. Others give it no formal role. Either way, the nomination itself must appear in a will that meets your state's signing rules. For the bigger picture, see our [guide to naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship) and your state guide, such as [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois).
