For many parents the first name that comes to mind is a grandparent. They already love the children, they live nearby or visit often, and they have raised kids before. Then the second thought arrives: they will be older, and what if they are not up to it?

Both thoughts are fair. This page covers what the law allows, how age and health should shape the choice, what it means for money, and how to name a grandparent in a way that works if things go differently than you hope.

## The short version

- Grandparents can be named guardian in a will, the same as any other adult.
- Judges usually respect the choice when the grandparent is healthy enough and willing.
- Age is a practical issue, not a legal one. Plan for what happens if the grandparent cannot continue.
- Money is the other practical issue. A trust or life insurance can pay for the added expense.
- Always name a backup, often a younger relative or friend.

## When is a grandparent a good choice?

A grandparent works well when four things are true. They are in good health, they have a stable home, they already know the children well, and they want the role. The last one matters more than parents expect. Some grandparents say yes out of duty and later find that raising teenagers at seventy is too much.

Here is one example. Marcus and Elena, both 41, have a 9-year-old and a 12-year-old. Elena's mother, Gloria, is 66, a retired teacher in good health, and lives six blocks away. Gloria says she would take the children without hesitation. Marcus and Elena name her first. Because the youngest will not turn 18 until Gloria is 75, they name Elena's younger brother Tomas and his wife as backups, and they write a letter asking that the children keep seeing Gloria weekly if Tomas ends up raising them.

That is a sensible shape. It uses the love and familiarity grandparents bring, and it avoids placing all the weight on one person's health.

## When should you think twice?

A grandparent may be the wrong first choice in a few situations.

- **Health.** A chronic illness, early memory trouble, or a recent hospitalization means the children may lose a second caregiver in a few years.
- **Age gap.** Raising a toddler into adulthood takes 15 or more years. A guardian who is 75 when the job starts will be 90 at the end.
- **Money.** A grandparent on a fixed retirement income may struggle with food, school costs and a bigger car. This is fixable with planning, but you have to plan.
- **Family tension.** If your parents and your spouse's parents do not get along, naming one set can start a fight the court has to settle.
- **Different values.** If you want the children raised with a particular faith, school approach or lifestyle, ask directly whether the grandparent will honor that.

None of these rules out a grandparent. They are reasons to name a backup and to say so honestly in your conversation.

## What the law says

No state bars a grandparent from serving. The general rule in a probate or family court is that any adult who is willing and capable can be appointed. The court looks at the same factors it applies to any guardian: the relationship with the child, the stability of the home, the person's health and finances, and the child's wishes if old enough. Our article on [how courts decide when no one is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) walks through that test.

Two points are worth knowing.

First, grandparents have no priority on their own. If you do not write a nomination, a grandparent is one of several possible petitioners. Second, some states give grandparents statutory standing to ask a court for visitation or custody in certain cases, such as when a parent is dead or unfit. Those are separate from the nomination in a will and differ from state to state.

## How do you pay for it?

The guardian is responsible for the child's care. The money is a separate question, and grandparents often need help with it. Several tools work together.

| Tool | What it does for a grandparent guardian |
|---|---|
| Life insurance on the parents | Provides a lump sum, ideally payable to a trust for the children |
| Trust for the children | Lets a trustee reimburse housing, food and schooling for the child |
| Monthly stipend in the trust | Pays a set amount to the guardian for the child's costs |
| Savings or a 529 plan | Covers college and education costs |

Parents often pick a different person as trustee, such as a sibling or a bank, so the grandparent does not have to ask the court for money or justify every purchase. We explain the split in [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee), and the trust options in [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children). The [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) can help you estimate how much coverage the children might need.

## How do you name a grandparent?

1. **Ask them in person.** Explain what the job involves and give them room to say no. A hesitant yes is worth less than an honest no.
2. **Talk through health and backups.** Ask whether they would be comfortable with a younger person named first or as a successor.
3. **Choose the order.** Many parents name the grandparent first and a younger adult second. Others reverse it and ask the grandparent to be a regular part of the children's lives.
4. **Write the nomination in your will.** Include the successor names. The steps for the will itself are in [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).
5. **Set up the money.** Name a trustee, fund the trust with insurance, and say how the guardian is reimbursed.
6. **Leave a letter.** Describe the children's routines, doctors and friends, and what you hope for the relationship between the guardian and the other grandparents.
7. **Review every few years.** Health changes quickly after 65.

## What if the grandparent is already helping raise the children?

Many families already lean on a grandparent for school pickups and summer care. That history helps, because a judge sees an established bond. It does not replace a nomination, though. A grandparent who babysits three days a week has no legal authority to sign a medical consent or enroll a child in a new school. If the children's parents are both gone, the grandparent still has to petition. Writing the nomination ahead of time turns that petition into a short formality.

It also helps to ask whether the grandparent has their own legal paperwork in order. A guardian who becomes ill needs a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) and a will of their own, or the children's care can stall again.

## What if both sets of grandparents want to be guardian?

This is common, and it can be painful. The simplest approach is to name one household and write a short letter explaining that the choice was about logistics, not love. Say that you want the other grandparents to see the children often. Courts like seeing that kind of thought, and it lowers the odds of a petition in your absence. Our guide to [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) offers phrases that help.

## How state law changes this

State rules differ on how much weight a nomination gets, who must receive notice, and whether grandparents have special standing. Some states require notice to grandparents even if they were not named, which gives them a chance to object. If you are in a state with a particular guardianship statute, such as [Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship), read the local article.
