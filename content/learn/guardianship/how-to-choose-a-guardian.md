If you have children under 18, the most important sentence in your will is probably the one that names who raises them if you cannot. It is also the sentence parents put off longest, because it forces you to imagine something you do not want to imagine. This guide gives you a way to make the choice without it turning into a months-long worry.

You will learn what a guardian actually does, which factors matter most, how to compare people fairly, how to have the conversation, and how to write the choice into your plan so a court has what it needs.

## What does a guardian of minor children actually do?

A [guardian](/glossary/guardian) of the person is the adult who takes over the daily job of parenting if both parents have died or cannot act. That means a home, meals, school, medical decisions, religion, discipline, and the emotional work of helping children through a loss.

The guardian's job is not the same as managing the money you leave. In many families a different person acts as [trustee](/glossary/trustee) for the children's inheritance. We explain that split in [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee). Keep the two decisions separate in your mind while you read this page, because the person best able to raise your kids is not always the person best able to handle a trust.

## What should you weigh when choosing?

Start with values and parenting style, then check practical ability, and only then think about money. A short list keeps the decision from being driven by guilt or family pressure.

| Factor | Questions to ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Values | Do they share your views on education, faith, discipline, and honesty? |
| Relationship | Do your children already know and trust this person? |
| Age and health | Will they have energy and good health for 10 to 18 years? |
| Home and lifestyle | Is there room, stability and time? Would the kids have to change schools or cities? |
| Willingness | Have they said yes, with a clear understanding of the job? |
| Existing family | Do they have children of their own, and could your kids blend in? |
| Marriage or partnership | Is the household stable, and would both adults agree to the role? |
| Money | Could they manage with some help from a trust you fund? |

Money deserves a note of its own. Many parents rule out a favorite brother or friend because he earns less than they do. If the household is loving and stable, a trust and life insurance can close that gap. The reverse mistake is picking the wealthiest relative who would be an indifferent parent.

### Do grandparents make good guardians?

Sometimes. Grandparents bring love, history and often a ready home. They also bring age. A guardian who is 68 when your child is 6 may face health problems before the child turns 18. If grandparents are your first choice, name a younger backup, and talk openly about what happens if their health changes.

### What about living in a different place?

A guardian in another state or country means your children would move, change schools and leave friends while grieving. That is sometimes still the right call. Weigh it honestly, and consider whether your will can express a preference for keeping the children in their current community and school if the guardian is willing.

## How do you compare two or three strong candidates?

Write the names down and score each one against the table above, privately and honestly. Then do three things.

1. Imagine the first month. Picture your children arriving at each home. Where do they sleep, who picks them up from school, what does a Tuesday look like?
2. Imagine year five. Which household would still feel right when the children are teenagers with strong opinions?
3. Ask your spouse or co-parent to do the exercise separately, then compare. Disagreement is common and useful. It shows which factor each of you weighs most.

If you land on a tie, choose the person who is more likely to say yes and keep the other as backup. Do not try to split the baby between two people unless they are a committed couple.

## What if you and your spouse disagree?

Disagreement is normal, and it is better to resolve it now than leave it to a judge. Each of you should explain the one or two things that drive your choice, such as faith, location, or a sibling relationship. Often a compromise appears: the guardian is one couple and the backup is the other. If you cannot agree, put your first and second choices in the will and revisit after a few months. A will with a thoughtful second choice is better than no will.

## How do you ask someone to be a guardian?

Ask in person and explain what you are asking. Say that you want them to consider raising your children if both parents die, that you also plan to leave money and support so it does not become a financial burden, and that you will respect a no. Give them a week.

A good conversation covers:

- Their honest reaction and their spouse's, if they are married
- What they would want to know about your children's routines, health, and needs
- Your hopes for schooling, faith, and keeping in touch with grandparents and cousins
- Who will manage the money and how the guardian can ask for it
- Where your documents and contact lists are kept

If someone declines, thank them. You have just avoided a harder conversation later.

## How do you name the guardian legally?

You name your guardian in your [will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will), usually in a section called "Nomination of Guardian." The will names a first choice, a successor if the first cannot serve, and often a second successor. It can also state your wishes about where the children live and how the guardian should handle photos, family contact, and keepsakes.

The nomination only takes effect through a court. A judge confirms the appointment, almost always following your wishes, which is why it is so useful to be specific. The will should also say whether the guardian must post a bond (many parents waive it) and how the guardian will be paid back for reasonable expenses. For the full picture of who a court turns to when there is no will, see [what happens if you die without naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named).

If you want coverage for a short-term emergency, such as both parents in a car accident and in the hospital, your will will not help, because it only works after death. [Temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians) explains the documents that fill that gap.

### What if the other parent is not part of the picture?

If your children's other legal parent is alive, the court generally turns to that parent first. If you think the other parent is unsafe or absent, your nomination is still worth making, but the plan requires more care. Document your reasons, keep records, and talk to an attorney in your state about the process for contesting custody.

## How often should you revisit the choice?

Look again every three to five years, and any time something major changes: a divorce, a death, a move, a new baby, a health diagnosis, or a falling out. Children grow, and the right guardian for a toddler may not be the right one for a teenager. The same thing is true of backups, whose health and finances also change. Our guide to [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists other triggers.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Naming someone without asking them
- Naming a couple without saying what happens if they separate
- Choosing based on who would be offended otherwise
- Forgetting a backup
- Leaving the guardian with no money or access to funds
- Never updating after a move or a death in the family
