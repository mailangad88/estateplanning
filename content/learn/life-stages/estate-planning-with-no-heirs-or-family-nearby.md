Helen is 76. Her husband died, they never had children, and her one brother passed years ago. She has a house, savings and a cat. When she asks who will be her executor, the honest answer is that she does not know anyone she could ask.

More people are in that position than you would think. Children move away, siblings die, friends age out of the role. This page covers who can serve when family cannot, what the fees look like, what happens to an estate with no heirs, and how to set up a plan so a stranger does not decide by default. For the wider life-stage picture, see our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages) and the page on [estate planning for single people](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people).

## The short version

- Fiduciary roles can go to friends, professionals or companies.
- You will probably need different people for different jobs.
- Costs are real, but a court-appointed stranger costs more.
- Without a will and without relatives, the state inherits.
- Pets, personal items and funeral wishes need specific people named.

## Who can serve as my executor if I have no relatives?

Anyone who is an adult, of sound mind and eligible in your state. Many states restrict out-of-state executors or require a bond, so check, but being unrelated is no obstacle at all. You have three realistic choices.

| Choice | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| A friend or neighbor | Knows you, often serves cheaply. | May be close to your age, may not want the work. |
| A professional fiduciary or attorney | Experienced, accountable. | Charges fees, less personal. |
| A bank or trust company | Permanent, regulated. | Minimums, percentage fees. |

A common design splits the roles. A friend is named to handle personal belongings, funeral wishes and the pet, while a professional serves as executor or trustee for the money. The two work together, and the friend is not stuck doing tax returns. Our guide to [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) covers the workload, and the [executor workload tool](/tools/executor-workload) shows how many hours an estate typically demands, which helps you judge what to ask of a friend.

## What is a professional fiduciary?

A professional fiduciary is someone who takes on roles such as trustee, executor, agent under a power of attorney or conservator as a business. They owe the same legal duties as a relative would, including loyalty and prudence, and they charge for it.

Some states license them, and the licensing usually requires background checks, education and continuing education. Others leave it to the market, so you need to ask. Before you name one, ask:

1. Are you licensed or certified, and by whom?
2. Are you bonded and insured?
3. What are your fees, hourly or flat, and what extras do you charge?
4. Who takes over if you retire or are unavailable?
5. Do you serve alone or as part of a firm?

Do not give the job to a person who advertises to elders but cannot show credentials. The page on [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) explains why checks matter.

## What is a corporate trustee, and when does it make sense?

A corporate trustee is a bank or trust company that holds and manages a trust. It does not get sick, move or retire, and it is regulated. That makes it a good fit for a trust that will last years, such as one for a niece with a disability or one that pays for a pet's care.

The downsides are cost and distance. Fees are typically a percentage of trust assets each year, and many companies set minimum sizes that exclude small trusts. A trust officer may not know your wishes personally, so a letter explaining them is worth writing. Some people name a friend as a co-trustee for the personal side and the company for investments. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) for the comparison of individuals and institutions.

## What happens if I die with no will and no heirs?

Every state has a list of relatives who inherit in a fixed order: spouse, children, parents, siblings, then further out, to nieces, nephews, grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins. If a court cannot find anyone on that list, the property usually goes to the state, which is called escheat. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains the order.

Two things surprise people. First, "no relatives" often turns out not to be true. Courts and heir-finding firms can trace cousins you never met, and they take a share. Second, the process takes months or years, during which your home sits, your pet waits and a court-appointed administrator you never chose runs the estate.

You can avoid all of it with a simple will. Name an executor, a backup, a charity or friend as the recipient and a person to care for the pet. If you want to see how state rules would apply today, the [who inherits tool](/tools/who-inherits) is a quick test.

## Who gets my pet if I have no family?

Whoever you name. Pets are treated as property, so without instructions an animal goes to the heirs, and with no heirs it goes to the estate. A caretaker should be asked in advance and told what the animal needs. Leave money for the care, either in the will or in a [pet trust](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) with a person who checks on the caretaker. A few lines on food, vet and routine help the new owner.

## Who should speak for me in a hospital?

This role is harder to fill than executor, because a person must be reachable and willing to say hard things. Your healthcare agent does not have to live nearby, but needs to be able to travel. Read [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) before you ask someone. Put the same names in your directive and HIPAA release, and consider naming a professional fiduciary as backup on the financial side, since the [power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) controls your money.

## What if I want a charity to inherit?

That is a good option for an estate without family. You can leave a percentage or a fixed amount to one or more charities in your will or on beneficiary forms for retirement accounts, where the charity pays no income tax on what it receives. Many people leave the retirement account to charity and other assets to people, because that pairing is usually the most tax-efficient. Name each charity by its legal name and tax ID, and a backup in case it closes. For more on giving, see [donor-advised funds and QCDs](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds).

## Who arranges my funeral?

Another small job with no obvious owner. In most states the law gives funeral authority to a spouse, then adult children, then other relatives, and with none of them it may fall to a public administrator or the county. You can name a person in writing instead. Many states allow a written appointment of an agent for disposition of remains, and you can state your wishes in your healthcare documents. Pre-paying is possible, but read the contract carefully and keep the paper where your executor can find it. A friend who knows what you want, plus a copy of your letter, spares everyone guessing. The [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) page covers what to put in it.

## How state law changes this

States differ on who is eligible to serve as executor, on fiduciary licensing, on how far the family tree extends before escheat, and on pet trusts. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) shows how one state treats these points, and your attorney can confirm your own.
