A new baby changes who depends on you overnight. Before the birth, a missing estate plan mostly affected you and your spouse. Now it affects a person who cannot make a single decision for themselves, and who would need a court to step in if both parents were suddenly gone.

This article walks through the five documents that matter most for new parents, in the order we suggest starting them. You do not need a large estate to need them. A modest home, a work retirement account and a term life insurance policy are enough to make the planning worthwhile. If you want the wider picture of how these documents fit together, see our [guide to estate planning by life stage](/learn/life-stages).

## What are the five documents new parents should sign first?

The five are a will with a guardian nomination, correct beneficiary designations, a plan for money the child will receive, a durable financial power of attorney, and a healthcare directive. Together they answer four questions: who raises the child, who pays for it, who manages that money, and who steps in if a parent is hurt but alive.

| Document | What it answers | Who it names |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Who raises our child if both of us die? | Guardian, backup guardian, executor |
| Beneficiary designations | Where does the life insurance and retirement money go? | Primary and contingent beneficiaries |
| Trust or custodial account | Who manages money left to the child, and until what age? | Trustee or custodian |
| Durable financial power of attorney | Who pays our bills if one of us is incapacitated? | Agent and successor agent |
| Healthcare directive | Who makes medical decisions if we cannot? | Healthcare agent and backup |

## 1. A will that names a guardian

A will is the only document that lets you name who would raise your child. If both parents die without naming anyone, a court decides, usually after a hearing where relatives may disagree. The judge will try to act in the child's best interest, but the judge does not know your family the way you do.

Name a primary guardian and at least one backup. Choose based on values, parenting style, health, location and willingness, not on who is closest to you or whose feelings you want to spare. Our guide to [choosing a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) covers how to decide, and [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) explains the court process you would be avoiding.

The same will names an [executor](/glossary/executor), the person who gathers your assets and carries out your instructions. For couples with young children, this is often each other, with a sibling or trusted friend as backup. See [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) for the tradeoffs.

Make sure both parents sign wills. In most states, a will cannot bind the other parent, and each parent's will should name the same guardians so the plan holds regardless of who dies last.

## 2. Beneficiary designations that match the plan

Life insurance and retirement accounts pass by beneficiary form, not by will. Whatever name is on the form controls, even if your will says something different. This is where new parents most often go wrong, because the forms were filled out years ago, often naming a parent, a former partner or no one at all.

Here is what to check on each account:

1. **Life insurance through work.** Many employers provide a policy worth one or two years of salary. Log in and confirm the primary and contingent beneficiary.
2. **Individual term life insurance.** If you have not bought it yet, most new parents should consider it. Term policies are inexpensive for healthy people in their 20s and 30s.
3. **401(k), 403(b) and IRAs.** A spouse is usually the default primary beneficiary on employer plans. Add a contingent beneficiary, which is who receives the money if your spouse is gone too.
4. **Bank and brokerage accounts.** Check whether a payable-on-death designation is in place.

If you name a minor child directly as a beneficiary, the insurance company will not hand a baby a check. A court will typically appoint a guardian of the estate to manage the money and report to the court until the child reaches adulthood. Our article on [life insurance beneficiary mistakes](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) goes through this in detail, and the [beneficiary designations pillar](/learn/beneficiary-designations) covers retirement accounts, payable-on-death accounts and contingent beneficiaries.

## 3. A trust or custodial account for the child's money

Money left outright to a minor is a problem, whether it comes from insurance, a house sale or a bank account. Either a court supervises it, or it is paid out in full when the child reaches the age of majority, often 18 or 21 depending on the state and the type of account. Few 18-year-olds are ready for several hundred thousand dollars.

Parents have two main options.

**A trust.** You write the terms. A trustee you choose manages the money and pays for health, education, housing and support. You can stagger distributions, for example one third at 25, one third at 30 and the rest at 35, or hold the money longer. A trust can be a standalone [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) or a trust that is written into your will and only starts at death. A standalone trust is more flexible and avoids court involvement for the assets placed in it. Our guide to [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) explains the common structures.

**A custodial account under the UTMA.** A [UTMA account](/glossary/utma) is simpler and cheaper. An adult custodian manages the funds, and the child receives whatever remains at the age set by state law. The drawback is you cannot control the age, and the money belongs to the child outright at that point. For smaller amounts that is often acceptable. See [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) for a side-by-side comparison.

One question that comes up often is whether the guardian and the trustee should be the same person. They do not have to be. Splitting the roles can add a check on how money is spent. The article on [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) lays out the reasoning.

Maria and Jorge, both 32, have a nine-month-old, a $380,000 house with a $290,000 mortgage, $140,000 in retirement accounts and a $750,000 employer-plus-term life insurance combination each. If both died, the child would inherit well over a million dollars in life insurance and retirement money combined. A trust holding the insurance proceeds, managed by Jorge's sister until the child is 25, gives them both control and protection in a way a simple will cannot.

## 4. A durable financial power of attorney

A [durable power of attorney](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney) lets someone you trust pay bills, access accounts and handle taxes if you cannot. New parents often skip this because it feels like a document for older people. But a serious accident or illness at 35 can leave you unable to manage money for weeks or months, and a child's expenses do not pause.

Without one, your spouse may find that accounts in your name alone are frozen. Joint accounts help, but they do not cover everything, such as your retirement account, an employer benefits portal or your own tax filings. The only alternative is a court-ordered conservatorship, which is slow and public. Our article on the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) explains what it covers, and the [power of attorney pillar](/learn/power-of-attorney) goes deeper on choosing an agent and avoiding bank problems.

Name your spouse as agent and a backup who is not your spouse, because an accident that injures both of you would otherwise leave no one in place.

## 5. A healthcare directive

A healthcare directive usually has two parts: a healthcare power of attorney naming who speaks for you, and a living will stating your wishes about life-sustaining treatment. Add a [HIPAA authorization](/glossary/hipaa-authorization) so doctors can share information with your agent and your family.

Most parents assume their spouse will automatically handle this. In many states that is true by default, but hospitals sometimes hesitate, and the default does not help if your spouse is also injured, or if the person you would choose is a parent or sibling. A written directive removes the doubt. See our [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives) for what each document does.

## What can wait until later?

Several things that matter in a mature estate plan do not need to be done in your first month as a parent:

- Tax planning. At 2026 levels the federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000 per person, so a young family is nowhere near it.
- Complex trusts for asset protection or business succession.
- Funding a trust with every account. Start with the essentials and complete funding over the following weeks. See [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
- 529 college savings account ownership details, though it is worth asking who would own the account if you died.

## How often should new parents update the plan?

Plan to review after each additional child, a move to another state, a significant raise or inheritance, a change in who your chosen guardian is, or a divorce. Even without those events, look at your documents every three to five years. Our guide on [when to review your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) provides a full trigger list.

A practical way to start: schedule one hour, decide on your guardian and backup guardian, list each insurance and retirement account with its current beneficiary, and gather these facts before you meet an attorney. The [what to bring checklist](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) helps with that.
