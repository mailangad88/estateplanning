A divorce settles property, support and custody. It does not go through your files and cancel every document that names your former spouse. Your 401(k) form, your life insurance policy, your power of attorney and your will may all still say what they said on the day you married. If you die or become ill before updating them, your ex can end up with money, authority or control over your children's inheritance.

This guide shows what to review and in what order. It is written for someone going through or just past a divorce, and it also helps if your ex's plan names you. For the broader set of situations that call for a plan review, see our guide on [estate planning by life stage](/learn/life-stages).

## Does a divorce cancel my will?

In many states a divorce automatically revokes any gift to your former spouse in your will and in your revocable trust, and treats your ex as having died before you. These "revocation on divorce" laws exist in most states, but they vary in what they cover, when they apply and how they handle exceptions.

Three cautions apply:

- **Not every document is covered.** Some statutes reach wills and revocable trusts only, not beneficiary forms or jointly held property.
- **Some states require a final decree**, not just a filing or a separation.
- **A fallback may not match your wishes.** If your will leaves everything to your ex and the gift fails, the property goes to the next person named, or passes by intestacy. That could be someone you never intended.

Relying on a statute is a bet. Signing a new will costs less than a will dispute. If you were using a joint trust or "we" will with your spouse, it needs to be replaced. Read our guide on [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) and [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor), since your ex was probably your executor.

## Will my ex still receive my retirement account or life insurance?

They can. A beneficiary form is a contract between you and the account holder, and many states' automatic-revocation laws either do not apply to those contracts or are overridden by federal law for employer plans. If the form names your ex and you die, the plan administrator may pay your ex, even if your will and your divorce decree say otherwise.

| Asset | Who controls payout | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Employer 401(k), 403(b), pension | Plan form, governed by federal law | Submit a new form. Confirm what your plan needs. |
| IRA | Custodian form, with state law in some cases | Submit a new form. |
| Group life insurance at work | Form on file with the insurer | Submit a new form through HR or the benefits portal. |
| Individual life insurance | Policy beneficiary | Contact the insurer for a change form. |
| Bank accounts with POD designation | Bank form | Update at the bank. |
| Home or accounts held jointly | Title or account type | Retitle after your divorce settlement addresses ownership. |
| HSA and annuities | Form on file | Update. |

An important exception: your divorce decree may require you to maintain life insurance for child support or alimony, or may assign a portion of a retirement account to your ex through a court order. Check the decree and talk with your attorney before changing a beneficiary that the decree covers. Our articles on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) explain the choices you have for naming replacements, and the [beneficiary designations pillar](/learn/beneficiary-designations) brings them together.

## Which roles did your ex hold, and who replaces them?

A married couple's plan concentrates roles in the spouse. After a divorce, each one needs a new name. Go through this list:

1. **Executor** of your will. Choose an adult child, sibling or friend, and a backup.
2. **Trustee** of any trust you created, and successor trustee.
3. **Agent under your financial power of attorney.** If it names your ex, replace it. In many states a divorce revokes the ex as agent, but banks and others may not know.
4. **Healthcare agent.** Update the [healthcare proxy](/glossary/healthcare-proxy) and the HIPAA release, and revoke any old releases you gave to providers.
5. **Beneficiary** of accounts, as covered above.
6. **Guardian of minor children.** See below.
7. **Emergency contacts** at work, school and doctors' offices.

For the choice of replacement agents, our guide on [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) helps you weigh siblings, adult children and professionals.

## What happens to the plan for your children?

If you have minor children, your plan after divorce usually involves three decisions: who raises them if you die, who manages money you leave them, and how the other parent fits in.

**Guardian.** If you die and the other parent is alive and fit, the child typically goes to that parent, whatever your will says. A guardian nomination matters if both parents are gone, or if the other parent is unable or has lost custody. Review our guide on [choosing a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) and the [guardianship pillar](/learn/guardianship). If you believe the other parent is not able to parent, a will cannot fix that. A family law attorney can advise on options during your lifetime.

**Money.** Do not leave money directly to a minor child, and think carefully before naming your ex as the person who controls it. Many divorced parents appoint a trustee who is not the ex, such as a sibling or a bank, so the money is used for the child and managed independently. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) and [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors). The pillar on [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) explains why splitting the roles can help.

**Support obligations.** Child support may continue after your death in some states, and a divorce settlement may require insurance. Your plan should be consistent with those obligations.

Elena, 44, divorced two years ago. Her 401(k) still names her former husband, Tom, and her will names Tom as executor and guardian of their 12-year-old. She replaces Tom with her sister as executor, creates a trust for her son with her sister as trustee, names her brother as backup guardian in case Tom cannot act, and files a new 401(k) beneficiary form naming the trust. Tom remains the father and would likely have custody on her death, but the money is no longer under his control.

## What should you do while the divorce is pending?

Be careful. Many courts issue standing orders when a case is filed that prohibit either spouse from changing insurance coverage, beneficiaries or ownership of property until the case concludes. Making a change in violation can lead to sanctions. Steps to take:

1. Ask your divorce attorney which changes are allowed now.
2. Update the documents that do not involve property, such as healthcare directives and your power of attorney.
3. Prepare a new will and trust so they are ready to sign.
4. Gather the account and policy numbers and form names, so changes can be made the day the decree is signed.
5. Submit the beneficiary forms immediately after the decree.

Do not wait until a "good moment." The period between the decree and your updates is when an unplanned death or incapacity would cause the most harm.

## What about assets that were titled jointly?

Joint ownership may continue after divorce unless your settlement and later paperwork change it. A deed that still names both of you, a joint bank account or a shared brokerage account can send the property to your ex if you die. The decree may order a transfer, but an actual new deed or title change often has to be recorded. Follow up on each account and each parcel of real estate until the title matches the decree. Our [property and assets pillar](/learn/property-and-assets) discusses titling.

## How do you handle digital accounts and other loose ends?

Remove your ex as an authorized user, beneficiary or contact on digital accounts, subscriptions, a password manager and emergency access settings. Update your emergency contact at your employer and school. Our guide on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) covers the practical side.

If you later remarry, you will want to revisit the entire plan, particularly if you have children from your first marriage. See [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) for the tools to consider.
