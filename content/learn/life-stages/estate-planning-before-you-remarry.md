You are engaged, or close to it, and it is not your first marriage. You own a house, there is a retirement account, and you have children who are watching this closely. The wedding date is on the calendar, but nothing about your paperwork is.

This page is a before-the-wedding checklist: what a marriage changes in the law, what to decide first and which documents to change in which order. It is part of our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages), and it pairs with our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families) for the longer planning questions.

## What changes legally when you remarry?

Your spouse acquires rights against your estate. In both Illinois and New Jersey, a surviving spouse can claim a share of the estate even if the will says otherwise, and a spouse is first in line if there is no will. Illinois spouse rights are covered in [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death), and the general rule in [can you disinherit a spouse](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse).

Your old will does not disappear. Marriage alone does not cancel it. That sounds comforting until you read what it says: a will written during your first marriage may name your late or former spouse, leave nothing to the new one and contain an executor who does not fit anymore. See [remarrying without updating your will](/learn/blended-families/remarried-without-updating-your-will).

## Which decisions come first?

Three, in this order. Make them before you hire anyone to draw documents.

1. **What do you want your children to receive, and when?** Everything depends on this. Some people want the house and savings to go to their children after the spouse's lifetime. Others want a fixed amount now and the rest to the spouse.
2. **What does your new spouse need to feel safe?** A place to live, an income, health care. Ask. Do not guess.
3. **Who will be in charge if you cannot decide?** Your spouse, a child, or a neutral person.

Our page on [how much to leave a second spouse versus your children](/learn/blended-families/how-much-to-leave-a-second-spouse-versus-your-children) helps with the numbers.

## What should you do about the house?

Decide before the wedding, because the usual mistake is the easy one: adding your new spouse to the deed. A deed that makes you and your spouse joint owners with survivorship sends the house to the survivor, and then it is theirs to leave to anyone. If your children were meant to inherit, that one document undoes it.

| Choice | Effect at your death | Fits when |
|---|---|---|
| Keep it in your name, will leaves to children | Children inherit; spouse may have to move | Spouse has own home or income |
| Joint with survivorship | Goes to your spouse outright | You trust the spouse completely |
| Life estate or right to live there | Spouse stays for life; children inherit after | You want both protected |
| Trust holds the house | Trustee follows your terms | Larger estates, tense families |
| Sell and share or split proceeds | Cash divided as agreed | You want a clean start |

Read [the family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) and [life estate for a surviving spouse](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse) before choosing. There are tax effects on the sale of a home and on a gift of a share, so ask before retitling anything.

## What about retirement accounts, pensions and insurance?

These pass by form, not by will, and the rules for a spouse are strong. A 401(k) or pension generally must go to a married spouse unless the spouse signs a proper waiver after the wedding. An IRA is more flexible. A life insurance policy pays whoever is named. Make a list of every account and write down what the form says today. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps, and [retirement accounts in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/retirement-accounts-in-a-second-marriage) explains the rules.

Check also if an account still names a prior spouse. See [what happens if an ex-spouse is still the beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).

## Will remarriage affect Social Security or a pension from your last spouse?

It can, depending on age and plan. For Social Security, a widow or widower who remarries before 60 generally cannot collect survivor benefits on the former spouse's record while married, and remarrying at 60 or later does not stop them. Some pension plans and annuities from a prior marriage also have remarriage terms. Call the Social Security Administration (ssa.gov) and each plan administrator before you set the date. Waiting a few months has changed the answer for some couples.

## Should you sign a prenuptial agreement?

If you have children from before and meaningful assets, usually yes, or at least have the conversation. A prenuptial agreement can record what stays separate, what each spouse waives at death and what happens to the house. It has to be signed before the wedding, with fair disclosure, and ideally with separate lawyers. Our page on [prenups, postnups and your estate plan](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans) covers validity and timing.

Allow lead time. A couple that raises the topic three weeks before the wedding has a hard conversation and a short deadline. Two or three months is more comfortable, and it lets each side ask questions.

## How and when should you tell your children?

Before the wedding, and with the plan in hand, not as an announcement. Adult children rarely object to the marriage itself. What worries them is the house and the money, and silence fills that gap with the worst guesses. A short conversation that says what each of them will receive, what your spouse will have the right to use and who will manage things if you cannot, is usually enough. You do not owe them a vote. You owe them clarity, and the clarity helps your spouse too, because it takes the stress off the question of who is pushing whom.

If your children are young or still dependent, add a guardian and a trust for their share. A new spouse is not automatically their legal guardian or a trustee of their money, as our page on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) explains from the other side.

## A pre-wedding checklist

Example only. Lena, 63, widowed, owns a Chicago-area home and a $400,000 IRA. Her fiancé Greg has a pension and a condo. Her two daughters are cautious. Her checklist, in order:

1. Write down what each daughter should receive and what Greg should have.
2. Meet an attorney for a prenup, with Greg meeting his own.
3. Decide on the house: a life estate for Greg, then to the daughters.
4. Update the IRA to name the daughters, since Greg has his own pension.
5. Sign a new will and a revocable trust.
6. Name health care and property agents, with a daughter as backup.
7. After the wedding, have Greg sign the waivers that must come after.
8. Tell the daughters in a short family meeting what the plan does.

Greg's agreement and Lena's choices are made with open eyes. The daughters may not love the plan, but they know it exists, and it does not depend on anyone's memory.

## What about powers of attorney and health care?

After the wedding your spouse will become your default decision-maker in a hospital in many situations, because health care laws list spouses first. If you would rather a daughter or son have that role, sign a health care power of attorney naming them. For the financial side, a durable power of attorney names an agent who can pay bills. Our guide on [who should make decisions in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/decision-makers-in-a-blended-family) covers the choice. A power of attorney does not give anyone control of your estate after death, so do not use it as a substitute for the will.
