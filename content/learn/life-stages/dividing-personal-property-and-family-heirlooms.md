When a parent dies, the family often agrees on the house and the bank accounts within a week. Then someone asks who gets the piano, and the room goes quiet. Items with no price tag, like a quilt, a watch or a set of dishes, carry the weight of a relationship, and each child may remember a different promise.

This page explains how to divide personal property in a way your children can live with, how to write it down so it holds, and what to say to them before you are gone. It sits alongside the legal questions in [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares), which covers money, and our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages) for the bigger picture.

## The short version

- Start by finding out what each child cares about. You may be wrong.
- Put your choices in writing and keep the list somewhere findable.
- For everything else, choose a fair process, not a formula.
- Talk to your children before you sign.
- Keep valuables, firearms and anything with a legal complication separate.

## Why do belongings cause so many arguments?

Because they are emotional and nobody agrees on their value. A child who remembers baking with Mom wants the mixing bowl, and a sibling sees a $20 item. A watch may be worth hundreds in a pawn shop and a lifetime to a son. Then there is the question of promises. A mother says "you'll have the ring" to two daughters in different years.

Estate lawyers see this repeatedly. Heated disputes over sentimental items in probate are common, and they can end in the sale of everything and a bitter split. A written plan stops most of it.

## How do I decide who gets what?

Ask. Walk through the house with each child, or send each a list of rooms, and ask what they would like to have. You will often be surprised. The son you expected to want the tools asks for your mother's recipe box.

Then choose a method.

| Method | How it works | Works well when |
|---|---|---|
| Your list | You name who gets each item. | You know who wants what. |
| Rotating choice | Children draw numbers, pick one item at a time. | Several children want similar things. |
| Appraise and equalize | Items are valued, and cash balances differences. | Valuable items such as jewelry or art. |
| Sell and divide | Everything is sold and the proceeds split. | Nobody wants the items. |

A rotating draft is simple and has a long track record in families. Pick a reverse order for each round, so the child who picks last in round one picks first in round two. Let each child skip a turn if nothing is left they want. It feels fair because everyone sees the same rules.

## How do I write a list that holds up?

Use a personal property memorandum. In many states, a will can refer to a separate signed and dated list, and the list is legally binding even though it is not part of the will. The advantage is that you can change it without a codicil. Some states do not allow this, or limit it to items under a dollar amount, and so a safer approach is to ask your attorney how it works where you live.

Steps that help:

1. Make a list of items with a short description and photo. "Mom's blue vase" is not enough if there are three.
2. Name the person who receives each item, and a backup.
3. Date and sign it, and note the date of any update.
4. Add a line in your will that refers to the list and says how to handle anything not on it.
5. Give the list to the executor and tell your children where it is.

Include a fallback in the will: items left unlisted are divided by agreement, or by rotating choice, with the executor deciding any dispute. That one sentence prevents a court fight. For more on writing the will itself, see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

## What does this look like in a real family?

Carol, 79, has three children. Ellen lives nearby and has cared for her. Mark lives across the country and rarely visits. Joy is the youngest and wants "anything with Dad's handwriting." Carol walks through the house with a notebook, then writes that Ellen gets the china she has always used, Mark gets his father's tools because he asked, and Joy gets the recipe box and the letters. For the rest, she writes that the three will take turns choosing, oldest first, then reverse. Total cost: an afternoon and one paragraph in the will. Compare that to the usual alternative, three adults in a garage with a dumpster rental.

Carol also told them what she had done, and Mark said he would rather have the clock. She changed the list that night.

## What about valuable items?

Jewelry, art, collections and firearms deserve separate attention. Have jewelry appraised so you know whether it matters financially. A collection, such as coins or guitars, may be worth more sold together than split, and it can create a tax question for the heirs. Most inherited items get a stepped-up basis at death, so selling soon after inheriting usually creates little gain; the article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains it.

Firearms have their own transfer rules, and a plan should address them; see [firearms in estate planning](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning). A vehicle needs a title transfer, and an heirloom that is jointly owned with a sibling needs a different approach altogether.

## Should I give things away while I am alive?

Often, yes. Giving a watch or a quilt at a birthday means you see the joy, you can tell the story, and nobody argues about it later. There is no gift tax return for ordinary household gifts, since they fall well under the $19,000 per recipient annual exclusion in 2026. Keep a note of what went to whom so the final division accounts for it. If you hand one child a valuable item, record whether it counts against their share.

## Should I tell my children what I plan to do?

In most families, yes, and sooner is better. You do not need to share every dollar. Tell them who the executor is, where the documents are, and any plan that treats the children differently. If one child gets the cabin and another gets the cash, explain why. Our article on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) gives scripts and timing.

A parent who says "I'll leave it to you to work out" is usually handing over a fight. A parent who says "here is the list, and here is how we will handle the rest" is handing over a plan.

## What should my family find when I die?

Build a binder or a digital folder. Call it whatever you like. The contents matter more than the name:

- The original will location or the lawyer's contact.
- Directives and powers of attorney.
- A list of accounts, debts and insurance policies, with contacts.
- Deeds, vehicle titles and a safe deposit box key.
- Passwords or a pointer to a password manager. See [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access).
- Funeral wishes and the personal property list.

A [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) can sit on top as a roadmap. Review it each year when you do your taxes. Your executor will need certified death certificates and these papers to start the work described in the [checklist for what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether a will may incorporate a separate list, on the dollar limits for personal items it can cover, and on how a court resolves disputes. Check your own [state guide](/estate-planning/ohio) as an example, and ask an attorney how a list works where you live.
