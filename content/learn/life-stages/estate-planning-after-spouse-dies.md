When a spouse dies, you are managing grief and a long list of practical tasks at once. The estate of your spouse needs to be settled, and your own plan also needs to change. Nearly every document you signed together probably lists your spouse first: as your executor, your agent under a power of attorney, your healthcare decision-maker, your trustee, your beneficiary. Those roles are now empty.

This guide separates what is urgent from what can wait. It assumes you are also dealing with your spouse's estate, so it points to our [after a death guide](/learn/after-a-death) for that part and focuses on your plan going forward. If you are looking at the whole life-stage picture, our guide to [estate planning by life stage](/learn/life-stages) includes related situations.

## What should you do first after a spouse dies?

Handle your spouse's estate and any immediate deadlines first, and give your own plan a few months. The urgent tasks include getting death certificates, locating the will and any trust, contacting the employer and Social Security, and securing the home and accounts. Our [week-by-week checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) walks through them, and the guides on [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) and [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) explain the formal work if you are the executor or successor trustee.

Two things in that first stretch deserve special attention because they affect your own plan:

- **Anything that can be disclaimed.** A [disclaimer](/glossary/disclaimer) must usually be made within a short window, often nine months, and it can pass assets to the next beneficiary without gift tax consequences. It may matter if you do not need an inheritance, or if a trust is set up to use it. Ask an attorney early.
- **Estate tax portability.** See below.

Avoid large irreversible choices for a while. Selling the family home, moving across the country, lending or giving money to children and making big investment changes are all easier to decide well after a few months.

## Which of your documents need to change?

Replace your late spouse in every role, because a plan with an empty slot defaults to someone you may not have chosen. Go through the list.

| Document | What likely needs to change |
|---|---|
| Will | Executor, guardian for minor children, main beneficiary (was your spouse) |
| Revocable living trust | Successor trustee, remainder beneficiaries, whether the trust divides into sub-trusts |
| Financial power of attorney | Agent and successor agent |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Healthcare agent and backup |
| HIPAA release | Add or replace the people allowed to receive information |
| Beneficiary forms | Primary and contingent on every account and policy |
| Payable-on-death accounts | Replace your spouse as beneficiary |

If you and your spouse had a joint will or mirror wills, review them carefully. A joint trust may split into a "survivor's trust" and a "decedent's trust" at the first death, each with its own rules. Do not assume you can change everything. A trust may have irrevocable parts after your spouse's death, and there may be contractual obligations from a prior agreement. An attorney should read the documents before you sign anything new.

## How do you choose new agents and an executor?

Choose people on the basis of capability and willingness, not birth order. Many widowed people automatically name the oldest child, then regret it. Ask yourself:

1. Who is organized and good with money? That person may be a better financial agent or executor than the one closest to you emotionally.
2. Who will speak up for your wishes in a hospital? That is your healthcare agent.
3. Does anyone live nearby or have the time?
4. Will the children work well together? If not, consider a professional or a co-agent arrangement with clear rules.
5. Is the person you want to name younger than you by enough years to be likely to outlast you?

Our guides to [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent), [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) go through the questions in more depth. For safeguards against misuse, such as requiring a second person to review statements, read about [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse).

## What happens to retirement accounts you inherit?

A surviving spouse has more options than any other beneficiary. Typically you can roll an inherited IRA into your own, treat it as yours, or keep it as a beneficiary account. Which one is better depends on your age, your income needs and when you need the money. For example, a spouse under 59½ who needs income might keep the account as a beneficiary to avoid the early withdrawal penalty on distributions, while an older spouse who does not need the money might roll it over and delay required distributions. These rules are detailed, so review them with a tax adviser before taking action. Our guide on [naming retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) summarizes the options and how they compare for your own heirs.

Then update the beneficiaries for the account you now own. Your children, grandchildren or a trust may now be the primary beneficiaries, and most non-spouse heirs must empty the account within 10 years. See the [beneficiary designations pillar](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Should you file for portability?

Consider filing a federal estate tax return to elect portability, even if no tax is due. Portability lets a surviving spouse add the deceased spouse's unused estate tax exemption to their own, which for a couple with $15,000,000 each in 2026 can reach $30 million. The election is made on a timely filed estate tax return, which is due nine months after death with extensions available, and the IRS has offered a simplified late-election option in limited circumstances. See the IRS website at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov) for current procedures.

Why do it if your estate is far below the exemption? Because laws can change, assets can grow, and an inheritance or business sale can push a modest estate higher. The cost of a return is small compared with the cost of losing the election. A few states also have their own estate tax exemptions that do not carry over between spouses. Read our guides on [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples), the [federal exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) and [state taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes). The [estate tax pillar](/learn/estate-tax) puts them in order.

Final income tax returns for your spouse are also part of the process; see [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns). You may still be able to file jointly for the year of death.

## How does the house and other jointly owned property transfer?

Property held jointly with survivorship passes to you automatically, but you must usually record the death to update the title. Assets owned as [tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety) or as joint tenants generally pass outside probate. Assets in your spouse's name alone follow the will or trust, and may require probate if there is no trust.

Basis matters. For many assets, the cost basis of the decedent's share steps up to the date-of-death value, and in community property states the entire asset may receive a full step-up. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). Keep records of date-of-death values for your tax adviser. Review any [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) before adding an adult child to your deed or accounts for convenience.

Ruth, 74, lost her husband Harold in March. They owned their house as tenants by the entirety, and the IRA named her as beneficiary. She took six months before meeting an attorney. By then she had recorded Harold's death certificate to put the house in her name, replaced Harold with her daughter as her financial agent and her son as healthcare agent, and filed an estate tax return to elect portability. Her new will divided her estate equally between her children, with her grandchildren as backups.

## Should you still make a plan if you are older and alone?

Yes. A widowed person has no spouse to fall back on, so the guidance for people living without a partner now applies, including backup agents and a plan for care. See [estate planning if you are single](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people) and [estate planning in retirement](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-retirees).
