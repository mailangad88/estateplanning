Estate planning is often described as something to do once, when you are older and wealthy. In practice it works more like a set of tools you pick up as life changes. A 19-year-old needs a short set of forms so her parents can help if she is hospitalized. A 34-year-old with a baby needs a guardian named in a will. A 62-year-old thinking about retirement needs to know how nursing home costs and inherited IRAs will affect his family. The documents overlap, but the priorities differ at each stage.

This guide gives you the big picture and sends you to the detailed article for your situation. Find your stage below, read the summary, then follow the link to the full guide. Everything here is general education, and the right answer for your family depends on your state and facts. An estate planning attorney can fit it to you.

## Why does your life stage matter more than your net worth?

The documents you need follow who depends on you, who could speak for you, and what could go wrong, not your balance sheet. A family with a $200,000 home and two young children needs more planning than a childless retiree with a $2 million portfolio and a long-standing trust. Wealth affects tax planning, but almost every other need is driven by relationships.

Three questions shape every stage:

1. **Who would raise or support the people who depend on me?** This brings in guardians, life insurance and trusts for children.
2. **Who speaks for me if I cannot?** This brings in powers of attorney and healthcare directives.
3. **Who gets my property, and who handles it?** This brings in wills, trusts, executors and beneficiary forms.

The answers shift with each life event, which is why the same family needs different advice at 25, 40 and 70.

## Which estate planning documents does everyone need?

Every adult benefits from the same core set: a durable financial power of attorney, a healthcare power of attorney with a living will, and a HIPAA authorization. These cover incapacity, which can strike at any age and does not depend on assets. A will or trust and correct beneficiary forms cover what happens at death. Our [core documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) explains each, and the [estate planning basics pillar](/learn/basics) covers the foundations.

| Stage | Highest-priority documents | Main risk if you skip them |
|---|---|---|
| Young adult (18 to mid 20s) | Healthcare POA, HIPAA release, financial POA | Parents locked out during a medical emergency |
| Newly married | Beneficiary updates, wills, POAs | Old forms naming parents or exes control |
| New parent | Will with guardian, life insurance, trust for kids | A court decides who raises your child |
| Unmarried couple | Wills or trusts, directives, titling | A partner inherits nothing and has no medical authority |
| Single, no children | Executor and agents, will or trust | Distant relatives or the state decide |
| Divorced | New will, beneficiary changes, new agents | An ex remains in control or still collects |
| Retired | Long-term care plan, beneficiary review, tax review | Care costs and taxes drain what you meant to leave |
| Widowed | Rebuild every role, portability, new documents | Empty roles in your plan default to the wrong person |

## What should young adults do at 18?

Sign three documents: a healthcare power of attorney, a HIPAA authorization and a durable financial power of attorney. At 18, parents lose automatic authority over medical and financial matters, and a hospital will not share information without written permission. A will is rarely urgent yet, though beneficiary forms on a first job's benefits matter. Our full guide, [estate planning at 18](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults), covers the paperwork, how to store it and what is different for young adults with disabilities.

If the young adult in your family has a disability, the decision at 18 about guardianship is a significant one. See the [special needs planning pillar](/learn/special-needs) for the alternatives.

## What changes when you get married?

Beneficiary forms, wills and powers of attorney all need to be rewritten to include your spouse, and your choices about how to title property now have consequences. Marriage creates default inheritance rights in most states, but those defaults rarely match what couples want. Our guide to [estate planning after you get married](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-newlyweds) lays out the order to do things in and the pitfalls of joint titling.

If either of you has children from a prior relationship, the standard couple's plan can leave them out. The [blended families pillar](/learn/blended-families) explains the tools, including [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) and [prenups and your estate plan](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).

## What do new parents need?

A will naming a guardian is the first priority, followed by life insurance with correct beneficiaries, a trust or custodial account to manage money for the child, and powers of attorney and directives for each parent. Our guide on [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) goes through the five documents in order, and the [guardianship pillar](/learn/guardianship) helps with the hardest part, choosing the people. Parents also benefit from reading about [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors), and the [trusts pillar](/learn/trusts) explains the structures available.

## What about unmarried couples?

Unmarried couples need to write down everything the law gives a married couple by default. Without it, a partner of decades generally inherits nothing and may have no say in medical decisions. Read [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) for the documents, the property titling options and the tax differences. The [healthcare directives pillar](/learn/healthcare-directives) is a useful companion.

## What if you are single or have no children?

The same planning applies and the stakes are different, because there is no spouse or child to fill the roles by default. Your plan needs an executor, agents and backups, and it must say who inherits. Our guide to [estate planning if you are single](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people) covers the roles, the intestacy order that applies when you have no plan and how to prepare for aging alone.

## How should you update your plan after a divorce?

Replace your former spouse in every role and on every beneficiary form, but check your divorce decree and any court orders first. Many states cancel gifts to an ex in a will, but beneficiary forms and federal retirement plans often do not follow the same rules. The full list is in [updating your estate plan after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce). If you have children, also read [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) and about [trustees for children's money](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).

## What should retirees review?

Retirees should review beneficiaries, agents and trust funding, and then plan for long-term care and the tax treatment of retirement accounts. Costs for care can exceed income, and most states look back 60 months at gifts when you apply for Medicaid. Start with [estate planning in retirement](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-retirees), then read the [elder care pillar](/learn/elder-care), which covers [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and [protecting your home](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs). Adult children trying to help a parent should see [how to help an aging parent get their plan in order](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan).

## What should you do after a spouse dies?

Settle your spouse's estate first, then rebuild your own plan over the following months. Your documents probably name your spouse in every important role, and portability of the estate tax exemption may be time-sensitive. Our guide on [updating your plan after your spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies) separates the urgent from the optional. The [after a death pillar](/learn/after-a-death) covers the practical steps for the estate itself.

## Which life events should trigger a review?

Marriage, divorce, a birth or adoption, a death, a move to another state, a new home or business, an inheritance, a major health change and retirement each call for a review. The most dangerous gap is the one you do not notice, such as a beneficiary form from a job you left fifteen years ago. A workable habit is to review every three to five years and after any event on the list. Our guide on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) provides a full list of triggers.

Here is a short checklist that works at any stage:

1. Confirm the current beneficiary on every retirement account, insurance policy and bank account.
2. Confirm that each person you named as agent, executor, trustee or guardian is alive, willing and still the right choice.
3. Check that the original documents can be found, and that agents have copies.
4. Check how your home and main accounts are titled.
5. Note any change in your state of residence, since rules differ.
6. Ask whether any asset or relationship in your life is not covered by your documents.

## What mistakes show up again and again across life stages?

The same few errors appear whether the person is 25 or 75, and nearly all of them come from assuming that one document does the work of the others.

- **Trusting the will to control everything.** Retirement accounts, life insurance and jointly owned property pass by form or title, so a perfect will cannot fix a stale beneficiary line. See the [beneficiary designations pillar](/learn/beneficiary-designations) and [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).
- **Naming the obvious person without asking.** An agent or executor who is unwilling, far away or overwhelmed does less good than a second choice who is ready.
- **Waiting for a bigger estate.** Incapacity documents cost the same at 30 as at 60 and protect against the likeliest problem.
- **Signing and forgetting.** A trust that was never funded, or a power of attorney the bank has never seen, fails when it is needed.
- **Planning only for death.** Many families first meet the system when a parent has a stroke, not when they die.

## How does state law change the picture?

State law decides signing rules, default inheritance, spousal rights, whether common-law marriage is recognized and whether the state has its own estate or inheritance tax. A document that is valid in one state is usually honored in another, but details can break at the border, so review after a move. Our state guides, such as those for [California](/estate-planning/california), [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) and [New York](/estate-planning/new-york), cover local rules, and the [estate tax pillar](/learn/estate-tax) explains the federal and state tax rules, including the $15,000,000 per person federal exemption in 2026.

## Where should you start?

Start with whichever problem would be hardest on your family tomorrow. For many people that is incapacity, so the first step is powers of attorney and a healthcare directive. For a parent it is the guardian. For a person with a stale beneficiary form it is that form. Our [plan finder](/plan-finder) asks a few questions about your family and shows a short list. Then use [what to bring to your first meeting](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) and, if cost is a concern, our article on [how much an estate plan costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost).
