Since the Supreme Court's 2015 decision in Obergefell v. Hodges, same-sex couples can marry in every state, and the federal government recognizes those marriages for tax, Social Security and retirement-plan purposes. That is a large change from the years when couples patched together paperwork to imitate it. If you are married, most of the legal safety net now exists by default.

Default rules still miss many LGBTQ households. Some couples never married, some are in registered partnerships, some have children with only one legal parent, and many people rely on friends and chosen family more than on relatives who may not accept them. This page covers where the law helps and where it does not. For a broader view, see our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages).

## What does marriage give you automatically?

A legally married same-sex couple has the same default rights as any married couple. Each spouse inherits first under the intestacy laws if the other dies without a will. Each can claim an elective share if left out. Each ranks first when a hospital looks for someone to make medical decisions, and each can transfer unlimited assets to the other free of estate tax under the [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction). A surviving spouse can also use the other's unused federal exemption through [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples), though the exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most couples will not face federal tax.

Retirement accounts follow the same rule. A surviving spouse can generally roll an inherited IRA into their own, which no unmarried partner can do. See [spousal rollover vs inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira).

Default rules are still not a plan. A spouse who inherits by default may get a share that does not match what you wanted, and the process runs through probate. Most married couples still want a will or trust, powers of attorney and updated beneficiary forms. If you married long ago, keep a certified copy of the marriage certificate with your documents, since some agencies will want to see it.

## Where are the gaps?

The gaps cluster in four places. Compare how each situation stands without extra paperwork.

| Situation | By default | What closes the gap |
|---|---|---|
| Unmarried partner | Inherits nothing; limited medical authority | Will or trust, healthcare agent, HIPAA release, beneficiary forms |
| Registered domestic partner | Varies widely by state | Same documents; confirm what your state grants |
| Non-biological parent | May lack legal parent status | Adoption or judgment of parentage |
| Chosen family and friends | No rights at all | Documents naming them for every role |

Partners who are not married should read [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples), which covers titling a home and the tax effects of leaving assets to a non-spouse.

## Do domestic partnerships and civil unions protect you?

They protect you to the extent your state says, and no further. A few states grant registered partners nearly all the rights of spouses, while others offer a short list covering hospital visits and some inheritance. Rights also may not follow you if you move. Federal programs usually treat a domestic partner as a stranger for retirement plans and Social Security purposes.

Some couples in older registrations were encouraged to convert to marriage. If you are still in a partnership and could marry, ask yourself whether you chose that deliberately. If it was habit, look at what marriage would change on both sides, including federal benefits, taxes and the surviving partner's retirement options. Remember that marrying also ends some benefits, such as means-tested aid, for a partner who receives them. An attorney can weigh these before you decide.

## How do you secure parental rights?

A non-biological parent should not rely on a birth certificate alone. Laws on presumed parentage differ, and some states automatically treat a spouse as a parent while others do not or do so only for certain couples. A court judgment of parentage or a second-parent or stepparent adoption is respected in other states and countries in a way a birth certificate may not be.

Why this matters for estate planning: if only one parent is legal and that parent dies, the surviving partner may have no automatic claim to the child. A will naming the partner as guardian helps but does not settle the question, and a biological relative might contest it. Our page on [guardianship, custody and adoption](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-vs-custody-vs-adoption) explains how those three work, and the child's right to inherit from both parents depends on legal parentage too. Couples who used a surrogate or donor should keep the agreements and any court orders together.

## What about relatives who might object?

A will or healthcare decision can be challenged by relatives who disapprove of a relationship. Estrangement is not a legal ground to overturn a plan, but a bitter family can still file a contest, argue that the signer lacked capacity, or claim [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence). You cannot prevent a lawsuit, but you can make a challenge hard to win.

Practical steps help:

1. Sign formally, with witnesses and a notary, and attach a self-proving affidavit where your state allows it.
2. Use a revocable living trust for the main assets so that nothing depends on a probate hearing where relatives can appear.
3. Name your healthcare agent and give a copy of the directive to the agent, your doctor and the hospital.
4. Sign a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) naming the people who may see records.
5. Name a person to control funeral and burial decisions. In many states, a signed designation outranks family.
6. Add a short, calm explanation of your choices to your will or in a separate letter.

Read more on how contests work in [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

Dana and Rosa have been together for nineteen years and married in 2016. Dana's parents have never accepted the relationship. Their plan names Rosa as agent for finances and health, holds the home and savings in a joint revocable trust, and states in the trust that Dana's parents are not to be consulted on end-of-life decisions. When Dana had emergency surgery, the hospital turned to Rosa without question.

## What if your chosen family is not related to you?

Friends and chosen family inherit nothing by default. If you want a close friend to receive your home, your retirement account or your pet, the document must say so. Retirement accounts and life insurance pass by beneficiary form, not by your will, so check each form. A friend who inherits an IRA is generally a non-spouse beneficiary and has to empty it within 10 years under current rules.

If you have no spouse or children, think through who would serve as executor and agent. A trusted friend may be a better pick than a distant cousin. Our guide on [estate planning with no heirs or family nearby](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-with-no-heirs-or-family-nearby) covers this.

## A short checklist for this quarter

- Marriage certificate, parentage judgments and adoption decrees stored with your estate documents.
- Will or revocable trust that names the people you intend, with backups.
- Financial power of attorney and healthcare power of attorney with a HIPAA release.
- Beneficiary forms checked against your will, with spouse or partner names exact.
- Funeral and disposition instructions signed and shared.
- Copies placed with your agent and your attorney.
