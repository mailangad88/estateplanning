Most parents start with the same sentence: I want to treat them all the same. It sounds simple. Then the details arrive. One daughter lives next door and drives you to appointments. One son borrowed $60,000 for a business. A third child has a disability and will never earn much. Same amounts for all three starts to feel like a different kind of unfair.

This page helps you decide whether equal shares fit your family, how to adjust when they do not, and how to keep the plan from starting an argument. It sits within our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages). The rules on cutting a child out entirely are in separate articles for [Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-illinois) and [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-new-jersey).

## Does the law require you to treat your children equally?

No. Adult children have no automatic claim on a parent's estate in Illinois, New Jersey or most other states. If you die without a will, state law splits your estate among your children equally by default, but when you sign a will or a trust you can divide it any way you choose. The exception is a young child you have not provided for in some states, and a spouse, who has special rights discussed in our article on the [elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse).

So the question is not "must I?" but "should I?" That depends on facts only you know.

## When are equal shares the right choice?

Equal shares work well when your children are in similar circumstances, you have given each a similar start, and the family gets along. They also help in a quiet, practical way: no one needs to ask why they got less.

Equal is also easiest to administer. The executor divides what is there into equal parts, and the paperwork is short. If the estate is mostly cash and investments, equal shares cost little to set up. If the main asset is a house, equal shares can force a sale, which is a different problem covered below.

## When is equal not fair?

These are the situations that most often lead parents to depart from equal. Each has a common fix.

| Situation | Why equal can feel unfair | Common fix |
|---|---|---|
| One child has a disability or depends on benefits | An outright inheritance can end benefits | A special needs trust with a larger share |
| One child received large gifts or loans | The others start behind | Count the gift as an advance in your will |
| One child provided years of unpaid care | Others got the benefit of that care | Pay while alive, or leave a documented extra share |
| Children differ widely in income | One needs more help | Weight shares toward need, and explain |
| One child is bad with money, or has an addiction | A lump sum may disappear | A trust with a trustee and set payments |
| One child will run the family business | Splitting equally may break it up | Business to that child, balance with other assets |
| A child is estranged | Equal feels hollow to you | Smaller share or a reasoned omission |

A short example. Rosa, 72, has three children and $900,000: a $450,000 house, $300,000 in investments and a $150,000 IRA. Her son Marcus has a developmental disability and receives SSI and Medicaid. Her daughters Elena and Carla are financially secure. Splitting in thirds would leave Marcus with $300,000 outright and no benefits. Rosa's attorney suggests a special needs trust for Marcus with about $400,000, and $250,000 for each daughter. She writes both a plan and a letter. Marcus's trust will pay for what benefits do not cover. Equal in dollars would have hurt the child it was meant to protect. Our article on the [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) explains how it works.

## How do you account for gifts you already gave?

State it in writing, or the law assumes you did not mean it to count. A will that divides your estate equally does not adjust for the $60,000 you lent Ben in 2021, unless you say so. Many states treat lifetime gifts as advances against an inheritance only when a document says that, though the details vary. Read our piece on [a parent who gave money or a house to one child](/learn/after-a-death/parent-gave-money-or-a-house-to-one-child-before-death) for what happens when nobody did.

Do these three things:

1. **Keep a gift ledger.** A page listing the date, amount and purpose of each large gift or loan.
2. **Decide the status.** A gift, a loan to be repaid, or an advance that reduces a share. Write the choice down.
3. **Say it in the will.** For example: "My son Ben received $60,000 in 2021, which I intend as an advance against his share."

Forgiving a loan also deserves a line, as a debt owed to the estate is otherwise an asset the executor has to collect.

## What if one child cares for you?

Pay for care while you are alive. A caregiver agreement with a stated hourly rate, signed and dated, turns an informal expectation into a clean arrangement. Pay is taxable income for the child, so ask a tax adviser. Medicaid also looks hard at payments to family members, which our article on [Medicaid and the look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) addresses.

If you want to give the caregiver more at death, say why: "to my daughter Elena, an extra $50,000 in recognition of her care for me from 2022 to 2026." Reasons that read as thanks, not as a reward for influence, hold up better. Our piece on [estate planning when you are a family caregiver](/learn/basics/estate-planning-when-you-are-a-family-caregiver) covers the other side of the question.

## Why do the shares come out unequal even when you meant them equal?

Because the will often is not in control. Assets pass in several ways, and each follows its own rules.

- **Beneficiary forms.** A retirement account that names one child passes to that child alone, whatever the will says.
- **Joint accounts.** An account you share with one child usually belongs to that child at death.
- **Payable-on-death accounts and deeds.** They go straight to the named person.
- **The house.** It may be titled in one child's name, or be stuck in a will that leaves it to "my children" jointly.

Take a parent with a $600,000 estate and a $200,000 IRA that names only the oldest daughter. A will dividing the rest equally among three children would give the oldest $400,000 and the others $200,000 each. Our [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) and the article on [leaving unequal shares with beneficiary forms](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-unequal-shares-with-beneficiary-forms) show how to check. Our article on a [will that says split equally but the assets are not equal](/learn/what-if/will-says-split-equally-but-the-assets-are-not-equal) shows the same trap from the other direction.

## How do you keep an unequal plan from starting a fight?

Children rarely fight over the number. They fight over the surprise and the silence. Three habits help.

1. **Explain it.** A signed note in your own words, kept with the will, is better than nothing. A family meeting while you are healthy is better still.
2. **Use a neutral executor or trustee.** Naming the favored child as executor adds fuel. Consider a sibling who gets the same share as others, or a professional.
3. **Review it.** Circumstances change. The child who needed help in 2018 may be fine in 2026. Our [life stage planning](/learn/life-stages/updating-your-plan-when-your-family-grows) reminders can help.

Talk with a lawyer if your plan is unequal. Our guide to [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) covers the legal risks in more detail.
