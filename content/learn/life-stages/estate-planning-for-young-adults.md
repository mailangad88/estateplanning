The day a young person turns 18, something quiet but important happens: their parents stop being their legal decision makers. Doctors will not discuss a hospitalized college freshman with a worried mother. A bank will not let a father touch his daughter's account. Parents have no authority unless the 18-year-old has signed documents giving it to them.

This article covers the short set of papers worth signing at the start of adulthood, for the young adult or for a parent helping one. It is a different problem from the one older adults face. The question is not how to pass on wealth. It is who can act if you are hurt, sick or out of reach.

## What changes legally when you turn 18?

At 18 you are an adult in nearly every state, which means your parents lose automatic authority over your health care, records and money. Before that, a parent can consent to treatment, speak with the school nurse and sign forms. Afterward, the law treats you as capable of making your own choices, even if you are in an ICU and cannot.

The practical effects include:

- **Medical privacy.** Under the federal privacy rule known as HIPAA, a provider generally needs your written permission to share information with anyone, parents included.
- **Medical decisions.** If you cannot decide for yourself, some states allow a family member to consent under a default hierarchy, but the rules vary, and a parent is not always first.
- **Education records.** Under a federal law called FERPA, a college can refuse to discuss grades or billing with parents once you attend, unless you sign a release.
- **Money and contracts.** A parent cannot sign for an adult child on a lease, loan, tax return or bank matter without authority.

The options when no paperwork exists are poor. A parent may need to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator for an adult child. That process takes weeks or months, costs thousands of dollars and puts a young person's private health information in a court file.

## Which documents should a young adult sign first?

Three documents solve nearly all of these problems, and they cost little when prepared together.

### A healthcare power of attorney

A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney), also called a healthcare proxy, names a person, called an [agent](/glossary/agent), who can make medical decisions if you cannot. For most young adults the agent is a parent, though it could be a sibling, grandparent or partner. Name a backup agent in case the first is unreachable.

Your agent's authority generally takes effect only when a doctor determines you cannot make your own decisions. While you can speak for yourself, you stay in charge.

### A HIPAA authorization

A [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) is a separate, simpler form that lets named people receive your medical information. It is useful even when you are fully conscious, for example if a parent coordinates care after a surgery or mental health crisis. Without it, doctors may decline even to confirm you are a patient. Our guide explains why [families get shut out without one](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization), and some attorneys include the release inside the healthcare directive.

You can decide how broad it is. Some young adults release all information. Others limit it to certain providers or time periods.

### A durable financial power of attorney

A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets your agent handle money matters: pay tuition, deal with the bank, manage a student loan, file a tax return or end a lease. "Durable" means it stays valid if you become incapacitated, which is the situation where it matters most.

Young adults are often worried about handing a parent the keys to their finances. Two features address that:

- You can limit the powers to specific tasks, such as banking and taxes.
- You can use a springing version that only starts on incapacity, though these can slow things down when a bank asks for proof. Our article on [springing vs. immediate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) explains the tradeoff.

You can revoke the document at any time while you have capacity. If a parent has been managing money in an unhealthy way or you do not trust them, name another adult. See our guide to [choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent).

## What about a living will?

A [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) states your wishes about life support and other end-of-life treatment. Many attorneys include a short one in the young adult package, because a catastrophic accident can happen at any age, and a clear statement spares your family from guessing. It does not need to be elaborate. A few sentences about whether you want to be kept on machines if there is no reasonable chance of recovery is enough.

## Does an 18-year-old need a will?

Usually not yet. A will mainly directs who gets your property and who handles your estate. If you own little and are unmarried without children, state law gives your property to your parents, and most young adults would choose the same.

A will becomes worth the effort when any of these apply:

1. You own a car, savings, investments or a share of a business.
2. You have a partner who is not your spouse. [Unmarried partners inherit nothing by default](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples).
3. You want to leave specific items or money to a sibling, friend or charity.
4. You have a child. See our guide to [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents).
5. You hold digital assets of real value, such as cryptocurrency. See the [digital assets pillar](/learn/digital-assets).

Even when a will is not needed, check **beneficiary designations**. A first job usually brings a 401(k) and group life insurance, and the enrollment form asks for a beneficiary. Leaving it blank or naming a parent is fine for now, but update it after marriage or a child. Our [beneficiary designations pillar](/learn/beneficiary-designations) explains why those forms control.

## What about young adults with a disability?

Families of a young person with a disability face a harder choice at 18. Full guardianship removes decision-making rights and is a serious step. Alternatives include supported decision-making, a power of attorney, a healthcare proxy and a representative payee for benefits. If this is your situation, read our guide on [turning 18 with a disability](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) and the [special needs planning pillar](/learn/special-needs). The right answer depends on the person's actual capacity, not on a diagnosis.

Daniel, 18, has just started college six hours from home. His mother asks him to sign a healthcare power of attorney naming her, with his older brother as backup, plus a HIPAA release and a financial power of attorney limited to banking, school and tax matters. Daniel keeps the original and gives copies to both. Three months later he has an appendectomy over a weekend and the hospital calls his mother with an update because the paperwork is in his phone, ready to send.

## How should a young adult store and share these documents?

A power of attorney that no one can find is of little use. Follow these steps:

1. Sign the originals in front of the witnesses or notary your state requires.
2. Give each agent a copy, and tell them where the original is.
3. Keep a scan in a shared folder or password manager that a parent can access.
4. Carry a wallet card or phone note naming your healthcare agent.
5. Ask your college health center and bank whether they want their own forms on file.

A common problem is a financial institution that rejects an ordinary power of attorney. Our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) covers it, and it is a reason to prepare the document in advance rather than during a crisis.

## When should these documents be updated?

Review them after graduation, a move to a new state, a first full-time job, marriage, or a significant change in your relationship with an agent. State rules for powers of attorney differ, and while most states honor a document signed elsewhere, the safest practice is to redo them after a permanent move. The [update checklist](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists the full set of triggers, and our [core documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) shows what to add as life grows more complicated.

A young adult who signs these papers at 18 is not preparing to die. They are making sure the people who care most can help in a crisis without going to court.
