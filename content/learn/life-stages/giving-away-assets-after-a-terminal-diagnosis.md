When a doctor tells you that time is limited, the urge to act is strong. You want to hand the lake house to your daughter now, take your brother off the life insurance, or give each grandchild something while you can watch them open it. Some of those moves are generous and simple. A few quietly cost the people you are trying to help.

This page sorts the two. It assumes you already have the basics from our guide to [estate planning after a serious diagnosis](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-a-serious-diagnosis), such as a healthcare directive and a power of attorney, and focuses on the narrower question of what to give, change or leave alone.

## Can you still change your beneficiaries?

Yes, if you have capacity. Capacity is a legal test, not a medical label: you understand what you own, who your family is and what the change does. A diagnosis of cancer or heart failure does not remove it. Dementia can, and the test applies to the day you sign. Our page on [making a will when time is short](/learn/what-if/too-late-for-a-will) explains how courts look at that and why a doctor's note on the day helps.

Changing a beneficiary is mechanical. You request the company's form, sign it, return it and wait for written confirmation. Three points trip people up.

- **The change counts only when the company receives it.** Many policies and plans ignore a letter or a note in a drawer. Keep the confirmation.
- **A spouse may have to consent.** Workplace retirement plans generally require a spouse's signed waiver before someone else is named. See [does your spouse have to approve your beneficiary choices](/learn/beneficiary-designations/does-your-spouse-have-to-approve-your-beneficiary-choices).
- **An agent usually cannot do it for you.** A power of attorney agent is generally barred from changing beneficiaries unless the document says so, which is why [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) matters here.

If you want to remove or reduce someone, or add a person your family does not know, expect scrutiny later. Do it with an attorney, write down your reasons, and sign when you are at your best. Challenges based on [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) are the most common problem after a late change, and a documented, independent process is the best answer.

## Should you give assets away now?

Rarely in bulk. There are three costs, and each is invisible until after you die.

### You lose the step-up in basis

When you die, most assets your heirs inherit are revalued to their market value at that date. That is the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). A gift works the other way: the recipient takes your original cost. A house you bought for $90,000 that is worth $450,000 passes at death with a $450,000 basis, so a sale costs little in capital gains tax. Give it away today and your daughter inherits your $90,000 basis, with a taxable gain of $360,000 when she sells.

### You may owe a gift tax return

Gifts above the annual exclusion, $19,000 per recipient in 2026, require a gift tax return. You will almost certainly owe no tax, since the lifetime exemption is $15,000,000 per person as of 2026, but the return is a chore for the family. Our guide to the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) explains the rules.

### Medicaid can look back

If you might need long-term care, gifts made within the look-back period, 60 months in most states, can trigger a penalty period. A terminal diagnosis does not mean care is short. See [the Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) before you move large sums.

## What works better than a gift?

A comparison helps.

| Goal | Gift now | Beneficiary form, trust or will |
|---|---|---|
| Daughter gets the house | She takes your low basis and pays more tax | She gets the stepped-up basis |
| Grandchildren get money | Up to $19,000 each is simple | A trust can hold more and set ages |
| Keep control of costs | You lose the money | You can still spend what you need |
| Medicaid planning | Gifts can create a penalty | No look-back issue for assets still owned |
| Visible, emotional moment | You see it happen | You can write a letter and give small items now |

Small gifts are different. Giving a grandchild a ring, a watch or a few thousand dollars while you are present is often the best use of money, and it stays within the annual exclusion. Our page on [dividing personal property and family heirlooms](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms) shows how families handle sentimental items before arguments start.

## What should the first two weeks look like?

Most people do better with a short order of operations than with a long list.

1. Confirm your healthcare directive and power of attorney are signed and that your agents have copies.
2. Pull every beneficiary form: retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, payable-on-death bank accounts. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) lists what to check.
3. Decide whether any change is really needed. Many forms were right when you signed them.
4. Make the changes you chose, in writing, and keep confirmations in one folder.
5. Update your will or trust, and ask your attorney whether a letter explaining your choices is wise.
6. Only then think about gifts, and keep them small unless an attorney has run the tax and Medicaid numbers.

This sequence is a small part of the wider picture in our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages), which covers other moments when plans need to change.

## What if you own a business?

A diagnosis puts the business on a clock. Employees, customers, lenders and a spouse all have questions, and the answer cannot be "when I feel better." Pick the three roles that matter: who can sign checks and contracts if you cannot, who will run daily operations, and who will own the shares. A [durable power of attorney](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney) and a successor named in an operating or shareholder agreement cover the first. A buy-sell agreement or a sale covers the last. We explain the full path in [business owner incapacity planning](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning). Do not give shares to children as a shortcut. It triggers the same basis problem as other gifts, and it hands them control before anyone knows whether they want it.

## Should you tell the family first?

This is a personal call, and there is no right answer. Some people want the paperwork done before anyone starts offering opinions. Others call a meeting. Hannah, 67, was told she had about a year. She changed her IRA beneficiaries to split among three children instead of leaving it all to her oldest, who had been her helper. She told all three in the same room, with the reasons. Two years on, nobody has contested anything. The change itself was ordinary. The way it was handled was the protection.

If you cannot face a family meeting, a short letter left with your attorney does much of the same work. You can also pair it with a plan for your own services, such as [planning your funeral in advance](/learn/basics/plan-your-own-funeral-in-advance), so your family has fewer decisions to make during a hard week.
