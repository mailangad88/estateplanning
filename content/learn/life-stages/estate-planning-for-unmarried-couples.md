Married couples have a built-in legal safety net. A spouse inherits by default, may be first to make medical decisions, can often claim a share of the estate even if left out, and passes assets to the other free of estate tax. Unmarried couples have none of this. A partner of twenty years can be a legal stranger on the day it matters most.

That does not mean you need to marry to be protected. It means you need to write down what marriage would have supplied automatically. This guide explains the gaps and the documents that close them. If one of you has children from a prior relationship, our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families) applies as well. For the wider look at planning across your life, see [estate planning by life stage](/learn/life-stages).

## What rights do unmarried partners have by default?

In most states, an unmarried partner has no inheritance rights and limited medical authority. Intestacy statutes list spouses, children, parents and siblings, and nothing for a partner. If your partner dies without a will, their property goes to their relatives, even if you paid for the house.

The main gaps are:

- **Inheritance.** No automatic share of the estate, no elective share, and often no right to stay in a shared home owned by the other partner.
- **Medical decisions.** State default lists usually put a spouse first, then adult children, parents and siblings. A partner may appear late or not at all.
- **Hospital visitation and information.** Federal rules protect visitation designated by the patient, but privacy law can still block information without a release.
- **Funeral decisions.** Many states give this to next of kin in a fixed order.
- **Taxes.** The unlimited [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction) is not available. Transfers to a partner count against the lifetime exemption, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026.
- **Benefits.** Social Security survivor benefits and many pension survivor options go to spouses, and some employers cover partners only if they are registered.

Some states recognize common-law marriage, and some allow registered domestic partnerships or civil unions that carry spousal-like rights. A few states give rights to long-term partners under limited circumstances. Because the rules differ widely, check the guide for your state, such as [California](/estate-planning/california) or [Texas](/estate-planning/texas).

## Which documents does an unmarried couple need?

Most couples need six core documents, signed by each partner. Each one replaces something a spouse would receive automatically.

| Document | What it fixes |
|---|---|
| Will or revocable trust | Names your partner to receive your property, and your executor or trustee |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Lets your partner decide medical care when you cannot |
| HIPAA authorization | Allows doctors to share information with your partner |
| Durable financial power of attorney | Lets your partner pay bills and manage accounts if you are incapacitated |
| Beneficiary designations | Directs retirement accounts and life insurance outside the will |
| Funeral and remains instructions | Records who has authority and your wishes |

### Healthcare documents

A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) naming your partner puts them in a position to act with legal authority in a hospital. Pair it with a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization), and give copies to both of your primary doctors. Keep a copy where a partner can reach it from a phone. Do not assume your parents will step aside. If a parent disagrees with your partner, a written directive is what a hospital will follow. Visit our [healthcare directives pillar](/learn/healthcare-directives) for the full set of forms.

### Financial power of attorney

A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) is just as important. Joint accounts help, but a partner cannot sign for your individual accounts, file your taxes or sell a car titled in your name without one. Without it, family members or a court may decide who manages your money. Our [power of attorney pillar](/learn/power-of-attorney) explains the options.

### Will or revocable trust

A will leaving everything to your partner is the simplest approach. It must be valid and follow your state's signing rules. If you want to avoid court, or you own a home, a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can hold it and transfer to your partner at death without probate. A trust can also keep your property in a plan that a hostile relative cannot easily challenge, though no plan is immune from a will contest. See [how to make a will harder to contest](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) if you expect family conflict.

Because unmarried partners are the kind of beneficiary a family might challenge, document your reasons and make the plan clean: independent witnesses, no changes by your partner on your behalf, and a copy of your intent in your own words.

## How should unmarried couples own a home?

How you hold title decides what happens when one of you dies, and most unmarried couples have never decided it. The main options are:

- **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship.** The survivor automatically owns all of the property. It avoids probate, but each person can be reached by the other's creditors, and one co-owner can end the arrangement in some states by transferring their share.
- **Tenants in common.** Each owns a share, which each can leave through a will. This allows you to leave your half to someone other than your partner, and also allows your partner's heirs to become co-owners with you.
- **Trust ownership.** One or both partners place the house into a trust with instructions for the survivor and for the eventual beneficiaries, including a right to stay for life.
- **Sole ownership by one partner.** The other partner has no ownership rights, so they need a will or trust provision to stay in the home.

Caution about adding a partner to a deed: a transfer of half the house is a gift, and the recipient does not receive the step-up in basis on that half at the donor's death. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) and [the hidden risks of adding someone to your deed or account](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

Priya and Alex, both 41, bought a house together four years ago. Only Priya's name is on the deed because she had better credit. Alex has paid half the mortgage and the renovation costs. If Priya died without a will, the house would go to her mother and brother. Alex would have no legal claim beyond a possible lawsuit. A deed adding Alex or a trust naming him the beneficiary solves it in one afternoon.

## What about gifts and taxes between partners?

You can give your partner up to $19,000 each year in 2026 without a gift tax return, per recipient, under the [annual exclusion](/glossary/annual-exclusion). Larger gifts count against the lifetime exemption and require a return, though most people will not owe tax. See the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) article. Because the marital deduction is not available, a large bequest uses part of your exemption, which is rarely a problem at current levels but matters for large estates. Some states impose their own estate or inheritance taxes that apply at lower thresholds, and a few tax non-relatives at higher rates. Our [state taxes guide](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) lists how that works.

## How do beneficiary forms work for partners?

Beneficiary designations override your will, so name your partner, with a contingent beneficiary, on retirement accounts and life insurance. A partner who inherits an IRA is generally treated as a non-spouse beneficiary and typically must empty the account within 10 years, with no spousal rollover, unless the partner qualifies for an exception. Add payable-on-death designations to bank accounts; see [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) for how these work. Review the [beneficiary designations pillar](/learn/beneficiary-designations) if you have several accounts.

## Should unmarried couples consider a cohabitation agreement?

A cohabitation or property agreement can state who owns what, how shared expenses and the home are handled, and what happens if you separate. It does not replace estate planning, but it can reduce disputes with your partner's family. Many couples treat the agreement and the estate plan as a package.
