Moving into assisted living is not an ending, but it is a line in the sand. Someone else now helps with medications. A daughter has become the one who answers the phone. The house may be empty. The documents that were fine when you were 68 and hale may not suit the person you are at 84, and the people you named years ago may have moved, aged or died.

This page is a working list for that stage, one of many in our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages). It sits beside our guide on [estate planning for retirees](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-retirees), which covers the earlier years, and our page for people who have received [a serious diagnosis](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-a-serious-diagnosis). Pick whichever applies and use the other as a check.

## Why does a change in health call for a plan check?

A plan only works if the people in it can still do their jobs. Most estate plans are written once and filed. The agent you named at 60 may now be 80. Your sister in another state may no longer be the person who can reach you by afternoon. Your successor trustee may be unwell too.

Health changes also affect your own signing ability. A power of attorney needs a person who understands what they are signing. Many people lose that ability gradually, and a bank or a lawyer may be reluctant to accept a document signed late in a decline. That is why this page puts unfinished documents first.

## What should you do in the first month?

Work through the core documents in order, starting with the ones that fail hardest if missing.

1. Locate your durable financial power of attorney and healthcare power of attorney. If either is missing, sign new ones now.
2. Call each named agent and backup. Ask if they are still willing and able, and update the names if not.
3. Check the form of the documents. Some banks and facilities want a recent date or their own forms. See [bank refuses power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) for fixes.
4. Confirm your living will or POLST says what you want now, and that your doctor and the facility have copies.
5. Sign a HIPAA release naming your agents and children so they can talk with staff.
6. Update your address, phone and emergency contacts with banks, insurers, the pension office and your attorney.
7. Check the beneficiary forms on every IRA, annuity and life insurance policy. See our [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations).
8. Update your will or trust if family has changed since it was written.

For a simple map of decisions that others may have to make for you, look at the [types of power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-are-the-three-types-of-power-of-attorney). Our page on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) explains how to plan if memory is the concern.

## Who should sign the assisted living contract?

You should if you can, and an agent should if you cannot. The contract matters because it can make someone personally responsible for payment. A resident or an agent acting under a power of attorney should be listed as signing in a representative capacity, not as a personal guarantor. Federal law bars nursing homes that take Medicare or Medicaid from requiring a third-party payment promise, but rules for assisted living are set by states and are less uniform. Ask a lawyer to read the document before signing, and look at the fees, the notice for moving someone out and what happens when care needs rise.

An agent can usually sign for you if your power of attorney or healthcare directive gives that authority, which is covered in [whether a healthcare agent can sign a nursing home admission](/learn/healthcare-directives/can-a-healthcare-agent-sign-nursing-home-admission). For costs and who pays, read [assisted living, memory care and hospice: who pays](/learn/elder-care/assisted-living-memory-care-and-hospice-who-pays).

## What should you do about the house?

Decide with a calm head and a plan for care costs, not under pressure from a facility or a relative. The house is often the largest asset and the one most affected by a move.

| Option | Typical effect | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Keep it and rent it out | Income, possible step-up at death | Landlord duties, insurance, repairs |
| Sell it | Cash to pay for care, capital gains rules apply | Large proceeds may count for Medicaid |
| Transfer it to a child | Looks simple | Gift tax filing, loss of step-up, Medicaid penalty period |
| Place it in a trust | Control and probate avoidance | An irrevocable trust must be set up well before it is needed |
| Leave it vacant | No action | Insurance often limits coverage on empty homes |

Medicaid reviews transfers made in the 60 months before an application in most states, and a gift can create a period when benefits are denied. Use the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) to see what your timeline looks like, and read our page on the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period). Our guide on [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) goes further.

Henry, 82, moved into assisted living in March. His daughter Ines suggested he deed the house to her so it would be protected. A call to an elder law attorney changed the plan. The deed would have started a penalty period if Henry later needed Medicaid, and Ines would have lost the step-up in basis on the house. Henry kept title, named Ines as agent under an updated power of attorney, and met with the attorney again to plan for care costs.

## How do you simplify without weakening the plan?

Simplify things your agents have to deal with and keep the legal protections. Consolidate small bank accounts. Close dormant ones. Write a list of what you own, where it is and how to log in, and a plain list of monthly bills. Update the inventory when you move, because a household that shrinks into one room often forgets what is in storage.

Sentimental belongings deserve a decision too. Give items away while you can enjoy it. Use a list or [guidance on dividing personal property and family heirlooms](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms) to avoid quarrels later.

## What about funeral planning?

Make wishes known and consider pre-paying only after careful reading. A pre-need contract may be backed by a trust or by an insurance policy, and the protections differ by state and seller. Ask whether money is refundable, whether it follows you if you move, and what happens if the provider goes out of business. See [planning your own funeral in advance](/learn/basics/plan-your-own-funeral-in-advance) for options that do not tie up funds.

## What if time is short?

Do not wait for a perfect plan. A signed healthcare power of attorney and a financial power of attorney today are worth more than a complete trust next spring.

If you are in hospice or very ill, tell the attorney at once. Documents can be signed quickly at a bedside with witnesses and a notary. Our page on [what happens if it is too late for a will](/learn/what-if/too-late-for-a-will) explains what remains possible and what does not.
