The first baby usually pushes a couple to sign a will. After that, life keeps moving and the documents stay in a drawer. A second child arrives. The oldest turns 18 and leaves for college. A daughter gets married. A grandchild is born. Each of these quietly changes what the paperwork ought to say.

This article takes the common family milestones one at a time and tells you what to check at each. For the whole range of milestones, start at our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages). For the first round of planning, read [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents). For the general rule about how often to review, see [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## A quick map of what changes

Use this table to find the milestone you are at.

| Milestone | What to check first | Document most affected |
|---|---|---|
| Another child is born | Guardian choice, trust shares, insurance amount | Will, life insurance. |
| A child turns 18 | Adult-child authority, trust ages | Healthcare directive, power of attorney. |
| A child marries | Trustee and agent roles, inheritance protection | Trust, will. |
| A grandchild is born | How descendants share, 529 plans | Will, trust, beneficiary forms. |
| A child is born with a disability | Benefits eligibility, trust structure | Trust, will. |

The sections below take each row in turn.

## What happens if I have another child after I sign my will?

In most states the new child is not left out. Laws on "pretermitted" children, meaning children born or adopted after the will was signed, generally give that child a share, often equal to what they would have received had you died without a will. The rules differ in detail, and some apply only when the will makes no provision for later children at all.

That default is not a plan. Say your will leaves everything to your spouse, then to your two children in equal shares, held in trust until each is 25. A third child arrives. The state rule might hand the youngest a statutory share outright, and if that child is a baby, a court may supervise it. Meanwhile your chosen trustee has no instructions for the new child at all.

Sit down within a few months of the birth and check four things:

1. Is the same guardian still right for three children? A sister who happily takes two kids may feel differently about three.
2. Does the will or trust give the new child an equal share, held on the same terms?
3. Is the life insurance enough? Another child usually means more years of dependence.
4. Do the beneficiary forms name a trust or adult custodian, not the child?

If your will is short, a [codicil](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) can add the child. When the guardian, trustee and trust terms all change, a fresh will is usually cleaner.

## Should I change beneficiary forms after a birth?

You should look at them. You should not simply write the baby's name in. Insurers and retirement plans pay what the form says, and a form that names a minor is the classic route into a court-supervised account.

For a parent whose spouse is the primary beneficiary, the contingent beneficiary matters: it is who receives the money if the spouse is gone too. Naming a trust for the children as contingent solves most of the problem. Our guide to [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) covers the mechanics, and [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) explains the backup layer. A term policy bought for the first child often needs a top-up; if you are weighing that, the tool above gives a rough starting number.

## What changes when a child turns 18?

The law treats your child as an adult overnight, and that has consequences you may not expect. A hospital will not discuss a 19-year-old college student's condition with a parent without the student's written permission. A parent cannot pay a young adult's bills from their account or see a student record. The article on [what happens when an adult child has a medical emergency and no HIPAA form](/learn/what-if/adult-child-medical-emergency-no-hipaa) shows how stuck a family can get.

So the fix is not for your will but for your child's paperwork: a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization), a healthcare power of attorney and a durable financial power of attorney, each naming you or the other parent. Most families do this in one afternoon before the first semester. Our [estate planning for young adults](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults) page is written for the child, and you can hand it over.

For your own plan, the 18th birthday changes one thing. If you left property to your children outright at majority, 18 is now the age at which it could arrive. That is rarely what a parent wants. Decide whether a trust should hold the money until 25 or 30, and revisit the question if the child is not financially ready.

## Does my child's wedding change my plan?

Not automatically, and your will does not need to change because a child marries. A child's spouse is not your heir. What a wedding changes is the risk around the money a child will one day inherit from you.

If an inheritance lands in a child's name alone and stays separate, it is generally protected in a divorce in most states. Mixed into a joint account or used to buy a shared house, it may not be. Parents who want the money to stay in the bloodline often leave it in a lifetime trust for the child rather than outright. See [protecting children's inheritance in a divorce](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) for the approach, which applies to first marriages too.

It also changes the cast of characters. If you named your daughter as your agent and her husband as backup, ask whether you still want that. If a new son-in-law will be your trustee's contact, make sure the relationship is one you want.

## What should I do when a grandchild is born?

You do not need to rewrite your plan in a hurry. You do need to decide how you want the next generation to be treated, because the default can surprise you. Under many wills, a grandchild inherits only when their own parent has died, and then through a clause such as per stirpes. Otherwise the grandchild gets nothing.

If you want to give them something, there are several routes, from a 529 plan to a trust to a named bequest. We cover them in [leaving an inheritance to grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/leaving-an-inheritance-to-grandchildren). Someone with many grandchildren should also look at how to describe them, such as "all of my grandchildren living at my death", so a late arrival is not argued about.

## What if a child is born with a disability?

This is the one milestone where a delay costs real money. A child who will rely on SSI or Medicaid can lose eligibility if they receive an inheritance directly, and that includes anything you or a grandparent leaves in a will. Update your own plan and tell relatives. The [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) page explains the structure, and the [special needs pillar](/learn/special-needs) lists the related steps.

## How state law changes this

States differ on pretermitted children, on the age at which a minor takes property, and on whether a parent's will or a trust can hold money past 18 or 21. Check the rules in your own [state guide](/estate-planning/california) as one example, and ask an attorney before you rely on a default.
