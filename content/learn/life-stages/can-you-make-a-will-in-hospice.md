A parent or spouse has weeks, or days, and there is no will. Someone asks whether it is too late, whether the medication matters, whether the nurse can sign. The short answer is that it is rarely too late, and what matters is what the person understands and how the signing is done.

This page explains the legal test, the signing rules in Illinois and New Jersey, what to do when time is short and which other documents to sign at the same visit. It is part of our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages).

## Can you make a will in hospice?

Yes. Hospice is a type of care, not a legal status. A person can make a will there as long as they have what the law calls testamentary capacity: they know they are making a will, they understand roughly what they own, they know who their closest family members are, and they can say who should receive their property. Courts apply that test at the moment of signing. A person can be gravely ill and still meet it.

Medication does not decide the question. Morphine or other pain medicine can cloud thinking, but many hospice patients have clear hours, usually in the morning or after a rested night. Schedule the signing then, and ask the care team when the person is usually most alert.

## What does a valid will need in Illinois and New Jersey?

A signed paper document and two witnesses. The details differ a little, and our pages on [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) and [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) lay them out.

| Requirement | What to plan for |
|---|---|
| Writing | A typed or printed will is the norm. Spoken wills are not a safe path |
| Signature | The person signs, or signs with a mark, or directs someone to sign in their presence |
| Witnesses | Two adults who watch the signing and then sign themselves |
| Beneficiaries as witnesses | Avoid them; use disinterested adults |
| Self-proving affidavit | A notarized statement from the witnesses, which lets the court accept the will without calling them |
| Capacity | A short written note by the attorney about the conversation helps later |

Spoken wishes are the biggest trap. Families often say, "Mom told the nurse she wanted Karen to have the house." That is not a will. Our page on [oral, military, statutory and international wills](/learn/wills/oral-military-statutory-and-international-wills) explains the narrow exceptions, and neither state's ordinary rules would treat a bedside comment as a valid will. Notes and letters are similar. See [do notes and spoken wishes bind your family](/learn/wills/notes-letters-and-spoken-wishes-do-they-bind-your-family).

## How fast can a will be signed?

Faster than most people think. A simple will leaving everything to a spouse or splitting it among children can be drafted in a day if an attorney has the facts. What helps most is a short list: full names and addresses of the person and the family, a rough list of assets, who should be executor and a backup, and who should receive the property. A will that says only that is enough.

1. **Call an attorney today** and say plainly that the person is in hospice. Many will come to the bedside.
2. **Send the facts** listed above, plus deeds and beneficiary forms if available.
3. **Decide who will be present.** The person, the attorney, two witnesses and a notary if the self-proving affidavit will be signed.
4. **Ask the care team** for the best time of day and permission to use the room.
5. **At the signing,** the attorney asks the person simple questions in their own words. The answers show capacity better than any statement afterward.
6. **Store the original** where the executor can find it, and give the attorney a copy.

If a long document is not possible, a short will is enough. Elaborate trusts and tax planning can wait, or may not be worth doing at all.

## Who should be in the room, and who should not?

Anyone who stands to gain should leave. The attorney meets the person alone for the key conversation, which avoids a later claim of pressure. Our page on [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure) describes what courts look at when a will is challenged.

Example only. Walter, 79, in hospice at home, wants to leave his house to his daughter Priya and divide the rest between Priya and his son Dev. Priya organizes the visit, so the attorney asks her to step out after introductions. Walter explains his wishes alone, the attorney reads the will aloud, and two neighbors witness. Dev later challenges. The attorney's notes, the neighbors' testimony and a physician's note from that day give the court what it needs.

The gifts in a hospice will often favor the child who provides care. That is not unlawful, but it draws attention. A short sentence in the will, stating why, can help.

## What else should be signed the same day?

The will is only one document, and often not the most useful. Add these.

- **A financial power of attorney.** It lets someone pay bills and manage accounts while the person is alive but too weak to do it. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
- **A health care power of attorney.** The hospice team needs someone to speak for the patient when they cannot. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [comfort care orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/palliative-care-hospice-and-comfort-care-orders).
- **Beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts and life insurance ignore the will. Check and update them. See [the beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit).
- **A funeral and burial instruction.** Put the wishes in writing and name who decides.

Property that has no beneficiary and is held alone will go through probate under the will. Your attorney can talk about whether a transfer on death deed or a small estate procedure fits. See the pages on [Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) and [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) small estate affidavits.

## What should the family not do?

Do not record a video of the person "saying" the will and treat it as the will. Do not have a relative type something up from a web template and bring it to the bed to be signed without a lawyer's check. Do not ask the hospice nurse to certify capacity for you; a nurse's role is care, and a physician's short note or the attorney's own notes serve better. And do not wait for a perfect moment. Families lose weeks deciding whether to bring it up with a dying parent, then find the person can no longer follow a conversation.

If you are the one in hospice, say it directly: you want your wishes written down so the family does not have to guess. Most children are relieved, not hurt, to hear it.

## What if it is already too late?

If the person has died or can no longer understand what is happening, no one can sign a will for them. A power of attorney does not allow an agent to make a will for the principal. The estate then passes under the intestacy rules. Those rules are covered in [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) and in [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey). The family can still agree among themselves to share things differently once an estate is open, and heirs may use a disclaimer or a family settlement. It is slower and not certain.
