You are 24, you have a job, a car you are still paying off and a loan balance that makes you wince. Someone says you should have a will, and it sounds like something for people with houses and grandchildren. Or you are 17 and wondering whether you can sign one early. This page answers the questions young adults actually ask, in the order they usually come up.

The short answer is that a will is rarely the first document a person in their twenties needs. The paperwork that decides what happens to your money at 26 is mostly sitting in your employer's benefits portal.

## What age can you legally make a will?

The minimum age is 18 in almost every state. A handful of states carve out exceptions for a minor who is married, serving in the armed forces or declared emancipated by a court. Those rules differ enough that you should not assume one applies to you.

Age is only half of the test. You also need what lawyers call [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity): you understand that you are signing a will, you roughly know what you own, and you know who your close family members are. A healthy 19-year-old clears that bar easily. The will also has to be signed and witnessed the way your state requires, which our guide to [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) walks through.

## Can a 17-year-old make a will?

In most states, no. A will signed before the legal age is invalid even if everything else is done properly. If you die at 17 without a valid will, state law decides who inherits, and that usually means your parents.

So what should a 17-year-old do? Almost nothing, honestly. If you have a trust fund, a settlement or an account that a relative opened for you, ask whoever manages it who the successor owner or beneficiary is. Then book a short appointment for the month you turn 18. That is also when parents lose automatic access to your medical and financial information, which we cover in [estate planning at 18](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults).

## Do you need a will when you get your first job?

You need the forms, and the will can come second. Here is what a first job puts in front of you and what each item does.

| Thing you now have | Who gets it | What controls that |
|---|---|---|
| 401(k) or similar retirement plan | The person on your beneficiary form | The form, not your will |
| Group life insurance through work | The person on your beneficiary form | The form |
| Bank account | Whoever is named as payable-on-death, otherwise your estate | The account paperwork, then your will or state law |
| Car, laptop, belongings | Your estate | Your will, or state law if you have none |
| Phone, email, social accounts | Depends on the company and your instructions | Terms of service and what you leave behind |

Many employers set up a default beneficiary or leave the field blank, and a blank field can send the money into your estate. If you are married, federal rules generally make your spouse the beneficiary of a workplace 401(k) unless the spouse signs a waiver. If you are single, name someone. Our guide to [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) explains the choices, and the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you check every account in one pass.

Take Dani, 25, who started a job last spring. She named her mother on her 401(k) form on day one and forgot about it. Two years later she is engaged and her partner would be the natural choice. If she never updates the form, and the plan does not require her spouse by law until they are married, her mother still gets the money. That is not a disaster. It is just not a decision Dani made on purpose.

## What happens to your student loans when you die?

Your family does not inherit your debt. Federal student loans are discharged when the borrower dies, meaning the balance is canceled once the servicer has proof of death. Parent PLUS loans are discharged if either the parent borrower or the student dies.

Private student loans are a different animal. Some lenders cancel the balance at death and many do not. If there is no cancellation, the lender can make a claim against your estate, which means it is paid out of whatever you left before heirs receive anything. A cosigner is the real exposure, because the cosigner signed a contract and may be asked to pay. Check your loan agreement for a death clause and ask your cosigner to do the same.

Debts rarely reach family members who did not sign, and in a few community property states a spouse can be affected by debts taken on during the marriage. Our page on [what happens to a mortgage, car loan and student loans after death](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-car-loan-and-student-loans-after-death) covers the order in which debts are paid.

## Do you need an estate plan if you rent and own very little?

Yes, a light one. Renting does not mean you have nothing, and our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages) shows how the list grows as you do. You probably have a bank account, a vehicle, a retirement balance, a phone full of photos and maybe a pet. Those things need an owner after you are gone, and without paperwork someone has to go to court to get authority over them.

For most people under 30 a workable starter plan is a short list of documents and forms:

1. A healthcare power of attorney, so a person you picked can talk to doctors.
2. A HIPAA authorization, so hospitals will actually speak to that person.
3. A durable financial power of attorney, so someone can pay your rent and loans if you are in the hospital.
4. A simple will once you own anything worth directing, or sooner if you have a partner, a child or a pet.
5. Beneficiary designations on every account that allows them.

Our broader page on [whether you need an estate plan with few assets](/learn/basics/do-i-need-an-estate-plan-with-few-assets) explains what a basic plan costs and when it is enough. When you are not sure where you stand, the [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) takes a few minutes.

## Who inherits if you die young without a will?

State law fills the gap through [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession). For someone in their twenties with no spouse and no children, the usual order is:

1. Your parents, equally if both are living.
2. Your siblings, if neither parent survives.
3. More distant relatives, such as grandparents, aunts and uncles.

A partner you live with, however long, receives nothing by default. That is the part that surprises people. If you have a partner, a will or a beneficiary form is the only way to include them. The page on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) shows the full order for each family shape.

There is a second cost people overlook. When two grieving parents inherit your car and your savings account, they also have to open an estate to claim them, sometimes with an estranged sibling or a former partner involved. A will naming one person as executor spares them that guessing game.

## What about your phone, email and social media?

Treat your phone passcode like a key to the house. Without it, your family cannot reach your photos, your two-factor codes or your accounts, and the companies are not obliged to open them. Write down how to unlock your phone and where your password manager lives, and give that note to someone you trust. Naming a person for your digital accounts in your will, as covered in [what happens to social media after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death), gives that person legal footing.

## When should you update what you sign?

Anything big resets the list: marriage, a child, a first home, a serious relationship where you share finances, a career change that brings new benefits. Until then, check your beneficiary forms once a year when benefits enrollment opens. It takes ten minutes.
