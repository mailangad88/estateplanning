A diagnosis can turn a week upside down. Between appointments and test results, legal paperwork may be the last thing you want to think about. Yet the documents in this article are the ones that let you stay in charge of your own life for as long as possible, and that spare the people you love from guessing.

This page leads with the checklist, then explains each piece. You do not have to do everything in a week. If you only do the first three things, you will have covered the situations that cause families the most trouble. For the short life-event summary, see [estate planning after a serious diagnosis](/life-events/serious-diagnosis). This longer article goes into signing logistics, capacity, benefits and how to talk with your family.

## What should you sign first?

Start with three documents that protect you while you are alive, then move to what happens to your property. Most people can finish the first group in one or two meetings.

| This week | Durable financial power of attorney | Someone can pay bills, deal with insurance and manage accounts | [ ] |

### The healthcare directive and agent

A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) names the person who makes medical decisions if you cannot, and a [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) records the treatments you do and do not want. Many states combine them into one advance directive. Choose the agent for steadiness, not for closeness. The person who cries easily may be the person you love most, and still not the one who can say no to a surgeon. Talk to them first about what matters to you, such as being at home, avoiding long stays on a ventilator or continuing treatment as long as there is a chance of recovery.

If your illness is advanced, ask your doctor about a [POLST or similar medical order](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst). It is a signed medical order, not an estate planning document, and it tells emergency workers what to do.

### The financial power of attorney

A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) names an agent to handle money if you cannot. The most useful versions take effect immediately, because a document that requires two doctors to certify incapacity can stall at the moment it is needed. Our article on [springing vs. immediate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) explains the tradeoff. Be careful with the choice of agent, since the role carries real power over your money. [Choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers the questions to ask.

A married person sometimes assumes the spouse can "just handle it." That works only for joint accounts. Anything in your name alone, including a retirement account or a car, may need your signature or an agent's.

### The will or trust

If your will is old, it may name an ex-spouse, a deceased executor or children who were minors then. Update it. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can also let a successor trustee take over your finances without court involvement if you cannot manage them, and it avoids probate for assets you have placed inside it. Funding takes some effort, so ask your attorney which assets to retitle first, and read how to [fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust). If you have a short time frame, a simple will plus a durable power of attorney is often better than an unfinished trust.

## What about my memory or thinking?

If your diagnosis may affect memory, judgment or attention, move early, because the legal threshold is capacity on the day you sign. For a will, the usual standard is that you understand in general terms what you own, who your family members are and what the document will do. A power of attorney or a trust may require a somewhat higher level of understanding, and the standard varies by state.

Capacity is not all or nothing. Many people with early dementia, or with treatment-related fatigue, have good hours and bad hours. An attorney will often meet you at your best time of day, talk with you privately, ask open questions rather than yes or no ones, and note the conversation. Some will ask for a short letter from your physician, or arrange for a witness who knows you. These steps do not make a document untouchable, but they make it much harder to challenge later, a topic covered in [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

Our article on [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) goes into the timing question in more detail. If you are a family member reading this for someone whose capacity has already gone, see [what to do when a parent has dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

## How can I sign when I feel unwell?

You do not need to sit through a long office meeting. Ask the attorney what the firm offers. Options sometimes include:

- Shorter meetings spread over several days, with documents drafted in advance.
- A signing at your home, or at a hospital or care facility, with the required witnesses and a notary present.
- Remote signing and remote notarization, where your state allows them.
- A signing between treatments, scheduled around your best hours.

State rules decide the details. Some states require two witnesses for a will, some require a notary for certain documents, and the rules for healthcare directives differ. A hospital will often have its own advance directive form and a social worker who can help complete it. Our guide on [whether a living will needs a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary) lists the common requirements. Our attorneys can tell you the rules where you live and will not promise a specific turnaround time before they have seen your situation.

## How does a diagnosis affect benefits and long-term care?

It can, if you may need ongoing care or already rely on disability benefits. SSI and Medicaid have strict asset limits. Medicaid long-term care coverage in most states looks back 60 months at gifts and transfers, and a gift made in that window can create a penalty period. That means giving away assets in a rush, or adding children to a deed, can backfire. See the [Medicaid look-back explainer](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) and the overview of [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) before you move anything.

If you have a spouse, rules protect some assets and income for the healthy spouse, and an attorney can explain how. Benefits also vary: Social Security Disability Insurance, employer disability coverage, VA benefits and life insurance accelerated death benefits each have their own rules. Gather those policies in one folder.

## How do I talk to my family?

Say what you are doing and why, and make clear that it is about being prepared, not about giving up. Many people find a short script helps:

> "I want to get a few legal papers in order so no one has to guess what I want. I've chosen you to speak for me if I can't. Here is where the documents are. I'd like to tell you what matters to me."

Tell the agents first, then the wider family. Give each agent a copy of the documents and the contact number of your attorney. If a family member is likely to object to a choice, a short conversation now, or a letter of explanation, can save conflict later. Our article on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) has suggestions, and a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) lets you add things the legal documents cannot, such as funeral wishes and messages.

## A worked example

Daniel, 61, learned he has early-stage Parkinson's disease. He is married to Marcus, owns a home jointly and has an IRA that still names his late mother as beneficiary. His doctor tells him he is likely to stay independent for years, but he wants his plan in place before that changes.

In week one he signs a healthcare directive naming Marcus as agent and his sister Elena as backup, and a durable power of attorney naming Marcus with Elena as successor. In week three he updates the IRA beneficiary to Marcus, with Elena as contingent, and signs a new will. He asks a bank officer to place a copy of the power of attorney in his file so the bank recognizes it later. He then writes a one-page list of accounts and the location of papers. Years later, when Daniel needs help with finances, nobody needs to go to court.

This is one person's choices; yours may differ.

## If time is short, what can an attorney do first?

An attorney can often prepare the most important documents quickly, sometimes in days, starting with the powers of attorney and a simple will. A short plan signed now is generally better than a comprehensive one that is never finished. If you cannot sign, because capacity is gone, the focus shifts to what family members can do, which may include a guardianship or conservatorship proceeding. Our comparison of [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains the difference.
