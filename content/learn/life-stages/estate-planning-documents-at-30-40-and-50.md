Nobody wakes up on a birthday and needs a will. What happens instead is that life adds things: a partner, a mortgage, a child, a retirement account, a parent who needs help. Each one makes a gap in your plan more costly.

So the useful question is not "what age am I" but "what is in my life now." Still, ages are a handy shorthand, because people tend to collect responsibilities in a similar order. This guide walks through the 30s, 40s and 50s and names the documents that fit each. Our [documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) describes each document in more detail, and the [life stages section](/learn/life-stages) has pages for specific events.

## What does everyone need, whatever their age?

Three documents protect you while you are alive, and they matter as much as a will.

1. **Durable financial power of attorney.** Names someone to pay bills and manage accounts if you cannot. Without it, your family may need a court-supervised guardianship. See [durable powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
2. **Healthcare power of attorney and living will.** Names a person to make medical decisions and records your wishes. Our guide to [healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives) covers both.
3. **HIPAA authorization.** Lets doctors speak with the people you name. Adult children and partners are often turned away at the hospital door without one.

Add current beneficiary designations on every retirement account, life insurance policy and bank account with a payable-on-death feature. These override your will. A [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) takes ten minutes and prevents more family disputes than any other single step.

## What should you have at 30?

At thirty, you probably have a job, maybe a partner, maybe a first home, and perhaps a child on the way. You have more to lose from an accident than from death.

| Situation | What to add |
|---|---|
| Single, renting, no children | The three core documents and beneficiary forms; a simple will if you want particular people to have particular things |
| Partnered but unmarried | A will or trust naming your partner, since intestacy usually ignores them; see [unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) |
| Married | A will, mutual powers of attorney, and a check that each spouse names the other as beneficiary |
| New home | Think about how title is held and whether a trust helps; see [buying a home](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-when-you-buy-a-home) |
| Expecting a child | A guardian named in a will, and term life insurance; see [new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) |

Student loans and credit card debt do not change the need for documents. Debts are generally paid from the estate first, but most federal student loans are discharged on death, and private loans depend on the contract and any co-signer. The loan servicer can tell you which applies to you.

## What should you have at 40?

The forties are the decade in which the stakes feel real. You have children in school, a mortgage, a retirement account that has grown, perhaps a business. If both parents died tomorrow, who would raise the children and who would manage their money?

- **A will naming a guardian.** Without one, a court decides. Read how to [choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) and name an alternate.
- **Life insurance.** Term coverage sized to replace income and pay off the mortgage until the children are independent. The [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) gives a starting figure.
- **A trust for the children's share.** A child who inherits outright at 18 has full control of the money. A trust lets you set the age and the rules. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).
- **A revocable living trust.** Often worth considering if you own a home, especially in a state where probate is slow or costly. It is not required for everyone; our page on [whether you need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) lays out the tradeoffs.
- **Business documents.** If you own a business, you need a plan for its continuation, which a will alone rarely supplies.

Marisol, 41, has two children and a husband, Dev. They each wrote a will naming the other, and each named Marisol's sister as guardian. But her old employer life insurance policy still names her mother as beneficiary, a form she filled out at 26. That form, not the will, decides who gets the money. A single afternoon of paperwork fixes it.

## What should you have at 50?

The fifties bring different questions. The children may be adults, retirement is within sight, and parents may need help.

- **Review the will or trust.** Executors and trustees named in your thirties may now be unsuitable. Guardians for minors are no longer needed, but you may want to name a guardian for an adult child with a disability. See [special needs planning](/learn/special-needs).
- **Retirement accounts.** The balance may be your largest asset. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years, so the plan for who inherits and how matters. Our page on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) explains the rules.
- **Your parents' plans.** If a parent has no documents, the cost of that falls on you. A conversation now beats a court case later. See [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan).
- **Long-term care.** Insurance is often cheaper when purchased in the fifties than in the sixties, and a wait can mean being uninsurable. Compare it against self-funding in our guide to [long-term care insurance](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance).
- **A list of what you own.** Accounts, passwords, insurance policies and advisers. Your executor will need it. See [how to inventory your assets](/learn/basics/how-to-inventory-your-assets-and-value-your-estate).

## Is it ever too early or too late?

Too early is rare. A document signed at 28 and revised at 35 costs little, and it covers the years in between. Too late is common, and it usually means the loss of capacity or a death before signing. A plan made at 55 is far better than none, but a plan made at 45 gives you ten more years of protection and a chance to adjust the details while everyone is healthy and agreeable. If you have been putting it off, start with the three core documents and add the rest as the pieces of your life arrive.

## How often should you revisit it?

Whenever something changes, and otherwise every three to five years. The events that trigger a review are the same at any age: marriage, divorce, a birth, a death in the family, a new home, a move to another state, a new business, a large gain in wealth or a serious diagnosis. Our guide on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) gives the full list.

## What are the common mistakes at each stage?

- **In your thirties:** assuming that you own too little to matter, and forgetting that the plan protects you in an emergency, not only after death.
- **In your forties:** leaving guardians unnamed, and leaving an ex-spouse or a parent on old beneficiary forms.
- **In your fifties:** leaving everything to adult children outright when one has creditor or marriage risk, and ignoring the question of who will manage your affairs if you decline.

The federal estate tax is not a concern for most people at any of these ages. The basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so a plan for most families focuses on control, guardianship and avoiding court rather than tax. Our page on the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) explains where tax planning begins.
