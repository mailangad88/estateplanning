A baby arrives, and a coworker says you need a million dollars of life insurance. Another says buy a policy that covers the mortgage. A third says skip it and invest the difference. You need an answer built from your own numbers, not a slogan.

This page gives you a formula you can run in ten minutes, a way to choose the length of the policy, a comparison of term and mortgage insurance, and the beneficiary choices that decide whether the money reaches your child. It builds on our guide to the [documents new parents should sign first](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents), which covers the whole plan, and it fits within our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages).

## How much term life insurance do you need with a baby?

Enough to pay off what you owe and to replace your income for as long as your child depends on you. Work through five lines. Debts means the mortgage and any loans. Income replacement means your yearly income times the number of years your family would need help, often until your youngest turns 18 or 22. Education is the amount you would want available. Final expenses and a short cushion round out the list. Then subtract savings, existing life insurance and the death benefit from work.

Here is an example, with invented numbers. Priya and Sam have a baby. Sam earns $85,000 and Priya earns $60,000.

| Line | Amount for Sam's policy |
|---|---|
| Mortgage balance | $280,000 |
| Car and student loans | $15,000 |
| Income replacement: 10 years at $85,000 | $850,000 |
| Education fund goal | $120,000 |
| Subtotal | $1,265,000 |
| Less: employer life policy | -$90,000 |
| Less: savings set aside for this | -$60,000 |
| Coverage to buy | about $1,115,000 |

Ten years of income is not a rule. A shorter period fits a family with a lot of savings or a partner who earns well. A longer period fits a family with several children. Our [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) lets you change each line.

## How long should the term be?

As long as the longest obligation your death would leave behind. Three dates compete: the day the mortgage is paid off, the day your youngest child finishes school, and the day your spouse could retire. A 30-year term bought at 32 covers a baby until the child is 30, which is longer than most families need. A 20-year term from the same date covers the child through college and into independence, and costs less.

Some families buy two policies, a 30-year and a 15-year, so that coverage steps down as obligations shrink. That works if you are comfortable managing two policies and two beneficiary forms. A single level policy is simpler, and simple is worth something when you are sleeping four hours a night.

Ask about conversion. A convertible term policy lets you switch to permanent insurance without a new health exam, which helps if your health changes while the policy is in force.

## Term life or mortgage life insurance?

Term is better for most families because it pays your family, and mortgage life insurance pays the lender. A mortgage policy ties the benefit to the loan balance, which falls over time while your premium stays the same. Your family could not use the money for anything else if they wanted to, such as keeping the house and paying child care first.

| Feature | Term life | Mortgage life |
|---|---|---|
| Who gets the money | Your named beneficiary | The lender |
| Benefit over time | Level | Usually shrinks with the balance |
| Can be used for anything | Yes | No, it pays the loan |
| Requires health check | Usually | Sometimes simplified |
| Tied to this house | No | Yes |

A mortgage policy can make sense if you cannot qualify for term coverage because of a health condition. Otherwise buy term and let your spouse decide whether to pay off the loan. You can also read how a mortgage is treated at death in [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/blog/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-when-the-owner-dies).

## Does the stay-at-home parent need life insurance?

Yes. Imagine Priya stays home with the baby instead of working. If she died, Sam would pay for child care, possibly cut his hours and hire help for work Priya did unpaid. Price those costs for five to ten years and you have the number. A policy on the stay-at-home parent is usually smaller than the earner's, and the premium is often the best-value purchase in the whole plan.

## Who should be the beneficiary of a new parent's policy?

The surviving spouse first, then a trust or a custodian for the child as contingent. Never your baby alone. A child cannot collect, and a court-supervised account lasts until 18 in many cases, with the child receiving everything at once. That result is exactly what most parents do not want.

Our guides on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) show how the forms work. For the money itself, read [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors). In Illinois a UTMA custodianship ends at 21, and in New Jersey it also ends at 21 unless you set an earlier age after 18, as covered in our [Illinois UTMA guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) and [New Jersey UTMA guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors).

A trust works better for a larger policy. The trustee pays for the child's needs and releases the balance at ages you choose.

## Is the death benefit taxable?

Generally not for income tax, when paid to a named beneficiary. The federal estate tax exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so a family-sized policy does not trigger it. New Jersey exempts life insurance paid to a named beneficiary from its inheritance tax, according to our [New Jersey inheritance tax guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax). Illinois has an estate tax at a lower threshold than the federal one, so a high-earning parent with a large policy and a large estate should ask about ownership. Our article on the [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) explains the option.

## What mistakes do new parents make with life insurance?

The first is buying the policy and never touching the beneficiary form. The default on many work plans is the spouse alone. If the spouse is also gone, the proceeds can end up in the estate and then in front of a probate judge. The second is letting the policy lapse. Premiums come out of a bank account, and a changed card or a closed account can quietly cancel coverage. Put a yearly calendar reminder on the policy anniversary.

The third mistake is waiting. Premiums rise with age, and a health condition that appears later, such as high blood pressure, can raise the price or end the offer. A healthy 32-year-old has the most options. The fourth is buying more than the formula supports because an agent suggested a number. Keep your own worksheet and compare any quote against it.

Finally, tell your spouse where the policy is. Families often never learn that a policy existed, and the benefit goes unclaimed. A one-page list of accounts and policies, kept with your will, prevents that.

## What to do this month

1. Run the formula above for each parent.
2. Check any coverage through work and its beneficiary.
3. Get quotes for a level term that matches the longest obligation.
4. Fill in the beneficiary form with a contingent that is not the baby.
5. Sign a will that names a guardian, using our guide to [choosing a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian).
