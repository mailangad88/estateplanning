A check arrives. It might be a parent's estate, a settlement, a stock payout or the proceeds of selling a company you spent twenty years building. For a few weeks you feel mostly relief and some dread, because you have been handed a problem that is also a gift.

Your estate plan was written for the life you had when you signed it. Money at this scale changes what the plan has to do. This guide covers what to check, in what order, and which decisions to postpone. It sits alongside our general advice on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) and the [life stages section](/learn/life-stages).

## What should you do in the first few weeks?

Mostly slow down. People who receive a windfall tend to make their worst decisions in the first three months, under pressure from relatives, advisers and their own excitement.

1. **Put the money somewhere safe and boring.** An insured bank or Treasury account will do while you think. Do not commit to investments or loans yet.
2. **Do not sign anything urgently** that an adviser, relative or salesperson puts in front of you.
3. **Find out the tax picture.** Inherited assets generally receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which often limits income tax on later sales. Lawsuit awards, bonuses and business sale gains can be taxed differently. Ask a tax professional what applies to you before spending.
4. **Keep the money separate** from joint accounts until you have decided how to hold it.
5. **Check your estate plan.** The rest of this page explains how.

## Which documents should you check first?

| Document | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Will or trust | Does it still divide the estate as you want, now that the estate is larger? |
| Beneficiary forms | Do new accounts, annuities or insurance policies name the right people? |
| Trust funding | Are the new assets titled in the trust's name? See [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) |
| Power of attorney | Does your agent have the skills to manage a bigger, more complex portfolio? |
| Executor and trustee | Is the person you named still the right choice for a larger job? |
| Guardian nominations | If you have minor children, would the money allow them to be raised as you wish? |

The most common problem is simple: a living trust that exists on paper and holds nothing new. If you receive money in your own name and it never moves into the trust, it may go through probate at your death. Our [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) shows what that can mean.

## What does a larger estate change?

Several things, and not all of them involve tax.

**Control.** A modest estate can be left outright to adult children without much thought. A larger one often justifies trusts that protect the money from a child's divorce, creditors or poor spending habits. See [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts).

**Probate.** Larger estates tend to have more assets in more forms, which makes probate slower and more expensive. A funded revocable trust can reduce that.

**Tax.** The federal estate tax basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and a married couple can shelter $30 million with portability. Most windfalls do not reach those levels. Some states tax estates or inheritances at much lower thresholds, so check [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker).

**Gifting.** You can give up to $19,000 per recipient in 2026 without filing a gift tax return. Larger gifts use part of your lifetime exclusion and require a return. Hold off on large gifts until you know what you need for yourself. Read [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) first.

## What if the money was an inheritance and you are married?

An inheritance in your own name is generally your separate property. The trouble starts when it is mixed. Deposit it in a joint account, use it to pay off the joint mortgage, or retitle a house into both names, and a court may later treat it as marital. Some people want exactly that, as a gesture to a spouse. Others are surprised.

If you want to keep the inheritance separate, hold it in an account in your name alone, keep records, and consider a trust with clear terms. If you are in a second marriage, the stakes are higher because the money may be meant for children from an earlier relationship. Our [blended families section](/learn/blended-families) covers the structures.

## What changes when you sell a business?

Before the sale, your business was a bundle of obligations: employees, customers, a successor, perhaps a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements). After the sale, it is cash and perhaps a note from the buyer. Several parts of your plan become obsolete the day the sale closes.

- **Succession plans and key-person insurance** may no longer matter. See [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).
- **Powers of attorney and the trust** may name someone chosen to run the business, not to manage investments.
- **Tax planning** may need to be done before closing, not after. Some strategies, such as certain trusts, must be in place before a binding sale agreement to work well.
- **Payment terms** such as installment notes or earnouts create assets that your family will have to collect after your death.

Talk to your attorney and tax adviser when the sale is a possibility, not once it is done.

## What about a second home or other new property?

A vacation property is often the part of an inheritance or windfall that creates the most family trouble. If a house in another state is in your name alone, your family may need a second probate there. Putting it in a trust, or using a transfer on death deed where available, avoids that. Our page on [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) explains the options. Think too about who pays for taxes and repairs after you are gone, because a house left to three children without a plan is a common cause of dispute.

## Who should know about it?

Fewer people than you think. Tell your spouse or partner, your attorney, your tax adviser and the agent who would step in if you were incapacitated. Everyone else can wait. News of a windfall travels quickly in families, and requests for loans tend to arrive before the check clears. It is reasonable to decide in advance what you are willing to give and say it once, kindly, rather than answering each request as it comes. A written plan for the money gives you a polite way to say that the decisions have already been made.

## A worked example

Raymond, 58, receives about $900,000 from his late aunt's estate. His own plan is a simple will from 2011 leaving everything to his wife, Lena, and then to their two sons. He deposits the money in a joint account because it is easy. A year later, he and Lena are talking about moving closer to her family.

His attorney asks three questions. Is the money meant to be Lena's, or reserved for the boys? Should it be in a trust? And what happens if one son is divorcing? Raymond realizes he never decided. They sign a revocable trust, retitle the account, and give each son a share in a separate trust with an independent trustee. The whole process takes about three weeks, which is much less than the time he spent worrying.
