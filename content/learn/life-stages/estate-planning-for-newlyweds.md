Marriage is one of the few events that rewrites your legal relationships overnight. Your spouse becomes your closest legal relative, may gain rights to your property, and often holds decision-making authority in a medical emergency. But the papers you signed before the wedding keep saying what they said. If your 401(k) names your mother, it still names your mother.

This guide covers what changes at marriage and what to do in the first months. If either of you has children from a prior relationship, read it alongside our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families), because the usual advice for couples can work against you in that situation. For the wider overview of how planning needs shift over the years, see our guide to [estate planning by life stage](/learn/life-stages).

## What changes legally when you get married?

Marriage gives you and your spouse default inheritance rights, decision-making priority in a medical emergency, and potential access to each other's property. But those defaults vary by state and rarely match what a couple actually wants, which is why newlyweds should write down their choices.

Typical effects include:

- **Intestacy.** If one of you dies with no will, [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession) gives your spouse a share. The size depends on whether you have children and in some states whether they are also the spouse's children.
- **Elective or forced share.** In most states a surviving spouse can claim a minimum percentage of the estate even if the will leaves less. This is the [elective share](/glossary/elective-share). Community property states handle it differently, as we explain below.
- **Medical decisions.** Many states put a spouse first in line to decide if you cannot, but a written directive is more reliable.
- **Prior documents.** Some states cancel parts of a will upon marriage, but do not count on that.
- **Taxes.** The unlimited [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction) lets you leave any amount to a U.S. citizen spouse without federal estate tax.

## What should newlyweds do first?

Start with the forms that override everything else, then move to the documents that name your spouse, then look at how you own property. A sensible order is:

1. **Update beneficiaries.** Do this the week after you return from the honeymoon, because it takes ten minutes online.
2. **Sign wills.** Each spouse needs their own, naming the other as primary beneficiary and executor.
3. **Sign powers of attorney.** Name each other as agent, with a backup.
4. **Sign healthcare directives.** Name your spouse as your healthcare agent and add a HIPAA release.
5. **Review how your assets are titled.** Decide on joint ownership, individual ownership or trusts.
6. **Revisit life insurance.** Consider coverage if either spouse depends on the other's income or a joint debt.

## Which beneficiary designations should change after marriage?

Change every account that asks you to name a beneficiary, starting with retirement plans and life insurance. A beneficiary form controls the account regardless of your will, so an outdated form can send money to the wrong person.

| Account | Why it matters after marriage |
|---|---|
| 401(k) or 403(b) | Federal law generally makes a spouse the beneficiary of an employer plan unless the spouse signs a written waiver. Update the form anyway so your contingent beneficiaries are accurate. |
| IRA | No automatic spousal rights. The form controls. See our guide to [naming IRA and 401(k) beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries). |
| Group and individual life insurance | The form controls. Add a contingent beneficiary. See [life insurance beneficiary mistakes](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries). |
| Bank and brokerage accounts | Add payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designations where available. |
| Health savings account | Naming a spouse allows the account to remain a tax-favored HSA after your death, which is a benefit not shared with other beneficiaries. |

A spouse is also the one beneficiary who can roll an inherited retirement account into their own IRA and delay required withdrawals, one of the spousal options noted in our [retirement beneficiary article](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

## Do both spouses need a will?

Yes, each spouse should have their own will, even if you plan to own everything jointly. A will names a guardian for children, handles assets in one person's name, and provides the fallback if you and your spouse die together. For most newlyweds without children, a simple will leaving everything to the spouse and naming a backup, such as a sibling or parent, works well.

If you may want a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), perhaps because you own real estate or want to avoid probate, our article on [will vs. trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) outlines when each fits. Many young couples start with wills and add a trust when their assets or family grow.

## How should newlyweds title their home and accounts?

Titling decides who owns what at death, regardless of the will. The three common forms for married couples are:

- **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship.** The survivor automatically takes the property. See [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy). It avoids probate but can disinherit children from a prior relationship.
- **Tenancy by the entirety.** Available in some states for married couples only, it adds creditor protection against one spouse's separate debts. See [tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety).
- **Community property.** A handful of states treat property earned during marriage as owned equally by both spouses. See [community property](/glossary/community-property). Some of these states allow a community property with right of survivorship form that avoids probate.

Maya and Ben bought a condo before the wedding. Maya paid the $60,000 down payment from savings, and the deed names only her. After marriage they are not required to add Ben, and doing so is a gift of half the equity. In a divorce, the condo may be treated as marital property in whole or in part depending on state law and how they paid the mortgage. They decide to retitle it jointly, knowing the reason and the cost: the survivor will own the whole condo without probate, and Maya gives up sole ownership of the equity she funded.

For a deeper look at the tradeoffs of adding a person to a deed or account, see [adding a child to your deed or bank account](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks), and our [property and assets pillar](/learn/property-and-assets).

## What about a prenuptial agreement?

A prenup or postnup can waive a spouse's inheritance rights, protect business or family property, and set rules for divorce. If you have one, it should be coordinated with your estate plan, not drafted in isolation, because inconsistent documents lead to disputes. A waiver of a spouse's inheritance rights usually has to be in writing and signed, and many states add disclosure requirements, so the details matter. We cover how they fit together in [prenups, postnups and your estate plan](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).

## Do newlyweds need to think about estate tax?

For nearly all newlyweds, no. The federal estate and gift tax exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and spouses can combine them through [portability](/glossary/portability). Gifts between U.S. citizen spouses are not taxed. If one spouse is not a U.S. citizen, special rules apply, and a consultation is worth it. See our [estate tax pillar](/learn/estate-tax) and the article on [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples).

## What if one of us has children already?

Then a simple "everything to my spouse" plan can leave your children with nothing if you die first and your spouse later changes their own plan. Our guides on [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) and [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) describe tools that provide for a spouse during their life while locking in who receives the rest.

## What about the paperwork that has nothing to do with money?

Add your spouse to emergency contacts, change your name on accounts if relevant, and tell each other where the documents are. Choose agents with care, and see [how to choose your power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) if you are unsure. Review your plan after your first child, a home purchase or an out-of-state move. Our [list of review triggers](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) covers them.

If you are buying a home together, read [estate planning when you buy a home](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-when-you-buy-a-home) before closing, and if you do not plan to have children, see [estate planning for couples without children](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-couples-without-children).
