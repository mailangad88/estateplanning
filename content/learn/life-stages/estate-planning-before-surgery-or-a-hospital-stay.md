Dev is 52 and has a heart valve operation in three weeks. His surgeon says the risks are small. He has no will, no directive, and a wife who has never seen the passwords to anything. He wants to know what to do in the time he has.

Most people never get a warning like that. If you have one, use it. This page lists what to sign, who gets copies, and what to do if the hospital date is close and an attorney has to come to you. If the situation is more serious than a planned procedure, see [estate planning after a serious diagnosis](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-a-serious-diagnosis) as well. The broader picture is in our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages).

## The short version

- The most useful documents are the ones that work while you are alive: a healthcare directive, a HIPAA release and a durable power of attorney.
- A will matters too, mostly for naming a guardian if you have minor children and an executor.
- Unmarried partners and friends are not default decision makers. Name them in writing.
- Give copies to the people named and to the hospital.

## Which documents matter most before surgery?

A bad outcome from surgery is more often a long recovery or a period on a ventilator than a death. That is why the first documents are about decisions during incapacity. The table shows what each does and who it names.

| Document | What it does | Who it names |
|---|---|---|
| Healthcare power of attorney | Lets someone consent to or refuse treatment when you cannot. | Healthcare agent and a backup. |
| Living will | States your wishes about life support. | Your agent applies it. |
| HIPAA authorization | Lets doctors talk to named people. | Family, partner, friends. |
| Durable financial power of attorney | Lets an agent pay bills and access accounts. | Financial agent and a backup. |
| Will | Names executor and guardian, and says who inherits. | Executor, guardian. |

Our articles on the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) explain the two halves of a directive. Choose your agent for steadiness under stress, not for closeness. If you are not sure who fits, [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) walks through it.

## What if I am not married to my partner?

Then the paperwork carries more weight. Without a directive, state law decides who speaks for you, and the list usually starts with a spouse, then adult children, then parents and siblings. A partner of ten years may not be on it. Federal hospital rules let a patient choose visitors, but that depends on being able to say so, and a patient under anesthesia cannot.

Name your partner as healthcare agent. Add a HIPAA release so doctors can share information without waiting for an administrator. Our page on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) covers what else a partner needs, including who may arrange funeral decisions if the worst happens.

## What about money while I am in the hospital?

A healthcare agent cannot pay your mortgage. Money needs its own agent under a durable financial power of attorney. Without one, a family may have to ask a court for a guardianship or conservatorship before anyone can touch your accounts, which takes weeks and costs money. The article on [being incapacitated without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney) shows how that plays out.

Pick an agent who is organized and lives within reach of your bank. Decide whether the power starts now or only when a doctor certifies incapacity. [Springing versus immediate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) explains the tradeoff, and a springing power can stall at exactly the moment you need it.

Also, give your agent the practical keys: a list of accounts, bills due, insurance policies, where the documents are, and how to get into your phone. A [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) is a good place for it.

## What should I check on my beneficiary forms?

Pull up the forms for life insurance, the 401(k), IRAs and any bank account with a payable-on-death name. These pass outside your will, so the form decides. If an ex-spouse, a parent who has died or a minor child is listed, change it before surgery. A beneficiary change is often one online form and takes minutes. See [payable-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) for how those work.

## Can an attorney come to the hospital?

Yes, and the sooner you ask, the easier it is. Most attorneys who draw wills will meet a patient at a hospital, rehab center or at home. Expect them to talk with you alone, ask what you own and who your family is, and note how clearly you answer. That record helps if anyone later questions your capacity.

Be realistic about logistics. Hospital staff often cannot serve as witnesses, and a notary may need to be booked in advance. Medication can make the afternoon a bad time. Schedule the signing for when you are most alert, and avoid having a family member who benefits from the will in the room. The page on [whether it is too late to make a will](/learn/what-if/too-late-for-a-will) covers capacity, witnesses and contest risk in more detail.

## What if I need a will in an emergency right now?

Do the simplest thing that is properly signed. A short will that names an executor, a guardian if there are minor children, and leaves the estate to named people is far better than none. State laws on signing differ, and a handwritten will is valid in some states and not others, so use an attorney if you can. [Handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) explains the risks.

If you cannot get a full will, do the other things in parallel: fix beneficiary forms, add a payable-on-death name on bank accounts, and sign the power of attorney. These work even if the will does not.

## What about children, pets and the house?

Think about the days you are away. If you are a single parent, name a temporary caretaker in writing and tell the school; a signed medical consent form for the child is a small thing that saves an afternoon of phone calls. The article on [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians) covers formal options. Pets need a named person and a note on vet records. If a pet's care could last years, a [pet trust](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) is worth a conversation. And someone should know how to get into your home and who has a key.

## A step-by-step plan for the weeks before surgery

1. Call an attorney as soon as you have a date, and ask whether they can visit.
2. Choose a healthcare agent, a financial agent and a backup for each, then ask them if they are willing.
3. Sign the directive, HIPAA release and power of attorney together.
4. Update beneficiary forms and check the will, if you have one.
5. Make three copies of each document: one for each agent and one for the hospital.
6. Bring the directive to the pre-op appointment and ask that it be added to your chart.
7. Write the letter of instruction: accounts, passwords, bills, pets, who to call.
8. Tell your agents where everything is.

## How state law changes this

States use different forms, different signing rules, and different lists of who can decide for you when there is no directive. A directive signed in one state is generally honored in another, but a local form removes doubts. Our [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas) shows how one state packages these documents, and your own state's page will show yours.
