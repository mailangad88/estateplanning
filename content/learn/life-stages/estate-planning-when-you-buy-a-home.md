You are about to sign a stack of papers that will make you the owner of the largest asset most people ever hold. Almost every page in that stack concerns the loan or the sale. One page, the deed, also answers a question you may not have asked: who gets this house when you die, and what happens if you cannot manage it yourself?

This article is about that page. It explains the ways to take title, what each does at death, how a trust interacts with your mortgage and insurance, and which other documents to sign the same month. For the short checklist version, see our [buying a home life-event page](/life-events/buying-a-home). Here we go deeper into the choices and their tradeoffs.

## Why is the closing table a planning moment?

Because the title company will record whatever names and ownership words you give it, and changing them later costs more than choosing well now. The deed is a public record that controls the home no matter what your will says. A will cannot override a deed that gives the house to a surviving co-owner.

Closing is also when you are already paying for a title search, signing before a notary and talking to a professional who handles deeds. Many buyers accept the default their agent or lender suggests, which is often "Jane Smith and John Smith, husband and wife" with no survivorship language. That default can mean very different things in different states, so read the vesting line on your closing documents and ask what it does.

## What are the ways to hold title?

The common forms differ in who inherits automatically, whether probate is needed and what risks come with them. Which forms exist, and what they are called, varies by state, so treat the table as a map and have your attorney confirm the details where you live.

| Title form | Survivor takes it automatically | Probate avoided | Main risk | Often suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole ownership | No | No (unless a TOD deed or trust is added) | Probate; no one named to act if you are incapacitated | Single buyers with a will or trust behind it |
| Joint tenancy with right of survivorship | Yes | At the first death | Second death is unplanned; a co-owner's creditors can reach the home; one owner can sever the tenancy in many states | Spouses and partners with simple plans |
| Tenancy in common | No, each share goes by will or intestacy | No | An unplanned share can pass to relatives you did not choose | Co-buyers with separate heirs, such as friends or siblings |
| Tenancy by the entirety (some states) | Yes | At the first death | Available to married couples only; ends with divorce | Married couples, for added creditor protection |
| Community property with survivorship (some states) | Yes, if the deed says so | At the first death | Rules vary widely; tax effects differ | Married couples in community property states |
| Transfer-on-death deed (many states) | Beneficiary takes at your death | Yes | Not available everywhere; beneficiary's creditors can matter; no help if you are incapacitated | Sole owners wanting a light tool |
| Revocable living trust | Successor trustee acts; beneficiaries take | Yes | The house must actually be deeded in; extra paperwork | Anyone wanting incapacity and probate planning in one tool |

If you are married and live in a state that offers tenancy by the entirety, it is usually the form lenders and title companies suggest for couples. In community property states such as California, Texas and Arizona, the vesting language also affects tax treatment at the first death. Our [community property glossary entry](/glossary/community-property) explains the basic idea, and our state guides for [California](/estate-planning/california), [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) and [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) describe local rules.

## What does each choice do at death?

It decides who ends up owning the house and how much court is involved, which are two different questions.

**Sole name.** The house goes through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) in the state where it sits. Your will, or the state's [intestacy rules](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) if you have none, decides the next owner. Probate also means a delay before your family can sell or even refinance.

**Joint tenancy.** The survivor owns the whole home immediately, with a recorded death certificate and a short filing in most states. That is smooth at the first death. At the second death, the home belongs to the survivor alone, so it goes through probate unless the survivor has updated the title. If one owner becomes incapacitated, the other still needs authority to sell or refinance, because the sick owner's share cannot be signed away without a valid power of attorney.

**Tenancy in common.** Your share belongs to your estate. A co-buyer who is your friend may find themselves co-owning with your sister, your ex or a stranger. If you buy with a partner, the arrangement should be in a written agreement. See [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) for how to protect a partner who is not an owner.

**TOD deed.** You keep full ownership while alive. At death the named beneficiary records an affidavit and the home passes outside probate. Our article on the [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) covers the pitfalls, and the [TOD deed vs. trust comparison](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust) shows when each fits.

**Living trust.** A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) lets a successor trustee manage or sell the home if you are incapacitated and distribute it at death without probate. It works only if the deed actually names the trust as owner. The steps are in our guide to [transferring your house into a trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).

## How does a living trust affect my mortgage, insurance and taxes?

Usually it affects them less than buyers fear, but you should check three things before you record a trust deed.

1. **The mortgage.** A federal law known as the Garn-St Germain Act generally prevents a lender from enforcing a due-on-sale clause when a borrower transfers a residence of one to four units into a living trust in which the borrower stays a beneficiary and keeps living there. Lenders sometimes still ask for notice, so call yours first and keep the answer in writing.
2. **Title insurance.** Some owner's policies continue to cover a transfer into your own revocable trust, and some need an endorsement. Ask the title company at closing, when it costs little to arrange.
3. **Property tax and transfer tax.** Many states exempt a transfer into your own revocable trust from reassessment and transfer tax, but the rules differ, and a few require a filing to claim the exemption.

Income tax is rarely affected during your life, since a revocable trust is generally ignored while you live. At death, a home held in your trust or sole name generally receives a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) to its value on the date of death, which can erase years of appreciation for your heirs.

## What if I am buying with a partner, a friend or a parent?

Write the arrangement down before closing, because title alone does not say what happens when one owner wants out, falls behind or dies. Co-buyers who are not married should consider a co-ownership agreement covering each owner's share of the down payment, mortgage and repairs, who can buy out whom and at what price, and what happens at death.

Consider two sisters, Priya and Anita, who buy a duplex as tenants in common, each putting in half. Priya dies unexpectedly. Without a plan, her half goes to her husband Dev under state law, and Anita is now a co-owner with a brother-in-law she barely knows. A buy-sell clause funded by life insurance, or a trust that gives Anita first right to buy, would have avoided that.

If a parent co-signs your mortgage and goes on the deed, ask about the gift, tax-basis and Medicaid effects first. The same cautions apply in reverse when you are later tempted to put an adult child on your deed to avoid probate. Our article on [adding a child to a deed or account](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) explains why that shortcut often costs more than probate would.

## How does homestead protection fit in?

Most states protect some or all of a primary residence's value from certain creditors, and some also shield it from forced sale for a surviving spouse or minor children. How title is held can affect whether and how those protections apply. For example, a transfer to an irrevocable trust, or to a co-owner, can change the analysis. The details are state-specific, so ask your attorney how the homestead rules where you live interact with the form of title you choose.

## What should you sign in the same month?

A house purchase should be followed within weeks by a short set of documents, because the deed covers only one asset.

1. **A will or a trust with a pour-over will.** It names who gets everything the deed does not control, and, if you have children, who will raise them. See [new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) for that step.
2. **A durable financial power of attorney** that expressly covers real estate, so someone can sell, refinance or deal with insurers if you cannot.
3. **A healthcare directive and HIPAA release.**
4. **Life insurance and beneficiary forms** sized to the mortgage. If a spouse's income pays the loan, term insurance running as long as the loan is often the cleanest protection. Check that the beneficiary is the person you now intend, and read [life insurance beneficiary mistakes](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) before you name a minor child.
5. **A note of what happens to the mortgage.** Heirs who inherit a home generally take it subject to the loan, and federal law restricts lenders from calling the loan when a relative inherits an occupied home. Our blog on [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/blog/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-when-the-owner-dies) walks through it.

## When can you wait on a trust?

Many first-time buyers do well to close in a simple, sensible title, sign the basic documents and revisit a trust later. If your home is your only significant asset, you live in a state with an affordable TOD deed or a streamlined small-estate process, and you have no incapacity concerns, a trust may not repay its cost yet. If you own property in two states, have children from a prior relationship, expect to leave the house to several people, or want to avoid a court-supervised conservatorship, a trust looks stronger. Our [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) article lays out the decision, and the [revocable living trust guide](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained) explains how the pieces fit.
