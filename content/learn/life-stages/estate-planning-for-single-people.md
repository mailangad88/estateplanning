If you are single and have no children, you may have heard that you do not need an estate plan because "there is no one to leave anything to." That reasoning runs in reverse. A married parent has a spouse and children who fill the key roles by default. You do not, which means the law will pick people for you, and its picks may not be anyone you would choose.

This guide explains who the law picks, which documents let you choose instead, and how to plan for the possibility of aging or becoming ill without a built-in caregiver. It also fits people who are widowed, divorced or whose children live far away. For a map of planning across the years, see [estate planning by life stage](/learn/life-stages).

## What happens to your property if you are single and die without a will?

Your property goes to your closest relatives under a fixed state formula, and if none are found it passes to the state. This is [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession). For a person without a spouse or children, the order is typically:

1. Parents, if living
2. Siblings, and if a sibling has died, that sibling's children
3. Grandparents, then aunts and uncles and their descendants
4. More distant relatives, with limits that differ by state
5. The state, in a process called escheat

Formulas vary, and some states divide between parents and siblings. The results can surprise people. A close friend who spent a decade helping you, a favorite niece's partner or a charity you supported gets nothing. A cousin you have never met, or a half-sibling you barely know, might inherit. Our article on [what happens when you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) shows the process, and the [wills pillar](/learn/wills) covers how to avoid it.

## Which roles do you need to fill?

A plan for a single person is mostly a matter of choosing people, so list the roles first. Each one needs a primary and a backup.

| Role | What the person does | How to choose |
|---|---|---|
| Executor | Handles probate, pays bills and debts, distributes property | Organized, trustworthy, willing, ideally lives nearby |
| Financial agent | Manages your money if you cannot | Honest and comfortable with finances |
| Healthcare agent | Speaks for you medically | Calm under pressure and will follow your wishes |
| Successor trustee | Manages a trust if you have one | Same traits as an executor, with long-term view |
| Pet caregiver | Takes in animals | Willing and capable; see [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) |
| Digital executor | Closes or preserves accounts | Comfortable with technology |

They can be the same person, but spreading roles can add a check. Our guides on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor), [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) and [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) list the questions to ask each candidate. If no one in your life is a good fit, a professional fiduciary or bank trust department can serve.

## Which documents should a single person sign?

The core set is a will or living trust, a durable financial power of attorney, a healthcare power of attorney, a living will and a HIPAA release. If you own a home or other substantial property, you may prefer a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), which lets a successor trustee take over immediately if you become incapacitated, with no court needed.

The incapacity documents carry the most weight for a single person. A married person who has a stroke has a spouse who can sign, speak to doctors and pay the mortgage. You have no one unless you appoint them. If no agent is named and you cannot decide, someone must petition a court for guardianship or conservatorship, which is public, slow and costly. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) for the comparison.

Consider these specifics:

- **Name a backup and a second backup** for each agent. A single person has fewer people to fall back on if the first agent dies or moves away.
- **Choose a healthcare agent who lives nearby or can travel**, and make sure your agent knows your preferences. See our [healthcare power of attorney guide](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and the [healthcare directives pillar](/learn/healthcare-directives).
- **Use a springing or immediate power of attorney** based on how much you trust the person. See [springing vs. immediate](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).

## Who should you name as beneficiary?

Anyone you like, because without a spouse or children you are generally free to leave property to anyone. Common choices include siblings, nieces and nephews, close friends, a godchild, a religious organization or a charity. Some people divide the estate among several of these.

Whatever you choose, apply it consistently:

1. Put the main plan in a will or trust.
2. Name beneficiaries on retirement accounts, life insurance and bank accounts.
3. Add contingent beneficiaries in case the first person dies before you.
4. Explain how you want shares divided if someone predeceases you, for example whether a nephew's share goes to his children.

Note that an inherited IRA left to a non-spouse beneficiary generally must be emptied within 10 years, which can create a tax burden for the heir. Leaving pre-tax retirement accounts to a charity and other assets to people is a common approach, because the charity pays no income tax. Our [retirement account beneficiaries guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) explains the rules.

Thomas, 52, is single with no children. He owns a condo worth $450,000, a $600,000 IRA, and a brokerage account. He names his sister as executor and healthcare agent, a close friend as backup, leaves the condo and brokerage account to his two nieces, and names his university and a local food bank as IRA beneficiaries. He signs a financial power of attorney naming his sister, with a professional fiduciary as backup. Four documents and three beneficiary forms replace a default that would have given everything to his estranged brother.

## How should you plan for aging without a spouse or children?

Plan early for who will help, who will pay and where you will live. Single people often have no obvious caregiver, so think about these questions:

- **Who will notice if something goes wrong?** Give an agent and a trusted neighbor or friend a way to reach each other.
- **How will care be funded?** Long-term care insurance, savings and Medicaid each have different qualifications. Read about [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and the [five-year look-back](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period), and our [elder care pillar](/learn/elder-care).
- **Who can monitor against exploitation?** Single older adults are frequent targets. Consider a second person who gets copies of statements. See [protecting older adults from financial exploitation](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse).
- **Do you want a professional?** A trust company or professional fiduciary can serve as trustee or agent for a fee, without the burden on a friend.
- **What are your wishes for dementia?** Our guide on [planning for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) covers timing and capacity.

## What about taxes?

For most single people, federal estate tax is not a concern. The 2026 exemption is $15,000,000 per person, and a single person cannot use a spouse's unused amount through portability. Some states tax smaller estates, and a few tax inheritances received by nieces, nephews and friends at higher rates than children. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and the [federal exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

## How often should you review the plan?

Review it after any move, a death or change of heart involving an agent, a change in health, a sale of a home, or an inheritance of your own. Single people also benefit from a yearly check that each named person is still willing and reachable. Our [update triggers guide](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists them.

If you are married but have no children, the questions are similar with an added second-death twist, covered in [estate planning for couples without children](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-couples-without-children).
