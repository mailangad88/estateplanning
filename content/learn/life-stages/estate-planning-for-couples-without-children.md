Most estate planning advice assumes that children are the final beneficiaries and, eventually, the helpers. If you and your spouse have no children, those assumptions fall away, and what is left can be more open than you realize. There is no one who inherits by default, no one who automatically steps in when you are ill, and no obvious answer to what happens after you are both gone.

This article covers what the law does for a childless couple who die without a plan, the documents that fill the gaps, and how to decide who inherits at the second death. It focuses on the choices that are particular to couples without children. If you are not married, our guide on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) is the better starting point, and our blog on [planning with no children](/blog/estate-planning-for-people-with-no-children) takes a shorter look at the same audience.

## What happens if one of you dies first without a will?

It depends on the state, and in many states the surviving spouse does not receive everything. Intestacy rules decide who inherits when there is no will, and they usually give the spouse everything when the deceased left children who are also the spouse's children. When there are no children, the rules differ more:

- Some states give the surviving spouse the entire estate if there are no descendants.
- Other states give the spouse the whole estate only if no parent of the deceased survives, and otherwise split it between the spouse and the deceased's parents.
- Some states split with the deceased's siblings, or with siblings' children, when the parents have died.
- Different rules often apply to property that was owned before the marriage or inherited.

The result can be surprising. Take Elaine and Tom, married 30 years, no children. Tom dies without a will. If their state shares the estate with a surviving parent, Tom's 88-year-old mother might receive a large part of his probate assets, and Elaine could need to deal with a guardian or conservator for his mother, or sell the house to pay her share. Jointly owned property and accounts with beneficiaries usually pass to Elaine regardless, which softens the problem but does not remove it. Our [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) article explains intestacy in more depth, and the blog post on whether [a spouse inherits everything when there is no will](/blog/does-a-spouse-inherit-everything-if-there-is-no-will) shows common patterns.

## Which documents should a childless couple prioritize?

Start with the documents that cover incapacity, because with no children, there is no automatic helper. Then cover death, and then the second death.

1. **Durable financial powers of attorney** naming each other first, then a younger backup. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
2. **Healthcare directives and HIPAA releases** naming each other first, with a backup who is willing to travel and to be firm. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
3. **Wills or revocable living trusts** that leave property to the surviving spouse and then to the people you choose.
4. **Updated beneficiary forms** on retirement accounts and life insurance, with contingent beneficiaries that are not blank.
5. **A letter of instruction** that tells your agents where things are and what you want. Our [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) article lists what to include.

If you are not sure whether a will or a trust fits, see [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust).

## How do you protect the surviving spouse?

The simplest approach is to leave everything outright to the spouse, and for many couples that is right. Outright gifts between spouses who are U.S. citizens qualify for the [unlimited marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction), so no federal estate tax is due at the first death. If the combined estate is large, filing a return at the first death to elect [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) can preserve the first spouse's unused exemption for the second.

A trust gives more control when you worry about the survivor's vulnerability or about what the survivor does later. Consider a trust if:

- The survivor might become incapacitated or easy to influence. A trust lets a trustee manage the money.
- You want assets to go to your relatives after the survivor dies, regardless of what the survivor decides.
- One of you has a family business or inherited property you want to keep in your family line.
- There is a significant age gap, and the younger spouse might remarry.

A trust that holds assets for the survivor and then passes them to named relatives is often a marital or QTIP-style trust. Our article on [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) explains the structure, even though it was written for blended families.

## What is the second-death problem?

It is the question of who inherits when the surviving spouse also dies, and without children, the answer must be chosen on purpose. The survivor's estate otherwise goes to the survivor's own relatives by intestacy, which can leave one spouse's family with everything and the other spouse's family with nothing.

Consider Ravi and Meera. They have no children. Ravi's two nieces are close to both of them; Meera's brother is estranged. They sign mirror wills leaving everything to each other and then to the nieces. Ravi dies first. A few years later, after her brother reconciles with her, Meera signs a new will leaving everything to him. The nieces get nothing, and nothing in the old wills prevented it.

A mirror will does not bind the survivor unless the couple signed a contract not to revoke it, which is hard to prove and varies by state. If you want your final wishes to hold, use a joint or sub-trust arrangement. For example, a trust can split at the first death into a survivor's trust, which the spouse can change, and a bypass or marital trust that is irrevocable and names the final beneficiaries. Your attorney can explain how much flexibility you want to leave the survivor.

### Scenarios to plan for

| Scenario | Typical default if no plan | Planning option |
|---|---|---|
| One spouse dies first | State intestacy; spouse may share with parents or siblings | Will or trust leaving everything to the spouse |
| Both spouses die together | Each estate passes to that spouse's own relatives, sometimes in an order that depends on who died first | A common-disaster clause and named final beneficiaries |
| Survivor remarries | New spouse may inherit, and may have elective rights against the estate | Trust for the first couple's chosen beneficiaries |
| Survivor becomes incapacitated | Court-appointed guardian or conservator | Successor trustee and durable power of attorney with younger backups |
| Survivor lives into old age with no family nearby | Hard to find help, higher risk of exploitation | Named agents, a professional fiduciary, and regular check-ins |

A common-disaster or simultaneous-death clause says what happens if you die within a short period of each other. Without one, rules about survivorship can produce odd results, such as an estate passing through one spouse to the other's family and then on.

## Who should be your agents, executor and trustee?

Choose people younger than you, because the roles may last for decades, and name at least two backups for each. Many childless couples ask nieces, nephews, younger siblings or close friends. Think about distance, finances and temperament. A niece in another country may be loving and unreliable for hospital decisions. Our guides to [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor), [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) and [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) provide questions to ask. Talk to each person before naming them.

If no one in your life fits, consider a professional fiduciary or a bank trust department. They charge fees, but they do not get tired, move away or have conflicts of interest. Some couples split the roles, with a family member as healthcare agent and a professional as co-trustee. The article on [estate planning if you are single](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people) covers the same questions for people without a spouse.

## Which assets skip the will?

Many do, so check them. Retirement accounts and life insurance go to the person named on the form, and jointly owned property passes by title. A couple that titles everything jointly may find that the survivor owns it all, outright, with no plan for the second death. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) and [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets), then read our guide to [naming beneficiaries for retirement accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries). Name your spouse as primary and a person or charity as contingent. For an IRA, a spouse has extra options, such as treating the account as their own. Naming a charity as a contingent beneficiary of a pre-tax IRA can be efficient, because a charity does not pay income tax on it.

## How should you plan for long-term care?

Plan for it early, since without an adult child, a spouse may be the only caregiver, and one spouse's illness can strain both. Consider how you would pay for care and who would coordinate it, and whether long-term care insurance fits. See [long-term care insurance and your estate plan](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance) and [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning). The Medicaid look-back in most states reaches back 60 months, so planning ahead matters. Name an agent who can manage finances when you cannot, and think about whether a professional care manager would help.

## What about charity, pets and digital accounts?

Childless couples often leave part of the estate to charity, and the tools are flexible. You can name a charity as a contingent beneficiary, leave a percentage in the will, give through a [donor-advised fund or qualified charitable distributions](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds), or set up a [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust) that pays income to the survivor and the remainder to charity. Pets need their own plan, since a will cannot ensure care; see [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts). Digital accounts and passwords can lock agents out, so see [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).

## Does estate tax matter?

Usually not at the federal level. The federal exemption in 2026 is $15,000,000 per person, so a married couple can shelter $30 million with portability. A few states have estate or inheritance taxes with much lower thresholds, and some tax bequests to siblings, nieces and nephews at higher rates than bequests to children. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) to check whether your state is one of them.
