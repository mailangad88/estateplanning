Ellen and Paul have lived in Paul's two-flat in Oak Park for eleven years. They are not married, and the deed says only Paul. If Paul dies tomorrow without a will, his two grown children from his first marriage inherit the building. Ellen has no legal right to stay a single night longer than they allow.

Many couples believe that years together make a partner family in the eyes of the law. They do not, in Illinois or New Jersey. This page explains who gets the home when an unmarried partner dies, what a partner's rights depend on, and the tools that let a partner stay. It builds on our guide to [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) and sits in our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages). This is general education, not legal advice.

## What happens to the home if you die without a will?

It goes to your closest relatives, not your partner. State intestacy laws list a spouse, children, parents and siblings in that order, and an unmarried partner is not on the list. Neither Illinois nor New Jersey allows couples to form a new common-law marriage by living together, however long it lasts. New Jersey does extend spousal treatment to registered civil union and domestic partners, as described in our article on [domestic partners and civil union partners in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/domestic-partners-and-civil-union-partners-in-new-jersey-estate-law), and Illinois recognizes civil unions. A couple who has not registered is treated as two unrelated adults.

The full picture of what unfolds is in [what happens if your unmarried partner dies without a will](/learn/what-if/unmarried-partner-dies-without-a-will). The short version for a home is that the owner's estate, run by an executor or administrator, controls the house until it passes to heirs.

## Does it matter whose name is on the deed?

It matters more than anything else. Look at your own situation in this table.

| How the home is owned | What happens when one partner dies | Is the surviving partner protected? |
|---|---|---|
| Joint tenants with right of survivorship | The surviving partner owns the whole home | Yes, and without probate |
| Tenants in common | The deceased partner's share passes by will or intestacy | Only if the will leaves it to the partner |
| Deceased partner's name only | The house goes through the estate to heirs or will beneficiaries | No, unless the will says so |
| Surviving partner's name only | The survivor keeps it | Yes, but the other partner has no rights |
| Living trust naming the partner | Trustee follows the trust terms | Yes, as the trust provides |
| Rental, both on the lease | The survivor is a tenant | Yes, subject to the landlord and lease |

Tenancy by the entirety, the protected form of ownership for spouses in both states, is not available to unmarried couples. Joint tenancy is, and it comes with its own risks, such as exposure to either owner's creditors. Our page on [joint tenants, tenants in common and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenants-vs-tenants-in-common-vs-entirety) compares them.

## Can the family really make a partner leave?

If the partner has no ownership and no lease, the heirs have the legal right to possession once they own the home, and the estate's representative can often act sooner. A family cannot lawfully change the locks or throw belongings onto the lawn in either state. They generally have to go through a formal process, which takes time. That time is a cushion, not a plan, and a surviving partner who stays has no right to remain once a court says otherwise.

What helps in practice:

1. Do not move out because someone says you must. Ask for the request in writing.
2. Keep records of rent, utilities and improvements you paid. A partner who contributed money to the house may have a claim against the estate, as explained in [who owns the house when a parent helped pay](/learn/property-and-assets/who-owns-the-house-when-a-parent-helped-pay-or-you-bought-before-marriage).
3. Check whether you are on the deed or the mortgage, a lease or a trust.
4. Get a lawyer before the first signed paper, because a request to "just sign here" can be a waiver.

## How can you make sure your partner can stay?

You have several options, each with different effects on your children and your taxes.

- **Add your partner to the deed as a joint tenant with survivorship.** Simple and avoids probate. The partner owns the whole house at your death. The cost is that you give up control, your partner's creditors can reach their share, and your children inherit nothing from this property.
- **Leave the house to your partner in your will or trust.** Straightforward, but anything in a will is subject to probate unless you use a trust. If your plan is not updated, a later change in the relationship can leave the paper behind.
- **Give your partner a life estate or lifetime right of occupancy.** Your partner lives in the house for life, and it passes to your children afterward. A trust version is more flexible. The main points are explained in [life estate for a surviving spouse](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse), and the same tools work for an unmarried partner. Decide who pays taxes, insurance and repairs, and what happens if your partner needs to move into care.
- **Use a transfer on death instrument.** Illinois allows one for real estate (see [Illinois transfer on death instruments](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument)); for a New Jersey home, ask an attorney which tool fits. It names a person to receive the property at death without probate while you keep ownership during life. Compare the idea with a trust in [transfer on death deed versus trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust).

Mixed families need care. A partner who inherits the house outright can leave it to their own children, and yours get nothing. That risk is the reason many people with children choose a life estate or a trust.

## What are the tax effects?

In Illinois there is no inheritance tax, but there is a state estate tax for large estates, which is covered on our [Illinois estate tax page](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). In New Jersey, an unmarried partner without civil union or domestic partner status falls into a higher inheritance tax class than a spouse, and a home left to them can generate a tax bill that the partner must pay, often in cash, within a deadline. Our [New Jersey inheritance tax page](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) explains the classes. A partner who inherits a home also generally receives a stepped-up basis, which reduces income tax on a later sale.

If the house is the main asset, check whether the heir will have the cash to pay the tax and keep the house.

## Who decides the funeral and medical choices?

These are separate from the house but come up the same week. A hospital may look to a spouse, adult children or parents before an unmarried partner, and a funeral home will do the same. The fix is paper. A health care power of attorney names your partner for medical decisions. In Illinois, a notarized agent form names them for burial and cremation, as covered in our article on the [Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act). A [HIPAA authorization](/glossary/hipaa-authorization) lets doctors speak to them.

## What about retirement accounts and life insurance?

An unmarried person can name anyone on a beneficiary form, so a partner can be named on a 401(k), an IRA or a life insurance policy. Because there is no spouse, there is no required consent. Check the forms and confirm them; a form that still names an ex or a parent will pay them. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you spot mismatches.

## A checklist for this month

- Look at the deed and write down exactly how title is held.
- Decide whether you want your partner to own the house, live in it, or neither.
- Write a will or trust that matches the decision.
- Sign a health care power of attorney and a financial power of attorney for each other.
- Name your partner on a funeral agent form (Illinois) or in your directive.
- Update beneficiary forms.
- Tell your children and your partner what you decided and why.
