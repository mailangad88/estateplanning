Ruth is 71, a widow, with two grown children and four grandchildren under 12. She wants the grandchildren to have something from her, maybe help with college, but she does not want a seven-year-old holding a bank account. She also does not want to cut her own children out of the decision.

That is the usual shape of this question. The legal answer is yes, you can leave money to a grandchild. The planning questions are how, when, and who manages it until the child is ready. This page covers wills, trusts, college savings and the special case of a grandchild with a disability. For the wider picture of how plans change as families grow, see our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages).

## The short version

- You can name any grandchild in a will, a trust or a beneficiary form.
- A minor grandchild should not inherit outright. Use a trust or a custodial account.
- Gifts while you are alive, including a 529 plan, can do more good than an inheritance, because the child gets the money when it is needed.
- Say what happens if a child of yours dies before you. Silence is how grandchildren end up with nothing.
- A grandchild receiving government benefits needs a special needs trust before any money arrives.

## Can I leave money to a grandchild directly?

You can, and in many cases that is a mistake. If a grandchild is under 18 when you die, a bank or insurance company will not pay them. A court usually appoints a guardian of the property, files reports and charges fees. When the child reaches the age of majority, which is 18 in most states and 21 in some, the remaining money is theirs with no strings.

Adults can inherit directly without trouble. The difficulty is age and timing. Our article on [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) walks through the court process, and the [what-if page on minors inheriting](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust) shows how it plays out when no one planned for it.

## Which tools work best for grandchildren?

Each tool trades simplicity against control. This table is a starting point, not a recommendation.

| Tool | Who manages the money | When the grandchild gets it | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trust for each grandchild | A trustee you choose | Ages you set, such as 25 and 30 | Larger gifts, or any worry about maturity. |
| One shared trust for all grandchildren | A trustee | Often when the youngest turns a set age | Equal treatment, less paperwork. |
| Custodial account (UTMA) | A custodian | Age set by state law | Smaller amounts. |
| 529 plan | You, as account owner | Paid to the school | College only. |
| Payable-on-death account | Nobody until you die | At once, if an adult | Adult grandchildren. |

A trust is the most flexible. You can tell the trustee to pay for tuition, medical bills and a first home, and to hold back the rest. Our guide to [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) covers the common designs, and it works the same way for grandchildren. One caution: pick a trustee who is not the grandchild's parent if you are worried about the parent's finances. Choosing between family and a professional is a real decision, and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) covers the tradeoffs.

## How do I leave money for a grandchild's college?

The simplest way is to open a 529 plan now and keep it in your name. You choose the investments, you decide when to withdraw, and if one grandchild skips college you can switch the beneficiary to another. Name a successor owner so the account does not land in your estate and wait for a court.

You can also contribute a larger amount at once. Federal rules let you treat up to five years of annual exclusion gifts as made in one year, which for 2026 means up to $95,000 per donor to one beneficiary, though you should confirm the current limit with a tax professional before relying on it. Paying tuition directly to a school is separate and has no dollar limit for gift tax.

Two things to keep in mind. First, a 529 is only for education, so it will not help a grandchild who goes into a trade with no qualifying costs, and withdrawals for other uses can bring tax and a penalty on the earnings. Second, college aid formulas have changed recently, and a grandparent-owned account is now treated more favorably than it used to be. Rules like that move, so ask a financial aid office before you decide.

If you want college money to arrive only after you die, a trust is the better vehicle. Write the trust to pay education costs first, then release what is left at a later age. That keeps the money from being spent on something else at 18.

## What if my own child dies before I do?

Your will or trust should say. Suppose Ruth leaves her estate "equally to my children." Her son Daniel dies, leaving two kids. Whether those kids receive Daniel's half depends on the wording and on state law. Many states have an anti-lapse statute that sends a deceased child's share to that child's descendants. Others limit it, and a will that says something different can override it.

The fix is a sentence. Use per stirpes language so each family branch keeps its share, or say plainly that Daniel's share goes to Daniel's children in trust. The glossary entries for [per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes) and [per capita](/glossary/per-capita) show how the two split the same estate differently. If Ruth's children and grandchildren should all share equally, that is a different formula, and the wording matters.

## What if a grandchild has special needs?

Do not leave money outright to a grandchild who receives SSI or Medicaid. Even a modest inheritance can push them over the asset limit and end benefits. A [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) fixes that, because the trustee pays for extras while the benefits continue. A third-party trust funded by you does not need a payback to Medicaid at the grandchild's death, which is one reason it is better to set it up yourself than to leave money to a parent and hope.

Tell the family, too. Parents sometimes name the grandparent's estate in their own plan without knowing the grandparent already has a trust in mind, and the two plans collide. The article on [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child or sibling](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling) explains the structure, and an [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) can sit alongside it for everyday spending.

## Does generation-skipping tax apply?

Only to large estates. A transfer to a grandchild is a skip, and the generation-skipping transfer tax can apply on top of estate tax. But each person has an exemption equal to the federal estate tax exclusion, $15,000,000 in 2026, and most families never come near it. If your estate is in the range where this matters, see [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) and talk to an attorney about structure.

## What to do next

1. Decide whether you want to leave money to grandchildren, give it now, or both.
2. Pick a trustee or custodian who is neither the grandchild nor a parent with money trouble.
3. Decide the ages. Many people choose staggered ages instead of a single date.
4. Open or review any 529 plan and name a successor owner.
5. Update your will or trust so it covers grandchildren born after you sign, and tell your children what you did.

## How state law changes this

States set the age of majority, the age for custodial accounts, and whether an anti-lapse statute saves a grandchild's share. A few states tax inheritances, and a gift to a grandchild is treated the same as one to anyone else. Our [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) show how local rules shape a plan; Texas is one example, and your own state's page is worth reading.
