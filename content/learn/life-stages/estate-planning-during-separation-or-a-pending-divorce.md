You moved out, or you filed, or you both just stopped pretending. The divorce will take months, maybe a year. Meanwhile you are driving to work thinking: if something happened to me next Tuesday, my estranged spouse would handle everything. Is that actually true?

For the most part, yes. The law treats you as married until a judge says otherwise, and the gap between "separated" and "divorced" is where most of the surprises sit. This page covers what protects your spouse in the meantime, what you can change on your own, and what a court may stop you from touching.

## Does separation change who inherits from you?

No. A separation agreement may divide property and support, and some include waivers of inheritance rights, but without that language your spouse keeps every right a spouse has. If you die with no will, a spouse is first in line under [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession). If you die with a will that leaves your spouse little or nothing, most states let a surviving spouse claim an [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) of the estate, often a third or a half of it, whatever the will says. Our guide to [disinheriting a spouse](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) explains where that right comes from and when a signed waiver changes it.

Divorce is the event that flips the default. In most states a final divorce automatically cancels gifts to a former spouse in a will and revokes a former spouse's role as executor or trustee. Filing does not do this, and neither does living apart. Take Ravi, who moved out in March. In June he was in a car accident. His divorce was not final, his will still left everything to his wife, and his 401(k) form still named her. All of it went where he had written it, and his lawyer could do little about it.

## What can you change on your own?

Here is a working split between things you can usually do immediately and things you should ask about first. The line moves by state and by court, so treat this as a map, not a ruling.

| Item | Can you change it now? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Financial power of attorney naming your spouse | Yes, by revoking and signing a new one | You can end an agent's authority at any time while you have capacity |
| Healthcare agent and living will | Yes | Same rule, and replace it so the gap never opens |
| Will | Usually yes | The will still cannot override the spouse's elective share |
| Guardian choice for minor children | Yes, with limits | The other parent usually has custody if you die |
| Life insurance and retirement beneficiaries | Often restricted once the case is filed | Many courts issue standing orders freezing changes |
| Title to the house or joint accounts | Often restricted | Transfers can look like hiding assets |
| Joint revocable trust | Depends on the trust and the court | Either spouse may be able to revoke, but a freeze may apply |

The restricted rows trip people up. In many courts a standing order or automatic restraining order takes effect when the petition is served. It generally bars either spouse from changing insurance, retirement beneficiaries or ownership of property outside the ordinary course. Breaking it can result in sanctions. Your divorce attorney can tell you what applies in your case, and our attorneys can coordinate the estate documents around that.

## What should you do the week you separate?

You do not need to rebuild the whole plan at once. This order covers the real risks first.

1. Sign a new healthcare power of attorney, and a new [HIPAA authorization](/glossary/hipaa-authorization), naming someone you trust who is not your spouse. If you collapse, your spouse is otherwise the default decision-maker in many states.
2. Revoke the old financial power of attorney in writing, tell the bank and anyone who holds a copy, and sign a new one. See [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) for how revocation works in practice.
3. Ask your divorce attorney whether a standing order is in effect, and list every beneficiary form that names your spouse.
4. Update your will to name a new executor and a guardian for minor children, even if the share to your spouse cannot yet be removed. A will drafted now is better than one that names your spouse as the person in charge of your estate.
5. Close or retitle accounts only as the court and your attorney allow.
6. Keep copies of everything you sign and when you sent it.

## What about the children?

Parents tend to focus on money and forget guardianship. If you die while the case is open, the other parent is usually the natural guardian of your minor children, and a guardian you name in your will matters only if the other parent is gone or unfit. You can still name one as a backup, and our page on [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) shows how to do it.

Money for the children is a separate problem. If you leave assets outright to a child, a court-supervised account may be required for a minor, and your ex-in-waiting could end up as the person managing it. A trust for the children with a trustee you choose is the clean answer, and it can be written now. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) for the options.

## Why not just wait until the divorce is final?

Because the gap is where the risk is. Divorces run long. A contested case with a house, a business or a pension can take well over a year, and some people never quite finish because the money runs out or the court calendar slips. During that time your documents quietly assume you are still a couple.

There is a fairness point too. Revoking a power of attorney or picking a new healthcare agent takes nothing away from your spouse, and no court will object to you choosing who speaks for your body. The sensitive moves are the ones that change who owns what, and those are exactly the ones to run past counsel. If you want the general picture of how plans change across a lifetime, our [life stages guide](/learn/life-stages) groups the other turning points, from [new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) to retirees.

## What happens to a joint trust or jointly titled property?

A joint trust is the document people forget. Both spouses are often trustees, and each is often the successor beneficiary of the other. If you die mid-divorce, the surviving spouse could take control. Ask your attorney early whether the trust can be revoked, split into two or frozen by agreement, and deal with it as part of the settlement. Jointly held bank accounts and the family home usually pass to the survivor automatically, even though the divorce would later have divided them. That is one reason a settlement signed quickly often beats a perfect one that drags on.

## What happens after the divorce is final?

Once the judgment is entered, the rules change: most states cut a former spouse out of a will and some, though not all, treat beneficiary forms the same way. Do not rely on that. Our page on [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) is the follow-up for finishing the job, and [ex-spouse still named as beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) shows what happens when it is not done. If your marriage was a second one, your documents may also need to protect children from an earlier marriage.
