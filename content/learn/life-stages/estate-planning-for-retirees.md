Retirement changes what an estate plan has to do. During your working years, the plan mostly protected a family that depended on your paycheck. In retirement the questions shift: who manages your money if your memory slips, how long your savings will last if you need care, which of your children is best placed to serve as executor, and how your accounts will be taxed when they pass to heirs.

This guide is a review checklist for anyone at or near retirement, or helping a parent who is. If you are an adult child trying to get a parent started, see our companion guide on [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan). For the full set of life-stage guides, visit [estate planning by life stage](/learn/life-stages).

## What should you review when you retire?

Review five things: your core documents, your beneficiary forms, your agents, how your assets are titled and your plan for long-term care. Most retirees find at least one problem that has existed for years without anyone noticing.

Use this checklist at retirement and every three to five years afterward:

1. **Will or trust.** Does it still reflect your wishes, and are the people named still alive, willing and suitable?
2. **Beneficiary forms.** Check each retirement account, annuity, life insurance policy and payable-on-death account.
3. **Durable financial power of attorney.** Is the agent still the right choice? Has the bank accepted this form?
4. **Healthcare power of attorney, living will and HIPAA release.** Are they current, and do your doctors have copies?
5. **Trust funding.** If you have a living trust, are the house and accounts in the trust's name?
6. **Titling.** Review joint accounts and deeds for unintended consequences.
7. **Long-term care.** What is the plan for paying for care?
8. **Inventory.** Can your executor find everything?

## How do you keep beneficiary designations up to date?

Beneficiary forms control retirement accounts and life insurance regardless of your will, so they deserve a line-by-line review. Common retiree problems include a former spouse still named on an old 401(k), a deceased beneficiary with no contingent named, an estate named by default, and one child named alone when you intended to treat children equally.

Pay particular attention to tax effects:

- A surviving spouse can roll an inherited IRA into their own and delay withdrawals.
- Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years under the SECURE Act. An adult child still in peak earning years may pay income tax at a high rate on those withdrawals.
- Exceptions called eligible designated beneficiaries include a surviving spouse, a minor child of the account owner, a disabled or chronically ill person and someone not more than 10 years younger than you.
- Charities pay no income tax on retirement account money, so many retirees leave pre-tax accounts to charity and other assets, which receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), to family.

Our articles on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) and [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) explain when each approach works. The [beneficiary designations pillar](/learn/beneficiary-designations) covers the wider set.

## Do you still need the same powers of attorney and directives?

Yes, and retirement is when they are most likely to be used. Review them with these questions:

- **Is the agent still able?** A spouse or sibling named years ago may now be unwell or the same age as you. Add a younger successor, often an adult child or a professional.
- **Do the documents fit your state's current law?** Rules for signing and acceptance change, and banks sometimes balk at documents more than a few years old. See [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).
- **Do your children know what you want?** A living will does nothing if no one knows about it. See the [living will guide](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) and our article on [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) for stronger documents when your health changes.
- **Have you considered cognitive decline?** Make your wishes known while you can. See [planning for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

Because a power of attorney can be used for harm as well as help, think about safeguards: a second agent required to co-sign large transactions, regular statements to another family member, or limits on gifting. Our [elder financial abuse guide](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) explains warning signs.

## How should retirees plan for long-term care?

Plan for it as a likely expense, because a substantial share of people over 65 will need some paid care. Costs for a nursing home or full-time home care can exceed $100,000 a year in many parts of the country, and Medicare generally does not pay for long-term custodial care. The options include:

| Approach | How it works | Main tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| Self-funding | Pay from savings and income | Can deplete assets quickly |
| Long-term care insurance | Premiums buy a daily benefit | Premiums can rise, and health is required to qualify |
| Hybrid life insurance or annuity | Combines death benefit with care benefit | Higher upfront cost |
| Medicaid | Pays for care once assets and income are low enough | Look-back and eligibility rules; see below |
| Family care | A relative provides care, sometimes under a written agreement | Burnout and family conflict |

In most states, Medicaid reviews transfers made in the 60 months before an application, and a gift in that period can trigger a penalty period when benefits are delayed. See [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning), [the look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) and [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs). Starting early gives you more choices, and the [elder care pillar](/learn/elder-care) is the best starting point. Rules differ by state and change, so an attorney who handles this regularly is worth a consultation before you move any assets.

## Should you give your house or assets to your children now?

Think twice before transferring significant property during life. A gift can create several problems at once:

- **No step-up in basis.** Your children take your original cost basis, and a house bought for $90,000 and worth $450,000 can mean a large capital gains bill on sale. Assets inherited at death usually get a step-up to current value.
- **Medicaid penalties** if the transfer falls within the look-back window.
- **Loss of control.** The property can be reached by your child's creditors, a divorcing spouse or a lawsuit.
- **Gift tax return.** Gifts above $19,000 per recipient in 2026 require a return, though tax is rarely due.

Our articles on [leaving your house to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) and the [hidden risks of joint titling](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) describe alternatives such as a transfer-on-death deed or a trust. See the [gift tax](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) article for the annual exclusion.

## What about estate taxes in retirement?

Most retirees do not owe federal estate tax. The exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and a married couple can shelter $30 million through portability. Your state may have its own estate or inheritance tax at lower thresholds. Review the [federal exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) and your [state's rules](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes), especially if you plan to move.

## How do you make things easy for your executor?

Organize before anyone needs to. Assemble a document locator with these items:

1. List of accounts with institution names and where statements go.
2. Insurance policies and agents.
3. Location of originals: will, trust, deeds, titles.
4. Advisers: attorney, accountant, financial adviser.
5. Digital accounts and how to open the password manager. See [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access).
6. Funeral wishes and any prepaid arrangements.
7. Recurring bills and subscriptions to cancel.

Frank and Linda, both 68, found during their review that Frank's will named his late brother as executor, Linda's IRA named a deceased sibling as contingent beneficiary, and their living trust was never funded, so the house would still go through probate. They corrected all three in one meeting and left a locator binder with their daughter.

Also review where the original will is kept; see [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will). If you are the surviving spouse in a couple, read [updating your plan after your spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies).
