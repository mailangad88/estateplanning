Joan, 71, lost her husband Frank in February. They bought their Naperville house in 1994, and she is alone in four bedrooms with a mortgage that has a few years left and a roof that needs work. Her daughter says to sell it. Her neighbor says never to decide anything for a year. Joan wants to know when she must choose and what choosing costs.

If you are asking whether to sell the house after your spouse dies, here is the order of questions that matters: what you owe, what the house costs to keep, what taxes apply and when, and who owns the house on paper. This page works through each. It is part of our guide to [life stages](/learn/life-stages), and it sits next to the broader page on [updating your plan after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies). This is general education, not tax or legal advice.

## Should you sell the house right after your spouse dies?

Not in the first weeks, and not because someone else says you should. Counselors and attorneys commonly suggest holding off on large irreversible choices for several months, if you can afford to. A sale cannot be undone, while a delay costs property tax, insurance and upkeep you were already paying.

There are real exceptions. If the house needs a $40,000 roof and you cannot pay the mortgage on one income, waiting may cost more than selling. If stairs are no longer safe for you, a move is a safety decision, not only a money one. Set a date for deciding, say nine months out, and spend the time gathering numbers.

## What does the house cost you each month?

Before you decide, write down the real monthly cost of keeping it, not just the mortgage. Use a simple worksheet.

| Cost | Monthly amount |
|---|---|
| Mortgage principal and interest | Your statement |
| Property tax and homeowner's insurance | Annual bills divided by 12 |
| Utilities, lawn and snow | Average of the last year |
| Repairs and replacements | A fair guess is a percentage of home value per year; use your own history |
| Total | Compare to your income now |

Compare that total to your income after your spouse's death, which may be lower. A pension may drop to a survivor benefit, and Social Security changes when one spouse dies. Our page on [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) explains the survivor rules. If the housing line takes more than about half of your income, take that seriously.

## Who owns the house on paper?

You cannot sell the house until the records show you as the owner. How you get there depends on the title.

1. **Joint with survivorship, or tenancy by the entirety.** Illinois and New Jersey both let spouses hold title this way, and the survivor generally becomes the sole owner when the other dies. You typically record a certified death certificate and any affidavit the county requires. See [Illinois joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).
2. **In your spouse's name only.** The house goes through the estate, and the executor or administrator usually must be appointed before a buyer's title company will close. Read [a house still in a late spouse's name](/learn/what-if/house-still-in-late-spouses-name) before listing.
3. **In a living trust.** The successor trustee, which may be you, signs the deed. This is usually the quickest path.
4. **As tenants in common.** Your spouse's half passes by will, trust or intestacy, which may include someone besides you.

Many surviving spouses assume they own the house because they lived there for decades. Title companies do not assume anything, and a missing step can add weeks to a closing.

## How do taxes work when you sell?

Two rules matter, and they work together.

**Stepped-up basis.** When someone dies, most of what they owned gets a new tax basis equal to its value on the date of death. For a house owned jointly by spouses in a non-community-property state such as Illinois or New Jersey, generally half of the house is treated as your spouse's and receives the step-up. Your own half keeps its old basis. The page on [step-up in basis for married couples and joint property](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis-for-married-couples-and-joint-property) explains the details.

**The home sale exclusion.** A couple selling a home they have lived in can exclude up to $500,000 of gain, and a single person up to $250,000, if they owned and used the home as their main residence for two of the last five years. A surviving spouse who sells within two years of the date of death, and has not remarried, can generally still use the $500,000 amount. Wait past two years and the limit drops to $250,000.

Here is an example with invented numbers. Joan and Frank paid $180,000 in 1994 and put $40,000 into the kitchen and a new roof over the years, so their basis was $220,000. The house is worth $560,000 when Frank dies.

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Joan's half, at old basis | $110,000 |
| Frank's half, stepped up to date-of-death value | $280,000 |
| New total basis | $390,000 |
| Sale price a year later | $560,000 |
| Taxable gain before exclusion | $170,000 |
| Exclusion available if sold within two years | up to $500,000 |
| Likely tax on the gain | None |

If Joan waited three years and the house rose to $700,000, her gain would be $310,000, more than the $250,000 she could exclude, and the amount over it could be taxed. The exact figure depends on your own records, so gather the closing statement and receipts for improvements, and have a tax preparer run the numbers. New Jersey does not charge inheritance tax on property passing to a spouse, but the sale itself is still taxed on any gain above the exclusion.

## What about the mortgage?

You do not have to pay it off or refinance when a spouse dies. Federal rules generally stop a lender from calling the loan due because a spouse inherited the home, and the servicer should treat you as the owner once it has the death certificate. Call and ask for the survivor or successor-in-interest department. Our article on [what happens to a mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) describes the process.

## Should you downsize, stay or move in with family?

Compare three paths on monthly cost, safety and what you want your days to look like.

- **Stay.** Best when the house is paid down, near people you know and safe for your health. Plan for repairs and consider a handyman and a cleaning service.
- **Downsize nearby.** Frees equity and lowers upkeep while keeping your community. Look at property tax in the new place.
- **Move closer to family.** Often the right call when loneliness or health is the driver. Do not sell until you know how the move will work for everyone, including when your children's own plans change.

A reverse mortgage and a sale-leaseback both turn equity into cash without a conventional sale, but they carry costs and risks that deserve a lawyer's review. They are not first steps.

## What else should you do about the house this year?

Selling the house is one decision. Other things come up in the same months.

- Add yourself as owner of the insurance policy and tell the insurer about the death.
- Update the deed and beneficiary forms so the house does not go through probate when you die. See [ways to avoid probate](/learn/probate/avoid-probate-on-your-house).
- Name new agents under your power of attorney and health care directive, because your spouse was probably your agent.
- Ask about the property tax breaks for older homeowners or surviving spouses in your county. Illinois has a homestead exemption; read our page on [the Illinois homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death).

If you want a guided look at your own situation, the [plan finder](/plan-finder) lists the documents that usually need updating.
