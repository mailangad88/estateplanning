Parents of a young adult with a disability often hear the same advice at age 17: get guardianship. Illinois now offers another option for adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities. A supported decision-making agreement lets the adult choose trusted people to help, and keeps the decisions with the adult.

This page explains what the Illinois agreement is, who qualifies, how to sign one, and how it compares with guardianship and a power of attorney. It is general education, not legal advice. It sits within our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), and the court process is covered in [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship).

## What is a supported decision-making agreement in Illinois?

It is a written agreement under the Supported Decision-Making Agreement Act (755 ILCS 9), which took effect on February 27, 2022. The adult, called the principal, names a supporter. The supporter helps the principal make everyday life decisions: medical care, where to live, work, finances and social life. The Act describes the arrangement as a less-restrictive alternative to guardianship for adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities who need help with daily-living decisions (755 ILCS 9/5).

Two points stand out.

- **The adult is presumed capable.** Illinois presumes an adult can make daily-living decisions unless a court decides otherwise. A diagnosis of a mental illness, intellectual disability or developmental disability does not by itself defeat that presumption, and neither does the way a person communicates (755 ILCS 9/15).
- **Signing proves nothing about capacity.** The agreement cannot be used as evidence that the adult has or lacks capacity, and the adult can still act on their own.

## Who can use an Illinois supported decision-making agreement?

The principal must be an adult, 18 or older, with an intellectual or developmental disability (755 ILCS 9/10). That is narrower than guardianship, which covers any adult with a disability who is not fully able to manage their person or estate. Someone with dementia, for example, falls outside the SDM Act as written, and the family would look to a [power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) while capacity lasts, or to guardianship if it does not.

The principal still has to understand what they are signing. If a person cannot understand the agreement at all, the tool will not fit and a different arrangement is needed. An attorney can help judge that.

## What does a supporter do, and not do?

Unless the agreement says otherwise, a supporter may (755 ILCS 9/30):

1. Help the principal understand options, responsibilities and consequences of a decision.
2. Help the principal get and understand relevant information, medical, psychological, financial, educational or treatment records included.
3. Help arrange and monitor services and supports.
4. Help find out the principal's wishes and advocate for them.

A supporter must act with the care, competence and diligence expected of someone in their position, and must seek training about the role. The Guardianship and Advocacy Commission provides public information, training materials and sample agreements.

A supporter may not (755 ILCS 9/35):

- Exert undue influence or make decisions on the principal's behalf.
- Obtain information unrelated to the help the principal asked for, without consent.
- Use information for any purpose other than helping with the decision.
- Be paid, except as the Act provides.

That last point surprises families. A supporter is not a paid caregiver role.

## How is it different from guardianship and a power of attorney?

| | Supported decision-making | Power of attorney | Guardianship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides | The adult | The agent acts for the adult | The guardian, within the court order |
| Court involved | No | No, unless disputed | Yes |
| Adult must understand the document | Yes | Yes | No |
| How it ends | Adult revokes at any time, or on substantiated abuse | Adult revokes while able, or it ends by its terms | Court terminates it |
| Signing rules | Two adult witnesses, statutory form | Witness and notary for the property form (755 ILCS 45/3-3) | Court petition and hearing |

The difference that matters most is who holds the pen. With a power of attorney the agent signs for you. With guardianship the court gives someone authority over you. With supported decision-making nobody can make the decision for the adult. A decision made with a supporter's assistance is recognized as the principal's decision and can be enforced by the principal or the supporter on the same basis as the principal's own decision (755 ILCS 9/60).

These tools are not mutually exclusive. A young adult might use a supported decision-making agreement for daily choices and also sign a [health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) naming a parent for emergencies, if they understand it. If the last agreement signed conflicts with an earlier one, the last signed agreement controls (755 ILCS 9/70).

## How do you sign one?

Follow these steps.

1. Decide who the supporter or supporters should be, and check the disqualification list (755 ILCS 9/20).
2. Use the statutory form in section 50 of the Act, or one that substantially follows it. It covers the supporter's duties, which decision areas the supporter may help with, releases of records, an end date and the supporter's consent.
3. Mark each decision area yes or no. Be specific. A person may want help with medical decisions and not with money.
4. The principal and supporter sign and date in front of two or more adult witnesses. The supporter cannot be a witness (755 ILCS 9/45).
5. Give copies to the doctor, bank, school or provider that will see the supporter.
6. Review the agreement from time to time and set an end date if you want one.

The Act does not require a notary. Some providers may still ask for more, so a family should call ahead. Our page on [what to do when a child with a disability turns 18](/learn/special-needs/what-to-do-when-a-child-with-a-disability-turns-18) lists the other documents that usually come up at that age.

## How does the agreement end?

The agreement lasts until terminated by either party or by its own terms (755 ILCS 9/70). The principal can revoke at any time by destroying it or having someone destroy it in their presence, by signing and dating a written revocation, or by saying so out loud in front of two witnesses. A supporter can resign by notice. The agreement ends automatically if the Office of Inspector General or Adult Protective Services substantiates abuse or neglect by the supporter, or if the principal obtains a restraining order against the supporter.

## What does it look like in real life?

Here is an example. Priya, 20, has Down syndrome and lives with her parents. She holds a part-time job and wants to keep control of her own choices. She signs an agreement naming her older sister Anita as her supporter for medical care and her aunt Meena for money questions. At a clinic visit, Anita sits in, helps Priya follow the doctor's explanation, and writes down the options in plain words. Priya chooses the treatment and tells the doctor herself. When a bank asks Priya about a savings account, Meena goes over what fees and withdrawals mean, and Priya decides.

Nobody in that picture signed for Priya. That is the point, and it is also the main limit. If a provider or bank insists on someone else's signature, a supporter cannot supply it, and the family may need a power of attorney as well. Our attorneys often see families sign one document and assume it does everything. It helps to ask the doctor's office and the bank how they treat the agreement before relying on it.

## Does it replace special needs planning?

No. It governs decisions, not money. A young adult who may receive benefits or an inheritance still needs a special needs trust or other plan, covered in [special needs planning in Illinois for a disabled child](/learn/illinois/special-needs-planning-in-illinois-for-a-disabled-child).
