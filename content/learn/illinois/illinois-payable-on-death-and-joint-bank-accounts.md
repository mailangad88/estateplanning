Bank accounts are where a lot of Illinois families make their first estate plan, usually by accident. A parent puts a daughter's name on a savings account "just in case." Or a widow signs a form at the credit union that says "POD" and never thinks about it again. At death those short forms decide who gets the money, ahead of anything in a will.

This page explains the three common set-ups in Illinois (see also our [Illinois pillar](/learn/illinois)), a payable-on-death account, a trust account and a joint account, and the traps in each. For the national overview of POD and TOD accounts, see our general article on [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts). The Illinois rules come mostly from the Trust and Payable on Death Accounts Act (205 ILCS 625) and the Joint Tenancy Act (765 ILCS 1005). This is general education, not legal advice.

## How does an Illinois payable-on-death account work?

You sign an agreement with the bank, savings association or credit union saying the account will pass to one or more named beneficiaries when the last account holder dies. The account and whatever balance it holds from time to time is then a POD account (205 ILCS 625/4). The Act covers banks, savings and loan associations, savings banks and credit unions, and comparable federal institutions (205 ILCS 625/2).

Unless the holders and the institution agree otherwise in writing, these rules apply during life:

- Any holder may change the beneficiary with a written instrument the institution accepts. The other holders and the beneficiary do not have to agree or even know.
- Any holder may add money or withdraw some or all of it at any time. Each withdrawal revokes the POD arrangement as to the amount taken out.
- The beneficiary has no claim on the money while the holders are alive.

That last point is why POD is popular with people who want to keep full control. Dolores, 81, names her son Andre as POD beneficiary on a $60,000 certificate of deposit. She can spend it, move it, or switch to her daughter next month. Andre cannot object.

## What happens at death?

Ownership turns on who is alive and in existence when the last holder dies (205 ILCS 625/4).

1. **One beneficiary alive.** That beneficiary takes the account as sole owner. A person must be living; a trust or charity must still exist under the law that created it.
2. **More than one beneficiary alive.** They take equal shares as tenants in common, with no right of survivorship between them. If one of three children has died, the other two split the account, and the deceased child's own children get nothing from it unless the agreement says so.
3. **No beneficiary alive or existing.** The proceeds go to the estate of the last surviving holder, which usually means probate, or a [small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) if the whole estate qualifies.

The split rule in step two is the one that most often disappoints families. If you want a grandchild to step into a deceased parent's place, a bank form rarely does that on its own. Ask the bank what its form allows, or use a trust.

## How is a trust account different?

A trust account, sometimes called a Totten trust, is held in the name of a trustee for designated beneficiaries under a signed agreement with the institution. The statute spells out its defaults (205 ILCS 625/3):

- Several trustees hold the account as joint tenants with right of survivorship unless they agree otherwise.
- Any trustee may change the beneficiaries, deposit, or withdraw without anyone's consent. Each withdrawal revokes the arrangement as to that amount.
- When the last trustee dies, a surviving beneficiary becomes the sole owner. Several survivors share equally as tenants in common. If none survives, the funds go to the last trustee's estate.

In practice the result looks like a POD account. The label on the signature card is what matters, so read yours.

## What about a joint account?

A joint account is a different animal, because the co-owner has access now. Illinois law lets a bank pay a deposit held in two or more names to any one of them, even after another has died, and a signed agreement among the holders protects the bank when it does (765 ILCS 1005/2). Credit union and savings association shares in joint names work the same way.

That is convenient, and it comes with costs.

| Question | POD account | Joint account |
|---|---|---|
| Can the second person withdraw today? | No | Yes |
| Can the second person's creditors reach it? | Not while the owner lives | Often a risk, since the co-owner has access |
| Who decides at death? | The beneficiary form | The account agreement and survivorship terms |
| Does a will control? | No | No, for money passing by survivorship |
| Fits a plan to share equally among children? | Yes, with several beneficiaries | Only if all children are on the account |

The creditor row is general information and depends on the facts; ask an attorney before relying on it.

The common mistake is the "convenience" joint account. Arlene adds her daughter Priya to help pay bills. At Arlene's death the bank pays Priya as the survivor, and Arlene's son Neil, who was supposed to share, finds the money was never part of the estate. Whether Arlene meant a gift or just wanted help is a question families end up arguing about. A power of attorney, rather than a joint name, is the cleaner way to get a helper. See our article on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).

## What are the traps?

- **Naming the estate or no one.** A form left blank means no POD. The account is simply the owner's, and it goes through the estate.
- **A beneficiary who dies first.** Without a backup, the account may drop into probate. Ask the bank whether it allows a contingent beneficiary.
- **Minors.** A child under 18 cannot easily collect, and a court may be needed to appoint someone to manage the money. Our article on [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) covers the options.
- **Unequal results by accident.** One child on a POD account holding most of the money can undo an equal-shares will. Our guide to [leaving unequal shares with beneficiary forms](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-unequal-shares-with-beneficiary-forms) covers the pattern.
- **Debts and taxes.** POD money is not part of the probate estate, but whether it stays out of reach of a creditor is a separate question. See [credit card debt after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois).

## Should you use a POD account, or a will and a trust?

It depends on how much is in the account and how neat your family is. A POD account is a good fit for a modest emergency fund you want to reach one named person quickly, with no court. It is a poor fit when the beneficiaries are minors, when one child is disabled and receives government benefits, or when the account is the largest asset you own and your will says something else.

Consider Gordon, 69, with a $240,000 savings account at one bank and a house. His will leaves everything to his three children equally. Years ago he named his youngest, Leah, POD on the savings account because she lived nearby. If nothing changes, Leah receives $240,000 and the others split the house. Nothing in the will can fix that. Gordon's choices are to name all three children on the account, to move the account into a [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts), or to accept the unequal result on purpose and say so in writing to the family.

A review once every few years catches most of these. Beneficiary forms get stale after a death, a divorce, or a move to another bank.

## How does a beneficiary collect?

1. Order several certified death certificates.
2. Call the bank and ask for its deceased account holder department. Do not move money first.
3. Bring the certificate, your photo ID and the account number or statement.
4. Sign the bank's claim form and ask for the funds in your name.
5. If the bank says the beneficiary form is missing, ask for the signature card and agreement on file, and bring the matter to an attorney.
