The weeks after a death are when bills arrive, accounts freeze and a spouse may have to keep paying a mortgage and groceries. Probate can take a year or more. Illinois has a way to put money in the family's hands during that time, called the spouse's award, with a related award for minor children.

Our article on [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) introduces the award alongside renunciation of a will. This page is the how-to: who qualifies, how the court sets the amount, how it is claimed and where it ranks among the estate's bills. Our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) lists other topics.

## What is the spouse's award?

It is a sum the court awards a surviving spouse, from the estate, for support. Under 755 ILCS 5/15-1, the court sets what it finds reasonable for the spouse's support for nine months after the death, in light of the spouse's way of life and the estate's size. The award cannot be less than $20,000, and the court adds at least $10,000 for each minor child of the decedent who lived with the spouse. As of 2026, confirm the figures on ilga.gov, since the legislature can change them.

The award applies whether or not there is a will. It is an extra amount. A spouse who inherits $150,000 under the will can still claim the award, unless the will says its gifts take the place of the award and the spouse does not renounce.

## Who qualifies and for how much?

The spouse of an Illinois resident whose estate is administered in Illinois qualifies. A civil union partner has the same legal protections as a spouse (750 ILCS 75/20). The amount depends on the household.

| Household at death | Minimum award |
|---|---|
| Spouse alone, no minor children at home | $20,000 |
| Spouse and one minor child of the decedent at home | $30,000 |
| Spouse and two minor children at home | $40,000 |
| Spouse and three minor children at home | $50,000 |

The court can award more than the minimum when the spouse's usual living costs and the estate justify it. A spouse who is used to $8,000 a month in household costs may ask for more than $20,000, and the judge weighs this against the debts and other heirs. Stepchildren who are not the decedent's children are generally not counted in the child add-on.

An adult child who was financially dependent on the decedent, lived with the spouse and may become a public charge can sometimes ask for an award of at least $5,000. The statute sets a 30-day deadline to give written notice, so ask an attorney quickly if this applies (755 ILCS 5/15-1).

## How do you claim the award, step by step?

1. Confirm that a probate case is open in the county where the decedent lived, or that one will be opened.
2. Collect the death certificate, a list of the household's monthly costs and the names and ages of children in the home.
3. Ask the estate's lawyer or the circuit clerk how your county handles the petition or request for the award.
4. File the request with the court. If you are not the executor, tell the executor in writing.
5. Attend the hearing if the court sets one. Bring proof of living costs.
6. Get a written order that states the amount and the time of payment. The court can order payment in no more than three installments.
7. Keep records of how the money is used, and keep the order with the estate papers.

## How does the award rank against debts?

It ranks high. In Illinois the claims against an estate are paid in classes (755 ILCS 5/18-10), and as we read the statute, the spouse's or child's award is in the second class, after funeral and burial costs and the costs of administering the estate. It ranks ahead of federal and state taxes and most ordinary creditors, such as credit cards. The statute also says the award is exempt from judgments, garnishment and attachment while held by the estate's representative. Our page on [creditor claims in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) shows the full order.

## What does a worked example look like?

Lena, 61, is widowed when her husband, Paul, dies. Their daughter, Ava, 15, lives at home. Paul's probate estate is $85,000. Paul's will leaves everything to Lena. The bills are: funeral $11,000, court and administration costs $6,000, a credit card $14,000 and a medical bill $9,000.

1. The court sets the award at the minimum of $30,000: $20,000 for Lena plus $10,000 for Ava.
2. The estate pays funeral and administration first: $17,000.
3. The award comes next: $30,000. That makes $47,000.
4. The remaining $38,000 pays the credit card and medical bill ($23,000) and leaves $15,000.
5. Lena receives the award of $30,000 plus the $15,000 left under the will.

Without the award, Lena would receive $15,000. If the estate had been $50,000, the award would still be paid ahead of the credit card, which could go unpaid in part.

## How does the award fit with a small estate or the homestead?

For a small estate, the affidavit's list of payments includes the spouse's award. See the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit). The award is separate from the homestead exemption, which protects a home from certain creditors. Read more in the [homestead exemption article](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death) and in the general piece on [family allowance and homestead in probate](/learn/probate/family-allowance-and-homestead-in-probate).

## What can reduce or remove the award?

Three things. A signed prenuptial or postnuptial agreement may waive the right. A will can state that its gifts are in place of the award. And the court may find the amount excessive for the estate. See [prenups and inheritance rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-prenup-and-inheritance-rights).

## What mistakes should a spouse avoid?

Do not spend the family's cash on estate debts that the executor should pay. Do not assume the award arrives automatically: the court must order it, and in the meantime the spouse may need help with immediate bills. Do not sign a release or waiver from an estate lawyer without reading how it treats the award. Do not forget the child add-on if minor children live at home. And do not assume the award replaces a renunciation: they are separate rights, with different deadlines, as explained in our page on [renouncing a will](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death).

If cash is tight right away, ask the executor or the court about a partial early payment, and ask the funeral home about payment timing. A short call to the circuit clerk can tell you how your county handles these requests.
