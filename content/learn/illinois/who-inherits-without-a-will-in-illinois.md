Many people assume that if they die without a will, everything goes to their spouse. In Illinois that is not always true. The state has a fixed list of heirs, and it applies to anyone who dies without a valid will, whatever they might have wanted.

This page explains that list. It covers who comes first, how the shares are split, who is left out, and what is not covered by these rules. For other Illinois topics, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). For the general idea in every state, read about [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## What does Illinois law say about dying without a will?

The rules are in the Illinois Probate Act, 755 ILCS 5/2-1. A person who dies without a valid will is called [intestate](/glossary/intestate). The law sorts relatives into groups, and a closer group shuts out the groups below it. No one gets to skip ahead.

The rules apply only to the probate estate. That is the property in the decedent's own name with no beneficiary or co-owner. See our explainer on the [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate) for details. A court appoints an administrator to collect the assets and pay the bills. Our [Illinois probate guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) explains that process.

## Who inherits first? The order of heirs

Here is the order, from the statute.

| If the decedent leaves... | Then the estate goes to... |
| --- | --- |
| A spouse and descendants | One-half to the spouse and one-half to the descendants, per stirpes |
| Descendants, no spouse | All to the descendants, per stirpes |
| A spouse, no descendants | All to the spouse |
| No spouse or descendants, but a parent, sibling, or a sibling's descendants | To the parents, brothers and sisters in equal parts. If one parent has died, the surviving parent gets a double share. A deceased sibling's descendants take that sibling's share |
| None of the above, but grandparents or their descendants | Half to the maternal side and half to the paternal side. If one side has no one, the other side takes all |
| None of the above, but great-grandparents or their descendants | Split the same way, half and half by side |
| None of the above | The nearest kin by degree, in equal shares |
| No spouse and no known kin | The county or the state (escheat) |

"Descendants" means children, grandchildren and so on. [Per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes) means that if a child has died, that child's own children share the parent's portion.

## Examples

**A spouse and children.** Elena dies with no will. She is survived by her husband Marcus and two children from her first marriage. Her probate estate is $400,000. Marcus gets $200,000. Each child gets $100,000. Marcus is not the children's parent, but the split is the same. This is one reason [blended families](/learn/blended-families) need a plan.

**A spouse and no children.** Daniel dies with no will and no children. His wife Grace is alive, and so are his parents. Grace takes the whole estate. His parents get nothing, because the spouse is first when there are no descendants.

**Parents and siblings.** Dev is single with no children. His father has died. His mother and one brother are alive. The statute splits the estate among parents, brothers and sisters in equal parts, with a double portion for the surviving parent. His mother takes two shares and his brother takes one, so two-thirds and one-third.

## Who gets nothing?

Some people feel like family but are not on the list.

- **Unmarried partners.** A couple that lives together for decades has no inheritance rights without a will. A civil union partner is treated the same as a spouse under Illinois law (750 ILCS 75/20).
- **Stepchildren.** They are not heirs unless adopted. See our article on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance).
- **Friends, caregivers and charities.** None of them inherit without a will or a beneficiary designation.
- **In-laws and ex-spouses.** A former spouse is no longer a spouse once the marriage ends.

## How are children and adopted children treated?

The statute treats children in several settings.

- **Adopted children** are children of the adopting parent (755 ILCS 5/2-4). Rules limit when they also inherit from their birth family.
- **Children born outside marriage** can inherit. For a parent to inherit from the child, the parent must have acknowledged the child, built a relationship and supported the child (755 ILCS 5/2-2). Paternity is shown by a court judgment or by clear and convincing evidence.
- **Posthumous children.** A child in the womb when the parent died inherits as if born during the parent's life (755 ILCS 5/2-3). Children conceived later face strict conditions, including written consent and notice to the estate within set time limits.

## What does a family do after someone dies without a will?

Start by looking for a will. Check the home, a safe deposit box and the person's attorney. Illinois law requires anyone holding a will to file it with the court clerk, so a will may turn up after a search. If none exists, the family opens an intestate estate in the circuit court of the county where the person lived.

The court names an administrator, usually a spouse or an adult child. The administrator lists the assets, publishes notice to creditors, pays debts and taxes, and then distributes what is left under the table above. This takes months. Our guide to [Illinois probate](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) walks through each step. If the estate is small, ask whether a simpler process fits.

## What does the Illinois intestacy law not cover?

Intestacy only reaches probate assets. These pass by their own rules:

- Accounts with a named beneficiary, such as a 401(k), an IRA or life insurance.
- Joint accounts and property held in joint tenancy with survivorship.
- Property in a living trust.
- Real estate with a recorded transfer on death instrument.

A spouse who receives a small share, or none, may have other rights. We cover those in [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death).

## What if there are no heirs?

If there is no spouse and no known kin, the real estate escheats to the county where it sits. Personal property in Illinois goes to the county where the decedent lived. Other personal property goes to the state and is delivered to the State Treasurer (755 ILCS 5/2-1(h)). It is rare, but it is the final step in the list.

## How do you avoid these rules?

A valid will lets you choose your own heirs, name an executor and, for parents, nominate a guardian for children. See [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) for how to sign one so that it holds up. A will can also leave gifts to people the statute leaves out. If you want to keep family from receiving a share, see [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).
