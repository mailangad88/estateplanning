A hospital call comes late at night. Your father is on a ventilator after a stroke, and the doctor needs to know what the family wants. You are the health care agent named in his form. You are not alone in feeling unready. Most agents have never faced this.

This page explains how Illinois agents approach life support decisions, with questions to ask and a way to record what you decide. For the medical meaning of each treatment, see [what it means to refuse life support](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-it-means-to-refuse-life-support). The [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links the related forms.

## What does the agent's authority cover?

The Illinois statutory health care form (755 ILCS 45/4-10) lets the principal name an agent to make health care decisions when the principal cannot. It includes space for the principal to state wishes about life-prolonging treatment. Our article on the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) covers signing and witness rules, and [witness rules](/learn/illinois/witness-rules-for-an-illinois-health-care-power-of-attorney) explain who can sign.

The agent is a decision maker for the patient. The role does not turn the agent into the owner of the patient's body. That is why the first step is always to learn what the patient wanted.

## How do you decide, step by step?

1. Read the documents. Look at the health care power of attorney, any living will, any POLST or DNR order and any notes the patient wrote.
2. Ask the doctor for a plain summary: the diagnosis, the likely course, the chances of recovery and what life would be like if the person recovers.
3. Ask what each treatment would do, what burdens come with it, and what happens if it is not used.
4. Recall what the patient told you about illness, independence and what matters most.
5. Ask whether a time-limited trial, such as a set number of days on a ventilator, is reasonable.
6. Meet with family so everyone hears the same facts from the same doctor.
7. Decide as the patient would, and state the decision clearly to the doctor.
8. Ask that the order is written in the chart, and keep your own notes of date, time and names.

## What should you ask about each treatment?

| Treatment | What it does | Questions to ask the doctor |
|---|---|---|
| CPR | Tries to restart the heart | What are the odds in this condition, and what are the risks if it works? |
| Ventilator | Breathes for the patient | Is recovery expected? Can we set a time limit? |
| Feeding tube | Delivers nutrition and fluids | Will it improve comfort or only prolong the condition? |
| Dialysis | Replaces kidney function | What is the expected gain, and how many visits a week? |
| Antibiotics | Treat infections | Does treatment fit the person's goals? |
| Comfort-focused care | Treats pain and distress | What does hospice or palliative care add? |

Our pages on [palliative care and hospice](/learn/healthcare-directives/palliative-care-hospice-and-comfort-care-orders) and [DNR and POLST orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) explain these paths. The Illinois version of the POLST is covered in [Illinois POLST and DNR orders](/learn/illinois/illinois-polst-and-dnr-orders).

## What if the patient's wishes are not clear?

Use the person's values. Ask what mattered most: independence, being at home, time with grandchildren, avoiding pain. Think about how the person reacted when a friend was ill. Then ask the care team how each option fits those values. Many agents find it helpful to say out loud, "If Dad could tell us now, he would say..."

A written living will can help too. Our overview of the [Illinois Living Will Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-will-act-what-it-covers-and-when-it-applies) explains when it applies. If there is no agent at all, the Health Care Surrogate Act sets a priority list of decision makers, as explained in [who decides under the Illinois Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).

## What if the doctor will not carry out the decision?

A doctor or hospital may decline for reasons of conscience. Ask for a transfer to another doctor or facility, and ask the hospital's ethics committee or patient advocate to help. Our article on [conscience clauses and living wills](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-is-a-conscience-clause-and-can-a-doctor-decline-your-living-will) covers the topic. A decision to continue treatment is also allowed if that is what the patient wanted.

## What if the family disagrees?

Hold a family meeting with the doctor and, if available, a social worker or chaplain. Family members who disagree with the agent can state their views, but the agent named in the form carries the authority. Our article on [who can overrule a health care directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive) explains the limits. If the conflict escalates, an attorney can advise about court options.

## Example: Dolores decides for her husband

Dolores, 68, is the agent for her husband Frank, 71, who has advanced lung disease and has been on a ventilator for four days after pneumonia. His form says he does not want to be kept on machines "if there is no reasonable hope of getting back to a life where I know my family." The doctor says the lungs may recover in one to two weeks, but the chance is about one in three. Dolores asks for a seven-day trial, with a meeting on day seven. She writes the plan in her notes, tells their two sons, and asks the doctor to write it in the chart. On day seven there is no progress, and she and the care team move to comfort-focused care, as Frank's form instructed.

## How can you prepare ahead?

If you are the principal, talk with your agent now, write a short letter of values, and sign a POLST if your doctor recommends one. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) can help you see which documents you have. If you are the agent, keep a copy of the form in your phone and in your wallet.

## How do you care for yourself while deciding?

The agent role is heavy. A few habits make it easier to carry.

- Take notes at every meeting with the care team, with names and dates, and ask them to repeat anything you did not follow.
- Ask one person, such as a sibling, to handle updates to the wider family so you do not repeat the same news all day.
- Ask for a palliative care or social work consult. They can help the family talk, even when the patient is expected to recover.
- Sleep and eat. Decisions made after 20 hours awake can feel different the next morning, and the team can often wait a few hours.
- Remember that following the patient's wishes is a form of care, even when the outcome is sad.

If the patient dies, your authority as health care agent ends. Our article on [a health care agent's authority after death](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-agent-authority-after-death) explains what happens next, including who handles funeral and organ donation questions.
