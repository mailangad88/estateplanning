You are thinking about leaving one of your children out of your will. Maybe you have not spoken in years, maybe a child has already received a large share during your life, or maybe a son's creditors would take whatever you left. Your first question is whether Illinois lets you do it. It does, with a few exceptions that matter.

This page covers what the law allows, the exceptions, a step-by-step way to do it clearly, what slips past a will, and how a contest tends to go. The general rules outside Illinois are in our page on [disinheriting someone or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares), and the rest of the state's rules are in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Can you legally disinherit a child in Illinois?

Yes. Illinois law does not set aside a minimum share for children. If your will leaves your property to others, a child you left out cannot demand a part of it only because they are your child. That is different from a few places in the world where children are protected by forced heirship. It is also different from a spouse, which we cover below.

The rule has two practical limits. First, it applies only if you have a valid will or trust. With no will, Illinois intestacy gives your children a share by default (see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois)), so a spoken wish or a note in a drawer does nothing. Second, your plan has to hold up against a challenge, which is the subject of most of this page.

## Which children and family members are protected?

Three situations sit outside the general rule:

| Person | Protection | Where to read more |
|---|---|---|
| Surviving spouse | Can renounce the will and take a statutory share; also has a support award | [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) |
| Child born after the will was signed | Can claim an intestate share unless the will provides for the child or shows an intent to disinherit (755 ILCS 5/4-10) | [Children born after an Illinois will](/learn/illinois/children-born-after-an-illinois-will-and-lapsed-gifts) |
| Adult child who was financially dependent, lived with the surviving spouse and is likely to become a public charge | May apply for an award of at least $5,000, with a 30-day written notice deadline (755 ILCS 5/15-1(a-5)) | [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) |

The last row applies in narrow facts and runs through the spouse's estate rights. It is a claim for support, not a share of the estate. Most disinherited adult children fall under none of the three rows.

## How do you disinherit a child so it holds up?

Follow these steps in order.

1. **Decide on the document.** A will works, and so does a revocable living trust. A trust keeps the terms private, since a will becomes a public court record once it is filed. Our page on [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) explains the choice.
2. **Name the child and state your intent.** Something like: "I have intentionally made no provision in this will for my son, Daniel. This is not an oversight." Naming the child makes it hard to argue that you forgot.
3. **Name the beneficiaries who take instead.** Say who receives the share and whether it passes to Daniel's own children. If you want the grandchildren cut out too, say so clearly. Under Illinois rules, a gift to a descendant who dies usually passes to that person's descendants unless the will says otherwise (755 ILCS 5/4-11), and you can use that rule or override it.
4. **Sign with the required formalities.** Follow [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) exactly. A flawed signing is the easiest way to lose a plan.
5. **Match every other document.** Check each beneficiary form and title, as the next section explains.
6. **Keep proof of capacity.** A short note from the attorney who supervised the signing, and the fact that you used independent advice, help later.
7. **Review when life changes.** A new baby, a death or a reconciliation can all change your decision. See [changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois).

## What can a will not stop?

A will controls only what passes under it. Several kinds of property go straight to a named person or a surviving co-owner, and a child can receive them even if the will excludes the child.

- **Beneficiary forms** on life insurance, IRAs and 401(k) plans pay whoever is named.
- **Payable-on-death and joint accounts** pass by the bank's form. See [payable-on-death and joint bank accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts).
- **Joint tenancy property** goes to the surviving owner. See [Illinois joint tenancy](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).
- **A transfer on death instrument** on real estate goes to the named person. See [the Illinois TODI](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).

Here is the mistake our attorneys see most. Marlene, 71, signs a new will that omits her son Kevin after a long estrangement. She forgets that years ago she added Kevin as a joint owner on her savings account "for emergencies." When she dies, the account passes to Kevin by survivorship and never touches the will. The other children get less than Marlene intended, and one of them contests the will in frustration, which does not fix anything. An audit of every account would have caught it. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) walks through the forms.

## How does a contest by a disinherited child work?

A disinherited child who wants to fight must show the will is not valid. Being left out is not enough. The usual grounds are:

- **Lack of capacity.** The parent did not understand the property, the family or the document when signing.
- **Undue influence.** Someone with a position of trust pressured the parent so the will reflects that person's wishes. See [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence).
- **Defective signing.** The will was not witnessed or signed correctly.
- **Revocation or a later will.** A different document controls.

In Illinois the petition must be filed within 6 months after the will is admitted to probate, and a missed notice does not extend the time (755 ILCS 5/8-1). The petition is filed in the estate. Either side may ask for a jury. Our page on the [six-month will contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) lays out the sequence and our general page on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) covers the grounds.

Contests succeed less often than families fear when the plan is documented, and more often when the parent changed the will late in life, left everything to a caregiver and had no independent witness. Courts look closely at a caregiver who also arranged the signing. A clear explanation in the parent's own words, a meeting with a lawyer alone, and a medical note close to the signing date all cut the risk.

## Should you use a no-contest clause?

Maybe, but it is a weak tool against a child who gets nothing. The clause works by threatening a gift, and a person with no gift has nothing to lose. It has more force when you leave a disinherited child a smaller amount. We cover the trade-offs in [no-contest clauses](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will), and how courts treat them differs by state.

## Are there gentler alternatives?

Often there are. A reduced share gives the child something to protect. A trust with a trustee who decides distributions keeps money away from a child with addiction or debts without cutting the child out. A lifetime gift that you record, with a letter explaining it, may satisfy the fairness question. Dana, 66, gives her daughter Rae $60,000 toward a down payment and states in her will that Rae's share is reduced by that gift. The family sees the arithmetic, which helps.
