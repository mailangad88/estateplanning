You love your child, and you are scared of what a check with their name on it could do. Maybe they have been through treatment twice. Maybe they are doing well right now, and you are afraid to jinx it by planning for a relapse. Either way, you want to leave them something without it becoming the thing that hurts them.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), walks through the choices in the order most Illinois parents face them: where the money should go, which Illinois trust rules protect it, who should hold the keys, and how to keep things fair with your other children. It is general education, not legal advice. If you would like to talk it through, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-to-leave-money-to-a-child-with-addiction-in-illinois&topic=trust) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Why is leaving the money outright risky?

Because an outright gift arrives all at once and belongs to your child the moment it lands. No one can slow it down, steer it toward rent, or hold it back during a bad month. That is true whether it comes through your will, a [payable on death bank account](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts), a life insurance policy or a retirement account.

Beneficiary forms are where most plans quietly fail. A parent signs a careful trust, then forgets that the 401(k) and the life insurance still name each child by name. Those forms override the will and the trust. The fix is to name the trust, or the child's separate share of it, on each form. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you list every account, and [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) covers the wording. Retirement accounts left to a trust also follow the federal 10-year payout rule for most non-spouse beneficiaries, so the trust should be drafted with that in mind.

Outright money also has nowhere to hide from your child's creditors. A trust changes that.

## What Illinois trust rules protect the inheritance?

Two sections of the Illinois Trust Code do most of the work. They are explained in full on our page about [protecting assets from creditors with a trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-protect-assets-from-creditors-with-a-trust-in-illinois), so here is only what a parent needs to know.

- **Spendthrift clause (760 ILCS 3/502).** Your child cannot sell, pledge or borrow against their interest, and the phrase "spendthrift trust" is enough to create the restraint.
- **Trustee discretion (760 ILCS 3/504).** When the trustee decides whether to pay, a creditor of your child cannot force a payment or attach the interest.

The protection has edges, and you should plan around them. A child, spouse or former spouse with a support order can reach the trust, as can certain Illinois and federal claims (760 ILCS 3/503). And a payment the trust says the trustee *must* make, once overdue, can be reached despite the clause (760 ILCS 3/506). That last rule is a quiet argument against "pay my son $2,000 every month" language. Words like "may pay" protect more than "shall pay."

For a child who already receives SSI or Medicaid, or may need them, the trust needs special needs terms instead. Do not try to adapt an ordinary trust on your own; read [special needs planning in Illinois](/learn/illinois/special-needs-planning-in-illinois-for-a-disabled-child) and [special needs trust rules in Illinois](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-rules-in-illinois) first.

## Which trust terms should you choose?

The terms are where your knowledge of your child goes into the document. This table pairs common terms with what they do and where they can go wrong.

| Term | What it does | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Spendthrift clause | Stops your child from signing away or borrowing against the share | Does not stop support orders or some government claims |
| Full trustee discretion | Trustee decides if, when and how much to pay | Needs a trustee with judgment and a spine |
| Direct payment power | Trustee pays rent, utilities, insurance and treatment to the provider | Your child may feel watched; explain it as help, not control |
| Treatment as a stated purpose | Lets the trustee pay for rehab, therapy, sober living and medication | List these clearly so no one argues it is outside the trust |
| Testing or treatment proof before cash | Trustee may ask for it when there are warning signs | Make it optional, not automatic, to avoid daily conflict |
| Recovery step-up | Trustee may increase support or release funds after stability | Avoid hard numbers like "exactly 24 months" |
| Lifetime trust with a remainder | Supports the child for life, then passes to grandchildren or siblings | Name who gets what is left |
| Trust protector | A named person can replace the trustee or adjust terms | Pick someone neutral, and say what they can and cannot change |

The national companion article, [trust for an adult child with mental illness or addiction](/learn/special-needs/trust-for-an-adult-child-with-mental-illness-or-addiction), discusses the benefits question in more depth. The distribution language itself is covered in [trust distribution standards](/learn/trusts/trust-distribution-standards-hems-and-discretion).

## Should a sibling or a professional be the trustee?

Choose the person who can tell your child no, kindly, for as long as it takes, and still come to Thanksgiving. Sometimes that is a sibling. Often it is not.

A sibling knows your child, costs less and can tell a real need from a story. But a sibling trustee faces the same phone calls at midnight, the same guilt and sometimes the same pressure from their own spouse. Being the "bank" can strain a sibling bond that your child will need long after the money is gone. A professional or corporate trustee takes the emotion out, keeps records and follows the rules, but charges a fee and does not know that your son always asks for money right before a slip.

Many Illinois families split the role:

1. A sibling, aunt or close friend serves as the trustee who makes distribution decisions.
2. A bank or trust company serves as co-trustee or agent for investments and record keeping.
3. A trust protector can replace either one if the arrangement stops working.

Whoever serves will have duties under Illinois law, including telling beneficiaries the trust exists within 90 days and giving yearly accountings (760 ILCS 3/813.1). Our page on the [Illinois trustee's duty to inform beneficiaries](/learn/illinois/illinois-trustee-duty-to-inform-beneficiaries) explains what that looks like, and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) covers the general tradeoffs. Never make the struggling child the only trustee of their own share.

## Do incentive terms work?

They can, but only when they are flexible. A trust that rewards recovery, for example by letting the trustee match earned income or pay for school after a period of stability, gives your child something to move toward. Rigid terms tend to fail. An automatic cutoff after one positive test can push a child away from the only support they have. A required payout at age 40 assumes recovery runs on a calendar.

A good rule: write your goals, then give the trustee the power to act on them. "My hope is that Daniel stays in treatment and builds a stable life, and I want the trustee to support that generously" ages better than a points system. A free [spendthrift and incentive trust guide](/free/spendthrift-and-incentive-trust-guide) has sample wording to bring to your meeting.

## What happens if your child recovers?

Plan for hope, not just risk. Recovery is common, and a trust that was right for a 28-year-old in crisis may feel like a cage to a 45-year-old with ten years sober and a family.

You can build in a way out. The trust can let the trustee make larger distributions, name the child as co-trustee after a stretch of stability, or end the trust early. Even without those terms, Illinois offers routes to change an irrevocable trust later, including decanting by the trustee, a nonjudicial settlement agreement among the interested people, and court modification. Our article on [changing an irrevocable trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-an-irrevocable-trust-in-illinois-decanting-and-court-modification) covers each one. A [trust protector](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-trust-protector-and-decanting) gives you a simpler, named person to make those calls.

## How do you keep it fair with your other children?

Separate the amount from the delivery. Your three children can each receive one-third, and only one third goes into a trust. Illinois gives adult children no forced share, so you may also leave unequal amounts, as explained in [can you disinherit a child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-illinois). Most parents in this situation choose equal shares delivered differently.

Example. Linda, 66, lives in Joliet with an estate of a house and about $450,000 in savings and a 401(k). Her son Daniel, 34, has been in and out of treatment for opioid use. Her daughters, Erin and Kate, are steady. Linda's living trust gives each child a third. Erin and Kate receive theirs outright. Daniel's third stays in a lifetime spendthrift trust with Kate as trustee and a trust company handling investments. Kate may pay Daniel's rent, health insurance and treatment directly, and may give him a monthly allowance she sets. If Daniel has two years of stability, Kate may release more. When Daniel dies, what remains goes to his children, or to his sisters if he has none. Linda updates her 401(k) and life insurance forms to name the trust for Daniel's share, and writes Kate a two-page letter about what has helped Daniel and what has not. These details are illustrative.

Two small steps prevent hard feelings. Tell your other children about the plan, and tell the trustee sibling why you chose them. If one child has already received large sums for rehab or legal bills during your life, Illinois's advancement rule (755 ILCS 5/2-5) counts those gifts only in limited cases, usually with a clear writing, so say in your will or trust whether you want them counted. See [advancements and lifetime gifts](/learn/illinois/advancements-lifetime-gifts-and-inheritance-in-illinois).

## What else should be in place?

A trust handles money. Your child's own documents handle a crisis. If they are willing, an adult child can sign an [Illinois Declaration for Mental Health Treatment](/learn/illinois/illinois-declaration-for-mental-health-treatment), a health care power of attorney and a HIPAA release while they are well. For the asset side, the free [Illinois inheritance creditor protection guide](/free/illinois-inheritance-creditor-protection-guide) shows which inherited assets creditors can reach, and the broader [guide to keeping an inheritance safe from a child's divorce, addiction or bad spouse](/learn/trusts/keeping-an-inheritance-safe-from-a-childs-divorce-addiction-or-bad-spouse) covers marriage-related risks.
