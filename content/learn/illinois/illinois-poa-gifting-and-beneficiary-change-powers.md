Most Illinois families sign a power of attorney expecting the agent can "handle everything." Then a crisis arrives. The agent wants to move money to a trust, give to a grandchild, or fix a beneficiary form, and the bank says the document does not allow it. This is by design.

This page explains the Illinois rule and shows how to write gift and beneficiary powers if you choose to grant them. For the general idea in all states, see [whether a power of attorney can make gifts](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts). For the base form, see our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois).

## What does section 3-4 say?

Section 3-4 of the Power of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45/3-4) defines each power on the short form. For the listed powers, it says the agent cannot make gifts, appoint property to others or change a beneficiary designation, unless the form gives specific authority. The same section says an agent cannot make or amend your will, and cannot revoke or amend a trust unless the document grants that power and refers to the trust.

That default protects you. An agent with broad money powers could otherwise give your savings to himself or herself. It also blocks legitimate planning, which is why you decide on purpose.

## Which powers do you decide on, one by one?

| Power | Default on the short form | If you want it | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual gifts to family | Not allowed | Name recipients, set a yearly cap | Agent shares unequally or runs the account dry |
| Gifts to the agent | Not allowed | Say yes or no, with a cap | Conflict of interest, claims of undue influence |
| Beneficiary changes | Not allowed | Name the account types and limits | Changes that cut out a beneficiary |
| Funding your living trust | Not clear | Name the trust and allow deeds and transfers | Property left outside the trust anyway |
| Amending or revoking a trust | Not allowed | Cite the trust and the limited change | Undoing your plan |
| Medicaid planning gifts | Not allowed | Require attorney advice and a written plan | Penalty period from the look-back |
| Disclaiming an inheritance | Not clear | Say whether allowed | Gives up property you might need |
| Forgiving debts owed to you | Not clear | Say whether allowed | Quiet loss of assets |

Read the table as a starting point. The right answers depend on your family and your accounts.

## How do you write a gift clause, step by step?

1. List why you want gifts: tax savings, helping grandchildren with school, or supporting a child.
2. Name the recipients or the class, such as "my three children and their descendants".
3. Set a maximum per recipient per year. Many families use the federal annual exclusion, $19,000 per recipient in 2026, but the number is your choice.
4. State whether the agent may receive gifts, and if so, cap them at the same level as everyone else.
5. Add a rule that gifts must not leave you without enough money for your own care.
6. Require a record of each gift in the agent's ledger.
7. Write the clause in the added powers space, and have the document signed before one witness and a notary as usual.

The sample words might be: "My agent may give up to $19,000 per calendar year to each of my children, in equal amounts, if my own needs are met."

## What about beneficiary designations?

Beneficiary forms for IRAs, life insurance and annuities usually control over your will. An agent who can change them can reshape your estate. Many people refuse that power. If you grant it, limit it. For example, allow the agent to correct a beneficiary only to match what you already decided, or to name a trust you created, and never to name the agent. Our article on [beneficiary changes by an agent or guardian](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary) covers how late changes get challenged, and the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you list every form.

## How does gifting interact with Medicaid?

Gifts count against you. Most states, including Illinois, look back 60 months at transfers when you apply for long-term care Medicaid. An agent who gives away money without a plan can create a penalty period. If you want gifts to be part of a plan, write that into the document and talk to an attorney first. Our articles on [Illinois Medicaid nursing home planning](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) and the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explain the timing.

## Example: Anita decides what to grant

Anita, 76, has $480,000 in savings, a house, and three grandchildren. She wants her son Daniel, as agent, to be able to help the grandchildren with college. She initials the standard powers, then writes: "My agent may give up to $15,000 per year to each grandchild for education, paid directly to the school if possible. My agent may not give to my agent." Annual gifts could reach $45,000 a year, so she adds that the total must leave at least $300,000 in her accounts. She grants no power over her IRA beneficiary forms. Daniel keeps a ledger.

## What if you grant nothing?

Then the agent can pay bills, manage investments and sell property, but cannot give gifts. That is usually fine. If gifts become wise later, you can sign a new document while you have capacity. If you lose capacity, a court may need to approve, as discussed in [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship).

## What safeguards belong in a gift power?

A gift clause is only as safe as its limits. Consider these protections when you draft.

- **A needs-first rule.** Gifts may be made only if enough money remains for your own care for a stated number of years.
- **Equal treatment.** Gifts to children must be equal unless you list exceptions, which prevents an agent from favoring themselves.
- **Direct payment.** Tuition and medical costs paid straight to the school or provider are easier to track.
- **A written plan.** Attach a one-page letter that explains your purpose, so a court or a sibling can see the intent.
- **A second set of eyes.** Require the agent to send a yearly summary of gifts to a named relative or advisor.

Unlimited authority is the risk to avoid. Our article on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) shows how open-ended gift powers have been misused.
