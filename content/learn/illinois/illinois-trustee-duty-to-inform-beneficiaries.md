Your father died in March. His living trust left the house and brokerage account to you and your sister, with your brother as trustee. Months later nobody has told you anything. You do not know what the trust says, what it owns, or what has been spent.

Illinois law does not leave that to the trustee's goodwill. The Trust Code sets out what a trustee must disclose and by when. This article covers the main rule, section 813.1, and what you can do when it is ignored. It is general education, not legal advice. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) puts it in context.

## The short version

- A trustee who takes over after a death owes beneficiaries a written notice, usually within 90 days.
- You can ask for a complete copy of the trust instrument.
- Accountings follow every year, and again when the trust ends.
- Older trusts can follow different timing rules, so check the date the trust became irrevocable.

## Which trusts does the rule cover?

Section 813.1 covers trusts that became irrevocable after the Trust Code's effective date, and revocable trusts, subject to section 603. Trustees who accepted their role before the effective date of a revocable trust are outside it. It applies going forward only (760 ILCS 3/813.1). The companion rule, section 813.2, applies to older trusts, and our [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) article mentions it briefly.

## What must the 90-day notice say?

The trustee must tell each qualified beneficiary three things (760 ILCS 3/813.1):

1. That the trust exists.
2. That the beneficiary may request a complete copy of the trust instrument, or only the relevant terms if the instrument allows.
3. Whether the beneficiary has a right to receive or request trust accountings.

The 90 days run from several events. The first is the trust becoming irrevocable or, if no trustee is acting, the trustee accepting the role. The others are the trustee learning of a new qualified beneficiary, or learning that a beneficiary gained or lost a representative.

## What other notices does the trustee owe?

The statute lists more than the first letter. A new trustee must notify qualified beneficiaries, within 90 days of accepting the role, of the acceptance and of the trustee's name, address and phone number. A continuing trustee must notify within 90 days when another trustee becomes incapacitated, dies, is disqualified or is removed. A change in contact information must be reported within 90 days after it takes effect. Compensation changes require advance notice, and resignations require notice.

| Event | What the trustee owes |
|---|---|
| Trust becomes irrevocable | Notice within 90 days |
| New trustee accepts | Notice with contact details, within 90 days |
| Each year | Accounting to current and presumptive remainder beneficiaries |
| Trust ends | Accounting to those entitled to the residue |
| Trustee leaves | Accounting from the former trustee, unless a co-trustee remains |
| Reasonable request | Complete copy of the trust instrument |

## What is in a trust accounting?

Section 813.1 requires accountings but does not prescribe a form. In practice a good accounting shows what the trust held at the start, what came in, what was paid out and to whom, the trustee's fees, and what remains. It should let a beneficiary tell whether the sale of Dad's house went through at a fair price. For assets without a readily available market value, the trustee decides whether to estimate, use a nominal value or hire an appraiser.

If you cannot read the accounting, ask questions in writing. Our article on [what trust beneficiaries can see and when](/learn/trusts/what-trust-beneficiaries-can-see-and-when) covers the broader national picture.

## Can a beneficiary give up these rights?

Yes. A qualified beneficiary may waive required information in a signed writing delivered to the trustee, and may withdraw that waiver for future accountings. We see this most often between spouses in a blended family, where the surviving spouse would rather not see the paperwork. Waive only if you have decided the trustee is trustworthy and the stakes are low.

## What are the limits on disclosure?

The duty is not unlimited. A trustee need not give advance notice of trust transactions. A beneficiary must agree to any confidentiality restrictions that bind the trustee before receiving protected information. A trustee has no duty to share information that the law prohibits sharing. A trustee may also share more than the statute requires, even with a beneficiary who is not entitled.

The upside for trustees is a safe harbor. Giving the required notices and the annual and termination accountings fully discharges the duty to inform and account. That is why a careful trustee sends the letter, even to a sibling who lives next door.

## What if the trustee stays silent?

Take these steps in order.

1. Write to the trustee, by mail and email, and ask for the notice, a complete copy of the trust instrument and the most recent accounting.
2. Set a reasonable response date and keep copies.
3. Ask whether the trust became irrevocable before the Code took effect, which could change which rule applies.
4. If there is no answer, talk with an attorney about asking the court to compel the information. The trustee's own duties are discussed in our article on [what happens to a living trust when you die](/learn/trusts/what-happens-to-a-living-trust-when-you-die). The broader duty to account is covered in [trustee duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account).

Do not guess at fraud from silence alone. Most silent trustees are overwhelmed, not dishonest, and a friendly letter works more often than a lawsuit. If you are the one named as trustee, our guide on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) helps you weigh what the job involves, and the [after a death guide](/learn/after-a-death) covers the practical sequence.

## A short example

Imran is named trustee of his mother's trust after she dies on June 1. His sister Leila and his niece are beneficiaries. By the end of August, Imran owes each qualified beneficiary the written notice. He should enclose a copy of the trust or tell them how to ask for one, and say whether they may receive accountings.

Suppose Imran discovers in October that his mother's trust also makes a cousin a qualified beneficiary. A 90-day clock starts again for that person from the date Imran learns of the cousin. That is the part trustees miss: the duty is ongoing, not a single letter in the first quarter.

Then comes the year-end. Imran sends Leila and his niece an accounting covering income, expenses and distributions. If he sells the house in the spring, he does not need to warn them first, but the sale must show up in the next accounting. A trustee who keeps clean records and sends these on time rarely ends up in court. One who skips them invites suspicion even when nothing is wrong.

If you are the beneficiary, keep a file of every notice and every accounting. If you are the trustee, calendar the 90-day date the day you accept the role. Either way, our [Illinois probate and trust guide](/learn/illinois) lays out the other deadlines that tend to sit beside this one, such as the [six-month will contest window](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) when a will is also involved.
