Priya is a single mother in Peoria with a seven-year-old. In March she learns she needs surgery and a long recovery that starts in two weeks. Her sister can take Mateo in, but the school, the pediatrician and the pharmacy will all ask who is allowed to make decisions. Priya's will names a guardian, but a will only speaks after she dies.

Illinois has two separate tools for exactly this kind of gap, and they are often confused. This page explains the short-term guardian and the standby guardian under the Probate Act, how each is created, and how to choose. It sits inside our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). For the general, multi-state picture, see [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians). This is general education, not legal advice.

## The short version

- A **short-term guardian** is named by a signed paper and takes over without any court filing.
- A **standby guardian** is also named in writing, but a judge appoints the person, and the paper is aimed at a larger event such as a parent's death or inability to act.
- A guardian named in a will is a third thing, and it only works after your death.
- Both tools are designed so that a parent keeps control: you can cancel or change the paper.

## What is a short-term guardian in Illinois?

A short-term guardian is someone a parent signs up, in writing, to care for a child for a limited stretch. Section 11-5.4 of the Probate Act lets a parent, adoptive parent or adjudicated parent whose rights have not been terminated make the appointment. So can the guardian of a minor's person. No court approval is needed (755 ILCS 5/11-5.4).

The paper must be dated and must identify three people: the appointing parent or guardian, the child, and the appointee. The parent signs, or directs another person to sign for them, in front of at least two adult witnesses. Neither witness can be the appointee. The appointee signs as well, though not necessarily at the same time. The statute includes a fill-in form, and each child needs a separate form.

The section also covers a child who has not been born yet and any unmarried minor.

### When does it start?

The appointment takes effect when the paper is signed, unless the paper names a later date or event. The form offers choices such as a written or physician-certified statement that the parent can no longer provide care, hospital admission, a calendar date, the start of active duty, or an administrative separation. Many parents sign now and let a hospital admission trigger the start, so nothing depends on a scramble later.

### How long does it last?

The default is 365 days from the date the appointment is effective. The paper may end it sooner by a date or event. Only one short-term appointment instrument can be in force at any time.

| Situation | Term |
|---|---|
| Ordinary appointment | 365 days from the effective date, or less if the paper says so |
| Parent on active military duty | Can be longer: the active duty period plus 30 days, with service dates listed |
| Administrative separation still in effect at the end | Guardian may renew for another 365 days |
| Temporary custodian appointed under the Juvenile Court Act | Court may vacate the guardianship after notice, if it serves the child's best interests |

## Does the other parent have to agree?

Often yes. If the child has another living parent whose rights are intact, whose whereabouts are known and who is able to handle day-to-day decisions, the appointment needs that parent's signed consent. The form lists the exceptions: the other parent has died, cannot be located, is unable or unwilling to make child care decisions, or the parents never married and no court has entered a parentage order.

Say Priya and Mateo's father are not married and no court has ruled on parentage. The form's exception for that situation can apply. If instead they are divorced and he lives in Joliet and is fully involved, she needs his signature. Skipping that step is the usual way these papers fail.

## What is a standby guardian?

A standby guardian is a person named now to step in later, usually when something serious happens to the parent. Under Section 11-5.3 a parent or guardian of a minor can designate a standby guardian in writing, including in a will. The person must be qualified to serve under Section 11-3. The designation can cover the child's person, estate, or both, and it can name a successor. As in the short-term statute, two adult witnesses sign, and neither can be the person named (755 ILCS 5/11-5.3, as amended by Public Act 101-120).

The difference is the court. A judge appoints a standby guardian on petition, if the appointment serves the child's best interest. The court lacks jurisdiction where a living parent with intact rights, a known location and the ability to make day-to-day decisions has not consented, unless that parent fails to object after notice or cannot consent because of an administrative separation. The law presumes such a parent is willing and able.

Section 11-13.1(c) then sets the clock: the standby guardian files a petition for guardianship under Section 11-5 within 60 days of learning of the parent's death, consent, or inability to make day-to-day decisions. Until then the standby guardian of the person may act without court direction for up to 60 days, unless a court limits it.

## Which one should you use?

<!-- visual: whatif id=no-guardian-named -->

Think about what the risk is.

1. **A planned absence or recovery** (surgery, deployment, a long hospital stay): use the short-term guardian. It is fast and cheap, and you can sign it this week.
2. **A serious illness that could end in death or lasting incapacity**: name a standby guardian, and also keep your will's guardian clause current.
3. **Only your death**: your will's guardian nomination is the main tool. See [what a guardian named in a will can do](/learn/guardianship/does-a-guardian-named-in-a-will-hold-up-in-court).
4. **Medical care for the child in the meantime**: ask whether the paper alone will satisfy the hospital, and pair it with the health information your pediatrician needs.

Most families need the first and the last, and some need all four. If your child could one day be left with no parent, read [what happens when no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) and think through [who should be your guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian). Our [Illinois guardianship article](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) covers court-appointed guardianship itself.

## What do these papers mean for the rest of your plan?

A short-term or standby paper is one piece of a plan, not the plan. A parent who is hurt may also need someone to handle money, which is a different document: see [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property). A parent who will be unable to sign for medical decisions needs a health care agent, covered in [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney). If you are unsure how large a fund your guardian would need if the worst happened, the [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) gives a starting figure.

And if you hold a will with a guardian clause from years ago, check that it still names someone who is willing, healthy and nearby. Of the three tools here, the will is the one that most often goes stale.

## Common mistakes

- **Signing with one witness**, or using the appointee as a witness. The statute needs two adult witnesses who are not the appointee.
- **Forgetting the other parent's consent** when the exceptions do not apply.
- **Letting the paper expire.** A 365-day term runs out, and nobody sends a reminder.
- **Never telling the school or the doctor.** A valid paper in a drawer helps no one at 2 a.m.
- **Assuming a short-term guardian replaces a will.** It does not, and the statute says it is not consent to a court guardianship.
