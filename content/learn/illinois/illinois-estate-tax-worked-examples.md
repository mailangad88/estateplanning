Most people reading about the Illinois estate tax see the $4 million exclusion and stop. The harder question is what the tax actually costs when an estate is a little over, or well over, that line. A family with a $4.5 million estate and a family with a $7 million estate are in very different places.

This page works through three estates with real numbers, then shows a married couple using a trust to reach zero. For the rules behind the math, start with our guide to [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). For the wider picture, see the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## How much is the Illinois estate tax at different sizes?

The tax is $0 at $4,000,000, about $143,000 at $4.5 million, $285,714 at $5 million and about $566,000 at $7 million. The Attorney General publishes the $5 million figure. The others are our own arithmetic from the state death tax credit table that Illinois uses (35 ILCS 405), so treat them as estimates.

| Estate size | Illinois tax (estimate) | Share of the estate | Federal tax (2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $4,000,000 | $0 | 0% | $0 |
| $4,100,000 | about $28,600 | 0.7% | $0 |
| $4,500,000 | about $142,900 | 3.2% | $0 |
| $5,000,000 | $285,714 | 5.7% | $0 |
| $6,000,000 | about $456,100 | 7.6% | $0 |
| $7,000,000 | about $565,600 | 8.1% | $0 |
| $8,000,000 | about $680,600 | 8.5% | $0 |

The federal column is zero because the 2026 federal exclusion is $15 million per person. Our page on [Illinois versus federal estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-vs-federal-estate-tax) explains why the two diverge.

## Why is the tax steepest right above $4 million?

For roughly the first $1.3 million over the line, the tax runs near 28.6 cents on each added dollar. Illinois ties its tax to a federal credit table from before 2002, but the tax can never exceed a figure tied to the $4 million exclusion. In that first stretch the cap controls, and it works out to two sevenths of the overage. Past about $5.3 million, the old table gives the smaller number and takes over.

You can see this in the table. Going from $4 million to $5 million adds $285,714. Going from $6 million to $7 million adds only about $109,500. So a small overage is expensive per dollar, and trimming it often pays well.

## What does a $4.5 million estate look like?

Ben, 71, is a widower in Naperville. His house is worth $900,000. His IRA is $1.4 million, his brokerage account is $1.2 million, and a life insurance policy he owns pays $1.0 million. Total: $4.5 million.

Ben did not think of himself as wealthy. But the policy and the IRA both count, because the estate tax looks at everything he owned or controlled, not only what goes through probate. Our article on [what counts toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-counts-toward-the-estate-tax) lists the pieces. His estimated Illinois tax is about $142,900, due in nine months, and it falls on a $500,000 overage. If Ben moved the policy into a properly built trust more than three years before death, the estate would drop to $3.5 million and the tax would be zero. See [life insurance and the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/life-insurance-and-the-illinois-estate-tax).

## What does a $5 million estate look like?

Carla, 66, never married. She owns a $1.1 million home, $2.4 million in investments and a $1.5 million business interest. The estate is $5.0 million. The Attorney General's example says the Illinois tax on that size is $285,714, and the federal tax is $0.

If Carla leaves $1 million to a charity she has supported for years, the taxable amount falls to $4.0 million and the tax drops to zero. Without the gift, her heirs would receive about $4.71 million after the $285,714 tax. With it, they receive $4.0 million. So the $1 million gift costs her heirs about $714,000, not the full $1 million. We explain that in [charitable gifts and the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/charitable-gifts-and-the-illinois-estate-tax).

## What does a $7 million estate look like?

Mohammed, 74, and Fatima, 72, are married and own $7 million together: $3.5 million titled to each. If Mohammed dies first and leaves everything outright to Fatima, no tax is due at his death. Fatima then owns $7 million and has only her own $4 million exclusion. Illinois does not allow a spouse to inherit the other's unused exclusion. Her estate owes about $565,600.

Now change one thing. Mohammed's plan sends $3.5 million to a trust for Fatima and the children, a credit shelter trust, and Fatima keeps her own $3.5 million. At Fatima's death, her estate is $3.5 million, below the line, and nothing is taxed. The trust is not part of her estate. The tax saved is about $565,600. How this works for couples is in [credit shelter trusts and the Illinois QTIP election](/learn/illinois/illinois-qtip-election-and-credit-shelter-trusts).

## How can you estimate your own Illinois estate tax?

1. List every asset at today's value: home, accounts, retirement plans, business interests, vehicles, personal property.
2. Add life insurance on your life that you own or can control.
3. Add taxable lifetime gifts, meaning amounts over the annual exclusion that required a gift tax return.
4. Subtract debts, funeral and administration costs, and amounts that go to a spouse or charity in a qualifying way.
5. Compare the result with $4,000,000. If it is at or below, the Illinois tax is zero.
6. If it is over, run the Attorney General's online calculator and compare the answer with the table above.

Step 4 is where plans change the result. The [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) gives a quick first read, and a signed plan can move the number a great deal.

## What are the limits of these examples?

They are simple on purpose. Real estates have out-of-state property, which lowers the tax by the share located elsewhere. They have debts, which lower the starting value. They have growth, because a $3.9 million estate today may be $4.4 million in a decade. The interrelated formula also means the exact result depends on deductions we have not listed. Our figures round to the nearest hundred dollars.

Remember too that the executor files Form 700 and the tax is due nine months after death. The mechanics are in our guide to the [Illinois Form 700 estate tax return](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-700-estate-tax-return). Who bears the tax depends on the will or trust, which we cover in [who pays the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/who-pays-the-estate-tax-executor-or-heirs).
