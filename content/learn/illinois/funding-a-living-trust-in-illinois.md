You signed the trust papers, and the lawyer said you were done. In Illinois, you are not done until the assets are in the trust. A trust owns only what is titled to the trustee or paid to the trust, and the title work is a separate set of small tasks.

This page is the Illinois working version of that task list: the deed and recording step, how each kind of account moves, and the assets that slip through. For the general method, see [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust), and for the self-check, see [how to tell if your trust is actually funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded). The Illinois rules on the trust itself are in our [Illinois living trusts guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts).

## How does funding work under the Illinois Trust Code?

Funding is the transfer of ownership to the trustee. The Illinois Trust Code lists the ways a trust can be created, and one is a transfer of property to a trustee (760 ILCS 3/401). Another is a declaration by the owner that the owner holds specific property as trustee. In practice, banks, brokers and county recorders want paperwork they recognize, not an argument about a declaration, so most plans retitle each asset in the trustee's name.

When you are your own trustee, the day-to-day feel does not change. You still use the house and write the checks. The difference shows up at death or incapacity, when your successor trustee can act on assets that are in the trust.

## What does Illinois require to deed a house into the trust?

You need a deed that names you, as trustee of your trust, as the new owner, signed and notarized, then recorded with the county recorder where the property sits. In Cook County that office is the Clerk's recordings division. Each of Illinois' 102 counties has its own fees and forms, so call before you go.

Three checks save trouble:

- **Transfer tax.** A deed with no real consideration is generally exempt from Illinois transfer tax if the actual consideration is under $100 (35 ILCS 200/31-45). The recorder usually wants the exemption cited on the deed or on a transfer declaration form. Ask which one.
- **Mortgage and title insurance.** Federal law generally keeps a lender from calling a home loan when a borrower who lives in the house moves it to a trust where the borrower stays the beneficiary. Still tell the lender, and ask your title company whether coverage continues for the trustee. Our page on [moving a house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) goes deeper.
- **Property tax exemptions.** Ask the assessor whether your homeowner or senior exemption carries over. See [whether a living trust affects your homestead exemption](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-affect-homestead-exemption-and-property-taxes).

An Illinois transfer on death instrument (755 ILCS 27) is a different tool that leaves a house to a named person without probate. It is not the same as funding. Our article on the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) compares them. Homes already in an Illinois land trust are a special case; see [land trust versus living trust](/learn/illinois/land-trust-vs-living-trust).

## How do you fund each type of asset?

Most assets need a form from the company that holds them. Use this table as a checklist.

| Asset | How to move it | Typical proof the bank or company wants |
|---|---|---|
| House or land | Recorded deed to you as trustee | Trust name and date; deed signed and notarized |
| Checking and savings | Retitle, or open a new trust account | Certification of trust; ID |
| Brokerage account | Retitle with the broker | Certification of trust; new account forms |
| IRA, 401(k), 403(b) | Do not retitle; use the beneficiary form | Beneficiary designation form |
| Life insurance and annuities | Change the beneficiary if the trust should receive it | Insurer's change form |
| LLC or business interest | Written assignment, with any consent the operating agreement needs | Assignment; operating agreement |
| Furniture, art, jewelry | General assignment listed on a schedule | Signed assignment |
| Vehicles | Often left out; see the FAQ | Secretary of State title |

A [certification of trust](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-certification-of-trust) lets you prove the trust exists without handing over the full document. Illinois protects a person who relies on one without knowing it is wrong (760 ILCS 3/1013).

## What are the steps, in order?

1. Make a one-page list of every asset with its current title and value.
2. Mark each item as "retitle," "beneficiary form," or "leave out on purpose."
3. Deed the house and any other real estate, one county at a time.
4. Ask each bank and broker for its trust account forms and bring a certification of trust.
5. Update beneficiary forms so the trust or the people you chose are named, with advice on retirement accounts.
6. Sign a general assignment for personal items, as in our page on a [schedule A and general assignment](/learn/trusts/schedule-a-and-general-assignment-for-a-living-trust).
7. Keep copies of every new deed, statement and form with the trust.
8. Put a reminder on your calendar to repeat the check after any purchase, inheritance or move.

## Which assets do people miss in Illinois?

The usual gaps are things acquired after the trust was signed, plus items that sit in the wrong box. These come up often:

- A second home or a vacant lot bought later.
- A bank account at a credit union that the family forgot to retitle.
- A brokerage account opened after a job change or a rollover.
- An inheritance received after the trust was signed, still in your own name.
- A refund, an unpaid bill owed to you, or a pending claim.
- Digital accounts and business domains. See [putting digital assets in a living trust](/learn/digital-assets/can-you-put-digital-assets-in-a-living-trust-what-you-can-assign-and-what-you-cannot).

Whatever stays in your own name is covered by your [pour-over will](/learn/illinois/pour-over-wills-in-illinois), which means probate. If the leftover total is under the limit, the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) may let the family skip court, but a house cannot be moved that way. Our page on [what happens when a trust was never funded](/learn/illinois/unfunded-living-trust-in-illinois) walks through the choices.

## Example: Henry and the lot in Kankakee

Henry, 68, signed a trust in 2019 and deeded his Naperville home to it. In 2022 he bought a $45,000 vacant lot near Kankakee and a $38,000 certificate of deposit, both in his own name. Henry's plan still works for the house. At his death, the lot cannot be moved by affidavit, so his executor would need to open probate in [Kankakee County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/kankakee) for a $45,000 lot. A single recorded deed in 2022 would have prevented that. This is why the check after every purchase matters.

You can see your own gaps with the [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter).
