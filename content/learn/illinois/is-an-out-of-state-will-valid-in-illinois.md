You and your husband sold the house in Ohio last spring and now rent in Naperville. Your will, signed in Ohio eleven years ago, sits in a drawer. Is it any good here?

Most likely yes, as far as the signing goes. The harder question is whether it still does what you want under Illinois law. This page covers what usually carries over, what changes the day you become an Illinois resident, and a practical order for updating. Our national piece on [whether a will survives a move](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) covers the general rule, and the rest of the state's rules are in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Is a will signed in another state valid in Illinois?

Generally yes. As a rule across the country, a will that was validly signed under the law of the place where it was made or where the person lived is accepted elsewhere, and moving does not cancel it. We have not tied that rule to a specific Illinois section here, so have an attorney confirm it for your document. What you should not assume is that the old will is as easy to use in an Illinois court as a new one.

Illinois has its own signing rule. A will must be in writing, signed, and attested in the signer's presence by at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3). A notary is not required. Our page on [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) walks through it. If your old state allowed a will with one witness, a handwritten will with no witness, or a will signed only before a notary, the fact that it was valid there is the only thing protecting it here. That is a thin protection to rely on.

A will that has a self-proving statement signed by the witnesses is easier to admit, because the court can accept it without calling the witnesses. Illinois allows this, and a will signed long ago in another state may or may not have one. An Illinois attorney can tell you in a minute.

## What actually changes when you become an Illinois resident?

Your domicile changes, and with it the court, the tax and the family rights that apply. Here is a side-by-side look at what to check.

| Issue | What to check after moving to Illinois |
|---|---|
| Which court | Probate is held in the circuit court of the Illinois county where you lived, not in your old state |
| Estate tax | Illinois taxes estates over $4 million. Your old state may have had none |
| Spouse's rights | A spouse can renounce an Illinois will within 7 months of its admission and take one-third or one-half |
| Executor | A non-resident executor may be required to post a bond |
| Witness and signing rules | Two witnesses, no notary. A self-proving statement helps |
| Powers of attorney | Illinois has a statutory short form for property and a separate health care form |
| Real estate left behind | A house in your old state may need its own court case unless it is in a trust or has a transfer on death deed |

### The estate tax

This is the item that surprises people most. Illinois taxes estates over $4 million, far below the federal exclusion of $15 million per person in 2026. The $4 million is a filing threshold, not a credit, and a surviving spouse cannot add a late spouse's unused amount. If you came from a state with no estate tax, your old will may be a simple "everything to my spouse," which wastes the first spouse's Illinois exclusion. Our article on the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) explains the cliff, and the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) gives a first pass. Most families are nowhere near the threshold, so do not let this paragraph scare you. It matters if your house, retirement accounts and life insurance together are close to it.

### The spouse's share

Illinois gives a surviving spouse the right to renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if there are descendants, or one-half if not. The renunciation is due within 7 months after the will is admitted. If your old will leaves a spouse little because of a prenuptial agreement or a blended-family plan, discuss it with an attorney. Our article on [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) has the details.

### The executor you named back home

Suppose you named your sister in Ohio as executor. She can serve in Illinois, because the statute asks for United States residency, not Illinois residency. The court may still require her to post a bond. If the house, the accounts and the creditors are all in Illinois, a nearby backup is a gift to her. See [who can be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-can-be-an-executor-in-illinois) for the full list of qualifications.

## Which documents should you redo first?

Do the ones used while you are alive before the will. Those get tested at a bank counter or in a hospital room, by a person with no time.

1. **Power of attorney for property.** Sign the Illinois statutory short form with one qualifying witness and a notary. Our page on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) explains who cannot witness.
2. **Health care power of attorney.** Sign the Illinois form. See [the Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).
3. **The will.** Have it reviewed, and re-sign it with two witnesses and a self-proving statement if the old signing looks thin.
4. **Beneficiary forms and account titling.** Retirement accounts and life insurance follow their own forms, not the will. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you list them.
5. **Real estate.** If you bought in Illinois, think about a transfer on death instrument or a living trust. See [the Illinois TODI](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).

An old house in your former state needs separate thought. Our guide to [probating a will from another state](/learn/probate/probating-a-will-from-another-state) explains why a second court case can follow.

## A worked example

Example only: Carol and Neil, both 62, moved from Florida to Illinois in 2025. Their Florida wills leave everything to each other, then to their two children. Their net worth is $3.6 million, including a $900,000 life insurance policy owned by Neil. They are under $4 million today.

Their attorney asks two questions. If Carol dies first and Neil inherits everything, does the combined $3.6 million plus growth cross $4 million before Neil dies? And could a trust at the first death use Carol's Illinois exclusion? Neither is a Florida question. The attorney also replaces their old powers of attorney with Illinois forms and notes that the Florida forms had no problem until the first bank asked. Nothing was wrong with the old wills. They just did not fit where Carol and Neil now lived.

## When should you update the will after a move?

Check it within a few months, and set a firm date at one year. Sooner if any of these apply.

- Your estate is within sight of $4 million.
- You are in a second marriage or have stepchildren.
- Your named executor lives in your old state and no Illinois backup is named.
- You own real estate in two states.
- The old will was handwritten, notarized only, or had a single witness.
