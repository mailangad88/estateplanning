Picture Dana, who owns a small landscaping company through an Illinois LLC. On Tuesday she dies of a heart attack. By Wednesday morning her crew expects a payroll deposit, a supplier wants a check, and the bank has just learned the only account holder has died. Nobody knows who is allowed to do anything.

That is the real question behind "what happens to my LLC if I die." The company survives on paper. The trouble is practical: who signs, who owns, and who gets paid. This page answers it for Illinois, and it sits inside our wider [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). ## What does the Illinois LLC Act do when a member dies?

The Act treats a member's death as an event of dissociation (805 ILCS 180/35-45). The person stops being a member, and the company carries on. Its bank accounts, contracts, lease and debts stay where they are.

What is left behind is the owner's transferable interest, which is personal property. It passes through the estate like a car or a brokerage account. The estate or the heir who receives it generally gets the right to distributions, but not an automatic vote or a say in management (805 ILCS 180/30-1). A transferee can become a full member if the operating agreement allows it or if the other members agree.

This default is a fallback. The operating agreement can change nearly all of it, and in a well-run company it does. Our general explainer on [what happens to an LLC or corporation when the owner dies](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) covers the national picture. The rest of this page is about what that means for you in Illinois.

## Who controls the business on Monday morning?

It depends on how many owners there are and what the paperwork says. In a multi-member company, the surviving members or the named manager keep running things. In a single-member company, nobody is automatically in charge.

| Setup | Who can act the next day | Typical problem |
|---|---|---|
| Single member, no trust, no named successor | No one until a court appoints your executor | Payroll, rent and taxes stall for weeks |
| Single member, interest held in your living trust | The successor trustee, if the agreement allows it | Banks may still ask for paperwork |
| Two or more members, a good operating agreement | The surviving members or named manager | Valuation disputes if the price is vague |
| Two or more members, no written agreement | The survivors, but with fights over authority | Heirs and partners disagree about the default rules |

Your executor is the person named in your will, and that person has no power over the interest until the court issues letters of office. See the [Illinois letters of administration and surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) page for how that step works, and the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) for the timeline. For a company that lives on weekly cash, even a few weeks is long.

## What do my heirs actually receive?

Your heirs receive whatever the operating agreement lets them receive. Read three clauses first.

1. **Transfer clause.** Can an interest go to a spouse, a child or a trust without the other members agreeing?
2. **Death or buyout clause.** Does your death give the company or the other members an option or a duty to buy your share?
3. **Admission clause.** Who has the power to admit a new member, and by what vote?

If the agreement is silent, the Act's default applies and your family gets economic rights only. That can leave a widow holding a share of profits with no information, no vote and no way to force a sale. It leaves the surviving partner with a silent co-owner who may be grieving and angry. Neither person asked for that arrangement.

## What if my partner's spouse becomes my partner?

This is the fear behind most buy-sell questions, and it is a fair one. Without an agreement, your partner's death can leave you sharing profits with a spouse or an adult child who knows nothing about the business and may want cash now. With an agreement, you and your partner decide in advance that the survivor buys the share.

There are two common designs.

- **Cross-purchase.** Each owner agrees to buy the other's share. Often each owner holds a life insurance policy on the other.
- **Entity redemption.** The company itself buys the share, and the company holds the policy.

Which fits depends on how many owners there are and how the company is taxed, so ask your attorney and your tax advisor together. Our guide to [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) lays out the choices, and [what happens when a partner dies without one](/learn/business-owners/business-partner-dies-without-a-buy-sell-agreement) shows the cost of waiting.

### A buy-sell example

Here is an example with invented numbers. Ravi and Tom each own 50 percent of a plumbing supply LLC they value at $800,000. Their agreement says the survivor buys the deceased owner's share at a price both sign each January, funded by $400,000 of life insurance on each man. When Ravi dies, the insurance pays Tom, Tom pays Ravi's widow Priya $400,000, and Tom owns 100 percent. Priya does not have to run a supply company, and Tom does not have a new partner.

Take away the agreement and the same death looks different. Priya holds a half interest with no vote. Tom has no cash to buy her out. Both of them hire lawyers.

## What does a good plan look like?

A plan has five working parts.

1. **A current operating agreement** with death, incapacity and transfer clauses that match your wishes.
2. **A named successor manager** who can act at once, plus a backup signer on the bank account.
3. **A buy-sell agreement** if there is more than one owner. It needs a price method, a payment method and a funding source.
4. **A decision on trust ownership.** Your living trust can hold the interest if the agreement permits it, which lets the successor trustee step in without court. The [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) page explains what a trust does and does not avoid.
5. **A power of attorney that covers the business** for the time before death. Our page on [business owner incapacity planning](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) covers it.

Your will should match all of this. A will that leaves "my business" to three children, sitting beside an agreement that requires a buyout, creates a fight the day it is opened.

## What about S corporations, lenders and leases?

If your company is taxed as an S corporation, who may hold the shares is limited by federal tax rules, and a trust that does not qualify can end the election. Talk to your tax advisor before you retitle anything into a trust. Banks may also treat a change of owner as a default, landlords may need to consent to a transfer, and licenses may not pass to an heir who is not qualified. Ask each lender and landlord now what a death would trigger.

You also remain on the hook for anything you signed personally. Our page on [personal guaranties and business debts at death](/learn/business-owners/personal-guaranties-and-business-debts-at-death) explains how those obligations reach the estate.

## What mistakes do Illinois owners make?

- **Assuming the LLC is protected from your death.** The company is separate from you. Your ownership is not.
- **Never signing an operating agreement.** Many small LLCs were formed online and never wrote one.
- **Leaving the interest to a spouse in the will when the agreement forbids the transfer.**
- **Naming life insurance beneficiaries that do not match the buy-sell terms.**
- **Letting the valuation go stale.** A price set in 2018 may be wrong by thousands of dollars today.
- **Giving only one person the bank login.** Passwords die with the person who knew them.
