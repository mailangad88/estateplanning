Nobody found a will, and the family has been told that someone has to be appointed "administrator" and may have to buy a bond. The first question is usually who. The second is whether the bond is a real expense or a formality.

This page covers who may serve when there is no executor, how the court chooses among relatives, how the oath and bond work, and how bond amounts are calculated in general terms. For the broader effect of having no will, see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). For the whole cost picture, our guide to [what Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) puts the bond premium next to the other charges. This is general education, not legal advice.

## The short version

- Letters of administration are the court's proof that you may act for an estate with no executor.
- The Probate Act ranks relatives, and a higher-ranked person can nominate someone else.
- The administrator signs an oath and gives a bond the judge approves.
- Bond size follows the value of the personal property. We do not list a premium amount because it depends on the surety company.

## What are letters of administration?

They are a certified court document. After the petition is heard, the court appoints the administrator, who takes the oath, files the bond, and receives letters. Banks, brokers and title companies want a certified copy before they release anything. When a will exists but the named executor is dead, declines or is disqualified, the court may appoint an administrator with the will annexed. Our page on [executor, administrator and personal representative](/learn/after-a-death/executor-vs-administrator-vs-personal-representative) explains the labels.

## Who is qualified to serve?

Section 9-1 of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/9-1) sets the basics. The person must be at least 18, a United States resident and of sound mind. They cannot be an adjudged person with a disability under the Act or have a felony conviction. Executors have a similar list in Section 6-13, with a narrow exception allowing a convicted felon to serve if the will names that person and acknowledges the conviction. That exception is written for executors, not administrators.

## Who gets preference?

Section 9-3 (755 ILCS 5/9-3) orders the people who may claim letters:

| Rank | Person |
|---|---|
| 1 | Surviving spouse, or the spouse's nominee |
| 2 | Legatees, or their nominee, with children preferred among legatees |
| 3 | Children, or their nominee |
| 4 | Grandchildren, or their nominee |
| 5 | Parents, or their nominee |
| 6 | Brothers and sisters, or their nominee |
| 7 | Nearest kindred, or their nominee |
| 8 | Representative of a deceased ward's estate |
| 9 | The Public Administrator |
| 10 | A creditor of the estate |

Two details matter in practice. First, only someone qualified to act as administrator may nominate another. Second, a person removed as a representative under the Act loses the right to name a successor. A guardian may nominate for a minor or an adult with a disability who is unqualified only because of that status.

Consider Tomas, 46, whose mother dies without a will. His father is gone and he has two sisters. All three are children and share the same rank. The sisters agree that Tomas, who lives closest, should serve, and they sign nominations or consents. Absent agreement, the court decides among them. If the sisters later grow unhappy with how he is doing, [what heirs can do about a slow Illinois executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor) explains their options.

## Is a surety bond required?

Usually yes. Under Section 12-2 (755 ILCS 5/12-2), an individual representative files a sworn oath to perform the duties faithfully and a bond that the court approves. A bond is a promise backed by a guarantor, called a surety, that the estate will be made whole if the representative mishandles money. The surety, usually an insurance company, pays and then pursues the representative.

Section 12-4 (755 ILCS 5/12-4) lets a will excuse the bond or set a maximum amount. Even then, the court can still require security if it suspects fraud or incompetence or believes the estate may not cover its claims. That excuse runs through the will, so an administrator in an intestate case should expect to be bonded. Whether a county will reduce or modify security in a given case is something to ask the clerk or an attorney.

Some situations differ. For a non-resident executor, the court may demand a bond in an amount and with a surety it sets, even if the will excuses surety (755 ILCS 5/6-13).

## How big is the bond?

Section 12-5 (755 ILCS 5/12-5) ties the amount to the personal estate. The bond must be at least double the value of the personal estate when individuals act as sureties or when the bond is excused. With a surety company, it must be at least one and a half times the value. If the representative takes possession of real estate, the court can increase the bond by an amount it sets, having regard to the income from the real estate.

So if Tomas's mother left $100,000 in personal property, the bond amount would be at least $150,000 with a surety company. That figure is not what he pays. It is the amount the surety stands behind, and the premium is a fraction of it.

## What does a bond cost?

The cost is a premium, a fraction of the bond amount, charged by the surety company. We do not quote a rate because premiums depend on the company, the bond amount, the administrator's credit and sometimes the type of estate. Ask the clerk's office or an attorney which sureties are commonly used in your county and get a quote before the hearing. The premium is typically treated as an expense of administration, which means the estate pays it, though the administrator should keep the receipt.

Two things commonly drive the price. One is the size of the bond, which follows the personal property, so an estate that is mostly a house starts from a smaller figure, although the court can add to it if the administrator takes over real estate. The other is the administrator's own finances, which a surety may review.

The Cook County Clerk of the Circuit Court's [Probate Division](https://www.cookcountyclerkofcourt.org/divisions/probate-division) is a starting point for the local forms and procedures, and each county's circuit clerk has its own.

## Steps to get letters

1. Gather the death certificate, a list of heirs with addresses, and an estimate of the personal and real property.
2. Decide who will serve and obtain written consents or nominations from others of equal or higher rank.
3. File the petition for letters of administration with the circuit clerk.
4. Give notice to the heirs as the court requires, and attend the hearing.
5. Take the oath and arrange the bond.
6. Receive certified letters and order several copies.
7. Open an estate account and begin the creditor notice described in [how creditor claims work](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate).

## Can you refuse the job?

Yes, with limits. A person named in a will has 30 days to start or decline. For the full explanation, see [can you refuse to be executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-refuse-to-be-executor-in-illinois). An administrator who has not yet been appointed can simply decline to petition, and the next person in line may do so. The [Illinois pillar](/learn/illinois) lists the rest of the topics.
