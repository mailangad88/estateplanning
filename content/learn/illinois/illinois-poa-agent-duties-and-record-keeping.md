If you have just become someone's agent, you may feel the weight of that job. The good news is that most of what Illinois asks of an agent is simple habit: keep money apart, keep receipts, and be ready to explain what you did.

For the national picture, see [what a power of attorney agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do). This page covers the Illinois rules, with a record-keeping system you can start today. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links the related forms.

## What duties does the Illinois Act put on an agent?

The Act requires an agent who exercises a power to act in good faith for the benefit of the principal, using due care, competence and diligence, in line with the terms of the document (755 ILCS 45/2-7). The notice that comes with the short form says the same in plain words. It also says the agent must:

- Keep a complete record of receipts, disbursements and significant actions.
- Avoid conflicts of interest and self-dealing.
- Keep the principal's money separate from the agent's own.
- Not borrow from the principal unless the document allows it.
- Consider the principal's existing estate plan and try to keep it intact where possible (755 ILCS 45/2-9).

These duties apply to family members as well as to paid professionals.

## What should the record actually look like?

The statute says "receipts, disbursements and significant actions," so a stack of bank statements alone is not enough. A simple ledger works. For each entry write the date, amount, who got paid, why, and where the receipt is. For significant actions, such as signing a lease or moving $50,000 into a new account, add a short note on why you did it.

| What to keep | Why it helps | How long |
|---|---|---|
| Bank and credit card statements | Show income and spending | Until the estate is closed, plus a few years |
| Receipts and invoices | Prove each expense was real | Same |
| A running ledger | Lets you answer any question quickly | Same |
| Copies of contracts and deeds signed | Show what you committed to | Permanently |
| Notes on large decisions | Show care and good faith | Until closed |
| Copy of the power of attorney | Proof of authority | Same |

## How do you set up the records, step by step?

1. Open or retitle a separate account for the principal, with the agent's role shown. Do not use your own account.
2. Make a copy of the power of attorney for the bank and one for your own file.
3. Photograph or scan every receipt the day you get it.
4. Start a ledger, on paper or in a spreadsheet, with one line per transaction.
5. Write a short note whenever you make a major decision, with the date and reasons.
6. Reconcile the ledger to the bank statement each month.
7. Review the log every quarter and ask yourself whether a stranger could follow it.

## Who can demand your records, and how fast?

The Act says the agent must provide a copy of the record when certain people ask (755 ILCS 45/2-7). They include the principal, a guardian, another fiduciary acting for the principal, and a government agency that protects the principal's welfare. After the principal's death, the estate's personal representative or successor in interest can ask too. Our article on [revoking an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney) describes the 21-day response period and the court costs that may follow if an agent does not answer.

An agent who ignores a lawful request invites a court case. A relative who is not on the list can still raise concerns with adult protective services, as explained in [how to report elder financial abuse in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-report-elder-financial-abuse-in-illinois).

## Can the agent be paid or reimbursed?

Reimbursement for reasonable expenses is built into the short form. Payment for time depends on a paragraph in the form that the principal may strike. See [paying or reimbursing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-agent-be-paid-or-reimbursed). Whatever the amount, record each payment with a date and a note, and never take a lump sum without a reason.

## What happens if you break the rules?

An agent who violates the Act can be liable to the principal or the principal's successors for restoring the property to the value it would have had, and for repaying attorney fees paid on the agent's behalf from the principal's funds (755 ILCS 45/2-7). The remedy is civil, but theft or exploitation can also be reported to law enforcement. The key difference between an honest mistake and misuse is usually the paper trail.

## Example: Jennifer keeps her mother's books

Jennifer, 55, is the agent for her mother Doris, 84. Doris receives $2,900 a month in Social Security and pension, and her assisted living bill is $4,800. Jennifer sets up a ledger. In March she moves $20,000 from Doris's savings to pay six months of the shortfall, and she writes a note explaining the math. She keeps every invoice. When Doris's brother asks questions after her death, Jennifer gives the executor the ledger and the records. The questions end there.

## What happens when the power ends?

The agent's authority ends at the principal's death, revocation, a stated end date, or the loss of any agent to serve. Stop acting, collect the records, and give them to the executor or the successor. Our [probate guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) explains what the executor does next.

## What are the common traps for family agents?

Most problems start with kindness, not greed. These are the ones we see most often.

- **Paying yourself back without a note.** A $400 monthly payment for gas and time looks fine to you and suspicious to a sibling. Write the reason, the date and the document that allows it.
- **Using the principal's card for shared shopping.** A trip for your mother and your own family's groceries should be two separate receipts.
- **Adding yourself to the principal's account.** This can change who inherits the account and may create tax or Medicaid problems. Talk to an attorney first.
- **Selling property to a relative below value.** A sale to the agent or the agent's family is a conflict of interest. Get an appraisal and consider court approval.
- **Waiting too long to get help.** If the work becomes too much, a paid fiduciary or care manager may be better than a missed bill. See [geriatric care managers and money managers](/learn/elder-care/geriatric-care-managers-money-managers-and-fiduciaries).

When in doubt, write down what you did and why on the same day.
