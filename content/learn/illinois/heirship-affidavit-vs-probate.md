Your father died in Cook County. The house is in his name alone, there was no will, and a buyer has offered a fair price. Someone told you that an affidavit of heirship will let the family sell without probate. Then the title company said no.

That mix-up is common. This page explains what an affidavit of heirship does, what it does not do, and which three papers a title company may ask for. It is general education and not legal advice. For the full law on proving who the heirs are, see [how to prove heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois). For the whole topic cluster, start at our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

If you would like an attorney to look at your deed and your buyer's requirements, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-heirship-affidavit-vs-probate&topic=probate).

## What is an affidavit of heirship, and what does it prove?

It is a sworn statement of facts about a family: who the dead person was, whether they married, who their children are, and who died before them. The Probate Act lets any person sign one, and it must be sworn before a notary or a judge (755 ILCS 5/5-3). It proves who the heirs are. That is all.

It does not say who may sign for the estate. An heir is a person who inherits. A personal representative is a person the court has authorized to act. A buyer's title company needs the second one, because only that person can deliver a deed for property that is still in the dead person's name. Our glossary entries on [heir](/glossary/heir) and [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary) mark the difference.

## What are the three papers a title company may ask for?

Most Cook County sales of an inherited house come down to one of three papers. The right one depends on how the house was titled and on that company's underwriting.

| Paper | What it proves | Who gets it | Cost and time | Where it usually works |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Affidavit of heirship | Who the heirs are, by sworn statement | Anyone with knowledge of the family | Notary fee; days | Supporting proof inside a case, or an old chain-of-title gap |
| Court order declaring heirship | Who the heirs are, found by a judge | A person who petitions in an open case | Court filing and attorney time; weeks to months | Backing for a sale when the family tree matters |
| Letters of office (probate) | Who may sign for the estate | The executor or administrator the court appoints | Filing fee, publication and attorney time; weeks to get letters | The usual proof to sell a house in the dead person's name |

Those columns describe typical practice, not a rule that binds every company. Each title insurer sets its own standards, and a company's willingness to insure over an affidavit can differ by lender, buyer and file. Ask yours what it will accept, in writing.

## Do you need probate to sell an inherited house in Cook County?

For a house in the dead person's name alone, usually yes. Someone with court authority has to sign the deed, and that comes from letters of office issued in a probate case. This is true with or without a will. Our article on [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois) walks through who signs and what the title company then asks for.

You can skip probate only when the house never needed it. The recorded deed decides:

- A transfer on death instrument recorded before the death passes the house to the named beneficiary without probate. See [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).
- A living trust or land trust lets the successor trustee sign.
- Joint tenancy with survivorship, or tenancy by the entirety for spouses, puts title with the survivor. Our page on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains it.

If none of these applies, the family is in probate territory. The [Cook County probate guide](/free/cook-county-probate-guide) walks through the court's steps.

## Can a small estate affidavit sell the house?

No. The Illinois small estate affidavit applies to personal property, with a limit of $150,000 for deaths on or after August 15, 2025, and it does not reach real estate (755 ILCS 5/25-1). It is the right tool for a bank account and a car. It is the wrong tool for a house, and families often confuse the two affidavits because both have "affidavit" in the name. The page on the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) covers what it can do, and the [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) helps you sort assets.

## Why does the title company care so much?

Because it is insuring the buyer against a claim that someone else owns part of the house. An unknown child, a former spouse, or a creditor with a claim could surface after closing. A sworn statement from one relative gives the company only that relative's word. A judge's order, or a representative with court-issued letters, gives it a record that protects its policy.

Illinois law also helps the buyer in one situation. A buyer who deals in good faith with an independent representative, and pays value, is not required to look into the representative's powers (755 ILCS 5/28-9). That is part of why letters are the paper that keeps closings simple.

Take Denise and her brother Curtis. Their father died with a Berwyn two-flat in his sole name and no will. They had an affidavit of heirship signed by a cousin and notarized, and a buyer under contract. The title company asked for letters of administration or a court order. Denise hired a lawyer, petitioned the court, and was named administrator. The sale closed after letters issued, and the contract had been extended to cover the wait. The affidavit was not wasted, since it supported the petition, but it was not the paper that closed the sale.

## What about recording an affidavit against the property?

Some families record an affidavit of heirship with the county so it appears in the property's records. It can help explain a gap in an old chain of title. It does not turn the heirs into owners of record and it does not give them power to sell. A recorded private affidavit is not a court order. The county clerk's guide to property after a death says the office cannot advise which option to choose and points families to an estate planning or real estate attorney. Companies differ, so check current practice with an attorney rather than relying on a neighbor's story.

## How do you decide which route to take?

Work through these in order.

1. Pull the recorded deed. Is the dead person alone on it, or is there survivorship, a TODI or a trust?
2. Find a will. Anyone holding one must file it with the clerk (755 ILCS 5/6-1). See [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois).
3. List the heirs, including anyone who died before the parent and left children. [Who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) shows the order.
4. Ask the title company, by email, what it requires for a house titled in a dead person's name.
5. If it asks for letters, open the estate. In Cook County, the clerk says you need an attorney to open one (see [handling an Illinois probate estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer)).
6. Use the heirship affidavit or an order declaring heirship inside the case if the family tree needs proof.

## What does it cost, and what slows it down?

The Cook County clerk lists a $479 filing fee for an independent administration as of October 2025. Add attorney time and newspaper publication of the creditor notice. Our [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) page has the breakdown. For timing, the claims period alone is at least six months, but a sale can often close before the case does. [How long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) explains why.

Two things slow families more than the court does. The first is a missing or disputed heir. The second is a buyer who walks while the family waits for a hearing. Ask the buyer for an extended closing date when you sign. If the estate has to run a supervised sale, the process takes longer, as explained in [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois).

## A common mistake

Paying for an affidavit and a recording before checking with the title company. The notary fee is small, but the lost weeks are not. Another is an affidavit signed by an heir who does not know the full family. If it leaves out a half-sibling or the child of a dead brother, it is wrong, and a wrong sworn statement can hurt the person who signed it. Say what you do not know.

Also keep certified copies of the death certificate on hand.
