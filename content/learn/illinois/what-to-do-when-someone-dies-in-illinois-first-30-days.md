A death in the family brings a wave of phone calls and forms, and Illinois adds a few of its own: a statute that says who decides about the funeral, a rule about filing the will, and a short list of state tax dates. You do not have to do everything in one afternoon. Most of it can be spread across about a month.

This page is part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). It puts the Illinois steps in date order and links the page that explains each one. For the steps that are the same in every state, such as notifying Social Security, the credit bureaus and insurers, use the [general checklist for when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist). If you want a lawyer to look at your situation, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-what-to-do-when-someone-dies-in-illinois-first-30-days&topic=probate) at any point.

## What should happen on day one?

Three things matter on the first day: the funeral decision, the will, and the safety of the home.

**Who controls the funeral.** Illinois does not leave this to whoever speaks first. Under the Disposition of Remains Act (755 ILCS 65/5), the person named in a written instrument controls first, then an executor acting on instructions in the will, then the spouse, adult children, parents and nearest kin. Same-rank relatives, such as three adult children, decide by majority. Unmarried partners and friends are not on the list unless the person named them in writing, so if you are a partner, ask early whether a notarized agent form or other paper exists. The full order and the dispute rules are on the [Disposition of Remains Act page](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act). Once the funeral is booked, the [page on paying for a funeral from an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/paying-for-a-funeral-from-an-illinois-estate) explains what the estate can reimburse.

**The will and the paperwork around it.** Look in the home, with the drafting attorney, and in any safe deposit box. A bank may open a dead person's box so a family member can look for a will and burial papers (755 ILCS 15/1), but nothing else may be removed that way. The page on [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) walks through where to look and who to ask. If the will cannot be found, see [what to do about a lost will](/learn/illinois/lost-will).

**The house and firearms.** Lock the doors, collect mail, and tell a trusted neighbor. If there are guns, lock them up, list them by serial number, and do not hand them to anyone. A FOID card does not pass to the family, and each heir needs a card in their own name (430 ILCS 65/2). The [firearms and FOID page](/learn/illinois/illinois-firearms-and-foid-cards-after-a-death) explains the lawful routes.

## What should you do in the first week?

Order death certificates, file the will, and sort the money.

**Death certificates.** The funeral director files the death record. Certified copies come from the county clerk where the death occurred or from the Illinois Department of Public Health in Springfield, and family members and the executor are the usual people who qualify. Count institutions rather than relatives. Most executors need about eight to twelve. Photocopies are rarely accepted. See [Illinois death certificates: who can order and where](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph), and check the current fee and ordering method on the county clerk or IDPH site, since both change. The general page on [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need) helps with the count.

**File the original will.** Section 6-1 of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/6-1) says anyone holding a will must file it with the clerk of the circuit court immediately after the death. It is a felony to willfully hide a will for 30 days after learning of the death. This applies even if no one expects to open probate, so do not keep the original in a drawer. The [Illinois probate process page](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) shows what comes next.

**Sort the accounts.** Illinois bank accounts fall into two groups. Accounts held in the dead person's name alone are usually frozen once the bank learns of the death. Joint accounts and payable-on-death accounts generally pass to the surviving owner or named beneficiary outside probate. The page on [POD and joint bank accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) explains the difference, and if the dead person was your spouse, [frozen accounts, bills and the house](/learn/illinois/spouse-died-in-illinois-frozen-accounts-bills-and-the-house) covers your position.

## How do you decide between probate and a small estate affidavit?

Look at what is in the dead person's name alone, and whether real estate is among it. A small estate affidavit lets a person collect personal property without opening probate if no letters of office are outstanding, no petition is pending, and the personal estate, not counting registered motor vehicles, does not exceed $150,000 (755 ILCS 5/25-1, as the [small estate affidavit page](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) explains). It does not work for real estate.

| Situation | Usual route |
|---|---|
| Sole-name personal property under $150,000, no real estate | Small estate affidavit |
| A house or land in the dead person's sole name | Probate, or an heirship route covered on the [heirship affidavit page](/learn/illinois/heirship-affidavit-vs-probate) |
| Sole-name personal property above $150,000 | Probate |
| Everything held jointly, or with beneficiary forms | Often neither |
| A car only | See [transferring a car title](/learn/illinois/transfer-car-title-after-death-in-illinois) |

If probate is needed, the court admits the will and issues letters of office. Most estates then use independent administration, which needs little court oversight; see [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois). The executor then opens an [estate bank account](/learn/illinois/opening-an-estate-bank-account-in-illinois), and creditors get notice under the process in [how creditor claims work](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate). Claims are barred two years after death in any event. The [overview of probate](/learn/probate) covers the general concept. If you were named executor and do not want the job, see [refusing to be executor](/learn/illinois/can-you-refuse-to-be-executor-in-illinois).

## What do you do about the house in the first month?

Keep it insured, heated and paid for, and do not retitle, sell or promise it yet. A house in the dead person's sole name needs a court order or another written route before it can be sold or transferred; a house held in joint tenancy or by the entirety, or in a living trust or land trust, may follow a different path. The pages on [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois), [a parent who died with the house in their name](/learn/illinois/parent-died-house-in-their-name) and [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) show which applies. If siblings disagree, see [siblings who disagree about an inherited house](/learn/illinois/siblings-disagree-inherited-house).

The homestead exemption also matters here. Illinois protects $15,000 of equity in a home you own and live in from judgment creditors, and after the owner dies it continues for a surviving spouse who stays in the home and for children until the youngest turns 18 (735 ILCS 5/12-902). It is a creditor shield, not a right to keep the house. See [the homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death), and for property tax breaks and bills, [property tax exemptions after a spouse dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies).

## Which Illinois tax dates should you write down now?

Two Illinois dates matter, and neither falls inside the first 30 days.

- **Final Illinois income tax return.** A person who dies during the year still owes an IL-1040 for the part of the year they were alive. The executor, administrator or surviving spouse files it, usually by the same April date as the federal return. Details are on the [final Illinois return page](/learn/illinois/final-illinois-income-tax-return-for-someone-who-died), and federal steps are on the [final tax returns page](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns). If the estate earns income after death, see [Form IL-1041](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-il-1041-estate-and-trust-income-tax-return).
- **Illinois estate tax.** An estate worth more than $4 million, counting certain lifetime gifts, must file an Illinois return, and the return and tax are due nine months after death. An extension request must be made within those nine months. An estate at or under $4 million generally has nothing to file. Check where your estate stands on [will my estate owe Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/will-my-estate-owe-illinois-estate-tax) and the [Illinois estate tax page](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).

## What does a worked example look like?

This is an invented example. Marguerite, 74, a widow in DuPage County, dies on a Monday. She leaves a will, a house in her name alone, a checking account of about $38,000 in her name alone, an IRA with her daughter named as beneficiary, and a locked cabinet with two shotguns. Her children are Paul, who lives nearby, and Dana, who lives in Arizona.

- **Day 1.** The funeral home asks who is authorizing arrangements. Marguerite's will says she wants cremation and names Paul as executor. Paul directs it, as the Act allows. Paul locks the house and leaves the cabinet closed. Dana does not have a FOID card, so the guns stay put.
- **Day 3.** Paul finds the original will in a desk. He takes it to the circuit court clerk and files it. He orders ten certified death certificates through the funeral director.
- **Day 6.** Paul lists accounts. The IRA passes to Dana by beneficiary form. The checking account is frozen and in her name alone.
- **Day 14.** Paul checks the small estate affidavit. The house is real estate, so the affidavit cannot cover it, and probate is needed. He books a consultation to open the estate.
- **Day 25.** Letters of office are issued. Paul opens the estate account, keeps the house insured, and writes in his notebook: final IL-1040 in April, and no Illinois estate tax return, since the total is well under $4 million.

Paul made no irreversible moves, which is the aim of the first 30 days.
