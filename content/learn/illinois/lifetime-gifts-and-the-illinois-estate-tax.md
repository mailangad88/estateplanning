Parents with a $4.8 million estate often ask whether they can simply give some of it away. In many states the answer is yes, because gifts leave the estate. Illinois treats gifts differently, and many families discover that after the fact.

This page covers how Illinois counts lifetime gifts, which gifts work, and which ones do little for the estate tax. The general federal rules are in our article on [whether lifetime gifts count toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax). For the state tax itself, see [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) and our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois).

## Does Illinois tax lifetime gifts?

No, Illinois has no gift tax. But Illinois does count certain gifts when it tests the estate. The Attorney General says the $4 million test uses the gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts, and Illinois uses the federal definition (35 ILCS 405). Adjusted taxable gifts are taxable gifts made after 1976, which means gifts above the annual exclusion in the year made.

So the system has three tiers:

| Gift | Federal gift tax return | Counts in Illinois estate test |
|---|---|---|
| Up to $19,000 per recipient per year (2026) | Not required | No, it leaves the estate for good |
| Tuition or medical bills paid directly to the school or provider | Not required | No |
| Above $19,000 per recipient | Form 709 required | Yes, as an adjusted taxable gift, at its value when given |
| To a spouse who is a citizen | Not usually required | No, and it can shift value between spouses |
| To a charity | Not a taxable gift | No |

Gifts to a spouse can help balance two estates, since each spouse has a separate $4 million. See [credit shelter trusts and the Illinois QTIP election](/learn/illinois/illinois-qtip-election-and-credit-shelter-trusts).

## What do the numbers look like?

Ruth, 74, a widow in Springfield, has a $5.0 million estate. Without any gifts, the Illinois tax is about $285,714 by the Attorney General's example. She has five relatives she would like to help: three children and two grandchildren. Here are three plans, with our own estimates.

| Plan | Estate at death | Counts in test | Illinois tax (about) |
|---|---|---|---|
| No gifts | $5,000,000 | $5,000,000 | $285,700 |
| $19,000 to each of five people for four years ($380,000 total) | $4,620,000 | $4,620,000 | $177,100 |
| One $1,000,000 gift to her son now | $4,000,000 | $4,981,000 | about $254,000 |

The annual exclusion plan is the clear winner per dollar. It removes $380,000 from the estate completely and cuts the tax by about $108,600. The lump gift removes the asset, but the taxable part, $981,000, is added back. It saves only a modest amount at the time it is made. The $1,000,000 gift also uses $981,000 of Ruth's federal lifetime exclusion, which is irrelevant if she is under $15 million.

Where does the lump gift help? In growth. If the $1 million asset grows to $1.6 million by Ruth's death and she had kept it, her estate would be $5.6 million with a tax near $413,000. Given away, the growth belongs to her son and never comes back. That is the real benefit of a large lifetime gift. These are rounded estimates, so run the Attorney General's calculator for real figures.

## What is the catch with large gifts?

The recipient takes your basis. Assets passing at death usually get a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) to their value at death, which can erase decades of capital gains. A gifted asset does not. If Ruth's son receives stock she bought for $200,000 that is worth $1 million, his basis is $200,000. If he sells at $1.6 million, the gain is $1.4 million.

For appreciated assets, the income tax cost can be larger than the estate tax saved. For cash or assets that have not risen in value, the catch is small. Our article on [step-up in basis for married couples and joint property](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis-for-married-couples-and-joint-property) shows the same effect with spouses.

Other limits to remember:

- A gift cannot be undone if you need the money back.
- Gifts of a house, farm or business have valuation and control issues.
- Gifts can reduce what a spouse or child gets from other plans.
- Gifts made to qualify for Medicaid are subject to a look-back period, which is a different system. See [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning).

## How do you make gifts the right way?

1. Estimate your Illinois estate first, with the tools on this site or with an attorney.
2. Decide whether you are aiming at the annual exclusion, direct payment of tuition or medical bills, or larger gifts.
3. Prefer cash or assets with a high basis. Hold appreciated assets until death if you can.
4. Keep a record of each gift: date, recipient, amount and how it was valued.
5. File Form 709 for any gift above the annual exclusion, by April 15 of the next year. See [Form 709](/learn/estate-tax/form-709-gift-tax-return-gift-splitting-and-net-gifts).
6. Tell your executor where the records are. The executor needs them for Illinois Form 700. See [Illinois Form 700](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-700-estate-tax-return).

Step 5 matters more than people think. A gift above the exclusion that was never reported can leave the executor guessing about the estate total.

## Who can make gifts on your behalf?

An agent under a power of attorney can make gifts only if the document grants that power and within its limits. Illinois documents often limit it to the annual exclusion. Read about this in [can a power of attorney make gifts](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts), and in our [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) guide.

## Are there gifts that work better than cash?

Yes, a few. Paying a grandchild's tuition directly to the school is not a taxable gift, whatever the amount. Paying someone's medical bills directly to the provider works the same way. Contributions to a 529 plan can be front-loaded, with five years of annual exclusions given at once, which moves a larger sum out of the estate in one step. Gifts to an irrevocable trust for children can also use the annual exclusion, if the trust is drafted so the gift qualifies.

Each of these is a way to move money out of an Illinois estate without creating an adjusted taxable gift. They work best for people who can afford to give steadily over many years, such as a couple in their sixties with a $4.4 million estate. For them, ten years of gifts to four people could remove $1.5 million and keep the estate below the line. See [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies) for more ideas.
