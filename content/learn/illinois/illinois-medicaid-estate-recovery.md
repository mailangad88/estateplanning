Your mother spent her last three years in a nursing home on Medicaid. She has died, and a letter from the state says it may file a claim. You wonder whether the house is gone and whether you now owe money.

Usually the answer is less grim than the letter feels. This article explains how the Illinois program works, using the Department of Healthcare and Family Services (HFS) program pages. For the national picture, see our general piece on [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery). This is education, not legal advice, and the program rules can change.

## The short version

- The claim is against the estate, not against you.
- Whether the home is at risk depends on whether it is in the probate estate and whether a protected family member is alive.
- The first $25,000 of the estate is shielded for recent deaths, and small estates are waived.
- A hardship waiver exists, but you have to ask and prove it.

## What can HFS claim against?

HFS states that estate recovery applies to Medicaid long-term services and supports: home-based services and care in long-term care facilities. For recovery purposes it describes the estate as the real and personal property of the deceased recipient that is subject to probate, such as a house, a car or cash.

The program does not reach everything. HFS's own examples of property it won't collect against include life insurance with a named beneficiary and bank accounts payable on death to another person. Its FAQ adds that assets passing directly to a beneficiary outside probate are generally not part of the estate, including retirement accounts, pension plans, jointly held accounts with survivorship rights and mutual funds. Our article on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains why some property skips the estate.

On the claim's size, HFS's liens page says claims cover cash assistance and medical assistance paid after age 55, and for medical-only recipients with a lien, all medical assistance regardless of age.

## When does HFS hold back?

The state will not seek repayment in these situations, according to HFS:

| Situation | Effect |
|---|---|
| A spouse is still living | No recovery |
| A child under 21 survives | No recovery |
| A child of any age is blind or permanently and totally disabled under Social Security rules | No recovery |
| The estate is worth $25,000 or less | Waived |
| Selling the property would cost more than it is worth | No recovery |
| Recovery would cause undue hardship to heirs | Waiver if requested and proven |

The $25,000 rule needs a closer look. For a date of death on or after July 1, 2022, no recovery is allowed against the first $25,000 of estate value. HFS gives this example: an estate worth $40,000 can satisfy a claim only from the $15,000 above the threshold, and if the claim is larger, the balance stays open in case other assets are found later.

Funeral costs, legal costs and a home mortgage are paid ahead of the estate recovery claim.

## How does the undue hardship waiver work?

HFS lists several circumstances that may qualify. One is a family business, farm or ranch that was the heirs' main income source and was run for at least 12 months before the death. Another is where heirs would need government assistance if the state filed the claim, or could lose assistance if the state did not. Other hardship circumstances may count as well.

The state will not grant a waiver unless the heir asks and supplies proof. The application can be downloaded from the HFS site, and questions go to the Bureau of Collections, Technical Recovery Section. The page I reviewed gave no response deadline, so do not wait; ask HFS in writing as soon as the notice arrives.

## What happens when the notice arrives?

After the recipient's death, HFS sends the estate representative or heirs a notice that it intends to file a claim and asks for information so it can decide. A typical sequence looks like this:

1. Read the notice and note who sent it and the case number.
2. Find out whether the estate is in probate and who the personal representative is. If a claim is filed, it goes through the probate court. See [how probate works in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process).
3. Gather the date of death, a list of probate assets and any mortgage.
4. Check whether a protected family member, such as a surviving spouse or a disabled child, exists.
5. Ask for a hardship waiver in writing if heirs would lose or need benefits.
6. Do not pay from your own pocket unless you choose to keep the house.

Debts of the estate must be paid before property is distributed under a will, and the Medicaid claim is one of them. HFS says heirs may use other funds to pay the claim so the home is not sold, which is a choice and not an obligation.

## What about liens?

HFS says Public Act 102-1037, effective June 2, 2022, bars filing new liens on real property in these cases. Liens filed before that date are still pursued, and a lien is paid from sale proceeds when the property is sold; the state does not force a sale. At death, the lien is not enforced when a spouse, a child under 21, a blind or disabled child, or in some cases a sibling occupies the property.

Property in another state can also be reached if it is part of the estate. If the person received Medicaid in two states, both may claim, and the probate court divides the estate.

## Can planning reduce the exposure?

Sometimes, and early is better. Because recovery follows the probate estate, how title is held matters. But gifts made inside the 60-month look-back period can cause a Medicaid penalty period in most states, so changing title without advice can backfire. The [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) is one tool people ask about; whether it fits depends on the facts and on how HFS treats it, which this article does not cover. Our [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and [exempt assets](/learn/elder-care/what-assets-are-exempt-from-medicaid) pages explain what is protected during life, and the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) maps the rest.

## A worked example

Walter, a widower, died in a nursing home in 2026 on Medicaid. His probate estate is a house worth $180,000 and a checking account of $6,000. He has two adult children, neither disabled. His funeral cost $9,000 and a small mortgage remains.

Funeral costs, legal costs and the mortgage come first. The first $25,000 of the estate is protected from recovery because he died after July 1, 2022. HFS may then claim against what remains, up to what it paid. Because no spouse, young child or disabled child survives, none of the deferrals apply. His daughter, who has been out of work and receives SNAP, could ask for a hardship waiver and should send proof with the request.

Now change one fact. Suppose Walter had named his son as beneficiary of a payable-on-death account holding $30,000. That account would generally pass outside probate, and the estate claim would not reach it. Facts like this are why we look at how each asset is titled before anyone sells or signs anything. Our [payable-on-death and joint accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) article covers the Illinois mechanics.

The practical lesson is to talk to the personal representative early. They control what is sold, what is paid and in what order, and their choices decide whether the family keeps the house.
