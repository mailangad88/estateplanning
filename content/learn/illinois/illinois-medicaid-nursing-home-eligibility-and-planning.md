Your father has just moved into a nursing home, and the monthly bill is more than his income. Someone mentioned Medicaid. Now you need to know what Illinois asks for, what a gift to the kids would do, and whether Mom can stay in the house.

This page is about qualifying and planning before and during the application. What happens after the person dies, including the state's claim against the estate, is a different topic, and our page on [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) owns it. This is general education, not legal advice. Asset limits and allowances change from year to year, so confirm current numbers with the Department of Healthcare and Family Services (HFS) or the Department of Human Services (DHS) before you act.

## The short version

- Medicaid pays for nursing home care only after the person meets a medical need standard and a financial test.
- The financial test looks at what the person owns and at what they gave away in the last five years.
- A spouse at home has protections that can keep the couple from being left with nothing.
- Planning is lawful and common, but timing and paperwork decide whether it works.

## What does Illinois test when someone applies?

Two things. The first is need: the applicant must require nursing facility level of care, which the state decides through its own assessment process. The second is money. The applicant's countable assets and income must fall within the program limits. DHS policy manuals treat long-term care alongside the AABD medical programs, and those manuals carry the dollar limits. We do not quote an asset figure here because the amounts are reset by the agencies, so ask for the current number when you apply.

Not every asset counts. Our national page on [what assets are exempt from Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/what-assets-are-exempt-from-medicaid) explains the usual categories. HFS adds that income-producing property, including farmland, is excluded from countable resources.

## How far back does the state look?

Sixty months. For applications on or after January 1, 2007, DHS treats transfers made more than 60 months before the application as allowable, and HFS extended the review period from three to five years effective January 1, 2012. A transfer for less than fair market value is presumed to be intentional, though that presumption can be rebutted with objective evidence. Our page on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains the idea in general terms, and the [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) helps you find the window.

When does the penalty start? HFS describes the penalty period for an improper transfer as beginning on the later of the transfer date or the date the person has entered a nursing home and been found Medicaid eligible. That detail changes planning. Giving the house to a child while the parent is still healthy does not start a clock that runs quietly in the background. The clock starts when the person is in care and otherwise qualifies. HFS also states that partial returns of a gift no longer reduce a penalty, so the whole transfer has to be returned to end it.

## Which transfers do not cause a penalty?

DHS lists several categories of allowable transfers:

| Transfer | Treatment |
|---|---|
| To the community spouse | Generally allowable |
| Home to a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child | Allowable |
| Home to certain siblings or a caregiving child | Allowable in listed cases |
| Personal effects, household goods and one vehicle to the community spouse | Allowable |
| Life estate purchased, after 12 months of residence | Allowable |
| Involuntary transfers, such as theft | Allowable |
| Transfers returned in full | Allowable |

"Community spouse" means the husband or wife who is not in the nursing home. The details of the spousal protections, such as how much of the couple's savings the spouse at home may keep, are in the state's spousal impoverishment rules, which the agencies update. Our national page on [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples) explains how those rules work in general.

## What about the house?

The home is where families get hurt most often, because it is both the biggest asset and the place everyone loves. In Illinois the answer turns on who lives there and on how it is held. The state's rules for a home a spouse still lives in are technical, so ask before assuming it is safe or unsafe. A transfer of the home to a child who cared for the parent, to a child under 21, or to a blind or disabled child is on the allowable list. A sale, a gift to an adult child who did not provide care, or a reverse mortgage can all produce a different result.

After death, the home is a separate problem, covered by the recovery page linked above. For a broader view of tradeoffs, see [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).

## Do annuities, loans and trusts help?

Sometimes, and the state watches them closely. HFS requires disclosure of annuities, promissory notes, loans and mortgages. The State must be named a remainder beneficiary of an annuity, and notes, loans and mortgages must be assigned to the State on death. Terms must be actuarially sound, and balloon payments and cancellation at death are restricted. An annuity that does not meet those rules is treated as a transfer for less than fair market value.

An irrevocable trust can also change what counts, but the transfer into it is itself a gift that the look-back reaches. See our page on the [Medicaid asset protection trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) for how that works and what you give up.

## Steps to take, in order

1. Gather five years of statements for every account, plus deeds, titles, insurance policies and any records of gifts.
2. Write down every transfer in the last 60 months, even small ones, with the date and the recipient.
3. Check what is countable. Do not pay anyone back or move money until you know the effect.
4. Meet with an attorney if there are meaningful assets, a spouse at home, or a recent gift.
5. Apply through ABE, or have an agent under a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) apply.
6. Respond to the caseworker's requests quickly. Missing documents are the usual cause of delays.
7. Read the decision notice, calendar the appeal deadline and ask for help if there is a penalty.

For a walkthrough of the application, the page on [how to apply for Medicaid for a parent](/learn/elder-care/how-to-apply-for-medicaid-for-a-parent) goes into more detail.

## Example

Evelyn, 82, lives in a nursing home in Peoria, and her husband, Frank, is at home. Their daughter suggests Evelyn simply gift her savings to the children. Their attorney points out that a gift made now falls inside the look-back and could trigger a penalty once Evelyn is eligible, during which Medicaid will not pay. Instead, Evelyn transfers assets to Frank, which is generally allowable, and they re-title their accounts with advice. The couple fills in the facts, applies and keeps copies of everything they send.
