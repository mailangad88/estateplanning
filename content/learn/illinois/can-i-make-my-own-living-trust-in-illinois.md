You have seen online living trust kits, or a friend made one at the kitchen table, and you wonder whether you can do the same. Illinois lets you. The harder question is whether your trust will do its job when your family needs it.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), covers what Illinois law requires, which is less than most people think, and the steps where do-it-yourself trusts tend to break, with a table to sort your own plan. If you already have a homemade trust and want someone to check it, you can [book a consultation about your trust](/book?from=learn-can-i-make-my-own-living-trust-in-illinois&topic=trust).

## Can you legally create a living trust without a lawyer in Illinois?

Yes. Nothing in the Illinois Trust Code (760 ILCS 3) says a trust must be drafted or signed by an attorney. The Code lists the ways a trust can be created, including a transfer of property to a trustee or a declaration that you hold identifiable property as trustee (760 ILCS 3/401). It then lists what a valid trust needs (760 ILCS 3/402), as our [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) page sets out:

- You have capacity.
- You show an intent to create the trust.
- There is a definite beneficiary.
- The trustee has duties to perform.
- The same person is not both the sole trustee and the sole beneficiary.

One Illinois rule deserves special attention when you use a form. A settlor may revoke a trust only if the document expressly says it is revocable or gives an unrestricted power to amend it (760 ILCS 3/602). A generic national template that skips that sentence can leave you with a trust you cannot easily change.

Oral trusts must be proved by clear and convincing evidence (760 ILCS 3/407), so put yours in writing. On online services and seminar pitches generally, see [online living trust versus a lawyer](/learn/trusts/online-living-trust-vs-lawyer). The free [Illinois trust validity and scam check](/free/illinois-trust-validity-and-scam-check) is a quick way to test a document you already have.

## So where do do-it-yourself trusts go wrong?

Almost never at the signing. Homemade trusts usually fail later, when the house is deeded, a spouse dies, a bank asks for proof, or life changes and nobody updates the paperwork. Wills show the same pattern, as [do I need a lawyer for a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/do-i-need-a-lawyer-for-a-will-in-illinois) explains, but a trust has more moving parts because it only works on property it owns.

| Step | Easy to do yourself | Where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Write the trust document | Yes, if the form fits your family | It does not say it is revocable, or names no backup beneficiary |
| Deed the house into the trust | The idea is simple | Wrong legal description, unrecorded deed, missed lender or assessor call |
| Retitle bank and brokerage accounts | Yes, with each institution's forms | Accounts opened later stay in your own name |
| Beneficiary forms for retirement accounts and insurance | Filling in the form is easy | Retitling a retirement account by mistake, or forms that contradict the trust |
| Sign a pour-over will | Possible with two witnesses | Treated as optional, or signed without proper witnesses |
| Choose a successor trustee | Naming someone is easy | No backup, or a person who cannot handle banks and siblings |
| Prepare a certification of trust | A short document | Missing when the bank or title company asks for it |
| Plan for the Illinois estate tax | Only for estates clearly well under the threshold | "All to the survivor" wastes a spouse's exclusion |
| Fit in a land trust | Rarely | The beneficial interest is never assigned to the living trust |
| Keep it current | Reading it once a year is easy | Divorce, a move, a new property or a death of a beneficiary goes unaddressed |

## Why is funding the step that breaks most homemade trusts?

Because the document owns nothing on its own. A house moves in only by a deed to you as trustee, signed, notarized and recorded with the county recorder. Bank and brokerage accounts move when the institution retitles them. Retirement accounts normally stay in your name and point to the trust, or to people, through the beneficiary form. The asset-by-asset method is on our page about [funding a living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/funding-a-living-trust-in-illinois), and the deed steps are in [how to put your house in a living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-put-your-house-in-a-living-trust-in-illinois).

Kits tend to stop at the signature page. A wrong legal description or a deed that never gets recorded leaves the house in your name. If a house is still in your name alone at death, it generally needs probate, and the small estate affidavit cannot move real estate (755 ILCS 5/25-1). Our page on an [unfunded living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/unfunded-living-trust-in-illinois) walks through the routes each leftover asset takes.

## Do you still need a will with a homemade trust?

Yes. A pour-over will catches anything you forgot to retitle and sends it to your trustee. Illinois allows a will to leave property to the trustee of a written trust (755 ILCS 5/4-4), but it is still a will. It needs two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3), and whoever holds it must file it with the circuit clerk within 30 days after death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). Only a will can nominate a guardian for minor children. A kit that ships the trust alone leaves those jobs undone. The details are on our page about [pour-over wills in Illinois](/learn/illinois/pour-over-wills-in-illinois).

## Who should you name as successor trustee?

Someone who can deal patiently with banks, title companies and family, plus a backup. Forms make this a blank to fill in, but the successor trustee takes over when you die or lose capacity, with real duties. For a trust that becomes irrevocable at death, that person must send a written notice to qualified beneficiaries within 90 days (760 ILCS 3/813.1). Our page on the [Illinois successor trustee's first 90 days](/learn/illinois/illinois-successor-trustee-first-90-days) shows the workload, and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) covers who fits.

The successor trustee will also need a [certification of trust](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-certification-of-trust), a short signed statement that proves the trust exists without handing over the whole document. Illinois protects a person who relies on one in good faith (760 ILCS 3/1013). Keep a signed one with your trust papers.

## What does a kit miss about the Illinois estate tax?

National forms are written around the federal estate tax, with its $15 million per person exclusion in 2026. Illinois has its own estate tax for estates over $4 million, and it does not allow portability, so a spouse's unused $4 million is lost unless the plan uses it. Our [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) page explains how that works. A living trust does not reduce the tax by itself.

Kit language usually leaves everything to the surviving spouse outright. For a couple near $4 million combined, that pushes the whole amount into the survivor's estate. The usual fix is a credit shelter trust at the first death, described in [credit shelter trusts and the Illinois QTIP election](/learn/illinois/illinois-qtip-election-and-credit-shelter-trusts). For how joint trusts work in general, see [a joint living trust for married couples](/learn/trusts/joint-living-trust-for-married-couples). You can get a rough figure from the [estate tax estimator](/tools/estate-tax-estimator).

Married owners also face a title question. Illinois lets spouses keep their homestead as tenants by the entirety inside a revocable trust when the married settlors are the primary beneficiaries and the deed says so (765 ILCS 1005/1c). A generic deed form will not include that wording. See [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).

## What if your property is already in an Illinois land trust?

Then the deed does not change. A land trust holds title, usually with a bank as trustee, and you own the beneficial interest. To bring that property into your living trust, you assign the beneficial interest to your living trust in writing, following the land trust agreement and the land trustee's paperwork. National kits rarely mention land trusts. Our page on [land trust versus living trust](/learn/illinois/land-trust-vs-living-trust) explains which document does what.

## How do later changes trip up homemade trusts?

Life moves and the trust stays the same. A new house in your own name, an inheritance, a divorce, a move or a beneficiary's death can each leave it out of step. Illinois does fix one of these by law: a divorce revokes provisions in favor of a former spouse unless the trust or the judgment says otherwise (760 ILCS 3/605). Our page on a [revocable trust after divorce in Illinois](/learn/illinois/revocable-trust-after-divorce-in-illinois) covers the details. Everything else needs you to act.

Change a trust by signed amendment or full restatement, never margin notes. See [how to change or revoke a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-change-or-revoke-a-living-trust), and the free [trust amendment versus restatement guide](/free/trust-amendment-vs-restatement-guide) for choosing between them. If you lose capacity first, only an agent under a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) with the right powers can fund or adjust things on your behalf.

## Example: Tom and Ruth's kitchen-table trust

This is an invented example. Tom, 66, and Ruth, 64, live in Schaumburg. They sign a joint revocable trust from an online kit and file it in a drawer. Their house, two 401(k) accounts, a brokerage account and life insurance come to about $4.6 million.

They retitle the brokerage account. They never record a deed, so the house stays in joint tenancy. The kit leaves everything to the survivor outright.

When Tom dies, the house passes to Ruth as surviving joint tenant and his 401(k) goes to her by beneficiary form. Now everything sits with Ruth, over $4 million, and Tom's Illinois exclusion is gone. When Ruth dies, her estate files an Illinois return, and the house, still in her name alone, needs probate before it reaches the trust. One recorded deed and credit shelter language at the first death would have changed both results. The document itself was valid the whole time.

## What should you do if you want to make your own?

1. **Decide whether a trust fits.** For a single house, a transfer on death instrument may be simpler. Compare them in [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house), or try the [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust).
2. **Check the form.** It should say it is revocable and name backup trustees and beneficiaries.
3. **Sign the pour-over will with two disinterested witnesses** at the same time.
4. **Fund it the same month.** Use the free [trust funding checklist](/free/trust-funding-checklist) and record the deed.
5. **Add up both spouses' assets.** Near $4 million, have the tax language reviewed.
6. **Keep a signed certification of trust** and tell your successor trustee where everything is.
7. **Recheck after every purchase, move, marriage, divorce or death.**
