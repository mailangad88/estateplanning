Your father has moved into a nursing home in Illinois, and the first bill is far more than his Social Security. Medicaid is the long-term answer, but he has savings, so it is not the answer yet. The family needs to know which money to use, in what order, and how to spend it without causing a problem with the later application.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is about the months between admission and Medicaid approval. The rules for qualifying, including the look-back and the allowed transfers, live on the page about [qualifying for Medicaid nursing home care in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning), and this page links to it instead of repeating it. If your parent's savings are about to run low, a [consultation about a parent's nursing home costs](/book?from=learn-paying-for-a-nursing-home-before-medicaid-in-illinois&topic=parent) can check the order before money moves.

## The short version

- Expect to pay privately until countable assets are within the program limit.
- Use the resident's money for the resident, at fair value.
- Do not give money to family to "speed things up." Gifts inside 60 months can delay coverage.
- Apply through ABE before the account hits zero, and keep every receipt.

## Does Medicare pay for the nursing home first?

Only for a short skilled stay, and only if the conditions are met. Medicare covers a skilled nursing facility stay after an inpatient hospital admission of at least three days in a row, for daily skilled care such as therapy or wound care. Original Medicare pays in full for the first 20 days. From day 21 through day 100 the resident pays a daily coinsurance that Medicare sets each year, and after day 100 Medicare pays nothing for that benefit period. Our page on [what Medicare covers for long-term care](/learn/elder-care/what-medicare-covers-for-long-term-care) explains each condition and the appeal when coverage ends early.

Two traps catch families. Nights "under observation" do not count toward the three days. And Medicare never pays for custodial care, meaning help with bathing, dressing, eating and supervision, which is what most long-term residents need. When the skilled need ends, often well before day 100, the bill becomes private pay.

## Which sources pay, and in what order?

Most families use a mix. This table lays out the usual sources before Medicaid, roughly in the order they come into play.

| Source | What it covers | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Medicare Part A | Short skilled stay after a 3-day inpatient admission | Full for 20 days, coinsurance to day 100, no custodial care |
| Medigap or a Medicare Advantage plan | Often the day 21 to 100 coinsurance | Does not extend past Medicare's limits |
| Long-term care insurance | A daily or monthly benefit once triggers are met | Elimination period and benefit cap; file the claim early |
| Monthly income (Social Security, pension) | Part of each month's bill | Keeps flowing after Medicaid starts, mostly to the home |
| Cash and savings | The bulk of the private-pay months | Spend for fair value and keep the receipts |
| Investments and retirement accounts | Later private-pay months | Taxes on withdrawals; check before cashing out |
| Sale of non-exempt property | A second home, extra vehicle, land | Income-producing property, including farmland, is excluded from countable resources in Illinois |
| VA benefits, if a veteran or surviving spouse | Possible monthly help | Separate VA rules; see [VA Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance) |
| Illinois Medicaid | Long-term custodial care once eligible | Need, asset and income tests, and the look-back |

The page on [long-term care insurance](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance) explains benefit triggers and the elimination period. The national comparison of [private pay versus Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/private-pay-vs-medicaid-in-a-nursing-home) shows what changes on the day the program takes over.

## What counts as spending down in Illinois?

Spending down means using the resident's countable money on things that benefit the resident, at fair value, until assets are within the program limit. The nursing home bill itself is the largest spend-down item for most families. Other fair-value uses commonly include paying the resident's own debts, medical equipment, hearing aids, dental work, clothing, and repairs to a home the resident or a spouse uses. The national page on [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down) has the full list and the traps.

The line Illinois draws is between an exchange and a gift. When someone applies, Illinois reviews transfers made in the 60 months before the application, and a transfer for less than fair market value can lead to a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay. HFS describes that penalty as starting on the later of the transfer date or the date the person is in a nursing home and found otherwise eligible. HFS also says partial returns of a gift no longer reduce a penalty. So a gift made now to "get under the limit" delays coverage at exactly the moment the money has run out. The [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) shows where the 60-month window falls.

A prepaid funeral is a common question. An HFS rule summary, quoted on the eligibility page, says that in the retroactive months before the application month, prepaid funeral and burial contracts up to $10,000 are not counted, and different rules apply after that. Ask the funeral home and the caseworker how a contract will be treated before buying one.

## Can I pay family for care?

Yes, with paperwork created before the first payment. A relative can be paid fair wages for real care, but payments with no written agreement can be read as gifts during the look-back. The page on [paying a family caregiver in Illinois](/learn/illinois/paying-a-family-caregiver) sets out the four things that matter: a written agreement signed before pay starts, a rate in line with local agency rates, a time log kept as you go, and payments from the parent's own account by check or transfer. The [personal care agreement guide](/free/personal-care-agreement-guide) has an outline an attorney can adapt.

Once a parent is in a nursing home, the facility provides most of the care, so price only the real tasks a relative still does. Paying a child a lump sum for "years of help already given," with nothing signed back then, is the pattern most easily called a gift. If the child is also the agent under a power of attorney, the document must allow the payment, because the agent is on both sides.

## What can a spouse keep?

More than most couples expect. Illinois generally allows transfers to the community spouse, meaning the spouse who is not in the nursing home, and lists the transfer of the home to a spouse as allowable. Under the spousal impoverishment rules, the community spouse can also keep a protected share of the couple's savings, often called the [community spouse resource allowance](/glossary/community-spouse-resource-allowance), and may receive part of the resident's income. Federal law sets a range for the protected savings, $32,532 to $162,660 for 2026 according to our [spend-down page](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down), and each state applies its own figure. Ask HFS or the caseworker for the current Illinois amount rather than relying on a number you heard.

Two cautions. The spouse at home needs a plan for their own possible care, and a will that leaves everything outright to the spouse in the nursing home can undo the protection. Our page on [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples) explains the allowances, and [protecting your house from a nursing home in Illinois](/learn/illinois/protecting-your-house-from-a-nursing-home-in-illinois) covers the home.

## What does this look like for one family?

Example, with invented names and figures for illustration only. Ruth, 86, a widow in Springfield, enters a nursing home after a hip fracture and a four-day inpatient hospital stay. She has $96,000 in savings, a $12,000 car she will not drive again, Social Security of $1,900 a month and an old long-term care policy that pays $100 a day after a 90-day waiting period. Suppose her home charges $9,000 a month.

1. **Months 1 to 3.** Medicare covers the first 20 days of rehab in full. Her therapy ends on day 34, so Medicare's coinsurance days end there too. Her Medigap plan pays most of that coinsurance. Her daughter, Joyce, who is Ruth's agent under a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property), files the long-term care insurance claim the first week.
2. **From month 4.** The policy pays about $3,000 a month after the waiting period. Social Security covers $1,900. Savings cover the remaining $4,100, plus the private-pay months before the policy began.
3. **Spending down.** Joyce pays Ruth's $3,800 credit card balance, buys her a hearing aid and new clothes, and sells the car at its market value, adding the proceeds to Ruth's account. She keeps every receipt.
4. **The gift that did not happen.** Ruth's son suggests moving $30,000 to the grandchildren. Joyce asks an attorney first and learns that the gift would fall inside the 60-month look-back and could start a penalty once Ruth is eligible, with the bill still due.
5. **Applying.** When the savings are on course to reach the limit within a few months, Joyce applies through ABE with five years of statements, using the documents checklist in the free [Illinois Medicaid nursing home guide](/free/illinois-medicaid-long-term-care-guide).

Change one fact. If Ruth's husband were alive and at home, the plan would look different: transfers to him would generally be allowable, and he could keep a protected share of the savings.

## What if the money runs out?

Then Medicaid should already be in motion. A nursing home that takes Medicaid can usually convert a resident in a certified bed, and nonpayment is an allowed reason for discharge, so a pending, complete application puts the family in a much stronger position than unpaid bills. Ask at admission whether the bed is Medicaid-certified. The page on [paying for a nursing home while waiting for Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/paying-for-a-nursing-home-while-waiting-for-medicaid) covers the pending months and refunds after approval.

Children are not required to pay from their own money. Federal rules generally bar a home that takes Medicare or Medicaid from requiring a family member's personal promise to pay as a condition of admission, as our page on [whether a nursing home can make your children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) explains. Sign admission papers as your parent's agent, not as a personally liable "responsible party." If no one holds that authority yet, see [how to get power of attorney for a parent in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-get-power-of-attorney-for-a-parent-in-illinois).

After death, HFS may make a claim against the probate estate for long-term care benefits. Recovery is deferred while a spouse, a child under 21 or a blind or disabled child survives, and for deaths on or after July 1, 2022, the first $25,000 of estate value is protected. Heirs are not personally liable. The page on [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) has the rest.
