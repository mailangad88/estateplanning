Grandparents want to give a little to a newborn. A father wants his will to leave something to a nephew, with no guardian and no court. The Illinois Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, found at 760 ILCS 20, is the standard answer: an adult gives property to a custodian who holds it for the child until a fixed age.

It is simple and cheap, and it has hard edges. This page explains how the Illinois version works, what the custodian can and cannot do, and when a trust fits better. For a side-by-side with trusts, see [UTMA account vs. trust for a minor child](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child). Other Illinois pages are in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What is an Illinois UTMA custodianship?

It is a way to hold property for someone under age 21 without opening a guardianship or drafting a trust. The statute defines a minor as a person under 21 and an adult as one who is 21 or older. The Act applies when the person making the transfer, the minor or the custodian lives in Illinois, or when the property is in Illinois.

The key legal features, all from the Act:

- The transfer is irrevocable. The property vests in the child, with the custodian in control.
- Each transfer is for one minor and one custodian.
- The account or title is registered in a prescribed form, along the lines of "Name, as custodian for Child under the Illinois Uniform Transfers to Minors Act." Section 10 lists the methods for different kinds of property, such as registration, delivery or a deed.
- Real estate can be held in a custodianship, and the custodian's powers end at the termination age without a new deed.

## How can property get into the account?

Section 4 lets a person who has the right to name a future recipient, for example in a will or beneficiary form, nominate a custodian in advance. Section 5 covers a gift made while alive. Section 6 allows an executor, trustee or another obligor to transfer property to a custodian when the governing instrument allows it, and it presumes that authority unless the instrument forbids it. Sections 7 and 8 let a fiduciary or a person who owes the minor money make a transfer in limited circumstances.

Those last two have dollar limits worth knowing. Section 7 requires court authorization for fiduciary transfers over $10,000. Section 8 limits a non-fiduciary obligor's transfer to $50,000 when no custodian was nominated.

A will can be the source. Marcus writes into his will, "I leave $15,000 to my nephew Eli, to be held by my sister Dana as custodian under the Illinois Uniform Transfers to Minors Act until he reaches 21." Dana does not have to be appointed by a court. She receives the money from the executor and signs a receipt.

## What does the custodian have to do?

Section 13 requires the custodian to control, register, manage and invest the property prudently. The custodian must keep it separate and identifiable and keep records. Those records have to be available to the child's parent or representative, or to the minor if he or she is 14 or older.

Section 14 gives the custodian the powers of an unmarried adult owner, but only in a custodial capacity. Section 15 lets the custodian spend or apply the property for the minor's benefit without a court order. A court may order distributions on petition, and a minor who is 14 or older can petition.

Compensation and bond are treated in section 16. The custodian can recover reasonable expenses, may elect reasonable annual compensation (not available to the person who made the gift under section 5), and generally does not need a bond. A custodian who takes no pay is liable only for bad faith, intentional wrongdoing, gross negligence or failure to meet the prudence standard.

Practical rules a custodian should follow:

1. Keep the money in its own account, titled correctly, never mixed with the family checking account.
2. Keep receipts for anything paid on the child's behalf, such as tutoring, braces or camp.
3. Do not use the account to cover ordinary household costs a parent would pay anyway. Ask an attorney if you are unsure where the line sits.
4. Keep a one-page annual record of balance and activity.
5. Ask an attorney or tax adviser before large withdrawals.

A custodian who gets this wrong is not in trouble with a judge by default. The usual trouble starts when the child turns 21, or when an angry relative asks for an accounting under section 20, and the custodian cannot show where the money went. A shoebox of receipts and a yearly one-pager answer that question before anyone asks it.

## When must the money go to the child?

Section 21 says the custodian must transfer the property to the minor, or to the minor's estate if the minor has died, at the earliest of three events.

| Event | Trigger |
|---|---|
| Transfer by gift or by a will or trust (sections 5 and 6) | The minor turns 21 |
| Transfer by fiduciary or obligor (sections 7 and 8) | The minor reaches majority under other Illinois law |
| Either kind | The minor dies |

Age 21 matters more than it looks. A nineteen-year-old with a custodial account cannot be told "wait two years" by a custodian who thinks he is not ready. At 21, the property must be handed over. For a gift of $2,000 for a college textbook budget, that is fine. For a gift of $200,000, many families would rather postpone it. A trust can set a later age or stagger distributions, as explained in [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children). The gap between the two is the heart of the choice, and our page on [what happens when a minor inherits without a trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust) shows the downside of leaving it open.

## What about taxes and aid?

The gift is the child's, so the donor's annual gift exclusion applies to it. As of 2026 that is $19,000 per recipient, as explained in [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion). Income earned in the account is generally reported by the child, and special rules can apply to a child's unearned income, so ask a tax adviser. Because the property belongs to the child, it can count as the child's asset on financial aid forms and for benefit programs that look at the child's resources. A child with a disability should not be given UTMA property without advice, since it may affect public benefits.

## When is a trust better?

Choose a UTMA account when the amount is modest, the child is healthy, and 21 is acceptable. Choose a trust when:

- The amount is large enough that 21 feels too early.
- You want a trustee with a written standard, not a custodian with broad discretion.
- The child may have special needs.
- You want to name a successor and set rules for the money.

A custodian under the Act can also move the property into a qualified minor's trust under section 15, which is a tool, not a plan.
