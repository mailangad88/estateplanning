You paid for a living trust to keep your family out of court, and now your lawyer wants you to sign a will too. That can feel like paying twice. It is not a mistake. The will does a few jobs the trust cannot, and it catches anything you forget to retitle.

Our general article on the [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) explains the idea. This page covers the Illinois rules: the statute, the signing, the filing deadline and what happens to the assets that pour over. The state hub is our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What does Illinois law say about pour-over wills?

The Probate Act lets a will give property to the trustee of a trust that is described in a written instrument (755 ILCS 5/4-4). The trust can be revocable, and the statute is written so that the gift does not fail merely because the trust can be changed. Whether later amendments to the trust are honored depends on the statute's wording, so have an attorney confirm the point for your documents.

The practical rule is simple. Name the trust exactly, by its title and date, in the will. Many wills say "to the trustee then serving under the [Name] Living Trust dated [date]." A wrong date or name can cause arguments after death.

## How is a pour-over will signed in Illinois?

It follows the same rules as any Illinois will. You sign it, and two credible witnesses watch you sign and sign in your presence (755 ILCS 5/4-3). Illinois lets a will be proved by a sworn attestation clause in the will, which avoids calling the witnesses to court (755 ILCS 5/6-4). Our pages on [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) and the [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) list the steps.

The trust has its own rules, and the two documents do not borrow from each other. A trust signed with a notary does not make a will valid.

## What happens to a pour-over will after death?

Someone must file the original will, an executor must open an estate, and the court must admit the will before any asset moves to the trust. The steps follow.

1. The person holding the original will delivers it to the circuit clerk within 30 days after death (755 ILCS 5/6-1).
2. The executor files a petition to admit the will, and the court issues letters of office.
3. The executor gathers assets in the decedent's name and publishes notice to creditors.
4. Claims are paid, and the executor files the paperwork to close the estate.
5. The executor delivers what is left to the trustee.
6. The trustee follows the trust terms, including the 90-day notice described in our page on [what an Illinois trustee must tell beneficiaries](/learn/illinois/illinois-trustee-duty-to-inform-beneficiaries).

Assets that pour over have a date-of-death tax basis, as most inherited assets do, so the family generally gets a step-up in basis. Creditors also keep their rights against assets that pass through the estate. Illinois generally bars claims not filed within two years of death (755 ILCS 5/18-12), and that clock does not stop because a trust exists.

## Can a small pour-over estate skip probate?

Sometimes. If what is left is personal property of $150,000 or less, not counting registered vehicles, with no letters outstanding, a family member may collect it by small estate affidavit (755 ILCS 5/25-1). Real estate is not included. A bank may ask extra questions when the will gives everything to a trust, so ask before you plan on it. Our article on the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) has the details.

## What do the will and the trust each do?

They split the work, and neither replaces the other.

| Task | Pour-over will | Living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Names guardian for minor children | Yes | No |
| Names an executor for leftover assets | Yes | No |
| Holds assets and avoids probate | No | Yes, for funded assets |
| Manages assets if you are incapacitated | No | Yes, for funded assets |
| Keeps terms private | No, it becomes public when filed | Mostly yes |
| Directs how heirs receive property | By pouring into the trust | Yes |

A guardian nomination in a will is one reason even a fully funded plan needs a will. Our page on [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children) shows how.

## Example: Marisol and the forgotten account

Marisol, 61, of Rockford, signed a trust and a pour-over will in 2020 and deeded her house to the trust. In 2023 she moved $34,000 from a CD into a new savings account in her own name. When she died, her son Luis found the account. Because the total in her name was well under $150,000 and there was no house in her name, Luis used an affidavit with the bank in [Winnebago County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/winnebago) and the money went to the trustee. Had she owned a lot in her name alone, he would have needed probate.

## How do you keep a pour-over will from doing too much?

Fund the trust and review it. The will should catch only strays. Use the [Illinois funding guide](/learn/illinois/funding-a-living-trust-in-illinois) to move assets, and read the page on an [unfunded Illinois trust](/learn/illinois/unfunded-living-trust-in-illinois) to see what happens if you do not. Store the original will where your executor can find it, as our page on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) explains.

## Should the executor and the trustee be the same person?

Often yes, but not always. One person handling both jobs avoids a hand-off and a second set of questions from the bank. The risk is that one person then controls the whole process with little oversight, which can strain siblings. Some families name a child as executor and a different child or a bank as trustee. The will can name a backup executor too. Our page on [who can be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-can-be-an-executor-in-illinois) lists the eligibility rules, and the article on [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains how much court oversight the executor will face.

Keep a copy of the signed will with your trust binder and tell your executor where the original is. A will that cannot be found sends the family into the process described in our article on a [lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will), which adds delay and cost.
