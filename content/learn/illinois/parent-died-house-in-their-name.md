Your mother or father has died. There was no will, or there was one nobody can find, and the house is still titled in their name. The mortgage statement keeps arriving, a neighbor has offered to buy it, and a sibling wants to move in. You are trying to find out what you can actually do this month.

This page lays out the order families usually go in for a house in Illinois. It is general education, not legal advice, and what applies to you depends on the recorded deed. For the wider picture, our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links every related topic. If you have a will and want the sale mechanics, read [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois).

## Who owns the house after a parent dies in Illinois?

Ownership depends on how the deed reads, and the recorded deed is the only reliable place to look. The family's memory of "Dad put my name on it" does not count until it is on paper at the county recording office.

<!-- visual: whatif id=house-stuck-in-probate -->

| How the deed or papers read | What happens at death | Probate for the house? |
|---|---|---|
| Parent's name alone, no will | Heirs by statute inherit, but need an administrator to act | Usually yes |
| Parent's name alone, with a will | The will controls, but the executor needs letters first | Usually yes |
| Parent and another person as joint tenants with survivorship | Survivor owns it | No |
| Parent and spouse as tenants by the entirety | Surviving spouse owns it | No |
| Transfer on death instrument recorded before the death | Named beneficiary takes it after filing paperwork | No |
| Living trust or land trust holds title | Successor trustee acts | No |

Our article on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains the wording that creates survivorship. Two names on a deed with no extra words usually make tenants in common, which means the parent's half still goes through the estate. The rules for the recorded instrument are in [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument), and the trust version is in [land trusts when the owner dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-land-trust-when-the-owner-dies).

## Do you have to go through probate if the house is only in your parent's name?

Usually yes. Real estate held in one person's name, with no survivorship and no recorded transfer on death instrument, is a probate asset. The court appoints an executor if there is a will, or an administrator if there is not, and that person receives letters of office. Until then, nobody can sign a listing agreement, a sale contract or a deed for the estate.

The small estate affidavit does not fix this. It covers personal property up to $150,000 and says nothing that reaches real estate (755 ILCS 5/25-1). Our page on the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) shows what it can do, which is useful for the bank account but not the house.

One more point surprises people. A deed signed by the heirs, with no court appointment, usually gets rejected by a title company even when every sibling agrees. The company is protecting its buyer from someone the family forgot. If you wonder whether a sworn family statement can stand in for a court, the companion article on [heirship affidavits versus probate](/learn/illinois/heirship-affidavit-vs-probate) covers that.

## Who inherits the house when there is no will?

Illinois divides the estate by statute (755 ILCS 5/2-1). With a surviving spouse and children, the spouse takes one-half and the children share the other half. With no spouse, the children share everything equally. If a child died earlier, that child's own children take that share. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) walks through the full order.

Take Rosa, whose father, Hector, died a widower in Berwyn. He had three children: Rosa, Luis and Mari. Mari died two years ago and left two sons. The house passes in thirds. Rosa gets one-third, Luis gets one-third, and Mari's sons split her third. Four people now own pieces of a bungalow, and every one of them has to be accounted for before a buyer's title company is satisfied. That is also where a stray, unknown heir can derail a closing.

## What should you do first, before any court filing?

Keep the house protected and keep the money moving. You can do all of this without authority.

1. Secure it. Change locks if several people have keys, and take out cash, medications, firearms and loose papers.
2. Call the homeowner's insurer. Say the owner died, and ask how long a vacant house stays covered. Many policies restrict coverage after a stretch of vacancy.
3. Gather the deed, the latest mortgage statement and the latest property tax bill. Look the parcel up with the county to see the recorded deed, since that decides which row of the table above you are in.
4. Look for a will. Anyone holding one must file it with the circuit court clerk (755 ILCS 5/6-1). Our page on [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) lists where to look.
5. Keep paying the mortgage, taxes and utilities, and save every receipt. Sort out reimbursement through the estate later. Our article on [who pays for an inherited house](/learn/property-and-assets/who-pays-for-an-inherited-house-upkeep-taxes-and-reimbursing-the-sibling-who-fronts-it) covers that.
6. Do not hand anyone a lease, a deposit or a promise to sell.

## How do you open the estate for the house?

Someone petitions the circuit court in the county where your parent lived. In Cook County, the clerk's probate page says to hire an attorney to open an estate, and the attorney files the forms. See [handling an Illinois probate estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer) for what the clerk says and what families do themselves. The steps run in this order:

1. File the petition naming the heirs and asking that someone be appointed administrator, or executor if there is a will.
2. The court issues letters of office and, usually, requires a surety bond when there is no will. See [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond).
3. The administrator gives notice to the heirs and publishes a notice to creditors (755 ILCS 5/18-3). The claims period runs at least six months, so most estates stay open that long.
4. The administrator can list and sell the house. In independent administration no court order is needed for the sale (755 ILCS 5/28-8), and [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains how the court decides which type fits.
5. The sale proceeds go into the estate account. After the claims period and costs, the heirs receive their shares.

An administrator without a will is often a child who volunteers. Heirs with equal priority can agree on who serves, and the court will usually appoint that person. A disagreement among siblings about who should serve is where cases slow down, and it is worth settling before anyone files.

## What if the siblings do not agree on what to do with the house?

Nobody can force a sale alone until the estate is open, and even after that each heir owns a share. Illinois courts can order a partition when co-owners cannot agree, and for one house that usually means a sale (see [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois)). Most families avoid this by agreeing on one of three paths:

- Sell and split the proceeds.
- One sibling buys out the others at an appraised value.
- Everyone keeps it for a set time, with a written agreement on who pays what.

Talk about money spent early. The siblings who fight usually fight about who paid the roof, not about the house. The guide to [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) covers the common standoffs.

## What does it cost and how long does it take?

Court costs vary by county. Cook County lists a $479 filing fee for an independent administration as of October 2025, and our [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) page covers the rest, including attorney fees and publication. On timing, a simple estate with a house to sell often takes longer than six months, because the creditor claim period sets a floor. [How long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) has the timeline.

Meanwhile the house still costs money each month. Illinois has no inheritance tax, but an estate over $4 million owes Illinois estate tax, so the families reading this page almost never face it. See [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) if the whole estate is large.

## A mistake we see

Families wait. A parent dies in spring, everyone is grieving, and no one files anything. By fall the insurer has dropped coverage on the vacant house, a tax bill is late, and the buyer who offered cash has gone. Opening the estate does not commit you to anything about the house. It just gives one person the authority to protect it and decide.

The other mistake is signing something. Heirs sometimes sign a purchase contract before letters issue. The title company then finds out and the deal stalls, and the heir who signed has made a promise the estate cannot yet keep.
