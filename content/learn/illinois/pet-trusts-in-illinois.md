Denise, 66, lives outside Rockford with a twelve-year-old retriever named Biscuit and a cat who is expected to outlive them both. Her son lives in Denver and does not want either animal. Her neighbor Joan has promised to take them. Denise's question is simple: how do I make sure Joan can afford to keep that promise, and that the money is actually spent on the animals?

Illinois answers that in the Illinois Trust Code. This article walks through what Section 408 allows, how to set up a pet trust, and the choices that make or break one. For the general, nationwide basics, see our page on [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts). This one is the Illinois version, and part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). It is general education, not legal advice.

## The short version

- Illinois treats a trust for a designated domestic or pet animal as valid.
- The money is for the animal. The trustee cannot spend it on themselves unless the document says so.
- Name a trustee, a backup, and a separate person to enforce.
- Say what happens to the leftover money, or a default order decides.

## What does Illinois Section 408 say?

Section 408 of the Illinois Trust Code covers trusts for domestic or pet animals (760 ILCS 3/408). The main points:

| Topic | What the section provides |
|---|---|
| Validity | A trust for the care of designated domestic or pet animals is valid |
| Reading the document | Courts read it broadly to bring the transfer within the section and treat it as a gift, not a mere wish; outside evidence can show your intent |
| Duration | Ends when no living animal is covered; exempt from the common law rule against perpetuities |
| Use of money | Neither principal nor income may benefit the trustee, serve another purpose or help anyone but the animals, unless the instrument says otherwise |
| Enforcement | The person named in the document, or if none, any individual may petition the court, which may appoint an enforcer |
| Excess | A court may reduce the amount if the property substantially exceeds what the care requires |
| Leftovers | Named recipients, then the settlor if living, then the will's residuary clause, then the transferor's heirs |

The statute was amended by Public Act 101-48, effective January 1, 2020, and Public Act 102-279, effective January 1, 2022.

## Why not just leave the pet to a friend with a cash gift?

<!-- visual: whatif id=pets-left-behind -->

You can, and for a low-cost animal and a trustworthy friend, it may be enough. The weakness is that a cash gift is the friend's money. Joan can spend it on a roof. If Joan dies, moves or changes her mind, nothing sends the animal and the cash elsewhere.

A pet trust, by contrast, restricts the spending to the animal. Under Section 408 the trustee cannot use principal or income for themselves or for any other purpose, unless the trust says otherwise. The trustee is a fiduciary, the person who holds the money, and the caregiver is whoever has the animal. They can be the same person, but splitting the roles is usually wiser. Denise might name her bank or her sister as trustee and Joan as caregiver, with the trustee paying Joan's vet bills against receipts.

## How do you set up a pet trust?

1. **Identify the animals.** Section 408 speaks of "designated" animals. Name them and describe them. If you may get a new pet later, think about whether to include future animals, and ask an attorney how to word it.
2. **Choose a caregiver and a backup.** Check that they actually want the job.
3. **Choose a trustee and a successor.** Add an enforcer if you want someone beyond the court process.
4. **Write the care instructions.** Food, vet, boarding, grooming, what happens at end of life. Specifics matter.
5. **Decide the payment method.** Reimbursement, a monthly stipend, or direct payment to the vet.
6. **Fund it.** A pet trust can sit in a living trust, or be created by your will. Funding during life avoids probate delay. See [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts).
7. **Name who gets the leftovers.** Do not rely on the default order unless it fits.

Choose among a stand-alone trust, a sub-trust in a revocable trust, and a will provision with our [trust types guide](/learn/trusts) and the [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust).

## How much should go into it?

The statute will not give you a number, and neither will we. Think in terms of: annual food and routine vet costs, boarding or a sitter, a reserve for a major illness, the animal's remaining life expectancy, and what the trustee will charge. A twelve-year-old retriever and a ten-year-old cat have different horizons than a parrot that can live for decades.

Section 408 is a check on overfunding. If the trust property substantially exceeds what the intended care requires, the court may reduce the amount transferred, and the reduction goes to the termination recipients (the order in the table above). So a pet trust is not a back door for leaving a large sum to a trustee.

## Who makes sure the trustee does the job?

A pet cannot sue. Section 408 solves this by letting the person designated in the document enforce the trust, and if none is named, any individual may petition the court, which may appoint an individual to enforce. Also remember that, except as the court orders or the instrument requires, the trustee has no duty to file reports or keep separate accounts. That default is thin. Write in an annual report to the enforcer, a vet-receipt rule, and a requirement to check on the animal. For how trustees generally owe information to beneficiaries, see [Illinois trustee duty to inform](/learn/illinois/illinois-trustee-duty-to-inform-beneficiaries). An animal is not a beneficiary who can ask for it, so you must build the check in.

## What happens when the last animal dies?

The trust ends when no living animal is covered. Leftover property is distributed in this order (760 ILCS 3/408):

1. recipients named in the trust instrument;
2. the settlor, if still living;
3. if the trust was created in a non-residuary clause of the transferor's will, the residuary clause of that will; and
4. the transferor's heirs under Section 2-1 of the Probate Act of 1975.

Many people name the caregiver, an animal shelter or a family member for the remainder. Whoever you name, say so plainly. Falling back to your heirs is rarely what a client wants, especially when the caregiver gave years to the animal.

## Mistakes we see in pet plans

- **A pet clause with no money behind it.** "My sister will take the dog" is a wish until a trustee holds funds.
- **No backup caregiver.** Caregivers get sick or move. Name a second and a third.
- **Instructions that are too vague.** "Good care" invites argument. Name the vet, the diet and the limits on costly treatment.
- **Forgetting the end-of-life decision.** Say who decides, and what you would want. A trustee deciding alone about euthanasia is a hard position.
- **Leaving it out of the rest of the plan.** A pet trust should match your will and, if you have one, your [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts).
