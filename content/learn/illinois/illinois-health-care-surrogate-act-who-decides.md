Your father has had a stroke. He cannot talk, and the hospital wants consent for a procedure. He never signed a health care power of attorney. Who gets to say yes?

In Illinois, a specific law answers that: the Health Care Surrogate Act (755 ILCS 40). It sets out who the hospital must look for and in what order. This article walks through the list, the limits, and the situations where it breaks down. It is general education, not legal advice, and it sits inside our wider [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## The short version

- The doctor first checks for a health care agent. Only if none is available does the surrogate list apply.
- The list is ranked. A higher-ranked person who is available and willing displaces everyone below.
- Surrogates must try to decide as the patient would have, not as they would choose for themselves.
- Withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment needs more: a qualifying condition and two practitioners in writing.
- If you want a specific person, such as a partner or a friend, you have to sign a document naming them.

## Who does the hospital call first?

The hospital first looks for a health care agent. When a patient lacks decisional capacity, the provider must check whether an agent under the Illinois power of attorney law is available and authorized. If not, the provider makes a reasonable search for surrogates, which can include checking the patient's personal effects and medical record. When a family member or agent is identified, the provider is to try to reach them by phone within 24 hours of determining the patient lacks capacity (755 ILCS 40/25).

Notice what this means in practice. The search is a duty on the provider, but it is a reasonable one. Nobody is expected to hire an investigator. If you are the person who should be called, make sure the hospital has your current number.

## What is the Illinois surrogate priority list?

For a patient with a qualifying condition, the Act lists these surrogates, in order (755 ILCS 40/25):

| Rank | Who |
|---|---|
| 1 | Guardian of the person |
| 2 | Spouse |
| 3 | Adult son or daughter |
| 4 | Either parent |
| 5 | Adult brother or sister |
| 6 | Adult grandchild |
| 7 | Close friend |
| 8 | Guardian of the estate |

The statute adds a further category after the guardian of the estate, which rarely matters to families. The top of the list is what counts. A spouse outranks every child. Children outrank parents. Siblings outrank grandchildren. A friend, however devoted, sits below all of them.

A provider may rely on a surrogate when, after reasonable inquiry, no agent or higher-ranked person is available. If a higher-ranked person later turns up and is willing, that person can be identified as the surrogate. The surrogate's name, address, phone number and relationship go in the medical record.

### What if the adult children disagree?

Surrogates at the same rank should try to reach consensus. If they cannot, a majority of the available people at that rank controls, unless the minority starts a guardianship case under the Probate Act of 1975. The provider is not required to seek a guardian itself (755 ILCS 40/25).

Take Rosa, who has three adult children. Two want comfort care only and one wants every intervention. The two prevail, because they are a majority of her available children. The third child's only route is court. That is slow, public and expensive, which is the strongest argument for putting your wishes in writing before anything happens. Our article on [what happens without an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive) covers that gap in general terms.

## How does a surrogate decide?

The Act uses two standards (755 ILCS 40/20). For an adult, the first is substituted judgment: the surrogate decides as closely as possible to what the patient would have chosen, using the patient's beliefs and values and weighing the burdens and benefits of the treatment. An advance directive, even a technically invalid one, can be evidence of those wishes, but having no directive creates no presumption either way.

The second is best interests. It applies when the patient's wishes remain unknown after reasonable effort, or when the patient is a minor. The surrogate weighs burdens and benefits and may consider what family and friends say.

## Can a surrogate refuse life-sustaining treatment?

Yes, but the Act adds safeguards. The rules in section 20 require these steps:

1. The attending physician determines, to a reasonable degree of medical certainty, that the adult lacks decisional capacity, and records the cause, nature and expected duration.
2. The patient has a qualifying condition, documented in writing by the attending physician.
3. Another qualified practitioner personally examines the patient and concurs in writing.
4. The surrogate states the decision to the attending physician and one adult witness, and the physician documents it.
5. The physician carries it out, unless the physician believes the surrogate is not meeting the Act's duties or cannot comply for reasons of conscience.

The Act defines "qualifying condition" in its definitions section, and this article does not restate that definition. Ask the care team how the hospital applies it to your family member.

For a patient who lacks capacity but has no qualifying condition, subsection (b-5) of section 20 applies the same surrogate structure to ordinary treatment decisions, but forgoing life-sustaining treatment is not permitted.

## What does this mean for planning?

The list is a safety net, not a plan. It cannot know your family. Consider what it does with these facts:

- You live with a partner you never married. The partner is not on the list by that name.
- You are estranged from your only child, who is next in line after your spouse.
- Your spouse has dementia and cannot serve, and your children live in different states.

Each of these is solved by a signed [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney), which puts your chosen agent ahead of the list. Pick someone who knows your wishes; our guide on [who should be your health care agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) covers the choice. If you already have a directive, the [property power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) is the separate document for money.

When no one on the list is available, or the family is in open conflict, a court may need to appoint a guardian. Our [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) article explains that process. If a hospital cannot share information with a family member, a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) helps.

## What the Act does not do

It does not give a surrogate power over everything. It does not displace a valid agent. And the surrogate's authority ends when the patient regains capacity, a guardian of the person is appointed, or the patient dies (755 ILCS 40/25).
