If your mother lived in Sterling, Rock Falls, Morrison, Fulton, Prophetstown or another Whiteside County town, her estate is usually a Whiteside County matter. The county sits in the 14th Judicial Circuit, the same circuit as Rock Island County. This page is part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

It covers where the courthouse and clerk are, how an estate opens, what to do with the original will, remote hearings, self-help and case lookup. It then takes up the case of a parent who spent the last years on Medicaid, and a letter from the state afterward. For your own facts, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-probate-works-in-whiteside-county&topic=probate) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Where do I file probate in Whiteside County?

File with the Whiteside County Circuit Clerk. The county's pages list the main courthouse at 200 East Knox Street, Morrison, IL 61270, with a Circuit Clerk phone of 815-772-5188. The Clerk's page describes its work as running from adoptions to the probate of an estate, and lists a Probate Division line at 815-772-5168.

The county also lists a Circuit Clerk office and court building at 101 East Third Street in Sterling, with its own number, 815-535-4530. Sterling and Rock Falls families sometimes assume the nearer office handles everything. The pages do not say that. They list the Probate Division under Morrison.

| What the county lists | Detail | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Circuit Clerk, Morrison | 200 East Knox Street; 815-772-5188 | Main filing office |
| Probate Division | Morrison address; 815-772-5168 | Estate questions |
| Circuit Clerk, Sterling | 101 East Third Street; 815-535-4530 | Local office; ask what it handles |
| Courts line | 815-772-5270 | Scheduling and hours |

The county pages checked for this guide did not list office hours or a probate calendar. Call the courts line before you travel, and ask which counter handles estates on the day you plan to come.

## How does a Whiteside County estate open?

The steps are the same as anywhere in Illinois. Someone files a petition asking the court to admit a will, or, with no will, to name an administrator. If a will exists, the court issues letters testamentary to the executor. Without one, it issues letters of administration. The page on [how to open a probate estate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-open-a-probate-estate-in-illinois) walks through the petition, the hearing, the oath and the notices to heirs.

Venue follows the person, not the family. The page on [probate venue when property is in two Illinois counties](/learn/illinois/probate-venue-property-in-two-illinois-counties) explains that the case goes to the county where the person lived at death (755 ILCS 5/5-1). A Rock Falls resident's estate belongs in Whiteside County even if every child lives in Joliet or Texas.

The time an estate stays open is covered in [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois).

## What do I do with the original will?

Hand it to the circuit clerk, and do it promptly. Illinois law requires whoever holds a decedent's original will to file it with the clerk of the proper county right after the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1), as the page on [filing the original will with the Illinois circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk) explains. Illinois Legal Aid also says a will must be filed within 30 days of death and that filing it is separate from opening a case.

The Whiteside pages do not describe how an original is received, whether by mail or in person, or whether a receipt is given. Ask, then keep whatever proof you get. Never hand the only original to a relative without a written note. If you cannot find it, read [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) and the page on a [lost original will](/learn/illinois/lost-original-will-in-illinois).

## Can I attend a Whiteside County hearing by Zoom?

Sometimes, but ask. The county runs recurring Zoom rooms for its Morrison and Sterling courtrooms, plus a call-in line. The county's page says people physically in the courtroom have priority, and remote cases may wait up to an hour depending on the day's call.

The same page sets conduct rules:

- Use your full name on your Zoom account.
- Stay muted unless the judge says otherwise.
- Do not record the hearing.
- Join from a quiet place, and never while driving.
- If you do not appear when called, the case may be heard without you.

The page lists which rooms are open and which are marked unavailable, and tells you to call a clerk if you do not know which judge is hosting. It does not mention probate, and does not say which case types may appear remotely. A lawyer who files a remote notice for some civil matters must do so at least 48 hours ahead, but that notice rule covers attorneys, and probate is not named. So ask the clerk whether your hearing can be remote, and get the answer in writing if you can.

For an accommodation, the county links a Court Disability Coordinator document and an ADA request form. Ask early.

## What self-help can a Whiteside family use?

The Circuit Clerk's page says plainly that the office cannot give legal advice. It lists no self-help center of its own. For guidance, use the state resources.

Illinois Court Help takes calls and texts at 833-411-1121 and offers guides to the court process anywhere in Illinois. It points to the statewide standardized forms page for court forms. Illinois Legal Aid walks through settling an estate and describes the small estate affidavit as the main self-help path, for personal property of $150,000 or less with no real estate. For more, see the [small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit), [handling an estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer) and the [fee waiver](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-fee-waiver). The county posts a fee schedule as a PDF; this guide does not repeat amounts, so read the current one.

## How do I look up a Whiteside County case?

The Circuit Clerk's page links two Judici tools: a county case search and a daily docket. The county's Records page lists "Judici - Court Records" the same way. It does not say which estate files appear, how far back they go or how current they are.

If a name does not come up, call the Probate Division rather than assume nothing was filed. If you are wondering whether a relative named you, see [how to find out if you are named in a will](/learn/illinois/how-to-find-out-if-you-are-named-in-a-will-in-illinois).

## What if my parent was on Medicaid in a nursing home?

This changes what you do after the petition. The Illinois Department of Healthcare and Family Services (HFS) can file a claim against the probate estate for long-term care benefits. The page on [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) explains the program, and the general piece on [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) gives the national picture.

Several points matter at the courthouse stage:

- The claim runs against the estate, not against you. Heirs are not expected to use their own money.
- Recovery is deferred while a spouse or a child under 21 survives, or a child is blind or disabled.
- For deaths on or after July 1, 2022, the first $25,000 of estate value is protected.
- Funeral costs, legal costs and a home mortgage are paid before the Medicaid claim.
- Assets that pass outside probate by a beneficiary designation are generally not part of the estate. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

So the first sorting job for the executor is to list what is in the probate estate and what passes by designation. The related pages on [paying for a nursing home before Medicaid](/learn/illinois/paying-for-a-nursing-home-before-medicaid-in-illinois) and [protecting your house from a nursing home](/learn/illinois/protecting-your-house-from-a-nursing-home-in-illinois) cover planning while a parent is alive, when gifts inside the 60-month look-back can cause a penalty.

| Question for the executor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Was the house in the parent's sole name? | It is likely in the probate estate |
| Is there a surviving spouse or a disabled child? | Recovery may be deferred |
| Do any accounts name a beneficiary? | They generally pass outside probate |
| Did HFS send a notice? | Note the sender and case number |
| Would a claim cause hardship to heirs? | A waiver exists, but you must ask and prove it |

## Example: the Delgado family in Sterling

This is an invented example. Rosa Delgado, 84, lived in a Sterling nursing home on Medicaid and died with a will. Her house on the Sterling side of town is in her name alone. Her daughter Elena, who lives in Rock Falls, is named executor, and her brother Marco lives out of state.

Elena finds the original will in a folder at the facility and calls the Circuit Clerk to ask how to deliver it, keeping any receipt. The estate is a Whiteside County case because Rosa lived there. A letter from HFS arrives a few weeks later. Elena notes the sender and case number and sorts the assets. The house is a probate asset. A small account naming Marco as beneficiary is not.

The attorney explains that no spouse or young child survives, so no deferral applies, but the first $25,000 is protected and funeral, legal and mortgage costs come first. Elena asks whether Marco can attend the hearing by Zoom, and the clerk's answer decides it.
