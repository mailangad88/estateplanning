A judge does not have to choose between giving a guardian everything and giving them nothing. Illinois law pushes the court to take only as much freedom away as the person's condition requires. The two labels you will hear are limited and plenary guardianship, and the choice between them often matters more to the person than the choice of guardian.

This page explains the difference and how an order is written. For the whole process, see [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship). For how the two jobs of person and estate fit with this, read [guardian of the person versus guardian of the estate](/learn/illinois/guardian-of-the-person-vs-guardian-of-the-estate-in-illinois).

## What is the legal rule behind the choice?

The Probate Act says guardianship should be used only as necessary to promote the person's well-being, protect them from neglect, exploitation or abuse and encourage self-reliance. It should be ordered only to the extent of the person's actual mental, physical and adaptive limitations (755 ILCS 5/11a-3). A related section on the order (755 ILCS 5/11a-12) reserves plenary authority for a person who lacks total capacity and for whom a limited guardian would not give enough protection.

The practical effect: the court starts by asking which tasks the person can still do. A person who cannot manage a bank account might still choose their doctor and their friends.

## What is a limited guardianship?

A limited guardian holds only the powers the order lists. The person keeps every other right. The order might say the guardian may consent to medical treatment and handle Social Security benefits, and that the person keeps the right to choose where to live and whom to visit.

Examples of limits that appear in orders include:

- Authority over one bank account but not over the house.
- Consent to major medical treatment, but not to a move to a nursing home without court approval.
- Control of benefits for housing and food, but not of a small spending account the person manages alone.

## What is a plenary guardianship?

A plenary guardian has the full set of powers over the area the court assigns. A plenary guardian of the person decides all personal matters. A plenary guardian of the estate manages all property. It fits a person who cannot understand or communicate decisions in that area.

Plenary does not mean unsupervised. The guardian must still act for the person's benefit, follow the statute and report to the court. Larger steps, such as selling real estate, generally need the judge's approval. See [annual reports and accountings](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardian-reports-and-accountings).

## How do limited and plenary guardianship compare?

| | Limited | Plenary |
|---|---|---|
| Person's capacity | Lacks some, keeps some | Lacks capacity for the area, with no lesser option that protects |
| Powers | Listed in the order | Full set for the assigned area |
| Court preference | Favored | Last resort |
| Example | Guardian handles money; person chooses home | Guardian decides care and money for a person in a late-stage illness |
| Changing it | Court can widen on request | Court can narrow on request |

## How does the court decide which one fits?

The judge looks at the physician's report, the guardian ad litem's findings, testimony and sometimes the person's own answers. The deciding questions are which decisions the person can make with or without help and how much risk comes from leaving them alone. A written plan from the family can help: a list of the daily tasks and who handles them now.

Ask the physician to address specific areas such as medication, money, safety and living arrangements. A report that says "cannot manage affairs" gives the judge little to work with.

## What steps can families take to ask for a limited order?

1. List what the person can still do alone, with help or not at all, in each area.
2. Ask the physician to address those areas in the report.
3. Ask for a limited order in the petition and explain which powers you need.
4. Share the list with the guardian ad litem.
5. Review the draft order and make sure it names each power and leaves the rest.
6. Ask the court to revisit the order if the person's abilities change.

## Example: Marguerite and her sons

Marguerite, 76, has a stroke that affects her memory and speech. She can state her preferences and dress herself, but she cannot track bills. Her son Paul asks for a limited guardianship of the estate with authority over her accounts and her benefits. The judge agrees and leaves Marguerite in charge of her daily life. Two years later, after another stroke, Paul returns to court and asks to add personal powers. The judge reviews the new report and grants a wider order.

The [Illinois supported decision-making agreement](/learn/illinois/illinois-supported-decision-making-agreement) fits some people better.

## What does an order actually say?

The wording matters because banks and facilities read the order literally. A limited order may state that the guardian of the person may consent to medical treatment, select a residence with court approval for a nursing home placement, and receive medical records, while the person retains the right to vote, to marry and to see visitors. A limited estate order may allow the guardian to manage one named account and the person's benefits while the person keeps a small spending allowance.

Ask for a draft order before the hearing and read each paragraph with the person's daily routine in mind. Missing powers can be added later, but unneeded powers can be hard to remove. Our article on [ending or changing an Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/ending-or-changing-an-illinois-guardianship) shows how.

## How do the labels relate to a diagnosis?

The label depends on function, not on a diagnosis. Two people with the same dementia stage can receive different orders because one lives with a spouse who handles bills and the other lives alone. Likewise, a young adult with an intellectual disability might need a limited medical guardian but manage a modest ABLE account. See [what to do when a child with a disability turns 18](/learn/special-needs/what-to-do-when-a-child-with-a-disability-turns-18). The judge cares about what can go wrong and who is there to help.
