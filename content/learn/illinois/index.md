Illinois law is not the same as the law in the next state over. The rules for witnessing a will, filing a claim against an estate, renouncing a will and signing a power of attorney each have an Illinois answer, and some of those answers surprise people. The state also taxes estates at a level far below the federal line.

This guide summarizes the thirteen Illinois articles on this site and links each one. It also points to our national guides on probate, wills, trusts and powers of attorney, so you can see where Illinois follows the common pattern and where it does not. Every Illinois rule below comes from those articles, which cite the statute. Our attorneys still have to confirm the details, so treat this page as a map and not as advice for your case.

## How does the Illinois system fit together?

Illinois estate matters run through three main laws: the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5), the Power of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45) and the Illinois Trust Code (760 ILCS 3). Smaller acts cover transfer on death instruments, electronic wills and the estate tax. Most court cases happen in the circuit court of the county where the person lived.

## What makes a will valid in Illinois?

A will must be in writing, signed by an adult of sound mind, and attested in the signer's presence by at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-1 and 4-3). A notary is not required. The witnesses are what count.

The witnesses should gain nothing. If a witness or that witness's spouse receives a gift under the will, the gift is void unless the will has enough other witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-6). So a neighbor is a safe witness, and a child named in the will is not.

Two more points matter in practice. A will signed after a court has appointed a guardian for you is presumed invalid, unless clear and convincing evidence shows you had capacity at signing. And a witness statement signed at the signing, called a self-proving clause, lets the court admit the will without calling the witnesses (755 ILCS 5/6-4).

Illinois also allows electronic wills and video witnessing under the Electronic Wills and Remote Witnesses Act (755 ILCS 6). The video rules are strict. Illinois must be named as the place of signing, each witness must be in the United States, and signature pages must be joined to the will within 10 business days. Paper is still the simpler path for many people.

The full details, including how to revoke a will, are in [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements). For the rules every state shares, read our [wills guide](/learn/wills), and for how Illinois compares with other states, see our [will rules by state](/estate-planning/will-rules) table.

## Who inherits if there is no will?

State law picks the heirs (755 ILCS 5/2-1). A spouse and children split the estate half and half. A spouse with no descendants takes everything. With no spouse, children take it all. If there are none, the estate goes to parents and siblings, then to more distant kin.

Here is a case. Elena dies with no will, leaving her husband Marcus and two children from an earlier marriage. Her probate estate is $400,000. Marcus takes $200,000 and each child takes $100,000. Her stepchildren, friends and unmarried partner would receive nothing, unless a will or beneficiary form said otherwise. A civil union partner is treated as a spouse under Illinois law.

Intestacy reaches only probate assets. Accounts with a beneficiary, joint property with survivorship, trust assets and real estate with a recorded transfer on death instrument pass by their own rules. See [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) for the full order of heirs, adopted children and what happens when no heirs exist.

## How does Illinois probate work?

Probate starts when the will is filed with the circuit court clerk, which the statute requires immediately after the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). The court then admits the will and issues letters of office to the executor. If there is no will, the court appoints an administrator.

After that the executor follows a set order:

1. Mail notice to heirs and legatees within 14 days after the order admitting the will (755 ILCS 5/6-10).
2. Publish a claims notice once a week for three weeks and mail it to known creditors (755 ILCS 5/18-3).
3. Collect assets, pay valid claims in the order the law sets and file final tax returns.
4. Distribute what is left.
5. File a closing report and ask the court to discharge the executor.

Most estates use independent administration, which the court must grant unless the will forbids it or supervision is required (755 ILCS 5/28-2). An independent executor can sell property and pay claims without a court order for each step. A supervised executor needs the judge's approval for major actions, which is slower and costs more.

Our article on [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) walks through each stage with an example and a comparison table. For the general idea outside Illinois, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## How long does probate take in Illinois?

A simple estate rarely closes in under about six months. The claim deadline is the later of six months from first publication or three months from mailing, so the clock itself sets a floor (755 ILCS 5/18-3). Heirs also have six months after admission to contest the will (755 ILCS 5/8-1). These two periods run side by side.

After the closing report, an independent executor waits 42 days before asking for discharge (755 ILCS 5/28-11). Illinois also bars most creditor claims two years after death (755 ILCS 5/18-12), which is the outer limit for debts.

Ravi's case shows how it plays out. He is the independent executor for his mother's estate, and the court admitted the will in March. The legal waiting periods ended in September. His mother's house did not sell until November, so he filed his closing report in December and waited the 42 days. The house set the finish date, not the statute.

Disputes, unsold real estate, tax questions and missing heirs are the usual causes of delay. See [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) for the full table of deadlines and a sample calendar.

## What does Illinois probate cost?

The cost comes in separate pieces. These are the court filing fee, newspaper publication, a bond premium if one is required, attorney fees and sometimes executor pay. Illinois has no fixed percentage schedule for attorney fees, so fees follow a reasonableness standard and a written agreement.

Cook County lists $479 as the filing fee for an independent administration, as of October 2025. Other counties publish their own schedules, and fees change, so confirm the current amount with your clerk. See [Illinois probate filing fees by county](/estate-planning/illinois-probate-fees) for ten counties' current fees. The estate pays all of these costs before heirs receive their shares.

Marcus, 52, is executor for his father's small estate in Cook County. He pays the clerk, pays for the newspaper notice and signs a written flat-fee agreement with an attorney. He waives executor pay because he is also an heir, since that pay would be taxable income to him. Each cost comes out of the estate account and goes into his closing report.

## Can a small estate skip probate?

Sometimes. Under 755 ILCS 5/25-1, a family member can give a bank or other holder a sworn affidavit instead of opening an estate. As of 2026 the limit is $150,000 in personal property, and registered motor vehicles do not count toward it. A 2025 amendment raised the limit from $100,000.

The affidavit has real limits. It cannot move real estate. No court reviews the work, and no claims deadline protects the person who signs. That person must pay the estate's valid debts in the order the statute sets, and must repay anyone who loses out because of a mistake.

Grace's father left a $40,000 checking account and a registered car, with no house. Grace and her sister were the only heirs. Grace used the affidavit, retitled the car and paid the funeral bill from the account. That fits the statute. If her father had owned a house in his name alone, she would likely have needed probate.

The article on the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) explains the contents of the form, the risks and what to do when a bank refuses it. Our [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) can help you total the assets.

## What is the Illinois estate tax?

Illinois taxes estates over $4,000,000, counting adjusted taxable gifts, for deaths on or after January 1, 2013 (35 ILCS 405/2). The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026. So an Illinois family with a paid-off house, retirement accounts and life insurance can owe state tax and no federal tax.

The $4 million is a threshold, not a credit. At or below it, there is no Illinois tax and no return. Above it, the Attorney General's calculation applies. In the Attorney General's example, a $5,000,000 estate located entirely in Illinois owes $285,714 in Illinois tax. Use the Attorney General's calculator for a real estate.

Illinois does not allow portability. If Tom dies and leaves everything outright to Maria, his unused $4 million is lost. Each of them owned $3 million, so Maria now holds $6 million against her own $4 million exclusion. Couples often use a credit shelter trust at the first death to use both exclusions. Illinois also allows a separate state-only QTIP election.

The return is Form 700, and the return and tax are due nine months after death. An extension must be requested within those nine months. Illinois has no inheritance tax, so heirs do not pay Illinois tax on what they receive.

A living trust does not lower the tax. Read [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) for the filing steps, out-of-state property and planning tools. Our [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) gives a first look, and our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) covers the federal side.

## What can a surviving spouse claim?

Illinois is a separate-property state, so each spouse owns what is titled in their name. To keep one spouse from being shut out, the law gives the survivor two rights, and they work alongside intestacy rules when there is no will.

The first is renunciation. A spouse can reject the will and take one-third of the estate if the decedent left descendants, or one-half if not (755 ILCS 5/2-8). The signed renunciation must be filed within 7 months after the will is admitted. Rosa's will leaves everything to her two children, and her estate is $900,000 after claims. Her husband Sam renounces and takes $300,000.

The second is the spouse's award, support for nine months. It can never be less than $20,000, plus at least $10,000 for each minor child of the decedent who lived with the spouse (755 ILCS 5/15-1). Confirm the current minimums. A spouse who renounces keeps the award. A civil union partner has the same rights.

Renunciation is final. Once filed, the spouse cannot go back and take the will's gifts. Prenuptial agreements can waive these rights, and assets outside the will, such as a trust or a joint account, raise questions an attorney should review. See [spouse rights at death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) for the steps and the deadline.

## What do Illinois powers of attorney look like?

Illinois has two separate statutory forms, and most adults need both. Each has different signing rules, and mixing them up is a common error.

| | Property power of attorney | Health care power of attorney |
|---|---|---|
| What it covers | Money, accounts and real estate | Medical decisions |
| Witness | One, who is not the agent | One adult, who is not the agent |
| Notary | Required (755 ILCS 45/3-3) | Not required (755 ILCS 45/4-10) |
| Gifts | Not allowed unless the form says so | Not applicable |

On the property form, the agent cannot make gifts, change beneficiaries, change your will or amend your trust unless the form gives that power (755 ILCS 45/3-4). The power can start at once or on a future event, and it ends at your death. See [the Illinois statutory power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) for the list of powers and what to do when a bank balks.

On the health care form, your attending physician cannot be your agent or your witness. Illinois also has a living will, signed by two adult witnesses, and a POLST, which is a medical order signed with a practitioner. [Illinois health care powers of attorney, living wills and POLST](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) explains the difference. For the national view, see our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## When does Illinois guardianship apply?

Guardianship is the court's answer when no one has legal authority. For an adult, the court needs clear and convincing evidence that the person is not fully able to manage their person or estate (755 ILCS 5/11a-3). A temporary guardian can be named in an emergency, and that order expires within 60 days.

The process is public and slow. It needs a petition, a doctor's report, 14 days' notice before the hearing and a guardian ad litem. The court should order only the help the person actually needs. Hal, 81, has memory loss but handles his daily routine, so a court might limit a guardian to his finances.

For children, a parent can name a guardian in a will or signed writing with two credible witnesses. A child 14 or older may nominate one. A guardianship of a minor ends at 18. Signed powers of attorney can often avoid the adult process, which is why the two documents above matter. Read [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) for the full steps, and our [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship) for choosing a guardian.

## How do living trusts and transfer on death instruments compare?

Both can keep a house out of probate. A living trust is broader, and a transfer on death instrument is simpler.

A living trust under the Illinois Trust Code (760 ILCS 3) should say expressly that it is revocable, or you may lose the right to change it (760 ILCS 3/602). It avoids probate only for assets actually moved into it. Dolores signs a trust but never deeds her house to it, so the house may still go through probate. A trustee often uses a certification of trust to prove the trust exists without sharing the whole document.

A transfer on death instrument, or TODI, passes real estate at death under 755 ILCS 27. It needs the owner's signature, two credible witnesses and a notary, and it must be recorded before the owner dies, or it is void. The beneficiary has no rights while the owner lives. An agent under a power of attorney cannot sign or revoke a TODI for you. A TODI does not shield the house from creditors or from a spouse's renunciation, and a living trust does not shield assets from your own creditors while you are alive.

| | Living trust | TODI |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | Many assets | Illinois real estate |
| Handles incapacity | Yes, through a successor trustee | No |
| Main step people miss | Funding the trust | Recording before death |

See [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) and the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument). Our national [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) explains the trust types.

## What should you do next?

Work through these in order, and stop where your situation is covered.

1. List your assets and note how each is titled and who is named on each beneficiary form.
2. Add them up with life insurance and retirement accounts counted. If the total nears $4 million, read the estate tax article first.
3. Check that your will, if you have one, was signed with two disinterested witnesses.
4. Put both powers of attorney in place while you have capacity.
5. Decide how your house will pass: will, trust or TODI.
6. If you are a spouse in a second marriage, decide how to provide for your spouse and for your children.
7. Review everything after a move, a marriage, a divorce or a death in the family.

If a death has just happened, file the will with the clerk right away, and mark the deadlines on a calendar. The 7-month renunciation window and the 6-month contest window start when the will is admitted.
