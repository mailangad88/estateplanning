If a parent lived in Rock Island, Moline, East Moline, Milan or another Rock Island County town, the estate is usually a Rock Island County matter. The county is in the 14th Judicial Circuit. Families searching for "probate Moline IL" land in the same place. This page is part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

It covers the courthouse and the clerk, how a case opens, what to do with the original will, remote hearings, self-help and case lookup. It then takes up a Quad Cities problem: a parent who lived in Illinois while an account or the executor sits across the river in Iowa. For your own facts, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-probate-works-in-rock-island-county&topic=probate) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Where do I file probate in Rock Island County?

File with the Rock Island County Circuit Clerk. The clerk's main page lists the Rock Island County Courthouse at 1317 3rd Avenue, Suite 101, Rock Island, IL 61201. It gives 309-558-3538 and hours of Monday through Friday, 8 a.m. to 4:30 p.m.

Other county pages describe the same street address as the Justice Center, and list 309-786-4451 and the same weekday hours. Rock Island County Administration is a separate building at 1504 Third Avenue. When pages differ, call before you drive, and ask which door and which counter handle estates.

| What the county lists | Detail | How a probate family uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Circuit Clerk page | 1317 3rd Avenue, Suite 101; 309-558-3538 | Filing questions, forms, fees |
| Probate and civil pages | Justice Center, 1317 Third Avenue; 309-786-4451 | Probate division contact |
| Probate walk-in | Daily, 9:00 to 9:30 a.m., Courtroom 402 | Uncontested openings and closings |
| Legal Self-Help Center | Online, and in person at the courthouse | Information and form guides |

The pages do not give parking details or a floor plan. If you need an accommodation, ask the clerk before the hearing.

## How does a Rock Island County estate open?

The county's form list shows the shape of the case. The central form is a Petition for Probate and for Letters. The form lets the petitioner choose among three paths: probate of a will with letters testamentary, probate with letters of administration with the will annexed, or letters of administration with no will.

The petition is signed under penalty of perjury. It asks for the date of death, the decedent's residence, estimated values, any safe deposit box, and a list of heirs and legatees. If the person named as executor will not serve, a Declination of Office form goes with it.

For the letters-of-administration paths, the form says the petitioner must mail notice of the hearing at least 30 days ahead, unless the person appears or waives by the hearing date. The county's estates list also includes acceptance of office, oath and bond, an inventory, claims forms, a final report and an order of discharge.

## What happens to the original will in Rock Island County?

Start with the statute. Illinois law makes whoever holds a will deliver the original to the circuit clerk after the death. The page on [filing the original will with the Illinois circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk) explains the duty and the two 30-day clocks. A photocopy cannot take the original's place.

The county pages checked for this page do not describe where to hand in an original will, or whether a receipt is given. The county's civil page lists "Wills" as a case type, but gives no steps. So call the clerk and ask how and where the original is received, and keep any receipt.

Do not hand the only original to a relative or a lawyer without a written receipt. If you cannot find the will at all, see [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois). Small estates may use the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit), which the county lists as a form. The county's list also includes forms for will proof.

## Do I have to e-file, and can I appear remotely?

Plan on e-filing. The county's civil division page says all civil cases and filings are required to be e-filed under Illinois Supreme Court rules, unless you qualify for an exemption. Every filing must carry your email address, and attorneys must add their registration number.

The clerk's main page says the in-office self-help computer setup can be used for electronic filings and credit card payments. It also points to Illinois Court Help, which you can call or text at 833-411-1121 for e-filing questions.

Remote hearings have a probate rule of their own. The county's Zoom page says that for probate case management, no in-person appearances are allowed, and a separate Zoom link is posted for those hearings. It also says that if you filed your annual report in advance, you do not need to attend, and a new report date is sent to you.

For other probate matters by Zoom, the page refers you to the court's rules and procedures document. It states general Zoom rules for every case type:

- Join at least 10 minutes before the start.
- Your Zoom name should show your full first and last name, followed by the case number.
- Learn the Zoom app first. Being unable to take part because you do not know how may count as a failure to appear.
- Expect to wait in a virtual waiting room until the court admits you.

Probate walk-in sessions are described as held in Courtroom 402. The pages do not say whether a walk-in can be joined remotely, so ask the clerk.

## What self-help does the county list?

The clerk's Legal Self-Help page describes an online self-help center, open at all hours, that gives information about legal problems. It can also be used in person at the courthouse if you lack internet access at home. The pages list no in-person hours for it.

Clerk staff can show you how to use the center, but the page says they cannot tell you what to put in court documents. The county also points to Illinois Court Help, the statewide guide, and a legal terms glossary on Illinois Legal Aid.

If cost is the worry, the county's fee page says you may ask the court to waive fees and that the fee waiver forms are e-filed with your other documents. The fee schedule itself is in a PDF whose amounts were not readable for this page. For other options, see [handling an Illinois probate estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer) and the page on the [Illinois probate fee waiver](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-fee-waiver).

## How do I look up a Rock Island County case?

The Circuit Clerk's page lists two tools on the Judici system: a Records Search and a Daily Court Docket. The county's traffic FAQ says to enter your name and click search. It does not say whether estate cases appear or how current they are.

If a search shows nothing, treat that as a reason to call the clerk, not as proof that no estate exists. For a relative who may have named you, see [how to find out if you are named in a will](/learn/illinois/how-to-find-out-if-you-are-named-in-a-will-in-illinois).

## What if the parent lived in Illinois but the executor or an account is in Iowa?

This is common in the Quad Cities, where Moline and Rock Island sit a short drive from Davenport and Bettendorf. Illinois law answers three questions here, and only Illinois law is covered on this page.

First, where is the case filed? The venue section of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/5-1) sends an Illinois resident's estate to the county where the person lived at death. As the page on [probate venue when property is in two Illinois counties](/learn/illinois/probate-venue-property-in-two-illinois-counties) explains, it is not the county of the executor or the hospital. A Moline resident's estate belongs in Rock Island County, even if every child lives across the river.

Second, can the executor serve? The page on a [non-resident executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/non-resident-executor-in-illinois) explains that 755 ILCS 5/6-13 asks for a U.S. resident, not an Illinois resident. The court may still require a bond with a surety it sets, even if the will excuses one. Ask the clerk early.

Third, what about the account or land outside Illinois? Per the page on [Illinois residents who own property in another state](/learn/illinois/illinois-resident-owning-property-in-another-state), land is controlled by the state where it sits, and Illinois letters stop at the Illinois line. Personal property such as a bank account generally follows the home state, but each institution sets its own paperwork. This page does not explain the rules of any other state, so a lawyer there should handle that side.

## Example: the Okafor family in Moline

This is an invented example. Grace Okafor, 78, lived in Moline and died with a will. Her son Daniel, who lives in Bettendorf, is named executor. Her estate includes her Moline house, a checking account at a local credit union, and a savings account she opened years ago at a bank across the river.

Daniel's first thought is to file where he lives. His attorney explains that Illinois venue follows Grace's residence, so the petition goes to the Rock Island County Circuit Clerk. The attorney e-files it, and Daniel asks the clerk about a bond.

Daniel finds the original will in Grace's desk and calls the clerk about how to deliver it, keeping any receipt. He appears by Zoom when the court allows it. For the savings account, the attorney checks what that bank wants and whether a lawyer on that side is needed. Daniel does not need a second Illinois case.
