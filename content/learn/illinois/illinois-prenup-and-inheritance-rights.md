If you are marrying for the second time, or marrying with a house, a business or children to protect, you may want your spouse to give up the right to claim part of your estate. In Illinois a prenup can do that, but only if it is built correctly.

This page covers the Illinois-specific pieces: which spouse rights a prenup can waive, what the other side must be told, and how the agreement fits with your will or trust. For the wider [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) and the general picture of marital agreements, read [prenups, postnups and your estate plan](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans). This is general education, not legal advice.

## What inheritance rights can a spouse have in Illinois?

An Illinois spouse who is left too little can renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if the decedent left descendants, or one-half if not, after claims are paid (755 ILCS 5/2-8). The spouse also has a right to a spouse's award for nine months of support, which cannot be less than $20,000 (755 ILCS 5/15-1). Our page on [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) walks through both, including the 7-month deadline to renounce.

These are the rights a prenup usually targets. Without a waiver, a will that leaves everything to your children from a first marriage can be undone, in part, by a surviving spouse who files a renunciation.

## Can a prenup waive those rights?

Yes, because the Illinois Uniform Premarital Agreement Act lets the parties agree on what happens to property at death and on making a will or trust (750 ILCS 10/4). The Act applies to agreements made in contemplation of marriage and effective on marriage. Two formalities matter: the agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties, and it needs no consideration (750 ILCS 10/3).

The statute does not hand you wording, so the drafting is where agreements succeed or fail. Our attorneys prefer language that names what is being given up:

- The right to renounce the will under the Probate Act.
- The right to a spouse's award.
- Any right to take as an heir if the decedent leaves no will.
- Any claim to a particular asset, such as the house or a business.

A line like "each waives all rights in the other's estate" is common and probably works in many cases. It also invites an argument about what "all rights" included. Whether a general waiver reaches the spouse's award in particular is the kind of detail an attorney should confirm before signing.

## What does each person have to disclose?

Each person must give the other fair and reasonable disclosure of their property and obligations, unless the other person waives disclosure in writing. That duty comes from the way the Act lets an agreement be challenged. The statute says a party can avoid enforcement by proving either of two things (750 ILCS 10/7):

1. The party did not sign voluntarily.
2. The agreement was unconscionable when signed, and the party also had no fair disclosure, did not waive it in writing, and did not have adequate knowledge of the other's property and obligations.

Read that second prong closely. A lopsided agreement alone is not enough. It takes unconscionability plus a failure of disclosure. A court decides unconscionability as a matter of law, not the jury.

The Act also lets a court require support when a support waiver causes undue hardship from circumstances that were not reasonably foreseeable at signing (750 ILCS 10/7). And an agreement cannot adversely affect a child's right to support (750 ILCS 10/4).

### A practical disclosure checklist

Attach a schedule to the agreement. Include:

- Real estate with approximate values and any mortgages.
- Bank, brokerage and retirement account balances, with a statement date.
- Business interests, and a note of how the value was estimated.
- Debts, including student loans and guaranties.
- Expected gifts or inheritances you already know about.
- Life insurance, and who is named on each policy.

Values need not be exact. They need to be honest and dated. A schedule that leaves out a $400,000 rental property is the single easiest way to give the other side an argument later.

## How does a prenup work with a will or trust?

The prenup removes claims. It does not give property to anyone. If you want your children to receive the house, your will or [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) must say so. If you want your spouse to have a life interest in the home, the plan must create it. Many people do this with a trust, as our article on the [family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) explains.

Here is how the pieces fit, using an example. Walt, 61, is marrying Denise, 58. Walt has two adult children and expects to leave an estate of about $1,200,000. These figures are an illustration only.

| Document | What it does for Walt and Denise |
|---|---|
| Premarital agreement | Denise waives renunciation and the spouse's award; Walt agrees to keep life insurance for her |
| Walt's trust | Gives Denise the right to live in the house for her life, then passes it to his children |
| Beneficiary forms | Name Denise on the life insurance, the children on the IRA |
| Denise's own plan | Mirrors the same limits for her estate and children |

Without the waiver, Denise could renounce Walt's will and take one-third of a $1,200,000 estate, which is $400,000, in place of the life interest. With it, she takes what the prenup and trust provide. Note what the prenup promises in return. A waiver that gives the spouse nothing, and nothing in the plan to replace it, is the type that tends to get litigated.

## What can't a prenup do?

Several things sit outside it.

- **Retirement plans.** A premarital waiver generally does not satisfy federal consent rules for employer plans. The spouse typically signs a separate consent after the wedding. See [retirement accounts in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/retirement-accounts-in-a-second-marriage).
- **Beneficiary forms.** Life insurance, IRAs and pay-on-death accounts go to the named person. A prenup can require you to name or keep someone, which is a contract promise, but the form controls payment.
- **Children's support.** Not affected by the agreement (750 ILCS 10/4).
- **Future changes in the agreement.** After the wedding, an agreement can be changed or revoked only by a signed written agreement (750 ILCS 10/6). Our page on [changing a prenup after marriage](/learn/blended-families/can-a-prenup-be-changed-after-marriage) goes through this.

One more point couples miss. If you later divorce, Illinois law cuts off a former spouse's gifts in a will or trust, as explained in [how divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan). The prenup still governs property between you during the divorce.

## What steps should you follow before signing?

1. Each person hires a separate attorney. One lawyer for both people makes the voluntariness question harder to answer later.
2. Exchange written financial schedules at least a few weeks before the wedding, not days.
3. Draft the agreement with the estate plan in mind, and have the estate documents ready to sign at the same time.
4. Sign the agreement well before the wedding, in writing, with both signatures.
5. After the wedding, retitle assets and update beneficiary forms to match.
6. Review everything after any major event: a birth, a sale of a business, a move.

Timing and independent counsel go to the voluntariness prong of the test. They do not decide the result, but they remove the easy arguments.
