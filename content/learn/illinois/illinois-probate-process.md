Someone you love has died, and you may be the person named in the will to handle things. Illinois probate has a clear order of steps, and it helps to see the whole path before you start. This page walks through that path and explains the choice between independent and supervised administration.

For the calendar side of this, see [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois). For money, see [what probate costs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs). For the big picture, start with our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What does Illinois probate actually do?

[Probate](/glossary/probate) is the court process that confirms who has authority over a dead person's property and makes sure debts are paid before heirs receive anything. In Illinois it happens in the circuit court of the county where the person lived. The governing law is the Probate Act of 1975 (755 ILCS 5).

Only assets in the decedent's name alone usually go through probate. Accounts with a named beneficiary, jointly owned property and assets in a living trust pass outside of it. Our general article on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the split.

## What are the steps of Illinois probate?

Here is the usual order for a will-based estate. Each step is covered below.

1. File the original will with the circuit court clerk.
2. Petition the court to admit the will and appoint the executor.
3. Receive letters of office from the court.
4. Notify heirs and legatees in writing.
5. Publish and mail a notice to creditors.
6. Collect assets, pay valid claims and taxes, and keep records.
7. Distribute what is left.
8. Close the estate and ask the court to discharge the executor.

### Step 1: File the will

Illinois law says that, immediately upon a death, anyone who has the will must file it with the clerk of the court of the proper county (755 ILCS 5/6-1). The court can compel someone who refuses. Willfully hiding or destroying a will is a crime under the same section. If you find a will in a drawer, do not wait for the family to decide what to do.

### Step 2: Ask the court to open the estate

The named executor, or another interested person, files a petition. The petition lists the decedent, the heirs, the legatees and the property. The judge holds a short hearing and, if the will meets the legal requirements, admits it. Our article on [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) explains what makes a will valid.

### Step 3: Get letters of office

After the will is admitted, the court issues letters of office to the executor. These are the papers you show to banks and others. The order must say whether the person is an independent executor or a supervised one. If there is no will, the court appoints an administrator instead. Some courts require the executor to sign an oath and, in some cases, post a bond. See the cost article for how bonds work.

### Step 4: Notify heirs and legatees

The executor must mail notice within 14 days after the order admitting the will (755 ILCS 5/6-10). The mailing includes a copy of the petition and the order. It explains the right to ask for formal proof of the will and to challenge its admission. If an address is unknown, the notice must be published instead. People who appeared at the hearing or waived notice do not need it.

Anyone who wants to contest the will has six months after admission (755 ILCS 5/8-1). Our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) covers how those cases work.

### Step 5: Notify creditors

The executor publishes a claims notice once a week for three weeks in a local newspaper and mails or delivers notice to every creditor who is known or reasonably findable (755 ILCS 5/18-3). The deadline for claims is the later of six months from the first publication or three months from mailing. Our [guide to debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains which debts get paid first.

### Step 6: Collect assets and pay debts

The executor opens an estate bank account, takes control of assets, files an inventory, pays valid claims in the order the law sets, and files the decedent's final tax returns. Keep every receipt. Our [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) lists the tasks in more detail.

### Step 7 and 8: Distribute and close

When claims are resolved and taxes are handled, the executor distributes the estate to the people named in the will. Closing differs depending on the type of administration.

## What is independent administration in Illinois?

Independent administration is the default path. Illinois courts must grant it unless the will forbids it or supervised administration is required (755 ILCS 5/28-2). An independent executor does most of the work without asking the judge for permission at each step. The court stays involved at the start and the end.

In practice, this means the executor can sell assets, pay claims and make distributions without a court order for each. It does not mean the executor is unaccountable. Heirs and legatees keep their rights to notice, and the executor still owes them a duty of care. Our article on [fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty) explains that standard.

## What is supervised administration?

Supervised administration puts the judge in charge of key actions. The executor asks for court approval before selling property, paying large claims or making distributions, and files reports along the way. Under the same section, if someone with a stake objects to independent administration, the court must require supervision, with exceptions. For example, if the will directs independent administration, supervision is required only if the court finds good cause. A creditor or a legatee who does not receive part of the residue needs only to show that supervision is necessary to protect that person's interest.

The court may also require supervision when a minor or a person with a disability has an interest that is not adequately represented.

| | Independent | Supervised |
|---|---|---|
| Court approval for sales and payments | Generally not needed | Usually needed |
| Reports to the judge | Fewer | More |
| Speed and cost | Usually faster and less costly | Usually slower and more costly |
| When used | The default | When a will directs it, or when the court requires it after an objection |
| Closing | Verified report, then a 42-day wait | Final account and court approval |

## How does an independent estate close?

The executor files a verified report with the court. It states that notice went to heirs, legatees and known creditors, that claims were resolved and paid in the right order, that death taxes were determined and paid or provided for, and that the assets were distributed. The executor mails a copy to the people entitled to notice. After the report is filed, the executor must wait 42 days before asking for an order discharging them (755 ILCS 5/28-11). Once the order is final, it generally binds those who signed approvals, absent fraud or mistake.

## Is there a way to skip these steps?

Sometimes. If the estate has only personal property below the statutory limit and no real estate, a family member may be able to use an affidavit instead of opening an estate. Read our guide to the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) and try the [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker). Planning ahead with a [living trust or a transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) can also keep assets out of probate.

## Example: Dolores and her brother's estate

Dolores, 61, is named executor in her brother Walter's will. Walter owned a condo and a bank account in his own name. Dolores gives the original will to the circuit court clerk, files a petition, and the judge admits the will and grants independent administration. She mails notice to Walter's two children, publishes the creditor notice and pays the funeral bill and a credit card from the estate account. After the claims period ends, she distributes the money, files her closing report and waits the 42 days. No one objects, and the court discharges her.
