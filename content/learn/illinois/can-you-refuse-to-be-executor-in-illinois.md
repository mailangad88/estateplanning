Your aunt died last month. Her will names you as executor, and you live in Arizona, work full time, and had no idea she picked you. Now a cousin is asking when you are going to "start the paperwork." Do you have to?

No. Nobody can be drafted into the job. But the Illinois Probate Act does put a clock on the choice, and it has a short list of people who are not allowed to serve at all. This page covers the deadline, the qualifications, what a non-resident should expect, and what the family does if the executor says no. For the whole process, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## How long do you have to decide?

You have 30 days from the time you learn you were named. Section 6-3 of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/6-3) says a named executor must, within 30 days after learning of the appointment, either begin a probate proceeding or formally decline to serve.

"Formally decline" matters. A phone call to your cousin does not count. The court needs something in the file showing that you will not act, so a backup can be named or the court can look at who else qualifies. An attorney can prepare the short document for you, and it usually takes a few minutes.

Staying quiet carries its own cost. If the 30 days pass without either step, the statute lets the court bar you from acting unless you show good cause, either on its own or on petition from an interested person. Letters can then issue as if you had been disqualified. The statute also lets the court admit the will without any petition once 30 days have gone by since the death. So nothing stays frozen forever, but the family loses weeks.

## What are the steps if you want to say no?

1. Read the will. Find the backup executor, if one is named, and any language that ties a gift to serving.
2. Tell the family and the backup right away, before you sign anything.
3. Do not take over the accounts, sell anything or promise to pay bills.
4. Have the declination prepared and filed with the circuit court clerk handling the estate. Our [county probate guides](/estate-planning/illinois) show which court that is.
5. Give the original will to the person who will file it. The Probate Act expects the will to reach the clerk without delay.

Declining takes you out of the job. It does not take you out of the family. You can still help your cousin find paperwork or answer questions.

## Who is not allowed to serve?

The qualifications are in 755 ILCS 5/6-13. An executor must be:

| Requirement | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| At least 18 | A minor cannot serve, even if the will names one. |
| A U.S. resident | Illinois residence is not required. |
| Of sound mind | A person adjudged to have a disability cannot serve. |
| Not in prison | Not currently held in a state or federal prison. |
| Not a convicted felon | Subject to the narrow exception below. |

The felony exception is tight. The will must name the person and must say in its own words that the testator knew about the conviction. The person cannot be barred by law from receiving a share of the estate. And the person cannot have a prior conviction for financial exploitation of an elderly person or a person with a disability, or for financial identity theft, in Illinois or elsewhere.

There is also a late-qualification rule. If a named executor was disqualified when the will was admitted but later becomes qualified, that person can petition for letters as a co-executor, or, if no executor has qualified, ask the court to issue letters and revoke any letters of administration the court already gave out.

## Can someone who lives outside Illinois be an executor?

Yes, if they live in the United States. Section 6-13 asks for U.S. residency, not Illinois residency. A daughter in Oregon can serve as executor of her mother's Chicago estate.

The wrinkle is the bond. The statute says the court may require a non-resident executor to post a bond, in an amount and with a surety the court sets, even if the will says no bond is required. A bond is an insurance policy against mistakes or theft, and its premium comes out of the estate. Our article on [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) walks through what a bond runs.

The practical problem is not the law. It is the house. A distant executor has to sign documents on short notice, deal with a locksmith, manage the mail, and attend a hearing,. A non-resident executor who is organized and willing to hire local help does fine. One who is hoping to handle everything from a phone often ends up paying more in the end.

## What can a named executor do before the court issues letters?

Not much, and that is deliberate. Section 6-14 (755 ILCS 5/6-14) lets a named executor, before letters of office issue, carry out a gift of the body, arrange the burial, pay necessary funeral charges, and preserve the estate. That is the list.

You cannot access a bank account, sell the car or collect the final paycheck with a will alone. For that you need [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary) from the court. Our explainer on [letters testamentary and letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) covers what banks ask for.

Section 6-14 also says that if the will is never admitted, the person who acted is not treated as liable, with two exceptions: refusing to deliver the estate to the person entitled to it, and waste or misapplication of the estate. So paying the funeral home from your own pocket and holding onto a receipt is fine. Using the account to catch up on your own bills is not.

## What happens after an executor declines?

Three things can happen, in this order of likelihood.

**A backup takes over.** Many wills name a second choice. That person goes through the same 30-day clock and can file the petition for probate.

**The court appoints an administrator with the will annexed.** The petition for probate has to list the heirs and others with equal or higher right to serve (755 ILCS 5/6-2), and each is mailed a copy. The person the court picks is bound by the will, but is paid and bonded like any other representative. Our article on [what happens if there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) gives the longer picture.

**A family member petitions to be administrator.** If no one named in the will will serve, the heirs and legatees get a say in who asks the court for the job. The [probate process guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) explains how independent and supervised administration differ once someone is appointed.

## Example: Priya and her father's estate

Priya's father died in Rockford and named her as executor, with her brother Anil as backup. Priya lives in Seattle, has a toddler and a new job. Within a week she called Anil, who lives 20 minutes from the house and was happy to serve. She signed a declination, and Anil filed the petition and the will. Priya inherited her half of the estate exactly as the will said, and she handled the online accounts by video call. Nothing was lost by saying no early. Weeks would have been lost by not saying anything.

## Should you decline?

Say yes if you are organized, live close enough to manage the house, and get along with the beneficiaries. Say no if you live far away with no local help, the family is already fighting, or the estate involves a business or real estate in several states. Our article on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) is aimed at the person writing the will, but it lists the same warning signs.

If you are the one writing the will, ask the person first, and name a backup. That one sentence prevents most of the trouble on this page.
