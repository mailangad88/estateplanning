Walt, 82, lived alone after his wife died. His son Greg drove him to appointments, paid his bills and found him a lawyer. Three months before Walt died, a new will left his $340,000 house to Greg alone. Walt's earlier will had split everything among his three children. The other two children now ask whether they can challenge it.

Undue influence is one of the most common grounds in a contest, and one of the hardest to prove directly. This page explains the Illinois approach, what evidence helps each side, and the steps to take. For the filing clock, see our page on the [Illinois six-month will contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline), and for the wider setting see the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What is undue influence under Illinois law?

It is pressure that destroys the maker's free choice and puts another person's wishes in place of their own. Illinois courts separate it from normal family influence. A child who asks a parent to be fair, or who is simply loved and trusted, has not committed undue influence. The influence must have been strong enough that the will reflects the influencer's plan and not the maker's. See the glossary entry for [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence).

Undue influence is different from [testamentary capacity](/learn/illinois/testamentary-capacity-in-illinois). A person can know exactly what they are signing and still be pressured. Both claims are often filed together.

## When do Illinois courts presume undue influence?

A presumption arises when four facts are shown. Illinois decisions, including the Supreme Court's DeHart v. DeHart (2013), describe them this way.

| Element | What it means | Example from Walt's case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fiduciary relationship | The maker trusted the person in a legal role, such as agent or trustee | Greg is Walt's agent under a power of attorney |
| Dependent maker, dominant beneficiary | The maker relied on the beneficiary | Greg handles bills and rides |
| Trust and confidence | The maker placed confidence in the beneficiary | Walt confides in Greg |
| Preparation or procurement | The beneficiary prepared the will or arranged it | Greg found the lawyer and attended |

When the contestant proves all four, a presumption of undue influence arises. It is not a final win. It moves the next step to the beneficiary, who may offer evidence that the gift was the maker's free act. Because this is a legal standard shaped by case law, ask an attorney how current decisions apply to your facts.

## How do you prove undue influence step by step?

Contestants usually build the proof in this order.

1. Calendar the deadline. The petition must be filed within 6 months after admission of the will (755 ILCS 5/8-1).
2. Get the drafting lawyer's name, the file and billing records, and note who called, paid and attended.
3. Collect medical records for the months around signing, plus bank and power of attorney records.
4. Map the relationships: who lived with the maker, who controlled access and who was cut off from visits.
5. List changes from the earlier plan, with dates, and compare the old and new wills.
6. Interview witnesses who saw the maker in the weeks around signing.
7. File the petition in the estate, serve the people the statute names and plan for a possible jury.

## What evidence can rebut the presumption?

The beneficiary can show that the maker acted freely. The most useful items are independent legal advice from a lawyer the beneficiary did not pick, a private meeting between maker and lawyer, a consistent statement of intent made years earlier, a rational reason for the gift, and good health at signing. A letter or recording written by the maker in their own words can also help. If Walt had told his other children for years that Greg would get the house because Greg lived there and cared for him, that history would matter.

## What kinds of facts usually fail?

Suspicion alone fails. So does an unfair result. A parent may lawfully leave unequal shares, as our page on [disinheriting a child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-illinois) explains. A visit to the lawyer with a family member, without more, is not enough either. The court asks whether the maker's will was overpowered.

## How does this look in a trust or a beneficiary change?

The same ideas reach beyond wills. A person who moves a parent's money into a joint account, or who changes a beneficiary form shortly before death, can face the same questions. Illinois courts apply the fiduciary-relationship test to trusts and deeds as well. Our page on [contesting a trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/contesting-a-trust-in-illinois-who-can-sue-grounds-and-deadlines) covers who may sue over a trust. If an older person is being pressured now, not after death, the remedy is different. See [how to report elder financial abuse in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-report-elder-financial-abuse-in-illinois), because a report can protect the person while they are alive.

Walt's other children would also look at the power of attorney. If Greg used it to move money to himself, that may support a separate claim against him as agent. Our page on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) describes the duties an agent owes.

## How can you reduce the risk when you make your own plan?

Meet the lawyer alone, and choose a lawyer who is not tied to the person who benefits. Have the lawyer note why the plan is unequal. Keep medical notes from near the signing. Consider an explanatory letter kept apart from the will. If a person with a power of attorney will benefit, consider naming someone else for that role. The [what-if page on a child who took a parent to change the will](/learn/what-if/what-if-one-child-took-a-parent-to-the-lawyer-to-change-the-will) shows the usual pattern.
