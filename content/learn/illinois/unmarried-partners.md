You have lived with your partner for ten, fifteen, maybe twenty years. You split the mortgage and the vet bills. Then someone mentions that if you died tomorrow, your partner could get nothing, and the hospital might not even take their call. In Illinois, that is not an exaggeration. It is how the law reads when there is no paper.

This page covers three moments that scare unmarried Illinois couples: the death, the hospital room, and the funeral home. It is part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). For the general version that applies in any state, see [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples). ## Does Illinois recognize common-law marriage?

No. Illinois does not let a couple become married by living together, even for decades. You are spouses in the eyes of Illinois law only if you have a marriage, or a civil union, which Illinois treats like marriage for state-law purposes (750 ILCS 75/20). Everything else on this page follows from that.

If the two of you are deciding whether to marry, that is a personal and financial choice, not only a legal one. Marriage gives automatic rights that no stack of documents fully replicates, including federal ones. But plenty of couples stay unmarried and plan well. The test is whether your papers say what marriage would have said for you.

## What does my unmarried partner inherit if I die in Illinois?

With no will, nothing. The Illinois intestacy statute (755 ILCS 5/2-1) lists a spouse, descendants, parents, siblings and other relatives. A partner is not on it. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) shows the full order.

The effect depends on how each thing is owned. This is the table couples should draw on a napkin.

| Asset | How it is held | What happens with no will |
|---|---|---|
| House in your name alone | No survivor or beneficiary named | Goes to your relatives; your partner has no right to stay |
| House as joint tenants | Deed says "not in tenancy in common but in joint tenancy" | Passes to your partner without probate |
| House as tenants in common | The Illinois default for two grantees | Your half goes to your relatives |
| Joint bank account with survivorship | Both on the account | Your partner keeps it |
| 401(k) or life insurance | Beneficiary form names your partner | Your partner is paid, whatever the will says |
| Car, personal property, sole accounts | Your name only | Your relatives |

Illinois does not assume survivorship. A deed to two people becomes a tenancy in common unless it uses the right wording, and an unmarried couple cannot hold as tenants by the entirety (765 ILCS 1005/1 and 1c). Our page on [joint tenancy in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains how a deed has to read. Check yours. Many couples who "bought the house together" discover they are tenants in common.

### A worked example

Dana, 49, and Priya, 47, have lived in Oak Park for eleven years. The bungalow is titled in Dana's name only, because she bought it first. Dana has a 401(k) naming her sister, a car, and a joint checking account with Priya. She dies without a will.

The checking account goes to Priya. The 401(k) goes to Dana's sister. The bungalow and car are probate assets, and with no spouse or children, they go to Dana's parents if living, or to her sister. Priya has contributed to the mortgage for eleven years and has no automatic claim. She may be asked to move out.

One recorded transfer on death instrument, one will, and a new 401(k) form would have changed all of it. See [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) for how the house piece works.

## Can the hospital refuse to talk to my partner?

It can, and the Illinois default list explains why. When a patient cannot decide and has named no agent, the Health Care Surrogate Act ranks who decides: a guardian, then a spouse, adult children, parents, adult siblings, adult grandchildren, and then a close friend (755 ILCS 40/25). A partner is not named as a partner. At best, they are a close friend, below every relative.

Picture Marcus, whose partner Luis has a stroke. Luis's mother flies in from out of town and, as the parent, outranks Marcus. If she and Marcus disagree on a procedure, the hospital follows the list. Nobody is being cruel. They are applying the statute.

The fix takes one signing. Illinois has a statutory short form for a health care power of attorney. It needs one adult witness and no notary, and the witness cannot be your agent or a provider caring for you (755 ILCS 45/4-10). Our page on the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) walks through it. Add a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so doctors may share records, and use our [health care agent worksheet](/free/choosing-a-health-care-agent-worksheet) to choose a backup agent.

## Who decides the funeral if we are not married?

Your family does, unless you say otherwise. Under the Disposition of Remains Act, the person named in a qualifying written instrument controls burial or cremation first. Next comes an executor following written instructions in a will, then a spouse, adult children, parents and next of kin (755 ILCS 65/5). A partner is not on that list. The named-agent form is signed by you and your agent, with your signature notarized. Details are in our page on the [Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act).

## What should an unmarried Illinois couple sign?

Each partner should put these in place, and the order below is the order that tends to matter first.

1. **Health care power of attorney and HIPAA authorization.** These matter the day you are ill, not only when you die.
2. **Power of attorney for property.** It lets your partner pay bills and handle accounts if you cannot. See the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
3. **A will or living trust** naming your partner, a backup beneficiary, and an executor or trustee. A trust avoids probate for the house and keeps the plan private. See [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts).
4. **A deed review.** Either hold the house as joint tenants with the correct wording, or keep it in one name and add a recorded transfer on death instrument.
5. **Beneficiary forms** on every retirement account, life insurance policy and payable on death account.
6. **A notarized funeral agent form,** so your partner can act in the first hours.
7. **A written agreement** about who owns what, if one partner paid for the house and the other did not. Courts are slow to infer such deals from conduct.

Our [unmarried couples planning guide](/free/unmarried-couples-planning-guide) is a checklist for sitting down with your partner and deciding these together.

## What about taxes and benefits?

An unmarried partner does not get the unlimited marital deduction. Federal estate tax applies only above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most couples owe none. Illinois taxes estates over $4 million (see [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax)), and an unmarried partner gets no spousal treatment there either, so a larger estate needs a tax review. Social Security survivor benefits and many pension survivor options also follow marriage. If you have a civil union, [civil union partners and Illinois estate law](/learn/illinois/civil-union-partners-and-illinois-estate-law) covers where it matches marriage and where it does not.

## What if we split up?

Update everything. Illinois automatically cuts a divorced spouse out of a will, but there is no divorce for unmarried partners, so an ex-partner stays on your forms until you change them. A beneficiary designation or a joint tenancy deed does not fix itself. Change them the week you separate.
