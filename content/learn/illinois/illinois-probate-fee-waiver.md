A parent dies with a car, a small bank account and a mortgage. The family has no spare cash. The clerk's office wants a filing fee, and the question is whether the court will ever forgive it. This page covers how Illinois court fees work in probate, who can ask for a waiver and the lower-cost options to try first.

For a full list of probate costs, read [what probate costs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs). If the problem is cash flow more broadly, see [paying for probate when the estate has no cash](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-pay-for-probate-when-the-estate-has-no-cash). The statewide guide is at [Illinois estate planning](/learn/illinois).

## What court fees does an Illinois probate case have?

The clerk charges to file the petition, to receive an appearance and for some later steps such as certified copies. The numbers are set county by county. As of the dates in our cost article, Cook County listed $479 for independent administration, effective October 2025, and DuPage County listed $300 to open an estate plus a $230 appearance fee, effective September 11, 2026. Treat those as examples and check your clerk's page.

| Cost | Paid to | Covered by a court-fee waiver? |
|---|---|---|
| Filing fee to open the estate | Circuit clerk | Possibly, if the application is granted |
| Appearance fee | Circuit clerk | Possibly |
| Certified copies of letters | Circuit clerk | Ask the clerk |
| Newspaper publication of the claims notice | Newspaper | Generally not a court fee; ask |
| Bond premium | Surety company | No |
| Attorney fees | Your lawyer | No |

## Can the court waive probate fees?

Illinois has a statute that lets a court waive fees for a person who cannot afford them (735 ILCS 5/5-105). The state uses a standardized Application for Waiver of Court Fees, which asks about income, benefits and expenses. In general, a person who receives certain public benefits or has income below a threshold tied to federal poverty guidelines may qualify for a full waiver, and some people qualify for a partial waiver. The clerk or judge decides, and the terms can change, so read the current form.

The harder question is whether the waiver fits an estate. The application is about the applicant's money, and an estate is a separate legal entity with its own assets. If the estate has cash, the court may expect the filing fee to come from it. If the estate has no cash and the executor is low-income, you can ask. A court may treat each case differently, so ask the clerk how it handles probate waiver requests before you file.

## What are the steps to ask for a waiver?

1. Get the current Application for Waiver of Court Fees from the Illinois Courts site or the clerk.
2. Gather proof: benefit letters, pay stubs, the last tax return.
3. Complete the form with true numbers. Mistakes can cost you the waiver.
4. Prepare the probate petition at the same time. See [what goes in the petition](/learn/illinois/illinois-petition-for-letters-of-office).
5. File the application with the petition, or as the clerk directs.
6. Wait for the clerk or judge to rule. If denied, you may be able to pay in full or ask for partial relief.
7. Keep all paperwork if the fee is later paid from the estate.

## What are cheaper paths than opening a case?

Consider these before you pay any filing fee.

- **Small estate affidavit.** If the personal property is within the limit in 755 ILCS 5/25-1 and there is no real estate, the affidavit goes to the bank, not the court, so there is no court filing fee. See [the Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) and the [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker).
- **Non-probate assets.** Accounts with a beneficiary or co-owner pass without a case.
- **Unclaimed property.** Money held by the Treasurer can be claimed directly, without a court filing.
- **Doing the paperwork yourself.** Our article on [handling an Illinois estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer) shows what is realistic.

## Who fronts the money if the estate is empty?

If the court will not waive the fee and the estate has no cash, someone usually advances it. The executor or an heir pays, keeps the receipt and is repaid from the estate when funds arrive. Do not mix personal and estate money. Put the receipt in the file and record it in the closing report. The [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) can help you add up what to expect.

## Example: Beatrice and an estate with $3,000

Beatrice, 66, is the only child of Walt, who died with a $3,000 checking account, a $9,000 car and a $1,200 final paycheck. No real estate. Her income is $1,900 a month from Social Security. She learns the personal property is far under the small estate limit, so she uses the affidavit. The bank releases the account for a notarized affidavit. She pays the debts first and keeps a list. No filing fee, no newspaper charge. If Walt had owned a $60,000 house in his name, she would have opened a case, asked about a fee waiver and, if refused, borrowed from her cousin to pay the filing fee.

## What should you ask the clerk?

- What is the filing fee for an independent estate today?
- Does the clerk accept the fee waiver form for probate?
- Are there local forms or cover sheets?
- Can the fee be paid by card or must it be check or e-filing?
- What certified copy fees apply?

## What happens to the fee after the estate has money?

A fee advanced by an executor or an heir is an estate expense. Once the estate account is open and funded, the executor reimburses the person from the account and keeps the receipt. If the court did waive the fee, the order may say the estate must pay it later from the first available funds, so read the order. Either way, the closing report lists court costs along with other expenses, and heirs may ask to see them. Other ways to cover early costs include selling a car or another asset the estate can part with.

## Does a fee waiver change anything else in the case?

No. A waiver relates to court fees only. The executor still has the same duties: mailing notices, publishing the claims notice, keeping records and filing the closing report. The waiver also does not make the executor personally liable for the estate's debts, and it does not limit what creditors can claim from the estate. If the estate later turns out to have more money than expected, for example because a house sells for $180,000, tell the clerk or your attorney, since the court may revisit whether the waiver should stand. Honesty on the application protects the executor, and a missing detail found later can undo a waiver and add penalties to a simple fee.
