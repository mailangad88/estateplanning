You just got married in Illinois, and your paperwork still describes your single life. Your 401(k) may name your mother. A will you signed at 25 may leave everything to your brother. The condo may be in one name only.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), explains what Illinois law changes the day you marry, what it leaves alone, and the order to fix things in during your first year. For the general version that applies in any state, see [estate planning after you get married](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-newlyweds). If you would rather sort it out together in one sitting, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-for-newlyweds-in-illinois&topic=will).

## Does marriage revoke my will in Illinois?

No. An Illinois will is not revoked by a change in marital status (755 ILCS 5/4-7(b)). Whatever your old will says, it keeps saying after the wedding, and it can still be admitted to probate.

Illinois protects the new spouse a different way. A spouse who is left out or left little can renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if you leave descendants, or one-half if you do not. The renunciation has to be filed within 7 months after the will is admitted to probate (755 ILCS 5/2-8). The spouse may also claim a spouse's award for nine months of support, which cannot be less than $20,000, plus at least $10,000 for each minor child living with the spouse (755 ILCS 5/15-1). Both rights are explained in [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death).

Those rights are a safety net, not a plan. A renunciation means a court filing, a deadline and a grieving spouse dealing with your relatives. The page on [how to change a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-change-a-will-in-illinois) explains why a marriage usually calls for a new will rather than a codicil, and [what happens if you remarry and never update your will](/learn/blended-families/remarried-without-updating-your-will) shows how it plays out in a second marriage.

## Who inherits if I die without a will after marrying?

It depends on whether you leave descendants. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-1, a spouse with no descendants in the picture takes the whole probate estate, and your parents and siblings take nothing. If you leave children or grandchildren, your spouse takes one-half and the descendants share the other half, even when the children are from an earlier relationship and are not your spouse's children.

| Your situation at death | Who takes the probate estate |
|---|---|
| Married, no children or other descendants | All to your spouse |
| Married, children with your spouse | One-half to your spouse, one-half to the children |
| Married, a child from before the marriage | One-half to your spouse, one-half to that child |
| Married, but the will names someone else | The will controls, subject to your spouse's right to renounce |

Two limits apply. First, these rules reach only the probate estate, meaning property in your name alone with no beneficiary or co-owner. Second, Illinois is a separate-property state with no community property, so marriage does not by itself make your spouse a co-owner of what is titled in your name. The full order of heirs is in [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois), and the free [Illinois intestacy chart](/free/illinois-intestacy-chart) puts it on one page.

## What should we update first?

Start with the papers that override everything else, because they take minutes.

1. **Beneficiary forms.** Life insurance, IRAs, workplace retirement plans and payable on death accounts go to the person named on the form, whatever a will says. For many employer plans, federal law gives a spouse rights of their own, and a waiver of those rights generally has to be signed after the marriage. The page on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) has the details.
2. **New wills.** Each of you signs your own, in writing, with two credible witnesses who watch you sign (755 ILCS 5/4-3). Use witnesses who receive nothing under the will (755 ILCS 5/4-6). See [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) and [how to make a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-make-a-will-in-illinois).
3. **Powers of attorney.** Name each other, plus a backup, in an [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) and an [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney). Without them, the people who decide for you are picked by default rules, described in [who decides under the Illinois Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).
4. **The deed.** Read how your home is titled, covered in the next section.
5. **Old documents.** If you were married before, check what [an Illinois divorce changes in your estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan) and what it leaves in place.

## Should we own the house as tenants by the entirety?

For many Illinois couples who live in the home they own, it is worth a serious look, but it only exists if the deed says so. Illinois does not assume survivorship. A deed to "Jordan and Priya" with nothing more creates a tenancy in common, and each half passes through that owner's will or intestacy (765 ILCS 1005/1).

Married couples have two survivorship options:

- **Joint tenancy.** The deed must expressly declare it, in words like "not in tenancy in common but in joint tenancy." The survivor takes the whole property without probate, and a will cannot redirect the share.
- **Tenancy by the entirety.** Available only to a married couple for their homestead, and only if the deed expressly says the spouses hold as tenants by the entirety (765 ILCS 1005/1c). The survivor keeps the whole property. A deed, mortgage or lease of the home is effective only if both spouses sign. A divorce converts it to a tenancy in common unless the court orders otherwise, and if you move and set up a new homestead together, the old entireties estate becomes a joint tenancy.

Neither form helps if you both die together, so each of you still needs a will. If one of you brings children into the marriage, survivorship can send the whole house to the stepparent and leave the children out of it, so read [blended family inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/blended-family-inheritance) before retitling. The comparison table and deed wording are on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety), and a [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) or a [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) are the main alternatives.

## Do civil union partners get the same rights?

Under Illinois law, yes. The Civil Union Act gives a civil union partner the same legal protections and benefits as a spouse (750 ILCS 75/20), so the will, intestacy, renunciation and spouse's award rules on this page apply to a partner too. The update list is the same.

The gap is federal. Federal tax and benefit rules are generally written around marriage, so a retirement plan or a federal program may treat a civil union partner differently. Ask each plan administrator, and read [civil union partners and Illinois estate law](/learn/illinois/civil-union-partners-and-illinois-estate-law) before deciding whether to convert the union to a marriage. Couples who live together without marrying or entering a civil union get none of these rights, because Illinois does not recognize common-law marriage; see [unmarried partners in Illinois](/learn/illinois/unmarried-partners).

## Example: Jordan and Priya, one year after the wedding

This is an invented example. Jordan, 31, and Priya, 30, marry in Naperville. They have no children.

Jordan signed a will at 26 that leaves everything to his sister, Dana. His condo, worth about $280,000, is titled in his name alone. His workplace 401(k) still names his mother. Priya has a $150,000 life insurance policy through work that names her father, and an IRA with no beneficiary at all.

**If Jordan died before updating anything.** His will is still valid after the marriage, so the condo and his other probate assets go to Dana. Priya could renounce the will within 7 months after it is admitted and, because Jordan left no descendants, take one-half of his estate, plus a spouse's award of at least $20,000. The 401(k) is a separate question, decided by the plan's rules and the federal spouse protections, not by the will.

**If Jordan had no will at all.** With no descendants, Priya would take his whole probate estate under 755 ILCS 5/2-1. The condo would still go through probate.

**What they did instead, over their first year:**

| When | What they changed |
|---|---|
| Month 1 | Jordan named Priya on the 401(k) and Dana as backup; Priya named Jordan on the life insurance and the IRA |
| Month 3 | Each signed a new will naming the other, with siblings as backups and two neighbors as witnesses |
| Month 3 | Each signed property and health care powers of attorney naming the other |
| Month 6 | Jordan deeded the condo to both of them as tenants by the entirety, since they live there |
| Month 12 | They checked every form again and set an annual reminder |

Now the condo passes to the survivor without probate, the accounts pay the right person, and Dana inherits only if both of them are gone.

## What does a first-year checklist look like for Illinois newlyweds?

It fits most couples without children; a baby adds steps, covered in [estate planning for new parents in Illinois](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-for-new-parents-in-illinois) and the broader [Illinois estate planning checklist](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-checklist-illinois).

- [ ] List every account with a beneficiary form, including old employers' plans.
- [ ] Change each form to name your spouse, with a backup.
- [ ] Update names on accounts and IDs if either of you changed your name.
- [ ] Find any will either of you signed before the wedding.
- [ ] Sign new wills with two disinterested witnesses.
- [ ] Sign property and health care powers of attorney.
- [ ] Pull your deed and check the wording.
- [ ] Decide on tenancy by the entirety, joint tenancy, a TOD instrument or a trust for the home.
- [ ] If you signed a prenup, make sure the wills and forms match it.
- [ ] If either of you has children, plan for them before retitling anything.
- [ ] Tell each other where the originals are kept.
- [ ] Set a yearly review date with the [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder).

The free [newlyweds estate planning kit](/free/newlyweds-estate-planning-kit) has a printable version of this timeline.

## Where does a prenup fit?

A prenup changes what a spouse can claim, not who receives your property. The Illinois Uniform Premarital Agreement Act lets future spouses agree in writing on what happens to property at death (750 ILCS 10/4), and a properly signed agreement can waive the right to renounce a will. After the wedding it can be changed only by a signed written agreement (750 ILCS 10/6). See [Illinois prenups and inheritance rights](/learn/illinois/illinois-prenup-and-inheritance-rights) and the free [prenup and estate plan checklist](/free/prenup-and-estate-plan-checklist).
