People ask this after a lawsuit scare, a business loss, or a child's second divorce. The honest answer depends on whose creditors you mean. A trust you create for yourself rarely keeps your own creditors out. A trust you create for someone else can protect them well.

This page walks through the Illinois Trust Code rules (760 ILCS 3, Article 5) that decide those cases. For the general national picture, see [does a living trust protect assets from creditors](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-protect-assets-from-creditors). This is general education, not legal advice, and part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Can a trust protect your own assets from your creditors in Illinois?

Usually not, if you can still get the money. The Trust Code is built around that idea (760 ILCS 3/505).

| Type of trust | Can your creditors reach it? |
|---|---|
| Revocable living trust | Yes, to the same extent as if you owned the assets directly |
| Irrevocable trust where you can receive distributions | Yes, up to the maximum that could be paid to you |
| Irrevocable trust for others, you keep no benefit | Generally no, but a transfer made to dodge a known creditor can be undone |
| Trust for a qualifying disabled person | Excluded from the settlor-creditor rule |

The first row is why a [revocable living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) is a probate and incapacity tool, not a shield. The second row catches the most hopeful plans: a trust you fund and name yourself a discretionary beneficiary of. Because the trustee could pay you, a creditor can reach what could be paid.

Some other states have enacted statutes that allow a self-settled trust to resist creditors under conditions. Whether such a trust would protect an Illinois resident is uncertain, and it comes with cost, a trustee in that state and litigation risk. Our attorneys would not promise it.

### What about a fraudulent transfer?

If you move assets into a trust for others after a claim arises, or when you are already insolvent, a court can treat the move as a fraudulent transfer and undo it. The timing is the whole issue. A gift to a family trust made years before any dispute, when you are solvent and keep enough to live on, is a different matter from one made the month after a lawsuit is filed. Ask an attorney before moving anything once a claim exists.

## What does a spendthrift trust protect for an heir?

A lot. A spendthrift provision is valid if it restrains both voluntary and involuntary transfers of the beneficiary's interest, and the phrase "spendthrift trust" is enough to do that (760 ILCS 3/502). Once the clause is valid, a creditor generally cannot reach the interest or a distribution before the beneficiary receives it. Our [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) page covers the design.

Add a discretionary standard and protection gets stronger. Where the trustee has discretion over distributions, a creditor cannot compel a payment or get an order attaching the interest (760 ILCS 3/504). This holds even if the beneficiary is also a trustee. If the trustee's discretion is limited by an ascertainable standard, such as health and support, the creditor's reach is limited to what it would be if the beneficiary were not acting as trustee.

Here is an example. Maureen leaves $600,000 in trust for her son Kyle, who runs a contracting business and has personally signed for several business loans. The trust is a spendthrift trust with an independent trustee who decides distributions for Kyle's health, education and support. When a bank gets a judgment against Kyle, it cannot demand money from the trust. If Maureen had left Kyle the money outright, the bank could have gone after it as soon as it landed in his account. These numbers are illustrative.

### Who can get past a spendthrift clause?

The Trust Code lists exceptions (760 ILCS 3/503):

- A child, spouse or former spouse holding a support judgment or order.
- A judgment creditor who protected the beneficiary's interest in the trust.
- Illinois or federal claims where a statute provides for them.

In those cases the creditor must show that traditional collection methods are not enough before getting an attachment. A mandatory distribution that the trustee fails to pay within a reasonable time after it is due can also be reached, regardless of a spendthrift clause (760 ILCS 3/506). That is a reason to prefer discretionary language for a beneficiary with debt problems, and to draft with care.

Our page on [keeping an inheritance safe from a child's divorce, addiction or bad spouse](/learn/trusts/keeping-an-inheritance-safe-from-a-childs-divorce-addiction-or-bad-spouse) covers the family side.

## Does a trust protect a beneficiary who receives Medicaid or has a disability?

A discretionary trust for a beneficiary with a substantial disability generally is not liable to the State or public agencies for aid provided, with exceptions. Certain Medicaid-compliant trusts must repay Medicaid at the beneficiary's death (760 ILCS 3/509). That is a separate design from an ordinary spendthrift trust, covered in our page on [special needs planning in Illinois](/learn/illinois/special-needs-planning-in-illinois-for-a-disabled-child).

## What other tools protect assets in Illinois?

A trust is only one piece, and often not the main one.

1. **Liability insurance and an umbrella policy.** For most households this does more than any trust.
2. **The homestead exemption.** Illinois protects $15,000 of equity in your home from judgment creditors, as explained in [the Illinois homestead exemption](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death).
3. **How title is held.** For married couples, tenancy by the entirety can matter; see [Illinois joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).
4. **Business entities.** An LLC can separate business risk from personal assets.
5. **Retirement accounts.** Many are protected by statute; ask which ones apply.
6. **A Medicaid asset protection trust.** This is aimed at long-term care costs, not lawsuits, and relies on the look-back, which is 60 months in most states. See [Medicaid asset protection trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust).

## What happens to your own trust's assets after you die?

The protection question flips at death. After the settlor's death, a formerly revocable trust may be liable for the settlor's debts, administration costs, funeral expenses and spousal or child awards if the probate estate is not enough. A trustee who distributes at least nine months after death, without having received a relevant written notice, is released from liability for those distributions (760 ILCS 3/505). Our article on [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) explains the probate side.

A trust can also be a useful place for a surviving spouse or child to hold an inheritance rather than receive it outright. Compare that with [disclaiming an inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois), which is a different decision.

## What should you do first?

1. List what you are worried about: a lawsuit, a business, a child's debts, nursing home costs.
2. Sort the assets by whether you need to keep control or access.
3. Check your insurance limits before you spend on a trust.
4. If the goal is protecting an heir, ask for a spendthrift trust with an independent trustee and discretionary distributions.
5. If a claim already exists, talk to an attorney before transferring anything.
