When a loved one leaves behind a modest bank account and a few belongings, opening a full probate case can feel like too much. Illinois has a shortcut for small estates, and the rules were changed recently. This page explains who qualifies, what the affidavit says and what the person signing takes on.

For the full court process, read [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process). For the general idea in other states, see our article on [small estate affidavits](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit). Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links the other pages.

## What is the Illinois small estate affidavit?

It is a sworn statement, authorized by the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/25-1), that a person gives to a bank or other holder of the dead person's property. The statute says the holder must then pay or deliver the property, or give access to a safe deposit box, to the person named. No court case is opened, so there is no judge, no creditor publication and no letters of office.

The tradeoff is that the person who signs takes on responsibility. They must pay the estate's valid debts and are liable if they get it wrong.

## Who can use it, and what are the limits?

You can use the affidavit only if all of these are true under 755 ILCS 5/25-1:

- No letters of office are outstanding on the estate and no petition for letters is contemplated or pending.
- The personal estate passing by intestacy or under a will is within the limit. As of 2026 the limit is $150,000 in tangible and intangible personal property, not counting motor vehicles registered with the Secretary of State.
- Registered motor vehicles can be handled separately and do not count toward the $150,000.
- The property is personal property. Real estate is outside the statute.

A 2025 amendment (Public Act 104-346, effective August 15, 2025) raised the limit from $100,000 to $150,000 and moved registered vehicles outside the cap. We believe the new rules apply to people who die on or after that date, but this should be confirmed. A later act (Public Act 104-624) restates the same $150,000 figure with an effective date of January 1, 2027. Check the statute for the current text before you sign.

## What counts toward the limit?

Think bank accounts that have no beneficiary, stocks and brokerage accounts in the decedent's name only, the final paycheck, tax refunds and personal items. Assets that already pass by beneficiary designation or joint ownership do not count, because they are not part of the estate to begin with. Our [probate versus non-probate assets article](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the difference.

Make a list with values at the date of death. If the total is close to the limit, get a second opinion on the figures. The [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) can help you sort the list.

## What does the affidavit say?

The statute lists the contents. They include the affiant's name and contact information, the decedent's name and date of death with a copy of the death certificate, the decedent's residence, and a statement that no probate is pending. They also state that the personal estate is within the limit, how the debts are classified and paid, the family members (spouse and children), and the heirs or legatees with their shares. The affiant also agrees to indemnify creditors, heirs and legatees for losses caused by their actions, and to pay valid claims.

Banks often have their own forms. Many will accept the form from the Illinois courts or a clerk's website. Each holder decides whether the affidavit meets the statute, so ask what they need before you start.

## How do you use it, step by step?

1. Confirm that no probate case is open or planned, and that no one has asked for letters.
2. List all of the decedent's property and add up the personal estate, leaving out registered vehicles.
3. Make sure the total is within the limit, and that no real estate must be transferred.
4. Gather certified copies of the death certificate.
5. Complete the affidavit with the required statements, and sign it before a notary.
6. Give it to each holder, such as a bank or broker, with the death certificate and your ID.
7. Pay the funeral bill and other valid claims in the order the law requires.
8. Distribute what is left to the people entitled to it, and keep records of every payment.

## What debts must the person who signs pay?

The affiant must pay valid claims against the estate before distributing money to heirs. The statute lists a priority order that includes funeral and burial expenses, the surviving spouse's award, federal debts, certain employee wage claims and state and local debts. Paying a lower priority debt ahead of a higher one is a mistake that can cost the signer. Our article on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains the idea.

## What are the risks of signing?

The affiant must agree to hold harmless the creditors, heirs and legatees who lose out because of the affiant's acts or omissions, and may owe their reasonable attorney fees and expenses. A holder that pays out in good faith on a proper affidavit is protected. This is why people with disputes, unknown creditors or unclear heirs often choose formal probate. The court process gives a creditor notice and a closing order. See the [timeline article](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) for how those periods work.

## How is the affidavit different from probate?

| | Small estate affidavit | Formal probate |
|---|---|---|
| Court case | None | Yes, in the circuit court |
| Letters of office | No | Yes |
| Creditor notice | Not published through a court | Published and mailed, with a claims deadline |
| Real estate | Not covered | Can be handled |
| Who is responsible for debts | The person who signs | The executor, under court rules |
| Limit | $150,000 personal property, vehicles aside | None |

The affidavit is faster because it skips the court. It is riskier for the same reason, because no judge reviews the work and no claims deadline protects the signer. For the full court path see the process article, and for dollar costs see the cost article.

## What if a bank refuses the affidavit?

Banks and brokers sometimes ask for extra documents or their own form. The statute says a holder that pays on a proper affidavit is protected, but it does not remove the holder's right to ask questions. Ask the bank in writing what it needs. If the holder still refuses, or if the estate turns out to be larger than you thought, opening a probate case may be the cleaner path. Our attorneys can help you decide.

## What if the estate is too large or has a house?

Then you generally need probate, or the property may pass another way. A house held with a spouse by survivorship, or covered by a recorded transfer on death instrument, can pass without probate. See our article on the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument). For costs of a formal estate, see [what probate costs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs).

## Example: Grace and her father's accounts

Grace, 44, learns that her father, Leonard, had a checking account of about $40,000 in his name only, a registered car and no house. No one has filed a probate case, and Grace and her sister are the only heirs. Grace checks the statute, completes the affidavit and has it notarized. She gives it to the bank, retitles the car through the Secretary of State and pays the funeral home from the account. She then splits the rest with her sister and keeps records.
