Dennis died in March. His will left his wife Carol the use of the family home for life and gave everything else to his two children from his first marriage. Carol, 66, now has a small income and few assets of her own. The will gives her a place to live but no cash. She asks whether she should renounce it.

Our page on [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) explains the statute. This page focuses on the choice: how to compare the two sides, what you give up, and the order of steps. See also the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What does renouncing give you?

It gives you a fixed share. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-8(a), the share is one-third of the entire estate if the decedent left a descendant, and one-half if not, figured after all just claims are paid. It replaces what the will says. The statute calls a filed renunciation a complete bar to any claim under the will (755 ILCS 5/2-8(b)). The spouse's award under 755 ILCS 5/15-1 is a separate right that stays.

## How do you compare the will with the statutory share?

Build a simple side-by-side. Here is Carol's case. The probate estate after claims is $600,000, and the home is valued at $300,000.

| Item | If Carol accepts the will | If Carol renounces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home | Lifetime use, not ownership | Possibly part of her share |
| Cash and investments | None | Part of her $200,000 share |
| Statutory share (one-third of $600,000) | Not available | $200,000 |
| Spouse's award | May be replaced if the will says so and she does not renounce | At least the statutory minimum of $20,000, plus $10,000 per qualifying minor child (confirm current amounts) |
| Control | Children own the remainder | She owns her share outright |
| Future home rights | Ends at her death or move | Ends if the house is sold to pay her |

On paper, renouncing gives Carol $200,000 plus the award. But if the share must come from the house, she may lose her home. If the will's life use is worth more to her than the share, she may accept. The value of a life use depends on her age and the home's rental value. A professional appraisal makes that comparison real.

## What steps should a spouse follow?

1. Get a copy of the will and the order admitting it. Note the admission date.
2. Count 7 months and put a safe date on the calendar.
3. List the probate estate: what passes under the will, with values and debts.
4. List what passes outside the will, such as joint accounts and beneficiary forms, to see what you already have.
5. Compare the packages using a table like Carol's. Include taxes, who controls each asset and your housing needs.
6. Check for a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement that may waive the right.
7. If you decide to renounce, sign a written instrument and file it with the court where the will was admitted.
8. If a related case is pending, file a petition for more time within the 7 months.

Use the [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) to separate the estate from non-probate assets.

## What do you give up by renouncing?

Everything the will gave you. That includes a life estate, a trust income interest and gifts of personal items. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-8(c), if the spouse had a life interest and renounces, the later interest generally takes effect as if the spouse had died, unless the will says otherwise. The children may get their share sooner, and may have to sell the house. You also give up the will's guidance on who keeps heirlooms, though a share of those items may be part of your portion.

## What does renouncing not touch?

The share reaches the estate under the will. Assets that pass by [beneficiary designation](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts), joint title or a living trust may be outside that claim, and the statute does not lay out how the share is measured against them. If the decedent moved most assets into a trust before death, the renunciation may reach little. Our page on [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) shows why trust assets sit outside probate.

## What other points affect the choice?

- Taxes: a spouse's share generally qualifies for the marital deduction, but check the Illinois estate tax if the estate is near $4 million.
- Public benefits: a spouse who receives Medicaid may be affected by taking or refusing property.
- Family: the children from the first marriage may contest the figures. A written settlement can trade a smaller share for a house for life.
- Planning: spouses who want to avoid this fight can sign a written agreement or arrange a trust, as our page on the [family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) shows.

## What if the spouse is uncertain or the numbers are close?

Take time, but not past the deadline. Ask the executor for an inventory and the date of admission in writing. If values are unclear, such as a business or farmland, ask for an appraisal now, because the choice rests on those numbers. If the executor delays, the spouse can file a petition for more time within the 7 months, and our page on [what heirs can do about a slow Illinois executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor) explains other tools.

A settlement is another route. Carol could offer the children a deal: she keeps the house for life, and receives $40,000 in cash now, in return for not renouncing. The children avoid a forced sale and she gets a payment the will did not give. Any deal should be in writing, signed by every adult heir, and made before the 7 months end. Where children are minors or not yet known, a court may need to approve it.
