Many divorced parents share one worry. If you die while the children are young, your former spouse will probably raise them, and the money you leave could pass through your ex's hands. You cannot always change the first part. You can almost always change the second.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is written for a parent whose divorce is final or nearly final. It covers who raises the children, how money reaches them without your ex in control, who to pick as trustee, what your divorce judgment may require about life insurance, and a checklist for the weeks after the judgment. If you already know your plan needs work, you can [book a consultation about your children's plan](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-for-divorced-parents-in-illinois&topic=kids) and bring your judgment with you.

## Will my ex control my kids' inheritance in Illinois?

Not through your own documents, but possibly through your children. Illinois removes a former spouse from much of your plan once the judgment is entered. Your will is read as if your ex died before you (755 ILCS 5/4-7(b)), and trust provisions favoring your ex are revoked unless the trust or the judgment says otherwise (760 ILCS 3/605), as our page on [how divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan) explains. A divorce judgment can also revoke an ex's designation on certain life insurance and retirement benefits (750 ILCS 5/503(b-5)), with exceptions and limits for federal employer plans, as covered on our page about [life insurance with no living beneficiary](/learn/illinois/life-insurance-with-no-living-beneficiary-in-illinois).

The gap is the money that goes to the children. A child under 18 cannot take an inheritance, and an insurer or bank will not pay one. If nobody was named to manage it, the probate court appoints a guardian of the child's estate, and a parent who is willing and able is the usual choice. That parent is your ex. Our page on [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) walks through that process, including the bond, the court reports and the handoff of everything left at 18.

There is a second, less obvious route. If a child receives money outright and then dies with no spouse or children, the Illinois intestacy statute sends the child's estate to parents and siblings (755 ILCS 5/2-1), as our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) sets out. Your ex would be one of those parents.

## Can I keep my ex from being guardian of the children?

Usually not, if your ex is a legal parent and fit to parent. When one parent dies, the surviving parent generally keeps or takes custody without a guardianship case. Our article on whether [the surviving parent gets custody](/learn/guardianship/does-the-surviving-parent-get-custody-if-you-die) explains why a line in your will cannot override that.

You should still name a guardian. Illinois lets a parent designate a guardian for a minor child in a will or other signed writing with at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/11-5), as described on our page about [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children). That nomination does its work if your ex has died, cannot be found, has had parental rights ended or is found unfit by a court. If you have serious concerns about your ex today, the place to address them is a custody order through your family law attorney, while you are alive, and a dated record of specific events kept with your will.

Illinois separates the two jobs that matter here. The guardian of the person raises the child, and the guardian of the estate handles the child's property, as our page on [guardian of the person vs. guardian of the estate](/learn/illinois/guardian-of-the-person-vs-guardian-of-the-estate-in-illinois) explains. You may not get to pick the first. A trust means a court never needs to pick the second.

## How does a trust keep the money away from your ex?

A trust replaces the court-appointed money manager with a trustee you choose, under rules you write. Your will can create a trust for the children at your death, called a [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust), or you can use a living trust. Either way, your life insurance and retirement forms name the trust as beneficiary, so the money never lands in a child's name.

The trustee then pays for what the children need. That can mean paying a school or a doctor directly, paying for activities and a car later on, and sending a set amount to the household raising them. Your ex can ask for money for the children, and the trustee decides. You can also set the ages when each child receives what is left, and name [contingent beneficiaries](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary) if a child dies before the trust is paid out, which closes the intestacy route above.

| Route for the children's money | Who manages it | Your ex's likely role | When the child gets control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Left outright, or child named on a form | Court-appointed guardian of the estate | Often appointed as that guardian | At 18 |
| UTMA custodian named in will or form | The custodian you name | None, unless named | At 21 (760 ILCS 20/21) |
| Trust for the children | The trustee you name | Can ask for funds, cannot control them | At ages you choose |

The UTMA age comes from our page on [Illinois UTMA accounts and gifts to minors](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors). A custodian you name keeps your ex out, but 21 is early for a large insurance payout, and a custodial account has none of a trust's spending rules. Our page on [UTMA vs. a trust for a minor child](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child) compares them, and the [trust for minor children planner](/free/trust-for-minor-children-planner) helps you sketch ages and spending rules before a meeting.

## Who should be trustee for your kids?

Someone who is not your ex and who can work with your ex. The trustee will deal with the parent raising your children for years, about tuition, braces, summer camp and the household budget. A trustee who is hostile to your ex can make the children's lives harder. A trustee who gives in to every request defeats the purpose.

Look for these qualities:

- Keeps records and pays bills on time.
- Can say no politely and explain why.
- Has no money problems of their own.
- Is not the guardian you named, unless you are comfortable with one person holding both roles.

Many divorced parents name a sibling or close friend, with a second person as backup. For a large sum, some name a trust company as co-trustee alongside a relative. Our guides on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) and [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) go further. Ask the person first, and tell them where the trust papers and the judgment are kept.

## Does your divorce decree require life insurance for the kids?

Read it before you assume either way. Many Illinois judgments require a parent to keep life insurance naming the children, or a former spouse for them, to secure child support, and the judgment controls until a court changes it, as noted on our page about [how divorce affects your plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan). Look for the amount, the length of the requirement and exactly who must be named.

Three practical points follow. First, if the judgment says to name the children, ask your divorce attorney whether naming a trust for the children meets it, because naming the children directly brings back the guardian of the estate problem. Second, if the judgment requires your ex to be named for the children, any coverage above the required amount can still go to the trust. Third, the judgment may also require your ex to carry insurance for the children, so the children may receive money from your ex's policy too. Your trustee should know about it. The [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you list every policy and form in one place.

## What should you change after the judgment?

Work through this checklist once the judgment is entered. Some steps may be limited while the case is pending, so check with your divorce attorney about timing.

1. Get a certified copy of the judgment and mark the sections on insurance, retirement accounts, the house and the parenting plan.
2. Sign a new will that names an executor, a guardian and a backup guardian, and creates a trust for the children or pours into a living trust.
3. Name a trustee and a backup trustee who are not your ex.
4. Change life insurance and retirement beneficiary forms to the children's trust, as far as the judgment allows, and keep the confirmations.
5. Remove the children as direct beneficiaries on any bank account or form.
6. Sign a new property power of attorney and a health care power of attorney.
7. Check deeds and joint accounts against the judgment.
8. Write a letter to the guardian and trustee about schools, doctors and what you hope the money pays for.
9. Set a reminder to review the plan after a remarriage, a move or a change in the parenting plan.

The [co-parenting after divorce estate checklist](/free/co-parenting-after-divorce-estate-checklist) gives you a worksheet for these steps. If you are raising the children mostly on your own, our page on an [estate plan for a single parent in Illinois](/learn/illinois/estate-plan-for-a-single-parent-in-illinois) adds the short-term guardian papers for emergencies.

## Example: Leah's plan after her divorce

This is an invented example. Leah, 41, lives in Rockford with Sam, 8, and Ava, 12. Her divorce from Mark was entered in the spring. The judgment requires her to keep $250,000 of life insurance for the children until Ava finishes high school. Leah has a $500,000 term policy that names Sam and Ava directly, a 401(k) of about $90,000 and a will from her marriage.

If Leah died with that setup, Mark would raise the children, which Leah accepts. But the insurer would not pay Sam and Ava, so a court would likely appoint Mark as guardian of their estates, and each child would receive what was left at 18.

Leah signs a new will that names her brother Joel as guardian if Mark cannot serve, and creates a trust for both children with her sister Nina as trustee and a close friend as backup. Her divorce attorney confirms that naming the children's trust satisfies the insurance clause, so she changes the whole policy and the 401(k) to the trust. The trust pays tuition and medical costs directly, sends Mark a monthly amount for the children's household costs, and pays each child's share in thirds at 25, 30 and 35. If a child dies first, that share stays for the other child.

If Leah dies now, Mark raises Sam and Ava, Nina manages the money, and no court guardianship of the estate is needed.
