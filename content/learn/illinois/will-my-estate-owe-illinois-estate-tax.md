Most Illinois homeowners in their fifties and sixties have heard that the estate tax is for the very rich. At the federal level that is still close to true, because the exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026. Illinois starts at $4 million. A paid-off house in Wilmette or Naperville, two retirement accounts and a work life insurance policy can reach that number without anyone feeling wealthy.

This page, part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), gives you a worksheet you can finish in about ten minutes, a worked example of a married couple, and a plain list of the tools that exist when the total is too high. The rules themselves are laid out on the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) page, so this one focuses on doing the arithmetic. If you would rather talk the numbers through, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-will-my-estate-owe-illinois-estate-tax&topic=trust) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## What counts toward the $4 million?

Everything you own or control at death counts, plus certain taxable gifts you made during life. The Illinois Attorney General says an estate over $4,000,000 must file Illinois Form 700 whether or not a federal return is required, and the test looks at the gross estate, not just the assets that go through probate.

That is the part people miss. Probate is the court process for assets in your name alone. The tax test is wider. Our guide to [what counts toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-counts-toward-the-estate-tax) walks through each asset type, and the short version is that a beneficiary designation changes who receives an account but not whether it is counted.

## The 10-minute worksheet

Use current balances, not what you paid. Round to the nearest ten thousand. You only need a statement or a recent online login for each line.

| Line | What to put in it | Your number |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Home, at what it would sell for today, minus the mortgage balance | |
| 2 | Other real estate (lake house, rental, land), minus loans | |
| 3 | 401(k), 403(b), IRA, pension lump sums, at full balance | |
| 4 | Life insurance you own or control: add the death benefit, including a group policy through work | |
| 5 | Brokerage, bank and savings accounts, including payable-on-death accounts | |
| 6 | Joint accounts and property with anyone, counted at full value for now | |
| 7 | Business interests, at what a buyer would pay | |
| 8 | Assets in a revocable living trust | |
| 9 | Large gifts to anyone beyond the yearly gift tax exclusion, from past years | |
| | Total of lines 1 to 9 | |

Compare your total with $4,000,000. If you are married, also write down your spouse's total on its own, and then add the two together. You need all three figures to see the problem described below.

A few notes on the lines. Line 4 is the one that surprises people. Life insurance is income tax free to the family, but it is part of the owner's estate, and a $1 million term policy on a working parent counts the same as $1 million in the bank. If the policy is owned by an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust), that is a different answer. Line 6 will overstate the numbers for a couple, since for spouses only part of a joint asset is generally counted at the first death. It is still the right figure to use, because by the second death the survivor holds all of it. Line 9 matters because Illinois adds taxable lifetime gifts back into the test, as the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) page explains.

## Why is there a cliff at $4 million?

The Illinois Attorney General describes the $4 million as "a taxable threshold and not a credit against tax." A credit would reduce the bill by a fixed amount. A threshold decides whether you are in the system at all, and once you are in, the tax is figured with an interrelated formula in which the tax and the taxable amount depend on each other.

The Attorney General's own example shows how fast it moves. For an estate of $5,000,000, all in Illinois, the Illinois estate tax is $285,714 and the federal tax is $0. That is a tax of roughly 28.6 cents on each dollar above $4 million, which is why a family that is a few hundred thousand dollars over the line should look at it carefully rather than shrug. Those are the Attorney General's illustrative figures. The office offers an online calculator for a real estate, and your own result will turn on your own assets and any deductions.

## Why does it hit married couples so often?

Illinois does not allow portability. The Attorney General's fact sheet says the carry-over of the unused federal exemption to a surviving spouse does not apply to the Illinois tax. So the $4 million belongs to each person alone, and whatever the first spouse does not use is gone.

Federal law is the opposite, which is why many couples assume they are covered. Our article on [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) explains the federal election. Illinois ignores it.

## A worked example: Dale and Ruth

Dale, 63, and Ruth, 61, live in Glenview. This is an invented example with round numbers.

| Asset | Amount |
|---|---|
| House, no mortgage | $1,400,000 |
| Dale's 401(k) | $1,100,000 |
| Ruth's IRA | $450,000 |
| Joint brokerage account | $600,000 |
| Dale's employer life insurance | $500,000 |
| Joint savings | $250,000 |
| **Combined total** | **$4,300,000** |

Neither of them feels wealthy, and most of this sits in retirement accounts they cannot spend freely. The combined total is $4.3 million.

Dale dies first. Everything passes to Ruth, by beneficiary form and by the way the house and accounts are titled. Nothing is owed at his death, because property passing to a surviving spouse is generally deductible. Ruth now holds the whole $4.3 million, including the insurance proceeds, and she has only her own $4 million threshold. Dale's unused $4 million cannot be added. Her estate is over the line and must file, and her children are the ones who will deal with a tax bill on the part that exceeds it.

Now compare the same couple with a plan. Dale's documents direct an amount up to the Illinois threshold into a trust for Ruth's benefit, sometimes called a bypass or credit shelter trust. Ruth can use the trust for her needs, but the trust assets are not counted in her estate later, so Dale's $4 million is used instead of wasted. We cover the difference in [bypass trust vs. portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability). The trade-offs are real: the trust has to be funded correctly, Ruth gives up some control, and the assets in it have to be tracked.

The pattern is common. The tax is not caused by wealth in any ordinary sense. It is caused by documents that leave everything outright to the survivor.

## What planning tools exist?

These are described generally. Which ones fit depends on the family, and none of them works in every case.

- **Use both spouses' exclusions.** Drafting a will or trust so that the first spouse's $4 million goes to a bypass trust instead of outright to the survivor.
- **Illinois-only QTIP election.** The statute allows a separate Illinois election for a marital trust, so planners have some room to manage state tax apart from federal tax (35 ILCS 405/2). See our overview of [the marital deduction](/learn/estate-tax/marital-deduction-explained).
- **Life insurance in an irrevocable trust.** Moving ownership out of your estate can take the death benefit off line 4, though a transfer of an existing policy has its own rules.
- **Charitable gifts.** Gifts to charity at death are deductible.
- **Lifetime gifts.** They can shrink what you own, but taxable gifts are added back into the Illinois test, so they do not always help.
- **A living trust on its own.** It does not reduce the total. Our page on [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) explains the reason.

If your total is near but under the line, read [estate tax planning when you are near the exemption](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-planning-when-you-are-near-the-exemption). Retirement balances are often the biggest line and have their own wrinkles, covered in [estate tax planning when your estate is mostly retirement accounts](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-planning-when-your-estate-is-mostly-retirement-accounts).

## Will the $4 million figure change?

The statute sets the Illinois exclusion at $4,000,000 for deaths on or after January 1, 2013 (35 ILCS 405/2). Bills to raise it have been introduced in the General Assembly, but the rule that applies is the one in force on the date of death. Check the Attorney General's site or ask an attorney before relying on any number, including the ones on this page.

## What should you do with your total?

Match your result to the next step.

1. **Total under about $3 million, no large changes coming.** You are probably fine. Recheck once a year.
2. **Total between $3 million and $4 million.** Markets, a home sale or an inheritance can move you over. Read the near-the-exemption article and repeat the worksheet every year.
3. **Combined couple total over $4 million, each spouse under.** This is the Dale and Ruth case. Have your documents looked at while both of you are healthy.
4. **Individual total over $4 million.** Planning matters more. Bring the worksheet, statements and a copy of your current documents to a consultation.
