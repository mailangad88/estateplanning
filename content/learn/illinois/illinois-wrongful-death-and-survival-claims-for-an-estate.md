When a death follows a crash, a fall, a workplace accident or a medical mistake, families often hear the words wrongful death and survival action in the same week they are choosing a casket. The words sound like one lawsuit. In Illinois they are two different claims with two different groups of people who benefit.

This page explains who files, who receives the money, how it differs from the probate estate and which deadlines to watch. It is part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). It does not tell you whether you have a good case, which is a question for a personal injury attorney, and the amounts in the example below are invented.

## Who can file a wrongful death claim in Illinois?

The decedent's personal representative, in the representative's name. Under the Wrongful Death Act the action is brought in the names of the decedent's personal representatives, and a claim exists only where the person who died could have sued and recovered damages had they lived (740 ILCS 180/1 and 180/2). If the person left a will, that is the executor once the court issues letters of office. With no will, it is the administrator.

Families sometimes expect the spouse or an adult child to sue in their own name. The statute does not work that way. The spouse or child benefits from the case without being the named plaintiff.

If nobody has opened an estate and the lawsuit is the only asset, you do not have to run a full probate case. Section 2.1 lets the court appoint a special administrator to bring or defend the action, on the motion of an eligible beneficiary and after notice to the heirs or legatees. When there is also a house, bank accounts and debts, a regular estate is usually opened, as described in [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process).

## Who gets the money from a wrongful death settlement?

The surviving spouse and next of kin, and not by the will. The recovery is for their exclusive benefit, and the damages can include pecuniary losses plus grief, sorrow and mental suffering (740 ILCS 180/2(a)). The court then divides the recovery in proportion to each person's dependency on the decedent. In an agreed settlement, the circuit court does the dividing, and when a case is tried the judge holds a hearing on each person's dependency (740 ILCS 180/2(b) and (i)). Next of kin includes an adopted child and adopting parent as if they were natural.

Here is an example, invented for illustration. Denise, 46, dies in a collision. She is survived by her husband Paul, her 19-year-old son Marcus who lives at home and attends community college, and her mother Lena, who lives independently. A $400,000 gross recovery is approved. The judge hears that Paul and Marcus depended on Denise's income and household work, and that Lena depended on her for very little. The court might award 55 percent to Paul, 35 percent to Marcus and 10 percent to Lena. The percentages are the judge's call on the evidence, not a formula in the statute. Attorney fees and case costs come out of the gross, and those arrangements are in the retainer agreement.

Denise's will said everything goes to her sister. The sister receives nothing from this money.

If no spouse or next of kin is entitled to recover, the Act sends the recovery elsewhere. Hospital and medical providers are each capped at $450. The personal representatives receive money for administration, litigation and funeral expenses, capped at $900 plus a reasonable attorney's fee (740 ILCS 180/2(c)). That is an unusual situation, so most families will never see those caps.

## What is a survival claim, and how is it different?

A survival claim belongs to the person who died. Illinois keeps certain claims alive after death, including actions for personal injury, injury to property, fraud and several others (755 ILCS 5/27-6). The representative can pursue what the decedent could have pursued, such as the decedent's own damages from the injury before death. Survival money is generally treated as an estate asset, so it can be used to pay claims and then follows the will or intestacy. Ask the attorney handling the case how any settlement is allocated between the two claims, because the split decides who is paid.

| | Wrongful death | Survival |
|---|---|---|
| Statute | Wrongful Death Act, 740 ILCS 180 | Probate Act, 755 ILCS 5/27-6 |
| Whose loss | The spouse and next of kin | The decedent's own injury claim |
| Who receives | Spouse and next of kin, divided by dependency | The estate, then creditors and heirs or legatees under the will or intestacy |
| Does the will control? | No | Yes, after debts and expenses |
| Who sues | Personal representative | Personal representative |

The allocation matters because a will leaving everything to one child produces very different results depending on which bucket holds the money. A family that settles both claims together should ask for the split in writing.

## How is this different from the probate estate?

The probate estate is what the person owned in their sole name: accounts, a house, a car. The executor collects it, pays claims and distributes it under the will. Wrongful death money does not belong to the person who died. It arises because of the death, so it passes outside that process to a different group. Compare the way a life insurance payout to a named beneficiary works, covered in [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

If the person had no will, the people who inherit under [Illinois intestacy rules](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) and the people entitled to the wrongful death recovery are often the same relatives, but the shares can differ because one is set by dependency and the other by statute.

## What deadlines apply?

The general limit is two years after the person's death. The Act extends it in specific situations (740 ILCS 180/2):

- five years after death if the death resulted from violent intentional conduct.
- one year after the final disposition of a listed criminal case, against the person charged only.
- two years after turning 18 for a beneficiary who was a minor when the claim arose.

Do not treat the longer periods as a plan. Claims against a government body, a hospital or an employer may carry their own notice rules or limits that start earlier. Talk to a personal injury attorney in the first weeks, and tell them about the estate so one person is named representative early. The probate calendar, the six-month creditor window described in [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate), and the lawsuit calendar run separately.

## What does the executor have to do?

Mostly coordinate. The executor signs the retainer on the estate's behalf, keeps beneficiaries informed, and gets court approval where the law requires it. An executor who is also a beneficiary needs extra care, because the family members' interests may conflict. For example, the spouse and the parents compete for the same dependency pie. Our [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) guide describes the fiduciary role, and the [executor workload tool](/tools/executor-workload) shows how much else is on the plate.
