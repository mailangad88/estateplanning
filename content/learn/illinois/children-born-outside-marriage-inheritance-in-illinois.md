Many Illinois families include children whose parents never married. When a father dies, the question is often whether those children, perhaps older now and living far away, are heirs. The law treats them as heirs, but a father's side needs proof that a mother's side does not.

This page explains the rule, the kinds of proof, the steps for a child or an estate, and the planning options for a parent. For the general order of heirs, see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

## Does a child born outside marriage inherit in Illinois?

Yes. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-2, a child born outside marriage is an heir of the mother and of the mother's relatives. The child is also an heir of the father and the father's relatives when the father-child relationship is legally shown. Once it is, the child ranks with any other child. Nothing in the statute reduces the share.

That makes the proof of paternity the main issue. The statute lists several routes. As we read it, they include:

- the parents later marry or enter a civil union
- a court judgment of paternity, including one under the Illinois Parentage Act of 2015 (750 ILCS 46)
- a signed voluntary acknowledgment of paternity
- clear and convincing evidence that the man is the father

The exact list and wording should be checked in the current statute. If you are in an open estate, the probate court decides whether the child has proved it.

## How can paternity be proved?

There are a few common kinds of proof. A court judgment is the strongest, because it is a legal finding. A voluntary acknowledgment signed under the state's program is also strong. The Illinois Department of Healthcare and Family Services runs the program and explains how acknowledgments work, including a short window to cancel one (hfs.illinois.gov).

When none of these exist, the child must offer evidence. Courts may look at DNA tests, the father's own statements, birth records, money he paid and how he introduced the child. Genetic testing can be ordered, and in some cases relatives are asked to take part. The standard, clear and convincing evidence, is higher than the usual civil case.

| Proof | Who benefits | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Parents married each other | Child | Strong, automatic |
| Court judgment of parentage | Child | Strong, legal finding |
| Signed voluntary acknowledgment | Child | Strong, after the cancel window |
| DNA test plus support and recognition | Child | Possible, must be clear and convincing |
| Birth certificate naming the father, nothing else | Child | Helpful, but may not be enough alone |

## What does a worked example look like?

Dennis, 63, never married. He has one daughter, Tasha, 31, whose mother, Lena, he dated for two years. Dennis paid child support through the state for 12 years and signed an acknowledgment at the hospital. Dennis dies without a will with $240,000 in his name. Because Tasha's paternity is established and he has no spouse, Tasha takes all $240,000.

Change the facts. Dennis never signed anything and paid no support. His mother and brother say he had no children. Tasha has a birth certificate with no father named and a DNA test from a cousin. She must ask the probate court to find that she is Dennis's heir, using clear and convincing evidence. The court may order testing and delay distribution. If she succeeds, she takes all of it ahead of his mother and brother.

## What are the steps for a child who believes they are an heir?

1. Gather what you have: birth certificate, acknowledgment, court orders, letters, photos, records of support.
2. Find out whether an estate has been opened. Search the circuit court clerk's website for the county where the father lived, such as [Cook County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/cook) or [DuPage County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/dupage).
3. Tell the administrator or the lawyer in writing that you claim to be an heir.
4. Ask for the notice of any hearing on heirship.
5. Consider a DNA test through a lab the court will accept.
6. Contact an attorney early. The estate may be on a schedule.

Our page on [proving heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois) explains how a court or an affidavit records the result.

## Can a parent inherit from a child born outside marriage?

The mother can. The father can only if he has acknowledged the child and supported the child as the statute requires. A father who vanished may be excluded, and the child's estate would go to other relatives. This matters in cases of a young adult who dies with a car, a bank account and a wrongful death claim. See [wrongful death and survival claims](/learn/illinois/illinois-wrongful-death-and-survival-claims-for-an-estate).

## How can a parent make this simple?

A parent can sign a voluntary acknowledgment, ask the court for a parentage judgment, and write a will that names the child by name. Beneficiary forms for life insurance and retirement accounts remove any doubt. This avoids a contested hearing and spares the child. Unmarried parents should also read [unmarried partners and inheritance](/learn/illinois/unmarried-partners), since a partner has no rights without documents. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) can help you check your forms.

## What mistakes should families avoid?

Do not assume that a missing name on a birth certificate ends the claim, and do not assume that a name on the certificate settles it either. A father who is named but never acknowledged the child may still be challenged. Do not wait for the estate to close before speaking up, since notice periods run and heirship is often decided early. Do not rely on rumor from family members; ask for records. Estate lawyers also warn against sending a DNA kit sample without a plan: a lab result that the court will not accept does not help. Ask the court or an attorney which labs and chain-of-custody steps are acceptable before you test.

On the other side, if you are the administrator, include every possible heir in your notices. Leaving out a child who later proves paternity can reopen the case and cost the estate time and money.
