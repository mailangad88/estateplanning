Farm families in Illinois often say, "We are not rich, we just have a lot of acres." The Illinois estate tax does not care how the money is held. It looks at the value of what you own at death, and in a state where farmland is expensive, the land by itself can be worth more than the $4 million line.

This page takes the Illinois angle on farm estates. It covers the state tax, the federal farm elections that Illinois law refers to, and what happens to a cash rent lease when the landowner dies. For the family side of the question, who runs the farm and how to treat children who left, read our general guide to [farm succession planning](/learn/business-owners/farm-succession-planning). The wider Illinois context is in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). This is general education, not legal advice.

## Does the Illinois estate tax apply to farms?

Yes. Illinois has no farm exemption of its own. An estate with a gross value over $4,000,000, including certain lifetime gifts, must file Illinois Form 700, whether or not a federal return is due. Our [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) article gives the full rules, including the nine-month due date and the fact that spouses cannot share the exclusion.

Farm estates feel this harder than other estates, for three reasons. Land is counted at market value, not at what it earns. Grain, machinery, livestock and bank accounts all count too. And life insurance owned by the farmer counts toward the total, even though the family bought it to pay the tax.

The Illinois Attorney General's published example of a $5,000,000 estate, all in Illinois, shows an Illinois tax of $285,714 and no federal tax. Use the Attorney General's calculator for a real number. For a farm, the lesson is that being just over the line is expensive.

## What do the numbers look like on a farm?

Take this as an example, with invented values. Walt and June own 500 acres at an assumed $11,000 an acre, the farmhouse, machinery and a grain bin.

| Item | Assumed value |
|---|---|
| 500 acres of cropland | $5,500,000 |
| Farmhouse and yard | $350,000 |
| Machinery and grain bin | $600,000 |
| Stored grain and bank accounts | $400,000 |
| Operating loan | -$250,000 |
| Net estate for the first test | about $6,600,000 |

If Walt dies and leaves everything to June, the marital deduction can defer the tax. When June dies, she has only her own $4 million Illinois exclusion, because Illinois does not allow a spouse to carry over unused exclusion. Her estate is far over the line, with a return due nine months after death. A bypass trust at Walt's death could have used his $4 million. Our [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) page explains that planning step.

Land prices differ a great deal from county to county, so run your own numbers, perhaps with an appraisal. The [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) gives a rough view.

## Does Illinois recognize the federal farm tax elections?

It refers to them. The Illinois Estate and Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax Act defines a qualified heir by reference to section 2032A of the federal code (35 ILCS 405/2). It also creates a special lien on property valued under that election (405/10). A parent or guardian may make the election for a minor or disabled heir (405/12).

In plain terms, special use valuation lets an executor value qualifying farmland by its farm use instead of its highest and best use, with a dollar cap. Heirs who sell the land or stop farming within ten years can owe some of the savings back. Our article on [estate tax elections including farm valuation](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-elections-alternate-valuation-deferral-and-farm-valuation) lays out the federal tests.

The practical point for Illinois is that a farm estate often owes Illinois tax when it owes no federal tax. The federal election has to be made on a federal return, so the executor may need to prepare Form 706 information even when nothing is due federally. Ask your tax adviser early.

## Can the Illinois tax be paid over time?

Sometimes. The Act lets Illinois tax be deferred when the federal tax is deferred, for example under the federal installment election for closely held businesses. Deferred amounts follow the federal schedule. Interest on tax unpaid after the initial due date runs at 10% a year, and at 6% on amounts properly deferred or paid in installments (35 ILCS 405/9). Confirm the current rate with the Attorney General before you plan around it.

The point is to avoid the forced sale. A family that has to sell 80 acres to pay a tax bill, in a bad price year, has lost something the plan should have protected. Life insurance owned in an irrevocable trust, held outside the taxable estate, is the usual cash source.

## What happens to a cash rent lease when the landowner dies?

The lease generally continues. A lease is a contract, and the contract binds the landowner's estate and heirs. The tenant keeps the right to farm the ground under the lease terms, and the rent now belongs to the estate until the land is distributed.

Read the document. These are the questions that matter.

1. When does the lease end, and how is it renewed or ended? Many Illinois cash rent leases run year to year.
2. Is there a death clause, a right of first refusal or an option to buy?
3. Who is the landlord named in the lease, the owner personally, a trust or an LLC?
4. Is the rent paid in one payment or two, and is any rent past due?
5. Who pays property taxes, drainage district assessments and insurance?
6. Is there a written lease at all? Many family farm leases are a handshake.

An oral lease is still a lease, but it is harder to prove and its terms are easier to argue about. If the tenant is a son or a neighbor, a short written lease signed by the executor can end a year of uncertainty. Avoid saying "you can keep farming" or "you are done" to a tenant until the executor has authority and an attorney has read the lease.

## Who collects the rent, and who owns the crop?

The executor, after letters of office, collects the rent and deposits it in the estate account. If the farm is held in a trust, the successor trustee does the same. Rent is income of the estate, and the estate's income tax return reports it. Our page on [Illinois Form IL-1041](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-il-1041-estate-and-trust-income-tax-return) covers that return.

Crops are a separate question. A tenant who planted under a valid lease usually owns the growing crop. Under a crop-share lease, the landowner's share is estate property. Settle the harvest arrangement in writing so a combine does not roll before the question is answered.

## What if the farmer needed nursing home care first?

Farmland that produces income is excluded from countable resources in the Illinois Medicaid rules, as our [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) page explains. The state can still claim against the estate afterward, with a hardship exception for a family farm that was the heirs' main income for at least 12 months before the death. Our [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) article covers the process. Do not give the land away to avoid this without advice, because gifts trigger the look-back period.

## A short checklist for a farm executor

- Find the deed, any land trust agreement and any LLC operating agreement.
- Get a date-of-death appraisal of the land.
- Collect the cash rent lease, crop insurance, and FSA and bank loan papers.
- Total the estate against the $4 million line.
- Ask a tax adviser about federal elections before the return is due.
- Do not sell or lease land to a family member without a written document.

Land held at death also receives a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which matters if the heirs sell soon. And when several children inherit with no plan, you may end up with [heirs property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property), where any co-owner can push for a sale. Our guide to [keeping family land in the family](/learn/property-and-assets/keeping-family-land-in-the-family) shows how entities and agreements prevent that.
