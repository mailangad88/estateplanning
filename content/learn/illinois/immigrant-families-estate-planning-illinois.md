Many families in the Chicago area have one foot in two countries. A house or farm back home. Parents who are getting older. A brother who would be the first person you would call if something went wrong. Then a will question comes up, and nobody is sure whether an Illinois document means anything across an ocean.

This page answers the questions we hear most: what an Illinois will reaches and what it does not, whether you can name a guardian who lives overseas, and what a spouse who is not a U.S. citizen changes. It builds on our wider guide to [estate planning for immigrants and non-citizens](/learn/basics/estate-planning-for-immigrants-and-non-citizens) and sits in our [Illinois estate planning library](/learn/illinois). When you want to talk through your own family, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-immigrant-families-estate-planning-illinois&topic=other). This is general education, not legal advice, and it does not cover the law of any other country.

## Does a U.S. will cover property in my home country?

Usually not on its own. An Illinois will governs what you own here and can say who gets it. Land and bank accounts in another country are generally controlled by the law and the courts of that country, and those rules can be very different from Illinois. Some countries reserve part of an estate for certain relatives no matter what a will says. Others require a local court or notary to confirm who inherits before a title can change.

An Illinois estate planning attorney advises on Illinois and U.S. law, not on the law of other countries. The sensible approach has three parts:

1. Tell your Illinois attorney about the foreign property at the start, so the Illinois will does not conflict with it.
2. Ask a lawyer in that country what happens to the property if you die with no local will, and whether a local will is advisable.
3. If you sign two wills, have both lawyers read both documents so the later one does not cancel the earlier one by accident.

If you do nothing, your family may face a court process in each country. Our page on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains the same problem for property in another U.S. state, and the same logic applies across borders. For the Illinois-to-another-state version, see [owning property in another state](/learn/illinois/illinois-resident-owning-property-in-another-state).

## What should I list before I meet a lawyer?

A one-page inventory by country is more useful than any document you could bring. Our [Immigrant Family Estate Planning Guide](/free/estate-planning-for-immigrants-guide) has a worksheet for this. At minimum, write down:

| What | Where | Whose name is on it | Who knows about it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Home or land | Country and town | Name on the title or deed | |
| Bank or savings account | Country and bank | | |
| Pension or provident fund | Country | | |
| Business share or family property | Country | | |
| Money owed to or by relatives | Country | | |
| Insurance and beneficiary forms | U.S. or abroad | | |

Add one line about each account: is it a joint account, and does it name a beneficiary? In the U.S., a beneficiary form usually overrides your will, so [check every one](/tools/beneficiary-audit) before you sign anything new.

Here is how this goes wrong. Imran, 54, lives in Schaumburg and keeps savings in a bank account in Karachi. (The example is invented.) His Illinois will leaves everything to his wife, Sana. Nobody in Illinois knows about the account, and the bank abroad will not talk to Sana without local paperwork. The money sits for years. A single sheet naming the account, the bank and a relative with the passbook would have saved her that search.

## Can I name a guardian who lives overseas?

You can name anyone you like in your will, but a judge approves the guardian. Illinois lets a parent name a guardian for a minor child in a signed will with two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/11-5), and the judge decides what serves the child's interest. Our page on [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children) lists the qualifications the Probate Act sets, including that the person live in the United States (755 ILCS 5/11-3). A guardian abroad may therefore face extra questions, and the court may be slower to approve. Ask an Illinois attorney how that works in practice before you rely on it.

The safer plan for most families:

- Name a guardian who lives in the U.S. as your first choice or your backup, even if your heart is with a relative abroad.
- Write a letter saying where you would like the children to grow up, what languages and traditions matter, and who they should stay in touch with. Our [guardian letter guide](/learn/guardianship/letter-of-intent-for-your-childrens-guardian) shows how.
- Keep the children's passports, birth certificates and school records in a place the guardian can reach.
- Fill out a [standby or short-term guardian](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children) form if you travel abroad often, so someone here has authority while you are away.

If you are weighing a family member abroad against a friend here, the [guardian picker](/tools/guardian-picker) can help you compare. Our general article on [choosing a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) also covers talking to the person first.

## What changes if my spouse is not a U.S. citizen?

The biggest change is the estate tax marital deduction. Between spouses who are both U.S. citizens, anything left to the survivor is free of federal estate tax. That unlimited deduction generally does not apply to property left outright to a spouse who is not a U.S. citizen. The usual fix is a qualified domestic trust, or QDOT, which defers the tax until principal is taken out. Our page on the [non-citizen spouse and QDOTs](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot) explains the mechanics, including that the rule may not apply if the survivor becomes a citizen before the estate tax return is filed.

Lifetime gifts change too. Citizen spouses can give each other unlimited amounts. For 2026, the first $194,000 of gifts to a non-citizen spouse is not treated as a taxable gift, and the figure is adjusted each year. That page also covers joint title.

Most families will never owe federal estate tax, since the federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026. Illinois is different. It taxes estates worth more than $4 million, and it does not allow a surviving spouse to carry over the late spouse's unused amount. Read [how the Illinois estate tax works](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) if your combined assets, counting property abroad, may approach that number. How foreign property counts and whether another country's tax gets a credit are questions for a tax adviser. Our page on [foreign inheritance and U.S. estate tax for non-citizens](/learn/estate-tax/foreign-inheritance-and-us-estate-tax-for-non-citizens) is the place to begin. The [Non-Citizen Spouse Planning Guide](/free/non-citizen-spouse-planning-guide) has a question list for your appointment.

## What happens if I die without a will?

Illinois law decides who inherits your Illinois property, using a fixed list of relatives. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) shows the order. If your closest relatives live abroad, someone has to be found, identified and, in some cases, reached through another country's offices. That can add months. A will names an executor here and removes the guesswork.

## What should I do this month?

1. Draw up the inventory above, one line per asset, and note where each document is kept.
2. Decide on a guardian here and a backup, and speak to both.
3. Name a financial agent and a health care agent who can act in person in Illinois. See [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
4. Ask a lawyer in the other country whether a local will is advisable.
5. Check beneficiary forms for retirement accounts and life insurance.
6. Put it together in a letter that your family can read, with a translation if needed.
