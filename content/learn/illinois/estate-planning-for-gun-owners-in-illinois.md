You own a deer rifle, your father's shotgun and a handgun in the nightstand. Your will, if you have one, says "everything to my kids." In Illinois that is not enough for guns, because the law cares about who ends up holding them.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is about planning ahead while you are alive. It covers who can receive your guns, what your heirs and executor need, how to word the will, when a gun trust fits, and what goes in a letter of instruction. If someone has already died and left guns behind, read [guns and FOID cards after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-firearms-and-foid-cards-after-a-death) instead. To talk through your own collection, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-for-gun-owners-in-illinois&topic=will). This is general education, not legal advice.

## Who can inherit my guns in Illinois?

Anyone you choose can be named, but only a person who may lawfully possess a firearm can actually take it. Illinois requires a FOID card issued by the Illinois State Police in that person's own name to acquire or possess a firearm (430 ILCS 65/2). Federal law separately bars some people, such as those with certain convictions or protective orders, and our general article on [passing on firearms safely and legally](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning) lists the federal categories.

So when you plan, sort your family into three groups:

- **People who already hold a FOID card.** They are the simplest recipients.
- **People who could get one.** An adult child who has never owned a gun may qualify, but the application takes time, so tell them now.
- **People who cannot or should not hold guns.** A relative with a disqualifying record, a minor, or someone struggling with addiction or depression. Leave them money from a sale, or a different item, instead.

Fairness does not require a gun for everyone; balance with cash or other property. Our page on [dividing personal property and family heirlooms](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms) has ideas for keeping that even.

## Does my heir need a FOID card?

Yes, in the ordinary case. The FOID Act's list of exemptions does not name heirs. Your own card is personal and stops working when you die, so a spouse who lives in the same house with the guns needs a card of their own, just like a son who lives across the state.

When the estate passes a gun under a will or court order, or one relative later gives it to another, Illinois skips the private-sale approval call (430 ILCS 65/3(a-15)). The receiver still has to have a valid card, though, because that requirement sits in a different section of the Act. The after-death page explains the transfer routes in detail.

So ask each person you plan to name whether they have a card, and if not, encourage them to apply early. The Illinois State Police sets the process and fee.

## Can my executor hold my guns?

Your executor can take charge of them as estate property, but holding them in hand is a different question. The FOID Act's exemptions do not name executors or administrators, so an executor who will move, transport or deliver your guns should have a valid card. That affects who you pick.

When you choose an executor, ask three questions:

1. Are they comfortable around firearms and willing to secure them?
2. Do they hold a FOID card, or would they get one?
3. Do they live close enough to check on a gun safe in an empty house?

A daughter who has never touched a gun can still be a good executor. Your will can tell her to leave the guns locked in place, unloaded, until each recipient shows a card, or to use a licensed dealer for storage and sale. Our guides on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [who can be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-can-be-an-executor-in-illinois) cover the rest of the decision.

## Should I list guns in my will?

Yes, if particular guns are meant for particular people. A general gift of "all my personal property" leaves siblings arguing over Grandpa's shotgun. A [specific gift](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) is clearer: "my Winchester lever-action rifle to my son Mark, or if he cannot lawfully receive it, to my nephew Joe."

A few drafting points matter more for guns than for other heirlooms:

- **Add a condition and a backup.** Make each gift conditional on the recipient being able to receive it lawfully, and name who takes it otherwise.
- **Authorize a sale.** Give the executor clear power to sell any gun no eligible person will take, through a licensed dealer, and say where the money goes.
- **Describe, but do not over-describe.** Maker and type are usually enough in the will itself. The serial numbers belong in a private inventory.
- **Deal with a minor.** Name an adult to hold a gun for a child, or leave the child the cash. Our page on [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) explains the money side.

Keep the will formal. Illinois requires a will to be in writing, signed and witnessed by two people (755 ILCS 5/4-3), as our page on [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) explains, so a sticky note on the safe saying "the pistol is for Amy" does not count. Also remember that after a death the original will is filed with the circuit clerk and is generally open for the public to view, as covered in [filing the original will](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk). That is the main reason the combination and the full list stay out of it.

A recipient who does not want a gun can [disclaim it](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois).

## What is a gun trust, and do you need one?

A gun trust is a trust written specifically to own firearms. Owners mostly use one for items regulated under the federal National Firearms Act, such as suppressors and short-barreled rifles. Those items are registered federally and need federal approval before they change hands, even to an heir. A trust can let several named trustees possess the items during your life and hand control to a successor trustee at your death.

It is not a way around the rules. Every trustee who will possess the items must be legally allowed to, which in Illinois includes holding a FOID card, and federal approval still applies. An attorney should draft it against current federal guidance.

Most owners of ordinary rifles, shotguns and handguns do not need one. A will, or a regular [Illinois living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) with firearm instructions, usually does the job, and [funding a living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/funding-a-living-trust-in-illinois) explains how personal property gets into it. The [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) can help you choose.

## What goes in a letter of instruction for your guns?

A [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) is an informal note to your executor. It is not binding, but for firearms it may be the most useful page in your file. Put in it:

- Where each gun is stored, including any in a vehicle, a cabin, a relative's house or a dealer's storage.
- Where the safe key or combination is kept, or which trusted person knows it.
- Where the inventory, receipts and any federal paperwork for regulated items are filed.
- The name of a licensed dealer you trust for an appraisal or sale.
- A reminder to the executor not to let anyone take a gun home until the transfer is lawful.

A [letter of instruction template](/free/letter-of-instruction-template) gives you a place to start.

## What if you can no longer handle your guns safely?

Plan for illness too. Write down who should take the guns into safekeeping if you develop dementia. Your agent under an [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) still has to follow the same firearm rules, including the FOID card requirement. See [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

## Checklist for Illinois gun owners

| Step | What to do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | List each gun with maker, model, caliber and serial number, plus photos | The executor can account for every item |
| Check eligibility | Ask each intended recipient whether they hold a FOID card | A gift to someone without one cannot be delivered |
| Name backups | Add a second recipient for every gun | The gift still works if the first person cannot take it |
| Authorize a sale | Let the executor sell through a licensed dealer | Gives a lawful exit when no one can or wants to keep a gun |
| Choose the executor | Pick someone comfortable securing firearms | They will hold the key to the safe |
| Separate the secrets | Keep the combination and serial list outside the will | A filed will is generally public |
| Flag special items | Note anything federally regulated or unusual | Those items need an attorney before anyone moves them |
| Review | Update after a purchase, a sale or a change in a recipient's life | An old list sends the executor looking for guns you sold |

A loss or theft must be reported to local police within 48 hours of discovering it, so an up-to-date inventory also protects your executor if a gun turns out to be missing. The [gun owners estate checklist](/free/gun-owners-estate-checklist) and our wider [Illinois estate planning checklist](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-checklist-illinois) cover the steps that go with it.

## A worked example: Ray and his three children

This is an invented example. Ray, 68, lives in Bloomington and owns two deer rifles, a shotgun, a handgun and his late father's old revolver. His will leaves everything equally to his three children.

His son Tom hunts and has a FOID card. His daughter Carla lives in Champaign and has never owned a gun. His youngest, Ben, has a conviction that bars him from possessing firearms.

Ray meets with an Illinois estate planning attorney and changes the plan:

- His new will leaves the rifles and shotgun to Tom, with his grandson Eli as backup once Eli is an adult with a card.
- Carla gets her grandfather's revolver if she obtains a FOID card within a set time after Ray's death. If she does not, the executor sells it through a licensed dealer and gives Carla the money.
- The handgun is to be sold, and the proceeds go to Ben. The will says plainly that no firearm goes to him.
- Tom is named executor, because he has a card and lives nearby.
- Ray writes a letter of instruction naming the dealer he uses, and keeps the serial number list with it, not in the will.

When Ray dies, Tom knows where the safe is, which gun goes where and what to sell, and no one receives a gun they cannot lawfully hold.
