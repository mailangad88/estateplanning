The call came this morning. Your mother's hospital stay is ending, rehab days have run out, and the social worker says she will go to a nursing home by Thursday. The admissions office has emailed forty pages and a line that says "responsible party." Your mother never signed a power of attorney, and you do not know what your name on that line will mean.

This page covers the Illinois side of that moment: who can sign what, which words to avoid and what to do when nobody has legal authority. It is general education, not legal advice. For help sorting out your own facts, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-hospital-no-power-of-attorney&topic=parent). The wider picture is in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## The short version

- Check first whether your parent can understand and agree. If yes, they sign and you help.
- If not, the Health Care Surrogate Act decides who speaks for care. It does not give you control of money.
- Never sign as a personal guarantor of the bill. Sign as a representative, strike personal-liability language and initial it.
- Ask the nursing home and the hospital for the papers a day ahead. A discharge date creates pressure, not a legal deadline for your signature.
- If money cannot move, the long-term fix is a court order or a power of attorney your parent can still sign.

## What does "responsible party" actually mean?

It means whatever the contract says it means. Nursing homes use the phrase for at least three different things.

| What the form says | What it usually means | Risk to you |
|---|---|---|
| Contact person for notices and calls | You get the mail and updates | Low |
| Agrees to use the resident's income and assets to pay | You handle the bill from your parent's money | Low, if you use it properly |
| Agrees to be "personally liable," "jointly and severally liable" or a "guarantor" | You promise to pay from your own funds | High |

The paragraph above the signature line matters more than the heading. Our page on [whether a nursing home can make your children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) explains why federal rules for facilities that take Medicare or Medicaid generally do not allow a facility to require a personal promise to pay as a condition of admission. The rule bars the requirement. It does not stop you from voluntarily signing one, and a signature you chose can bind you. The healthcare directives library has a section-by-section look at [what an agent signs and what to refuse](/learn/healthcare-directives/can-a-healthcare-agent-sign-nursing-home-admission).

Illinois nursing homes are licensed under the Nursing Home Care Act (210 ILCS 45), and residents have rights under it and under federal law. The [Illinois nursing home admission and rights checklist](/free/illinois-nursing-home-admission-and-rights-checklist) walks through a packet line by line.

## Who can consent to the move when there is no power of attorney?

That depends on whether your parent can still decide. Run through these in order.

1. **Your parent has capacity.** Capacity is the ability to understand the choice and what it means. Illinois has no score that removes it, and a diagnosis alone does not decide it. If your mother understands that she is going to a facility for care, she can consent, and she can also sign a power of attorney today. If that is possible, call an attorney before Thursday. Our page on [dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney) explains how capacity is judged and how a signing is done.
2. **Your parent lacks capacity, and no agent exists.** The Health Care Surrogate Act applies. A physician finds the patient cannot decide, and the provider works down the list: guardian of the person, spouse, adult child, parent, adult sibling, adult grandchild, close friend (755 ILCS 40/25). Children at the same rank decide by majority if they disagree. [Who decides in Illinois when there is no directive](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides) covers the list and its limits, and the [surrogate order chart](/free/illinois-health-care-surrogate-order-chart) puts it on one page.
3. **Nobody on the list can serve, or the family is divided.** That is the path toward [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship).

Notice what the Act is about. It covers health care decisions. Whether a particular facility will accept a surrogate's signature on an admission contract is a business decision the facility makes, and many do accept it, particularly when the hospital's discharge team has documented the surrogate. Ask the admissions office what they will rely on, and ask them to write it down.

## What can a surrogate not do?

A surrogate cannot step into your parent's financial life. The statute that ranks surrogates is a health care statute. It does not authorize access to bank accounts, sale of a house or signing a Medicaid application as if you held a power of attorney.

That gap is where families get stuck. Your mother's care is arranged, but her Social Security goes to a checking account only she can use, and the nursing home bills monthly. Three realistic routes:

- **She can still sign.** An Illinois power of attorney for property needs her signature, one witness and a notary for the statutory short form (755 ILCS 45/3-3). See the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
- **Benefits can be redirected.** Social Security can name a representative payee for her benefits. That covers only that income.
- **A court appoints someone.** Guardianship of the estate requires a petition, a physician's report, notice and a hearing. A judge can appoint a temporary guardian when the situation is urgent, but that order expires within 60 days or sooner if a regular guardian is appointed (755 ILCS 5/11a-4).

If Medicaid will be needed, the application and the rules about transfers are separate subjects. Start with [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning), and see [paying while waiting for Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/paying-for-a-nursing-home-while-waiting-for-medicaid) if a gap is likely.

## A worked example

Example only. Carol, 79, breaks a hip and is moved from a Peoria hospital to rehab. After three weeks, rehab coverage ends and the team recommends long-term nursing care. Her son Dev has the admission packet at 4 p.m. on Wednesday, with a Friday discharge.

Dev does these things. He asks the hospital's physician whether Carol can make this decision, and the answer is that she follows the plan but cannot manage her money. He sits with Carol and reads her the choice of facility. She agrees and signs the consent herself. In the financial section, he finds "Responsible Party agrees to be personally liable for all charges." He crosses it out, writes "signing as Carol's contact; resident's funds to pay" and asks the business manager to initial. The facility accepts the change. Next, he calls an attorney that afternoon. Carol, on a good morning, can still understand a power of attorney, and a signing is arranged with the attorney alone with her.

Had Carol been unable to decide, the path would have been different. The attending physician would document her incapacity, the hospital would identify Dev as surrogate (an adult child, if she has no spouse), and the money question would have gone to the guardianship steps above.

## What should you do in the next 48 hours?

1. Ask the discharge planner for the facility's admission agreement today, not at the door.
2. Ask the physician in plain words: can my parent decide this, and where is that written?
3. Find out who the hospital treats as decision-maker, and make sure they have your current phone number.
4. Look for old papers: a power of attorney, a living will, a trust. Check the home, safe deposit box and the attorney who drew up any will. A document signed years ago may already solve this.
5. Read the payment paragraphs. Strike personal-liability language, write "signing as resident's representative only," and initial.
6. Photograph every page you sign. Keep the copy.
7. Ask whether the facility accepts Medicare or Medicaid, because that affects what it can require.
8. Book an attorney conversation about the power of attorney, or the court route if one is needed.

The [hospital discharge planning checklist](/free/hospital-discharge-planning-checklist) has the questions to ask the discharge team, in order.

## Common mistakes

- **Signing in a hurry because of the discharge date.** A hospital has its own pressure, but you are not required to accept a contract the same afternoon.
- **Signing the parent's name.** It is not a shortcut, and it can create problems for you later.
- **Moving money into your name to pay the bill.** It can create Medicaid penalties and exposes the funds to your creditors. Our guide to [dementia without a power of attorney](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney) covers why.
- **Assuming a sibling agrees.** If you are signing and a sister is not, tell her in writing. Disagreement between children at the same rank can end in court.
