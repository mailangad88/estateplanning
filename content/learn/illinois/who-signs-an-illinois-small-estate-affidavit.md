When a parent dies with a bank account and no house, the family often agrees that "one of us" will handle the paperwork. In Illinois, that person usually signs a small estate affidavit. The choice sounds like a favor, but the signature carries real responsibility.

This page is about the signer, not the form. For the rules on the dollar limit and what the affidavit covers, read our [Illinois small estate affidavit overview](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit). Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links the rest of the process, and our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) shows how other states compare. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Who is allowed to sign an Illinois small estate affidavit?

The affiant is the person who is entitled to the property or who is collecting it for those who are. In practice that means an heir under Illinois intestacy law or a person who receives property under the will. The affidavit form asks for the affiant's name and contact details, then lists the decedent's family and the people who take, with their shares. Who counts as an heir depends on whether there was a will. Our article on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) shows the order.

The statute is 755 ILCS 5/25-1. Each bank or other holder decides whether the person in front of it fits the statute, so a stranger to the family will almost always be turned away. A friend, a landlord or a funeral director cannot use the affidavit to collect the money for themselves.

## Which family member should sign?

Choose the person who is organized, lives close, and will keep records. The signer does not need to be the oldest child or the largest heir.

| Question | Better choice | Riskier choice |
|---|---|---|
| Can they track every payment? | Keeps a ledger and receipts | Pays bills in cash |
| Do the heirs trust them? | All heirs agree in writing | One heir is angry or silent |
| Where do they live? | Illinois, near the bank | Out of state, hard to serve |
| Do they have debts or lawsuits? | Stable finances | Judgments could reach the money they hold |
| Are they also a creditor of the estate? | No conflict | Paid themselves first |

A written family agreement helps. One page can name the signer, say how costs will be repaid, and promise a final accounting. It does not replace the affidavit, but it prevents the "you never told me" fights.

## What exactly does the signer swear to?

The signer swears, in front of a notary, to the facts the statute lists. As described in the statute and on the court-published forms, these include the following.

- The decedent's name, date of death and residence, with a death certificate attached.
- That no letters of office are outstanding and no petition for letters is pending or planned.
- That the personal estate is within the statutory limit, which is currently $150,000 not counting registered vehicles.
- The decedent's surviving spouse and children, and the heirs or legatees with their shares.
- How the debts will be classified and paid.

The signer is promising that each answer is true. If a second bank account turns up later, or an heir was left off, the affidavit was wrong, and the signer answers for that. Our article on [proving heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois) covers how to confirm the list of heirs first.

## What personal liability does the signer take on?

The signer agrees to pay valid claims and to make creditors, heirs and legatees whole if they lose money because of the signer's acts or omissions. Reasonable attorney fees can be added. Nothing in probate court would normally protect the signer, because no judge reviews the work and no creditor claims period starts.

Here is a worked example. Marisol, 52, signs for her late mother, Elena, whose only asset is a $48,000 checking account. Marisol pays $9,500 for the funeral, then splits the remaining money with her brother, $19,250 each. Three months later a hospital bill for $14,000 arrives, which is a valid claim. Marisol did not pay it. The hospital can pursue Marisol for the amount, and her brother can be asked to return part of his share. If the funeral and the hospital bill had been paid in the right order first, the split would have been smaller but safe. Our article on [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) explains the priority list the signer must follow.

## How does the signer actually do the job?

1. Get the family to agree on who signs, and put it in writing.
2. List every asset and every debt you know about, with date-of-death values.
3. Confirm the total is within the limit and no probate case is open or planned.
4. Order several certified death certificates.
5. Complete the affidavit with the heirs and shares, and sign before a notary.
6. Give a copy to each holder and wait for the funds to be released.
7. Pay the funeral bill and other valid claims in the order the statute requires.
8. Distribute the rest, keep a ledger, and send each heir a short written summary.

The [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) can help with step 3. Banks usually have their own preferences, which our article on [using the affidavit at a bank or brokerage](/learn/illinois/using-an-illinois-small-estate-affidavit-at-a-bank) walks through.

## When should the family skip the affidavit and open probate?

Open probate when an heir is missing, when the family disagrees, when the debts may exceed the assets, or when the total is close to the limit. Probate adds cost but gives the representative a court order and a creditor claims period that ends the risk. If the estate is just over the limit, see [what to do when an Illinois estate is just over $150,000](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-just-over-the-small-estate-limit).

Do not sign if the signer would be both the main creditor and the person judging the claims. A court-supervised process is cleaner. Our article on [the cheapest way to settle an estate in Illinois](/learn/probate/cheapest-way-to-settle-an-estate-in-illinois-or-new-jersey) compares the routes.

## Can the signer be reimbursed?

Yes, for real costs such as the death certificates, the notary and the funeral bill, which are high-priority claims. Keep receipts. Paying yourself a fee out of the account without the other heirs' written agreement invites a dispute.
