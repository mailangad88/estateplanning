Your father signed a power of attorney years ago, and you have been the agent ever since. You paid his bills, talked to his bank and handled the insurance company. Then he dies on a Thursday, and by Friday the bank teller wants to know who you are. The honest answer is that you are not his agent anymore.

This page explains what changes in Illinois the moment a person dies: who handles the money, who decides about the body and the funeral, what an agent may still do, and what to do in the first week. It is general education, not legal advice, and it belongs to our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). The national version of this question is covered in [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end). If you want an attorney's help with a specific estate, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-does-a-power-of-attorney-end-at-death-in-illinois&topic=probate).

## The short version

- The power of attorney stops at death. A durable form survives incapacity, not death.
- Money now follows the will and the court. The executor, or an administrator if there is no will, takes over after the court issues letters of office.
- The funeral follows a different statute with a different ranked list.
- The former agent keeps duties: records, honesty and, if asked, an accounting.

## Does an Illinois power of attorney end at death?

Yes. The Illinois [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) ends when the principal dies, along with revocation, an end date stated in the form, or the loss of every available agent. The word "durable" does not change that. It only means the form keeps working if the principal loses capacity. After death, the agent cannot sign a check, withdraw cash, sell a car, or call the broker. The [revoking page](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney) lists the other ways an agency ends.

The same is true of a health care power of attorney in the sense that matters here. Its job is decisions about care for a living patient. What happens to the body is a different legal question, covered below.

## Who handles the money after death?

The executor named in the will, once the court has admitted the will and issued letters of office. If there is no will, the court appoints an administrator. Until then, sole-name accounts tend to be restricted, because the bank needs someone with court authority. The [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) walks through the steps.

Authority before letters is narrow. A named executor may carry out a gift of the body, arrange the burial, pay necessary funeral charges and preserve the estate (755 ILCS 5/6-14). That is the list. Selling the house, closing accounts and paying heirs are not on it. Anyone holding the will must file it with the circuit court clerk immediately upon the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1).

If you are the person named, you do not have to accept. You have 30 days after learning of the appointment to start probate or formally decline (755 ILCS 5/6-3). The page on [refusing to be executor](/learn/illinois/can-you-refuse-to-be-executor-in-illinois) explains both paths.

### Does everything have to go through probate?

No. Money that passes by beneficiary form or joint ownership never goes through the executor. Joint accounts and payable-on-death accounts pass to the survivor or the named beneficiary, as the page on [POD and joint bank accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) explains. If the only thing left is modest personal property, the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) can release it without a court case. As of 2026 the personal property limit is $150,000, not counting registered motor vehicles, and the affidavit does not reach real estate. The [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) can help you sort the list.

Once letters issue, the executor needs a place for the money. [Opening an estate bank account in Illinois](/learn/illinois/opening-an-estate-bank-account-in-illinois) covers letters, the estate EIN and what banks ask for.

## Who decides about the body and the funeral?

A separate statute does. The Illinois Disposition of Remains Act ranks the people who control burial or cremation (755 ILCS 65/5). In order, they are:

1. A person named in a written instrument that meets the Act's requirements.
2. An executor or legal representative, following written instructions in the will.
3. The surviving spouse.
4. Surviving adult children, by majority.
5. Surviving competent parents.
6. The nearest adult next of kin, by majority.

Further ranks cover organizations and public officials. Directions in a will must be carried out immediately, without waiting for probate (755 ILCS 65/40). A health care power of attorney can carry disposition powers, so read it before the funeral home meeting. An agent under a property power of attorney is not on the list just for being the agent. Our page on the [Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act) has the full table, and the [Illinois funeral rights and costs kit](/free/illinois-funeral-rights-and-costs-kit) is a useful companion.

Say Ray dies. His daughter Dana was his property agent, and his son Tom lives in Tucson. Ray left no funeral instructions. Dana and Tom are his surviving adult children, so a majority decides, and with two children that means they must agree. Dana's power of attorney gives her nothing here. If Ray had named Dana in a notarized disposition form, she would have ranked first.

The people with the right also carry responsibility for the reasonable costs, and written directions are carried out only so far as the estate or the controlling person can afford it (755 ILCS 65/40). Keep the funeral receipt. The estate can repay it, because funeral and burial costs sit in the first payment class (755 ILCS 5/18-10), as explained in [paying for a funeral from an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/paying-for-a-funeral-from-an-illinois-estate).

## What may the former agent still do?

Very little, and none of it involves spending. Here is how it breaks down.

| The former agent | After death |
|---|---|
| Pay bills from the principal's accounts | No. No authority remains. |
| Withdraw cash, even for the funeral | No. Pay personally and be repaid later. |
| Sign for the principal on a deed or title | No. The executor or the court process does this. |
| Keep and hand over records | Yes. This is a duty, not a power. |
| Give an accounting | Yes, if the executor or a successor asks (755 ILCS 45/2-7). |
| Notify the bank of the death | Yes. Anyone may tell a bank. |
| Keep the original power of attorney | Yes, and give a copy to the executor. |

The Act requires the agent to keep records of receipts, disbursements and significant actions, and to provide a copy on request to the personal representative or successors in interest after death. An agent who violates the Act must restore the property to the value it would have had, and repay attorney's fees paid on the agent's behalf (755 ILCS 45/2-7). That is why a tidy ledger is the former agent's best protection.

An agent also could not make gifts, change beneficiaries or amend the will or a trust unless the form gave that authority (755 ILCS 45/3-4). A transfer made before death that the form did not allow is something an executor can question.

## What should you do in the first week?

Do these in order, and stop when you hit something that needs a court.

1. **Stop using the power of attorney.** Do not sign anything as agent from the moment of death.
2. **Secure the originals.** Find the will, the power of attorney, insurance policies, account statements and the deed. Keep them where they are safe.
3. **File the will.** Whoever has the original will files it with the circuit court clerk for the county where the person lived (755 ILCS 5/6-1).
4. **Order certified death certificates.** The [Illinois death certificate guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph) says who can order. Each bank and insurer will want one.
5. **Contact the funeral home** and confirm who controls the arrangements under Section 5 of the Disposition of Remains Act.
6. **Tell the bank,** in writing. The [letter to the bank template](/free/letter-to-bank-after-a-death-template) helps. Do not move money.
7. **Pause the autopay and the mail.** Check what is paid automatically, but do not cancel insurance on a house before someone has authority.
8. **Write down what you did as agent,** from the last statement to the date of death. A one-page ledger is enough to begin.
9. **Decide who is the executor,** and whether that person will serve. Talk to an attorney if the family disagrees.

## What if the family disagrees about the funeral or the money?

Disputes between people ranked in Section 5 of the Disposition of Remains Act go to court, and the Act directs resolution within 30 days after the dispute is filed (755 ILCS 65/50). Money disputes run through the estate. An interested person can ask the court for a hearing on a specific issue during independent administration (755 ILCS 5/28-5). Our page on [executor personal liability](/learn/illinois/executor-personal-liability-in-illinois) covers what happens when an executor or former agent is questioned.

If a sibling thinks the agent took money before the death, ask for the records first. The accounting right in Section 2-7 exists for that reason. If the principal had capacity and the agent acted within the form, there may be nothing wrong. If not, take it to an attorney quickly.
