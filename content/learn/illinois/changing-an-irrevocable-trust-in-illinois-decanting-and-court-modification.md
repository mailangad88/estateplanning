"Irrevocable" sounds final, and in a sense it is. The person who made the trust cannot simply take the assets back or rewrite it. But trusts are written years before they are used, and families change, tax law changes and trustees move away. Illinois built several ways to adjust a trust without starting from scratch.

This page walks through the three that matter, decanting, settlement agreements and court modification, and when each fits. It is general education, not legal advice. For the background on what an irrevocable trust is, read our [irrevocable trusts guide](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts). The wider state picture is in the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## How can you change an irrevocable trust in Illinois?

Pick the route by the size of the change:

| Route | Who acts | Court needed? | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decanting (Art. 12) | A non-settlor trustee with discretion | Usually no | Updating terms by pouring into a new trust |
| Nonjudicial settlement agreement (s. 111) | Interested people, often all beneficiaries and the trustee | No, unless ending the trust | Administrative fixes, trustee changes, moving the trust |
| Court modification (ss. 411-416) | A trustee, beneficiary or the Attorney General petitions | Yes | Changes beneficiaries disagree on, or unforeseen events |

Before any of them, read the trust itself. The trust can limit these tools, and some of the Code's rules cannot be overridden. Section 105 makes the court's power to modify or terminate under sections 411 through 416 mandatory. A trust cannot take that away.

## What is decanting, and how does it work in Illinois?

Decanting means a trustee pours some or all of a trust into a second trust with updated terms, much as wine moves from one vessel to another. Illinois covers this in Article 12 of the Trust Code (760 ILCS 3/1201 to 1227). It applies to express irrevocable trusts, and to revocable trusts only with the consent of the right person. It does not apply to trusts held solely for charitable purposes.

The person who decants must be an "authorized fiduciary," meaning a trustee who is not the settlor and who has discretion to distribute principal to current beneficiaries. There are two levels of power:

- **Expanded discretion (s. 1211).** If the trustee's discretion is not tied to a standard such as health, education, maintenance and support, the trustee has wider freedom in designing the second trust.
- **Limited discretion (s. 1212).** If a standard limits the trustee, the second trust must give each beneficiary interests substantially similar to those in the first. Partial decanting is allowed.

### What notice does decanting require?

Written notice at least 60 days before the exercise (760 ILCS 3/1207). It goes to the living settlor, qualified beneficiaries, anyone who can remove or replace the fiduciary, other fiduciaries, and the Attorney General's charitable bureau if a charity has an interest. The notice must describe how the power will be used and the proposed effective date, and enclose copies of the first and second trust instruments. Everyone entitled to notice can sign a waiver to shorten the wait. Receiving notice does not stop a person from going to court later.

### What cannot be decanted away?

Several limits protect beneficiaries and tax results.

- A second trust generally cannot add current beneficiaries, or remainder beneficiaries who were not already in the first trust, and cannot reduce or eliminate a vested interest (s. 1211).
- The second trust cannot defeat tax benefits the first trust was built to get, such as the marital or charitable deduction, the gift tax annual exclusion, S corporation status or a zero generation-skipping tax inclusion ratio (s. 1219).
- A trustee cannot raise their own compensation in the second trust beyond the first trust's terms without the qualified beneficiaries' signed consent or court approval (s. 1216).
- If the original instrument prohibits decanting, that controls, although a general spendthrift clause or ban on amendment does not by itself block it (s. 1215).
- The trustee has no duty to decant, and still owes fiduciary duties while doing it (s. 1204).

Imagine Walter's irrevocable trust for his grandson Eli, written in 2005 and directing the trustee to hold everything until Eli turns 40. The trust contains an outdated provision that blocks the trustee from changing investment advisers. A trustee with expanded discretion could decant into a new trust that fixes the advisers clause and keeps Eli's interest intact. What the trustee could not do is cut Eli out or give a cousin a share. For a plain-English look at the concept and at trust protectors, see [what a trust protector and decanting are](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-trust-protector-and-decanting).

## What is a nonjudicial settlement agreement?

It is a written agreement among interested people that settles a trust matter without a judge. Section 111 lists what it can cover, including:

1. Interpretation or construction of the trust terms.
2. Approval of a trustee's report or accounting.
3. Exercising or not exercising a trustee power, or granting administrative powers.
4. Appointing or removing a trustee, advisor or protector.
5. Trustee compensation, liability or indemnity.
6. Moving the trust's principal place of administration, including the governing law.
7. Modifying administrative terms.
8. Ending the trust, but only with court approval.

A valid agreement binds the trustee and all current and future beneficiaries as if a court had ordered it (760 ILCS 3/111(e)). Any beneficiary or interested person can ask the court to approve it, and that petition is due within 60 days after the agreement takes effect. If a charity has an interest, the Attorney General must get written notice at least 60 days before the effective date. A trust can opt out by expressly naming section 111.

This route works well for the quiet fixes. Say three siblings are beneficiaries and their trustee aunt wants to resign. They agree on a successor and sign. Compare that with [removing a trustee](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-remove-a-trustee) against the trustee's will, which usually means a petition. Our article on [the trustee's duty to inform beneficiaries](/learn/illinois/illinois-trustee-duty-to-inform-beneficiaries) matters here too, because the people you need to sign must know the trust exists.

## When can a court modify an irrevocable trust?

A trustee, a beneficiary or, for a charitable interest, the Attorney General can petition (760 ILCS 3/410). Several grounds exist.

- **Consent of all beneficiaries (s. 411).** A court may modify a noncharitable irrevocable trust if every beneficiary consents and the change is not inconsistent with a material purpose. Ending it needs a finding that continuing is not necessary to achieve any material purpose. A spendthrift clause is a factor but does not bar the change.
- **Unanticipated circumstances (s. 412).** The court may change terms, or end the trust, if circumstances the settlor did not foresee mean a change would further the trust's purposes. Administrative terms can be changed if they are impracticable or wasteful.
- **Mistake (s. 415).** Reformation to match the settlor's intent requires clear and convincing evidence.
- **Tax or benefits goals (s. 416).** A court can modify to achieve the settlor's tax objectives or to qualify the beneficiary for government benefits, if consistent with the settlor's probable intent.
- **Small trusts (s. 414).** A trustee may end a trust holding less than $100,000 after notice, if costs would substantially impair its purpose; the court has broader power with no dollar figure.

The "material purpose" test is where most disputes land. A trust that keeps money away from a spendthrift son until age 35 may serve a purpose even if every adult beneficiary agrees to end it early. A judge decides, not the family.

## What should you do first?

Work through this list.

1. Get the full trust instrument and every amendment.
2. Check for clauses that bar decanting, ban section 111 agreements or name a protector with power to amend.
3. List the qualified beneficiaries and any charity with an interest.
4. Decide the goal: a drafting fix, a new trustee, a tax result or a distribution change.
5. Match the goal to a route and get an attorney's view before notices go out.

If a dispute over the trust's validity is brewing, read our companion piece on [contesting a trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/contesting-a-trust-in-illinois-who-can-sue-grounds-and-deadlines), because changing the trust does not erase a pending claim.
