Your spouse's estate is one job. Your own plan is another, and it is easy to put off because nothing about it feels urgent. But the will, powers of attorney and beneficiary forms you signed as a couple were built around a person who is no longer here. Each one now points at an empty seat.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), covers that second job: redoing your own plan as a widow or widower, in order, over the first year. For the first weeks after the death, see [frozen accounts, bills and the house](/learn/illinois/spouse-died-in-illinois-frozen-accounts-bills-and-the-house), and for the general picture see [updating your plan after your spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies). When you are ready to have your documents read, you can [book a plan review](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-after-your-spouse-dies-in-illinois&topic=review).

## What in your own plan stopped working when your spouse died?

Almost everything that named your spouse first. Married couples usually sign mirror plans: each spouse leaves everything to the other, names the other as executor, and names the other as agent for money and health decisions. When one dies, the survivor's documents still exist, but the first name in every role is gone.

| Document | What it probably says now | What usually needs to happen |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Everything to your spouse, spouse as executor | New will naming who inherits and a new executor |
| Power of attorney for property | Spouse as agent | New agent and at least one successor |
| Health care power of attorney | Spouse as agent | New agent, successor, and HIPAA access |
| Retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities | Spouse as primary beneficiary | New primary and contingent beneficiaries |
| Payable on death and transfer on death accounts | Spouse as beneficiary | New beneficiaries, or a deliberate choice to let them fall to the estate |
| House | Was joint, now yours alone | Decide on a TODI, a trust, or a will and probate |
| Joint living trust | May split into sub-trusts at the first death | Attorney reads the terms before you change anything |

## Does your old will still work after your spouse dies?

It is still valid, but it may not do what you want. A will does not lapse because a beneficiary died first. Instead, the gift to your spouse fails and the will looks for a backup. Most mirror wills say "to my spouse, or if my spouse does not survive me, to my children in equal shares." If yours does, the will may still send your estate roughly where you want it.

Problems show up when the backups are stale. Maybe the will names a guardian for children who are now adults, a brother who has since died, or a child you would now treat differently. Under Illinois default rules, a lapsed gift to a descendant passes to that person's descendants, and other lapsed gifts fall into the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate). If the residue itself went only to your spouse, the will may fail entirely and the intestacy rules take over (755 ILCS 5/4-11). Our page on [lapsed gifts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/children-born-after-an-illinois-will-and-lapsed-gifts) walks through those rules.

The usual fix is a new will rather than a patch. It names your new executor, the people who now inherit, and backups for each. It must be signed and witnessed under Illinois formalities (755 ILCS 5/4-3); [changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois) explains when a codicil is enough and when it is not.

## Who should be your agent on both powers of attorney now?

Choose this first, because these documents protect you while you are alive. If your spouse was your only agent on your [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property), no one has authority to pay your bills if you have a stroke next month.

Illinois lets you name [successor agents](/glossary/successor-agent) who step in when the first agent dies, resigns, becomes incapacitated or declines to serve (755 ILCS 45/2-10.3). The statutory short forms allow successors acting one at a time, not co-agents acting together. Our page on [co-agents and successor agents in Illinois](/learn/illinois/co-agents-and-successor-agents-in-illinois) explains the difference. If your old form listed a child as successor, that child may already be your agent, but a fresh form makes that clear to banks.

Do the same on your [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney). Without a named agent, Illinois falls back on a statutory list of surrogates, explained in [who decides under the Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides). Your adult children could then share that role and disagree.

## Which beneficiary forms still name your spouse?

Probably more than you think. Check your IRAs, 401(k) or 403(b), any pension with a death benefit, life insurance, annuities, and every bank or brokerage account with a payable on death or transfer on death beneficiary. These forms pass money outside your will, and they override it.

Two points are easy to miss. If you rolled your spouse's IRA into your own, the new account needs its own beneficiary form; see [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries). Also name a [contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary) on every form, because your new primary beneficiary could also die before you. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) and the free [beneficiary designation audit](/free/beneficiary-designation-audit) help you list every account in one place.

## What should you do about the house now that it is in your name?

Start by confirming the title. If you held the house as joint tenants or as tenants by the entirety, it passed to you at the death, and you usually record proof of death with the county recorder to clear your spouse's name. Our page on [removing a deceased owner from a deed](/learn/illinois/how-to-remove-a-deceased-owner-from-a-deed-in-illinois) covers that, and [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains how the deed had to read.

Once the house is in your name alone, survivorship is gone. If you do nothing, the house goes through probate at your death. Two common ways to avoid that:

- **A transfer on death instrument.** Under 755 ILCS 27, a [TODI](/glossary/transfer-on-death-instrument) names who receives the house at your death. It must be signed, witnessed by two credible witnesses, notarized and recorded before you die. See [the Illinois TODI](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).
- **A revocable living trust.** You deed the house to yourself as trustee, and a successor trustee can manage it if you cannot. See [putting your house in a living trust](/learn/illinois/how-to-put-your-house-in-a-living-trust-in-illinois).

The side-by-side comparison lives in [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house). Adding a child to the deed is the shortcut many widowed parents consider; [should I add my child to my deed](/learn/illinois/should-i-add-my-child-to-my-deed-illinois) explains why it often causes more trouble than it saves.

## Is your estate now closer to the Illinois estate tax line?

It may be. Illinois taxes estates over $4,000,000 (35 ILCS 405/2), and the Illinois Attorney General's fact sheet says the federal carry-over of a spouse's unused exemption, called [portability](/glossary/portability), does not apply to the Illinois tax. Federal portability can add your spouse's unused federal exclusion to your own $15 million federal figure for 2026, but Illinois ignores it. You now have one $4 million threshold, not two.

That matters because a couple's assets are often in your name now: the house, your spouse's IRA rolled into yours, life insurance paid to you, and joint accounts. The [Illinois estate tax worksheet](/learn/illinois/will-my-estate-owe-illinois-estate-tax) shows what counts. If your spouse's plan set up a [credit shelter trust](/glossary/credit-shelter-trust), assets in it generally stay out of your estate; [credit shelter trusts and the QTIP election](/learn/illinois/illinois-qtip-election-and-credit-shelter-trusts) explains how. If it did not, a [disclaimer](/glossary/disclaimer) of part of what you inherited may still be possible, but it is irrevocable and is lost if you accept the property first (755 ILCS 5/2-7). See [disclaiming an inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois) before you move or spend inherited assets.

## What about your property tax exemptions?

Check them in the first year. The Cook County Assessor describes the homeowner and senior exemptions as renewing on their own, while the senior freeze must be filed every year. If your spouse's age was what qualified the household for the senior exemption and you are under 65, you may lose it until you turn 65. Household income for the freeze also changes after a death. Our page on [property tax exemptions after a spouse dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies) has the steps, and [the homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death) covers the separate creditor rule.

## Example: how one widow worked through the year

Example (invented names). Joan, 71, lives in Naperville. Her husband Frank died in February. Their mirror wills left everything to each other, then to their two sons. Frank was Joan's agent on both powers of attorney.

By April, Joan had signed new statutory short form powers of attorney naming her son Tom as agent and her son Luis as successor. In June, she listed what she now owned: the house at about $650,000, her own IRA at $400,000, Frank's IRA rolled into hers at $900,000, a joint brokerage account at $700,000, $500,000 of life insurance paid to her, and $450,000 in savings. The total was about $3.6 million, all in her name. Frank's plan had no credit shelter trust.

At her review in September, her attorney pointed out that market growth could push her past $4 million. Joan signed a new will and a living trust, deeded the house into the trust, and named both sons on every beneficiary form with grandchildren as backups. She also called the assessor about her senior freeze filing.

## What should you do first, and what can wait?

A common order follows. Your timing may differ.

**Months 1 to 3**
1. Find your own will, powers of attorney and beneficiary forms, and note every place your spouse is named.
2. Do not sell the house, retitle accounts or spend inherited funds until you know whether a disclaimer is worth considering.
3. Sign new powers of attorney for property and health care, plus a HIPAA release.

**Months 3 to 6**
4. Record proof of death on the house title, and retitle joint accounts into your name.
5. Update beneficiary forms on retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities and payable on death accounts, with contingents.
6. Check your property tax exemptions, and file the senior freeze if you use it.

**Months 6 to 12**
7. Add up your estate against the $4 million Illinois threshold.
8. Sign a new will, and decide between a TODI and a trust for the house.
9. Store the originals and tell your new agents and executor where they are.
