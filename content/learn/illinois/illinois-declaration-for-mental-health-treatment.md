Some conditions come in episodes. Between them you are clear about what helps and what hurts. During one, you may refuse the medication that works, or agree to something you would never choose when well. Illinois has a document for this: the Declaration for Mental Health Treatment.

It is created by a specific law, the Mental Health Treatment Preference Declaration Act (755 ILCS 43), and it is not the same as the health care power of attorney most Illinois families sign. This page covers what the declaration does, how the two documents differ, and who should sign one. For the general idea behind these documents, see our article on the [mental health advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/mental-health-advance-directive). It is part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). Information here is general, not legal advice.

## What is the Illinois Declaration for Mental Health Treatment?

It is a written statement, made by an adult of sound mind, of the mental health treatment you do and do not want if you later become incapable of deciding. The Act defines mental health treatment narrowly: electroconvulsive treatment, psychotropic medication, and admission to and retention in a mental health facility for up to 17 days.

You may state preferences or instructions, including consent to treatment and refusal of treatment. You may also name a competent adult as your attorney-in-fact, plus an alternate, to act for you on those same three subjects. The Act includes a model form, and a document should follow it substantially.

## How is it different from a health care power of attorney?

The health care power of attorney is broad. The declaration is narrow and specific. Our [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) page explains the general form, which needs one adult witness and no notary. The declaration has stricter rules and a shorter list of subjects.

| Question | Declaration for Mental Health Treatment | Health care power of attorney |
|---|---|---|
| What does it cover? | Psychotropic medication, ECT, facility admission | Medical decisions of nearly any kind |
| Governing law | 755 ILCS 43 | 755 ILCS 45 |
| Witnesses | Two competent adults, none related to you | One adult witness, no notary |
| How long does it last? | Three years to invoke, longer if in use | Until revoked, unless the document says otherwise |
| When does the agent act? | Only when you are found incapable by two physicians or a court | When you cannot decide, as the document says |
| Who may serve as agent? | Not your treating providers or facility staff, unless related | Not your attending physician or other providers |

Two documents can coexist, and they should point the same way. A common problem is a person who signs a declaration refusing a drug, then signs a health care power of attorney naming an agent who would consent to it. Doctors then face two instructions from the same patient. Name the same agent in both, or write the scope of each clearly.

## What can the declaration say?

The model form walks through the topics. You can state your anticipated symptoms, consent to or refuse particular psychotropic medications with conditions, state your view on electroconvulsive treatment, and state whether you consent to admission to a facility. The form notes that your consent cannot extend retention beyond 17 days. You can also leave additional instructions, and you can choose one of the two physicians who will evaluate whether you are incapable.

Illinois also has a separate law for who decides when you have no document at all, explained in our [Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides) article, and a court-appointed [guardian](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) is the last resort a declaration helps you avoid.

Be specific. "No strong drugs" will not help a psychiatrist at 2 a.m. "No haloperidol, which caused severe stiffness in 2021; lithium is acceptable" will. Include the dosage that worked if you know it, and the hospital you prefer.

## When does it take effect?

It becomes operative when you deliver it to your attending physician. It stays valid until revoked or expired. The physician must follow it if you are found incapable, and must still seek your informed consent whenever you are capable.

"Incapable" has a precise meaning here. Two physicians, or a court, must find that you cannot effectively receive and evaluate information or communicate decisions, so that you lack capacity for mental health treatment decisions at that time. Your attorney-in-fact's authority begins then, not before. The agent must have accepted the appointment in writing, and must follow your expressed wishes, or act in good faith in your best interest if those wishes are unknown.

## Can anyone override it?

Yes, and you should know the limits before you sign.

- A court order that contradicts your wishes controls.
- An emergency endangering your life or health permits treatment against your wishes.
- The Act does not limit the Mental Health and Developmental Disabilities Code provisions on taking a person into custody or admitting, retaining or treating them in a facility.
- A provider who is unwilling to comply may withdraw from treatment, but must promptly notify you and your attorney-in-fact and note it in your record.

Providers must place the declaration in your record and comply with it to the fullest extent possible, consistent with reasonable medical practice, the availability of treatment and applicable law. That wording gives some room, which is why a conversation with your psychiatrist beforehand matters.

## Who should sign one?

Most adults do not need one. These people often should consider it.

- People with bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, severe depression or another condition that has led to hospital stays.
- Anyone who has had a bad reaction to a drug or to ECT and wants to prevent a repeat.
- Parents who want to say who should care for their children while they are in a hospital. The declaration does not cover that, so pair it with a [standby or short-term guardian](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children) designation.
- People in early dementia, who may have episodes of agitation. Our [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) page covers the broader picture.
- Anyone who has been involuntarily admitted and wants more say next time.

A person in the middle of a crisis cannot sign one. It must be made while you are of sound mind, which is why people write them after recovery, with a clinician who knows them.

## How do you sign it properly?

1. Get the statutory form from your attorney or a mental health provider and fill in each section.
2. Talk to your psychiatrist about your choices, especially medication.
3. Choose an attorney-in-fact and an alternate who are not your providers and who agree to serve.
4. Sign in front of two adult witnesses who are not related to you and not your providers.
5. Have the attorney-in-fact sign the acceptance.
6. Give copies to your attending physician, your attorney-in-fact, your hospital of choice and your family.

The Act also allows electronic signatures that meet its technology standards. No one may require you to sign or refuse to sign a declaration as a condition of insurance, health or mental health services, or discharge from a facility.

## What does a good example look like?

Imani, 34, has bipolar I disorder and has been hospitalized twice. After her second stay she sits down with her psychiatrist and her sister Renee. She states that lithium works, that olanzapine left her unable to function, and that she consents to a short admission at the hospital near her home. She names Renee as her attorney-in-fact and her friend Dev as alternate. Two coworkers witness it. A year later she has a manic episode. Two physicians find her incapable, her declaration is in her chart, and Renee speaks for her within the limits she wrote.
