Your mother named you executor, and then someone told you that executors "can be sued personally." You do not know whether that is true, whether it applies to a family estate with a house and a few accounts, or whether you should say no. 

The short answer: the debts belong to the estate, not to you. The risk is in how you handle them. This page covers the four ways Illinois executors end up paying from their own money, a simple traffic light for spotting them, and the habits that keep you out of trouble. For the decision to accept or decline, see [can you refuse to be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-refuse-to-be-executor-in-illinois), and if you are weighing the pay side, see [what an Illinois executor gets paid](/learn/illinois/how-much-does-an-executor-get-paid-in-illinois). If you want to talk through your own situation, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-executor-personal-liability-in-illinois&topic=probate).

## Am I personally responsible for the debts of the person who died?

No. Debts of the person who died are claims against the estate. The executor collects the assets and pays valid claims from them in the order the Probate Act sets (755 ILCS 5/18-10). When there is not enough money, the lower classes get less or nothing, and the executor does not make up the shortfall. Our page on [credit card debt after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois) has the full class table.

Two exceptions are worth knowing. If you co-signed a loan or are on a joint account, you owe on your own account, not as executor. And if you personally promised a creditor that you would pay, you have made a new debt. Do not write "I will take care of this" on anything.

## What mistakes make an executor personally liable?

The pattern is consistent. An executor is a fiduciary, which means a court holds you to a higher standard than an ordinary person handling money. A loss caused by your breach can be ordered repaid from your own funds. Honest decisions made with care and paperwork are defensible. Shortcuts are not.

| Mistake | Why it costs money | Safer move |
|---|---|---|
| Paying a lower-class bill while a higher class is unpaid | In a short estate, the skipped creditor can look to you for the difference | Keep a ledger with each bill's class (755 ILCS 5/18-10) |
| Distributing to heirs before the claims period ends | A late valid claim has nothing left to be paid from | Wait for the bar date, or get court approval for an early share |
| Paying heirs while a tax is unpaid | The IRS ranks ahead of heirs | Hold a reserve until returns are filed and tax is paid |
| Mixing estate money with your own | Looks like conversion even if you meant well | Open an estate account under the estate's tax number |
| Taking executor pay early or in a large lump | Heirs object, and the amount is tested against the work done | Log hours, pay yourself late, disclose it |
| Skipping notice to a creditor you knew about | Weakens the protection of the bar date | Mail notice to every creditor you can find |

## Which debts should I pay first, and when is it safe to pay?

Pay the funeral and administration costs first, then follow the classes. You do not have to pay anything the day it arrives. The statute lets an executor pay all or part of a claim that is not barred, has not been disallowed by the court and appears valid (755 ILCS 5/18-11). It does not require speed.

Here is a short example, with invented numbers. Dan is executor for his father. The estate holds about $41,000. Funeral costs were $9,500. Dan gets a $6,200 bill from a credit card company on day 12 and pays it to make the calls stop. Four months later, a hospital sends a $38,000 claim for care in his father's last year. Hospital bills for the final year of life rank above the credit card in the Probate Act's order, and the estate no longer has enough to pay them in full. Dan is now explaining to the court why a lower class was paid first. If he had waited, the estate would have paid funeral and administration costs, then the hospital, and the card issuer would have shared in whatever was left, probably nothing. 

The rule that would have saved him is boring: do not pay general creditors until the claims period has run and you know what the higher classes total. The full claim process is in [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate).

## What protects me from creditors I never heard about?

The notice process does. An Illinois executor publishes a notice once a week for three weeks in a newspaper in the county, files proof of publication, and mails notice to creditors whose names and addresses are known or reasonably ascertainable (755 ILCS 5/18-3). The bar date must be at least six months after first publication or three months after mailing, whichever is later.

An executor who gave proper notice and used reasonable care to find creditors is generally protected from personal liability to the ones who missed it (755 ILCS 5/18-12). That protection depends on doing the steps. An executor who knew of a medical bill and never mailed notice is on weaker ground than one who published, mailed and kept a list.

Section 18-12 also sets an outer limit: claims that could have been barred are barred two years after the death. Until then, an unbarred claim can reach undistributed assets and, within limits, an heir who received a distribution.

## Can I distribute early if everyone agrees?

Agreement among heirs does not bind a creditor. If the estate has enough to cover every claim, the court can permit an early distribution, and the person receiving it must post a bond for double the value of the share (755 ILCS 5/24-4). Without that, a family that splits the money in month three has a problem in month eight if a valid claim arrives.

A reasonable middle path is a partial distribution. Many executors pay out personal items and keepsakes early, because they have no meaningful value to a creditor, and hold the cash until the bar date passes. Discuss the cash question with the attorney handling the estate before you write any checks.

## What about taxes?

Taxes are the part of liability that surprises people most, because federal law puts the IRS ahead of heirs. As our page on [IRS paperwork for an executor](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810) explains, an executor who pays other creditors or distributes assets while knowing of an unpaid federal tax can be personally responsible for it. Three practical points follow.

1. **File the final income tax returns** and any estate income tax return, and see our pages on the [final Illinois income tax return](/learn/illinois/final-illinois-income-tax-return-for-someone-who-died) and the [Illinois estate income tax return, Form IL-1041](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-il-1041-estate-and-trust-income-tax-return).
2. **Check the Illinois estate tax threshold.** Illinois taxes estates worth more than $4 million, counting non-probate assets such as life insurance and retirement accounts. The return (Form 700) and the tax are due nine months after death (see [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax)). Do not distribute the whole estate until you know whether it applies.
3. **Consider discharge.** After the returns are filed, Form 5495 asks the IRS to relieve the executor from personal liability for tax later found due on those returns, and Form 4810 asks for a faster review. Both are optional tools for larger or messier estates.

If the estate may owe tax, who actually pays is covered in [who pays the estate tax, the executor or the heirs](/learn/estate-tax/who-pays-the-estate-tax-executor-or-heirs).

## What if I acted before the court appointed me?

You have less authority and less exposure. Before letters issue, a named executor may carry out a gift of the body, arrange the burial, pay necessary funeral charges and preserve the estate (755 ILCS 5/6-14). If the will is never admitted, the person who acted is not treated as liable, with two exceptions: refusing to deliver the estate to the person entitled to it, and waste or misapplication of the estate. Paying the funeral home and keeping the receipt is fine. Using the dead person's account for your own bills is not.

## Does the bond or independent administration change my risk?

A bond protects the estate, not you. Under 755 ILCS 5/12-2, an individual representative files a sworn oath and a bond the court approves. A will may excuse the bond (755 ILCS 5/12-4), but the court can still require security if it suspects fraud or doubts the estate can cover its claims. For the details, see [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond).

Independent administration lets you act without asking a judge at each step (755 ILCS 5/28-1), and it is the default. It does not remove accountability. Any interested person can ask the court for a hearing on a specific issue (755 ILCS 5/28-5) or to end independent administration (755 ILCS 5/28-4). Our page on [independent vs. supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains the difference.

## How do I close the estate and cut off liability?

You close it with an accounting and a discharge. In independent administration, the executor mails or delivers an accounting to interested persons and files a verified report (755 ILCS 5/28-11). Heirs who sign a receipt for full payment do not need the mailing. In a supervised estate, an order approving an account binds those who got notice unless fraud, accident or mistake is shown (755 ILCS 5/24-2). The steps are in [closing an Illinois probate estate](/learn/illinois/closing-an-illinois-probate-estate-final-account-and-discharge).

Here is a short checklist to keep from day one:

- A separate estate account, with every deposit and payment going through it.
- A running claims ledger: creditor, amount, date received, class, allowed or disallowed.
- Copies of the published notice, proof of publication and the mailing list.
- A time log, if you plan to take pay.
- Written receipts from every heir at the end.
