When a family opens an Illinois probate case, one of the first choices on the paperwork is whether the estate will be handled independently or under the court's supervision. Most people sign without thinking about it. The difference shapes how much the judge sees, how much the executor can do alone and what an unhappy heir can do about it.

This page explains how the two modes work, who can push an estate from one to the other and how an independent estate gets closed. For the case from start to finish, read [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process). The rest of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the surrounding topics.

## What is independent administration in Illinois?

It is probate with the court in the background instead of the foreground. The Probate Act says the executor or administrator may administer the estate "without court order or filings" unless the Act requires one, or an interested person asks the court to step in (755 ILCS 5/28-1). Everything else in the Act's rules for decedents' estates still applies, as long as it does not conflict with this article.

In practice that means the executor can collect assets, pay bills and distribute property without asking a judge for permission at each step. The court still issues letters of office, and the letters say the person is appointed as independent executor or independent administrator (755 ILCS 5/28-2).

## What is supervised administration?

Supervised administration is the older model, where the court tracks the estate closely and the representative comes back for approval of key steps. It costs more time, and usually more money, because each step involves a filing or a hearing.

Illinois starts from the opposite assumption. If the petition for letters does not ask for supervision, the order granting letters makes the representative independent. A representative who started under supervision can also petition at any time to switch to independent administration, with notice to interested persons as the court directs (755 ILCS 5/28-2).

## When can a judge refuse independent administration?

Only in a few situations. The court must grant independent administration unless the will expressly forbids it or a protection rule for a minor or disabled heir applies (755 ILCS 5/28-2).

| Situation | What the statute says |
|---|---|
| Will says no independent administration | The court does not grant it |
| Minor or disabled heir not adequately represented, with no guardian of the estate | The court may require supervision, or allow independent administration on conditions that protect the ward, if supervision is necessary |
| An interested person objects | The court must require supervision, with the exceptions below |
| Will directs independent administration and someone objects | Supervision only if the court finds good cause |
| Objector is a creditor or a non-residuary legatee | Supervision only if necessary to protect that person's interest; the court may order other protective steps instead |

A non-residuary legatee is someone who receives a specific gift, such as a ring or a set sum, rather than a share of whatever is left over. Our article on [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) explains the difference.

## Why would an heir object?

Usually because trust has broken down. Consider Dolores, who is one of three children. Her brother Marcus is executor, he lives out of state, and he has not shared a single bank statement in four months. Dolores does not want to remove him, she just wants a judge watching.

She can object to independent administration or later petition to end it. A petition goes to the clerk of the court, and the statute puts no deadline on it (755 ILCS 5/28-4). Because the will in this example does not direct independent administration, and she is an heir rather than a creditor, the court must enter the order ending it.

## What happens when independent administration is terminated?

The executor is then subject to the rules for supervised estates. The termination order sets how and when the executor must do whatever would have been required earlier, such as filing an inventory or an account. The executor may no longer use the powers of independent administration and is liable for damages from doing so. The executor must mail notice of the termination to known interested persons and file proof of mailing (755 ILCS 5/28-4).

Acts the executor took before anyone learned of the termination are not automatically undone. The statute says they stay valid as to people other than beneficiaries and creditors, unless those people knew of the termination.

## Can heirs get court help without ending the whole thing?

Yes. At any point during independent administration, an interested person may ask the court for a hearing and an order on any matter related to managing the estate (755 ILCS 5/28-5). That is a narrower tool than termination. A disagreement over one sale, one bill or one accounting question can go to the judge while the rest of the estate keeps moving.

The same section works the other way. If an independent executor asks the court for instructions on how to use a discretionary power, the executor gives up discretion over that matter and the court substitutes its own judgment. So executors who are nervous about a close call can get cover, at the price of control.

## How do you notify heirs about their rights?

The independent executor has a duty most families never see. With each notice to heirs or legatees that goes out under sections 6-10 or 9-5, the executor must include an explanation of the heirs' and legatees' rights and the form of petition to terminate independent administration. The Illinois Supreme Court sets the form and content of those notices by rule. The executor must also file proof of mailing with the clerk (755 ILCS 5/28-2).

So if you are an heir and got a notice, look at the attached form. It is your petition to end independent administration, and it was put there on purpose.

## How does an independent estate get closed?

The executor does not simply stop. To get discharged, the executor mails or delivers an accounting to all interested persons and files a verified report with the court (755 ILCS 5/28-11). Here are the steps once the work is done.

1. The executor distributes the remaining assets to the people entitled to them and pays claims, expenses and any death taxes.
2. The executor prepares the verified report. It states that required notices were given, that creditor notice was published and known creditors were notified, that the inventory and accounting went out, and that each filed claim was allowed, disallowed, compromised, dismissed or barred.
3. The report also covers whether fees were approved by all interested persons, and lists the names and addresses of everyone entitled to notice.
4. Within 14 days of filing, the executor mails a copy to each entitled person who has not waived notice, stating that the estate will close if no objection is filed within 42 days of filing.
5. If a name or address is missing, or the estate was opened on a presumption of death, the executor publishes notice once a week for three successive weeks, starting within 14 days of filing.
6. After the 42 days, with no objection pending, the executor applies for discharge and the court closes the estate.

Some people do not need the mailing. Creditors who approve the report in writing or were paid under the statutory order, heirs and legatees who signed receipts for full payment, and trust beneficiaries when a trustee is an interested person can be left out. If nobody needs notice, the court can discharge the executor right away (755 ILCS 5/28-11).

Once discharged, the order binds everyone who approved the report or signed a receipt, and everyone properly notified, unless fraud, accident or mistake is shown. A ward who has no personal fiduciary acting for them is the exception. This is why a signed receipt deserves a careful read before you hand it back.

## How should you decide as an executor or heir?

If you are drafting a will, the choice is partly yours. A will that directs independent administration raises the bar for anyone who wants supervision. Many families like that, because it keeps the costs down. If you have a child who might clash with the executor, or an heir who is a minor, you can still keep independent administration and rely on a well-chosen executor and clear instructions. Our guide to [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) walks through that decision.

If you are an heir, the more useful question is whether you want a judge reviewing everything or only the one point that is troubling you. A hearing request under section 28-5 is cheaper and less hostile than a termination petition.
