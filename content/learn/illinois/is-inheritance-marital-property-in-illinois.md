You inherited money or a house from a parent, and now you are wondering what it means for your marriage. Maybe the marriage is shaky. Maybe you are only being careful. Or maybe you are the parent, and you want to know whether what you leave your married child could end up split in a divorce.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), answers the question for Illinois. It covers the general rule, the habits that undo it, how people prove an inheritance was separate, and how a parent can leave an inheritance in a way that is hard to divide. If you are in the middle of this right now, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-is-inheritance-marital-property-in-illinois&topic=trust) with an Illinois estate planning attorney. For the general national framework, see our page on [whether an inheritance is marital property](/learn/after-a-death/is-an-inheritance-marital-property).

## Is an inheritance marital property in Illinois?

Generally no. Illinois divides marital property in a divorce, and the law treats property that one spouse gets by gift or inheritance as that spouse's non-marital property. That is true even when the inheritance arrives during the marriage. A court begins by sorting what each spouse owns into marital and non-marital property, and an inheritance usually lands on the non-marital side.

Two cautions go with that rule. First, "generally" does the work. The rule describes the starting point, and what you do with the money afterward can move it. Second, this page is general education. It does not cite the divorce statute's text, and the exact wording and how courts apply it are worth confirming with an attorney before you rely on it in a real case.

## What can turn an inheritance into marital property?

Mixing it in. The most common ways are:

- **A joint account.** Deposit inherited cash into an account you share, and it may become indistinguishable from the household's money.
- **A joint mortgage or a shared home.** Using inherited money to pay down a mortgage on a house you own with your spouse, or to build an addition, may be treated as a contribution to the marital estate.
- **Retitling.** Adding your spouse's name to a deed or an investment account can be read as a gift of a share.
- **Using the money for family life.** Paying everyday bills from the inherited funds blurs the line over time.
- **Shared effort on an inherited asset.** If you inherit a rental or a business and both spouses work on it, a court may look at what part of its value grew through that work.

None of these is automatic in every case. Each one gives the other spouse something to argue, and each makes the money harder to follow. ## What does it look like in practice? An example

This is an invented example. Priya inherits $180,000 from her mother in 2021. She and her husband Marcus own a home with a mortgage. Priya deposits the money in their joint checking account to make the transfer easy, and over the next year about $90,000 goes to extra mortgage payments and the rest to ordinary bills.

By the time of a possible divorce, Priya's records show where the estate check came from. They also show that it landed in a joint account, and that the money was spent on a house in both names. She can argue that the $90,000 should be credited back to her. Marcus can argue it was a gift to the marriage. The outcome is uncertain. Had Priya put the money in a separate account in her name alone and used it for nothing else, her position would be far stronger. If she had wanted to help with the mortgage anyway, a short signed note saying the payments were a loan or a credit would have helped.

## How do you trace an inheritance?

Tracing means showing, with documents, that specific money is the same inherited money you started with. The spouse claiming an asset is non-marital usually carries the burden of showing it. A clean trail has three links:

| Link in the trail | What proves it |
|---|---|
| The money was an inheritance | Estate distribution receipt, trust distribution letter, check stub, deed from the estate |
| It went where you say it went | Statement of the account that received it, with the deposit shown |
| It became the asset you claim | Closing statement, account statements, or invoices showing the purchase or payment |

Keep the paper. Photograph the check. Open a new account for the deposit and give it a clear name. Ask the executor or trustee for a written accounting of what you received. If the estate is still open, our page on the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) explains when distributions usually come.

## What should you do right after you inherit?

Follow a short checklist.

1. **Open a separate account** in your name alone for the inheritance and nothing else.
2. **Do not add anyone** to the account or the deed, even to be kind, until you have talked to an attorney.
3. **Save the source documents** in one folder: the will or trust excerpt, the distribution paperwork, and the first deposit record.
4. **Keep inherited funds out of household spending**, including mortgage payments, unless you decide in writing how it should be treated.
5. **Write down any decision to share.** If you do want to help with a joint purchase, a signed note or agreement about whether it is a gift or a loan will matter later.
6. **Talk to a lawyer before major moves**, such as buying a house with the money or retitling an asset.

The right time to set these up is before a conflict. Afterward, you may be reconstructing records from years ago.

## Can a prenup or postnuptial agreement protect an inheritance?

Yes, and it can do more than the default rule. In Illinois, spouses can agree in writing about how property is treated, and our page on [Illinois prenup and inheritance rights](/learn/illinois/illinois-prenup-and-inheritance-rights) explains what a premarital agreement can cover and how it is signed. A written agreement can say that inherited property, and what it grows into, stays non-marital. It does not replace good records, but it answers questions the default rule leaves open.

## How can a parent protect what they leave a married child?

Leave it in a trust. An outright gift puts the money in your child's hands, where it can be mixed or retitled. A trust holds it in its own name. With an independent trustee, discretionary distributions and a spendthrift clause, the child does not have a fixed right to the money, which makes it harder to treat as the child's property in a divorce. Our page on [protecting assets with a trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-protect-assets-from-creditors-with-a-trust-in-illinois) describes how a spendthrift clause works and where its limits are, and our page on [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) covers the design.

A few points make such a trust work:

- Name a trustee who is not the child's spouse.
- Make distributions discretionary, rather than a set amount on a set date.
- Pay for things directly from the trust, such as a house held in the trust, instead of handing cash to the couple.
- Name who receives what is left if the child dies, so your wishes, not default rules, decide.

A trust does not promise that a court will ignore it. A judge dividing property and setting support can consider a spouse's financial resources, including what a trust pays. But a protected trust moves the starting point strongly in your child's favor. ## Does divorce change your own estate plan too?

Yes. If you are the person who inherited and you are now divorcing, your own documents need attention. Beneficiary forms, a will, and powers of attorney that name a spouse may need to change. Our page on [how divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan) lists each document and what to check. Do not assume the court order updated your accounts for you.
