The funeral director hands you a folder and says the death certificates will be ready soon. Then the bank, the insurer and the pension office each ask for a certified copy, and you realize you ordered three. In Illinois you have two places to go for more: the county clerk and the state health department.

This page covers who can order a certified Illinois death certificate, which office to use, and a way to count how many an executor really needs. For the national rules of thumb and what each copy costs elsewhere, see our general page on [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need). Everything here is Illinois specific. The wider estate process is in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## How does an Illinois death certificate get created?

The funeral director completes the personal facts, such as name, date of birth and parents, usually with a family member's help. A doctor, coroner or medical examiner certifies the cause of death. The record is then filed with the local registrar and reaches the state vital records system. A certified copy is a copy issued from that filed record.

Two things follow from this. First, the funeral home is the fastest place for the first batch, since it already has the record in motion. Second, the facts on the certificate are only as good as what the family told the funeral director, so read the draft. If a name or a Social Security number is wrong, our page on [correcting a mistake on a death certificate](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-correct-a-mistake-on-a-death-certificate) explains what comes next.

## Who can order a certified death certificate in Illinois?

Expect a certified Illinois death record to be limited to people with a connection to the death. The offices ask the requester to show a personal or legal interest. In practice, these people qualify:

- The surviving spouse, children, parents and siblings.
- The executor or administrator, with letters of office.
- A person with a legal or property right that depends on the death, such as a named beneficiary or an insurer's claimant.
- An attorney acting for one of the above.

The exact wording of the eligibility rule, and the identification each office asks for, are on the county clerk and IDPH request pages. Bring a government photo ID and, if you are not a close relative, something that proves your interest: letters of office, a beneficiary notice, or a signed authorization from the family.

## County clerk or IDPH: which should I use?

Either may work, but they suit different situations.

| Question | County clerk | IDPH (state vital records) |
|---|---|---|
| Where is the record held? | The county where the death occurred | Statewide, in Springfield |
| Best for | Recent deaths, in-person pickup, local speed | Cases where you are unsure of the county, or an older record |
| Typical delay | Often shorter for walk-in service | Depends on the current backlog |
| What to confirm before you order | Fee, hours, whether appointments are required | Fee, processing time, accepted ID |

Our advice to executors is simple. If the death was in a county you can reach, start with that county clerk, because an in-person request can sometimes be filled the same day, though you should call to confirm. If you live out of state or do not know where the record was filed, IDPH is the single address to deal with. Neither fee nor timing is the same every year, so read the current numbers on each website instead of trusting an old forum post.

## How many certified copies does an Illinois executor need?

Count institutions, not relatives. Each place that needs an original gets one, and you hold two or three spares for surprises. Here is a worked example of an executor's count for a typical Illinois estate. The numbers are an illustration, not a rule.

Priya, 47, is the executor for her father, Sanjay, who owned a house in Naperville, a checking account, an IRA, a life insurance policy and a car. She counts:

| Who needs a certified copy | Copies |
|---|---|
| Probate court (DuPage County) | 1 |
| Checking account bank | 1 |
| IRA custodian | 1 |
| Life insurance company | 1 |
| Secretary of State for the car title | 1 |
| Pension or Social Security survivor claim | 1 |
| Title company, if the house is sold | 1 |
| Spares | 3 |
| Total | 10 |

If Sanjay had also held a second bank, a brokerage account and a pension, she would add three. A small estate with one account and no real estate may need four. The reason to order too many at the outset is that a second request can mean new forms, a second wait and another processing charge.

Some institutions will photocopy the certificate and hand it back. Ask before you surrender it, because a returned original is a copy you did not have to buy. For the car in particular, see our page on [transferring a car title after death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/transfer-car-title-after-death-in-illinois), which says what the Secretary of State will want.

## Do I need a death certificate to start probate or use a small estate affidavit?

Yes for both. A probate petition lists the date and place of death, and the clerk expects a certified copy. A bank asked to honor a small estate affidavit will want one too. Our overview of the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) shows where the certificate fits in the first filings, and the page on [letters of administration](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) explains what happens when no one has been appointed yet.

One useful habit: keep a log of where every certified copy went and when it was returned. It saves you from guessing at month four.

One Illinois detail worth knowing: a death in a hospital in one county does not always mean the record sits where the family lives. The record belongs to the county where the death occurred, so a father who died in a Chicago hospital while living in Joliet has a Cook County record. Ask the funeral director which county filed it before you drive anywhere.

## What mistakes do executors make with death certificates?

The first is ordering only what the funeral home suggests. Funeral homes tend to quote a small number, because families are tired and the fee adds to the bill. The second is mailing an original to an institution that would have accepted a photocopy or an electronic upload for a first look. Ask each place what it takes, then spend a certified copy only where it is required.

The third is mixing up the certificate with other paper. A funeral home's statement of goods and services is a bill, and an obituary is a notice. Neither proves a death to a bank or a court. Our [checklist for what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) and the page on [who to notify](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) show which calls need the certified copy and which need only a phone call.

The fourth is forgetting the cause-of-death line. If the cause is pending, tell each institution up front, since a few will wait for the final version before they pay.
