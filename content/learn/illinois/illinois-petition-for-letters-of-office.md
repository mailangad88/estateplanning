The petition is the first real document in an Illinois estate. A clerk will check it for names and numbers, a judge will rely on it at the hearing, and every heir will receive a copy. Getting it complete the first time is the simplest way to avoid delay.

This page walks through what the Probate Act expects in the petition for a will-based estate and how the no-will version differs. For the full opening sequence, see [how to open a probate estate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-open-a-probate-estate-in-illinois). For the bigger picture, see our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois).

## What is in the petition to admit a will?

The petition tracks the content the Probate Act requires for admitting a will (755 ILCS 5/6-2). The form your clerk provides has blanks for each of these:

1. The decedent's full name, residence at death, and the date and place of death.
2. The names, relationships and last known addresses of the heirs.
3. The names and addresses of the legatees, meaning the people and charities in the will.
4. A statement that the original will is filed with the clerk, or is attached.
5. The name and address of the person asking to serve as executor.
6. An estimate of the value of the real estate and the personal property.
7. A statement of whether the will excuses a surety bond.
8. A request for independent administration, unless the will forbids it.
9. A request that the court admit the will and issue letters of office.

Court forms and local cover sheets change, so use the version your clerk's website posts today.

## Who counts as an heir and a legatee?

An heir is a person who would inherit if there were no will. A legatee is a person named in the will to receive something. They overlap, but not always. A disinherited child is an heir who is not a legatee, and a friend who receives $5,000 is a legatee who is not an heir. Both groups are listed, because both get notice. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) shows the order of heirs, and [proving heirship](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois) covers hard cases.

| Person | Where they go on the petition | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Surviving spouse | Heir, and legatee if named | Gets notice either way |
| Adult children | Heirs, and legatees if named | Get notice even if disinherited |
| Grandchildren of a deceased child | Heirs in that child's place | Stand in the child's spot |
| Friend or charity in the will | Legatee | Gets notice and a copy of the order |
| Named executor | Petitioner | Signs the petition under oath |
| Minor or disabled heir | Listed with the parent or guardian | The court may appoint a [guardian ad litem](/learn/illinois/guardian-ad-litem-in-illinois-probate) |

## How do you estimate the property?

State real estate and personal property separately and keep the figures honest and round. A house may be valued from a recent tax bill, a broker's opinion or a comparable sale. Personal property includes bank accounts, vehicles, a brokerage account held alone and tangible items. Do not list accounts with a named beneficiary or jointly owned property in the estimate unless the form asks for them separately. Our article on [how an executor values the estate](/learn/after-a-death/how-an-executor-values-the-estate) explains the methods.

The estimate matters for two reasons. It helps the court decide how much bond, if any, to require. It also tells the clerk and the judge whether the case looks like a small estate that could have used an affidavit.

## What about the bond and independent administration?

If the will says no bond is needed, say so in the petition. The court can still require one, especially if the executor lives out of state (755 ILCS 5/6-13). Our article on [letters and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) shows how a bond is sized.

Ask for independent administration in the petition. If you do not ask for supervision, the order granting letters makes the executor independent, and the executor can act without court approval at each step (755 ILCS 5/28-2). Our [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) page explains what an objecting heir can do.

## How is the no-will petition different?

When there is no will, the petition asks for letters of administration. It lists the heirs under the intestacy statute, names the person who wants to be administrator, and explains why that person has priority. Priority follows a statutory order that starts with the spouse and then the children, and several heirs can sign consents. A bond is almost always required because no will excuses it.

## What are common mistakes on the petition?

- Leaving off an heir whose address is unknown. Say that the address is unknown, so the court can order publication.
- Listing only the people in the will and forgetting heirs who were left out.
- Using a nickname or a married name that does not match the death certificate.
- Stating a very low property value that later does not match the inventory.
- Choosing supervised administration by checking the wrong box.

Elena, 63, is executor for her mother Ines. She lists her two siblings as heirs and legatees, notes that Ines's grandson Mateo, 15, takes $10,000 under the will, and estimates $290,000 for the house and $41,000 for accounts. She also checks the box for independent administration. The clerk sends the petition back once, because Mateo's guardian is not named. A one-day correction is much better than a hearing that has to be reset.

## How do you sign and file it?

The petitioner signs under oath, often in front of a notary or the clerk, or with the verification that the clerk's form uses. File through your clerk's electronic filing system if it is required. The clerk gives a case number and a hearing date. Our [probate cost article](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) lists the court fees, and the [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) adds up the typical items.

## What happens after the petition is filed?

The clerk assigns a case number and, in most counties, a hearing date. In many courts the executor does not need to say much at the hearing. The judge confirms the will is on file, reviews the petition, checks that the heirs and legatees are listed and admits the will if it looks valid. Where the will has an attestation clause signed by two witnesses, the court can generally admit it on that proof, so the witnesses do not need to appear. If any heir files an objection or asks for formal proof, the judge sets the matter for a later date, and the estate waits. That is why a clean, complete petition matters: it leaves the court nothing to question and gives an unhappy heir nothing to point to.

Keep a copy of the filed petition. You will refer to it when you prepare the notice, the inventory and the closing report.
