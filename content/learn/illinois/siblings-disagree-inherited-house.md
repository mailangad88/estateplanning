Your mother died in the spring. Your sister has been living in the house since before that and plans to stay. Your brother wants it sold this summer. The executor, who is your other brother, answers every question with "it's being handled." You are in the middle, and everyone has a point.

This page is for that standoff in Illinois. It puts your options in order, from the cheapest to the most expensive, so you can stop at the first one that works. The general rules of who owns an inherited house are on our page about [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois), part of our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois), and we will not repeat them here. If you want a sense of where your family stands, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-siblings-disagree-inherited-house&topic=probate) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Who controls the house right now?

It depends on whether anyone owns it yet. If your parent held the house alone and left no trust or transfer on death instrument, the house sits in a probate estate. Title does not pass to the children until the estate is administered and the executor or the court moves it. During that time the executor is the person who can list it, insure it and sign a sale, with court oversight depending on the type of administration. Our page on [Illinois independent and supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains the difference.

That is why "I want it sold" and "I want to keep it" are both early arguments. Neither sibling can act alone yet. The executor owes a duty to every heir, which means the sibling who is the executor cannot quietly favor the one living in the house. If the house passes by trust, the trustee holds the same kind of position. Once title reaches the heirs, they become co-owners with undivided shares, and the rungs below apply in full.

## What are the four ways out?

Think of a ladder. You climb only when the rung below fails.

| Rung | What happens | Cost and time | Fits when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Talk | A family meeting with numbers on paper | Nearly free, days to weeks | Everyone still answers the phone |
| 2. Buyout | One sibling pays the others for their shares | Appraisal and closing costs, weeks to months | One sibling wants to keep it and can finance it |
| 3. Mediation | A neutral person helps reach a signed deal | A mediator's fee, a few sessions | Talk stalled but nobody wants court |
| 4. Partition | A court orders the house divided or, usually, sold | Highest cost and longest, months to years | The other rungs failed |

We left out dollar amounts on purpose. Costs depend on the house, the county and the lawyers, and anyone quoting you a fixed figure before reading the deed is guessing.

### Rung 1: Talk, with paper

Most sibling fights are two different fights hiding inside one. The first is about the house itself: keep or sell. The second is about money: who paid the tax bill, who fixed the roof, who drove Mom to dialysis. Separate them. Ask each sibling to write down what they have paid, what they want and what they would accept. Put a rough value on the house from a broker's opinion of value. Then hold one meeting with a single agenda.

If the sibling in the house wants to stay, ask him to propose terms: how long, who pays the taxes and insurance, what happens if he stops. A deal on one page is worth more than a promise at the kitchen table.

### Rung 2: Buyout

If one sibling wants the house, the others can sell their shares to that sibling. Price it with an appraisal rather than an argument, and decide in advance how to treat repairs and unpaid bills. Our property guide on [buying out a sibling or forcing a partition](/learn/property-and-assets/buying-out-a-sibling-or-co-owner-of-an-inherited-house-or-forcing-a-partition) covers pricing, financing and closing papers, so we will not repeat them. A sibling who cannot get a mortgage can sometimes pay over time with a written note, which should be secured by the house.

### Rung 3: Mediation

A mediator does not decide anything. The mediator sits with all of you and helps you find a deal you can sign. This works better than people expect when the real issue is feeling unheard. It is also private, unlike a court file, and a mediated agreement can be written up as a binding contract or presented to a judge. Some courts encourage or require mediation in family property cases, and the rules vary by county, so ask the clerk or an attorney what your court expects.

### Rung 4: Partition

If nothing else works, a co-owner can file a partition case. Illinois courts can order a partition when co-owners cannot agree, and with one house that almost always means a sale, with the proceeds divided by each person's share. Costs and fees can come out of the sale. The result is often less than a negotiated sale would have brought, since a forced sale may bring a lower price. Because disputes like this can turn into litigation, you should speak with an attorney before you file or before you ignore papers served on you.

## What if the executor will not share information?

Treat it as a separate problem and solve it early, because you cannot negotiate over facts you cannot see.

1. **Check that you got your notice.** Within 14 days after the will is admitted or denied, or a representative is appointed, the representative or petitioner must mail the petition and order to each heir and legatee listed (755 ILCS 5/6-10). If you never received one, ask the clerk.
2. **Ask the circuit clerk what is on file.** The file shows whether letters issued and whether an inventory or account has been filed.
3. **Send one written request.** Ask for the will, a list of assets and debts, what has been paid and what the executor plans to do with the house. Give a date about 30 days out.
4. **Escalate only after that.** An interested person may petition to end independent administration, which puts the executor under court supervision (755 ILCS 5/28-4), or to remove the executor on a ground such as waste, mismanagement or failing to file a court-ordered inventory or account (755 ILCS 5/23-2).

Slow is not the same as removable, and the court needs a reason. Our page on [what heirs can do about a slow Illinois executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor) sets out the grounds and a sample sequence, and the general guide to [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) compares the approach across states. If the executor is also the sibling who lives in the house, he is wearing two hats and a court will watch for a conflict between them.

## Example: three siblings and one house in Elgin

Marisol, Dennis and Tomas inherit their father's house in Elgin. Marisol lives there and wants to stay. Dennis, the executor, wants to sell and says very little to the others. Tomas lives out of state and just wants to know what is happening.

Tomas writes Dennis a polite letter asking for the asset list and bills paid and gets the list within three weeks. A broker's opinion puts the house at a figure all three can see. Marisol cannot get a mortgage for the full buyout, so the siblings sign a one-page agreement: she pays the taxes and insurance, she has 18 months to buy out the others at an appraised value, and if she misses the date the house is listed. The agreement is signed by all three. It did not need a judge, and it cost a fraction of what a partition case would.

## Common mistakes we see

- **Changing the locks.** A co-owner generally cannot shut the others out of a house they own. Doing it hands the other side a better case.
- **Fixing things without telling anyone.** A new roof is easier to recover when the others knew about it first.
- **Letting the tax bill go unpaid.** Penalties grow and the title can be affected. The siblings can argue about who owes whom after it is paid.
- **Signing a quitclaim deed to make peace.** A deed is hard to undo. Get advice before you sign anything that transfers your share.
- **Waiting until the family stops speaking.** A fight that is two months old can be settled over coffee. After two years, it usually needs a lawyer.

## Can you prevent this for your own family?

Yes, and the fix is short. A parent who owns the house can leave it to one child with an equalizing gift to the others, direct a sale, or name a trustee to decide.
