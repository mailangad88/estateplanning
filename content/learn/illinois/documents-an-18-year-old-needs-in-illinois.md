Dana's son Theo turned 18 in July and moves into a dorm at Northern Illinois University in August. In September he is taken to an emergency room with a burst appendix. The front desk will not tell Dana whether he is there. She is his mother, she pays his tuition, and she has no right to an answer.

This page covers the Illinois side of that problem: which forms to sign, who can witness, what a parent can still do without them, and what happens when nothing is signed. For the wider picture of planning at this age, see [estate planning at 18](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults). For the story of a family caught without the forms, see [what happens if your 18-year-old has a medical emergency with no HIPAA release](/learn/what-if/adult-child-medical-emergency-no-hipaa).

## What can parents do once a child turns 18 in Illinois?

Once your child turns 18, you can still help, love and advise, but you can no longer sign for them. Illinois treats an 18-year-old as an adult for consent to treatment and for contracts, and federal privacy rules protect their medical information from you as from any stranger.

What changes on the birthday:

| Area | Before 18 | After 18 without forms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medical consent | Parent signs | Only the young adult signs |
| Medical information | Parent can ask the doctor | Doctor needs the patient's written permission |
| Bank account | Parent is on the account or manages it | Parent has no access unless named on the account |
| School records | Parent can see grades and bills | College can decline, under the federal student records law (FERPA) |
| Lease or loan | Parent signs | Parent can only co-sign as a separate party |

A parent who pays the bills is still not the student's agent. Paying for something and having legal authority to act are different things, and hospitals and banks know the difference.

## What happens if an Illinois 18-year-old is hurt and has signed nothing?

Illinois has a default ladder, and a parent is on it, but not at the top. If a patient cannot decide and has no health care agent, the Health Care Surrogate Act lists, in order: a guardian of the person, a spouse, an adult son or daughter, either parent, an adult brother or sister, an adult grandchild, and a close friend (755 ILCS 40/25). A single 18-year-old has no spouse and no adult child, so a parent is usually the first person who qualifies. Our full guide to the [Illinois Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides) walks through the list.

Three limits make that ladder a poor substitute for paper:

- **Divorced or separated parents.** Both parents sit at the same rank. If they disagree, the majority of those available controls, and with two parents a tie leaves a dispute.
- **Scope.** The list is aimed at serious medical decisions, such as withholding life-sustaining treatment, and some of those need a qualifying condition and a second practitioner's written agreement. It does nothing for records, money or a lease.
- **Preference.** Theo may want his older brother, or his girlfriend, or his aunt, to decide. The statute does not ask.

## Which three forms should an Illinois 18-year-old sign?

Most families need three papers. Each does a different job, and the first two are statutory Illinois forms under the Powers of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45).

1. **Health care power of attorney.** Names an agent, and a successor, to make medical decisions when the student cannot. Read the Illinois rules in our guide to the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).
2. **Power of attorney for property.** Names an agent to deal with money, accounts and, if you choose, the lease and taxes. See the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
3. **HIPAA release.** Lets named people receive medical information and speak with providers. Our [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) article covers it.

Why a separate HIPAA release when the health care form already exists? Because the agent's power usually begins only when the student cannot decide. A parent who wants a discharge summary after a weekend in the infirmary, while Theo is fully awake, needs a release that works now.

## How do you sign each Illinois form correctly?

Signing rules are where Illinois forms most often fail, and the two powers of attorney are not the same. The details below come from the statute as described in our other Illinois guides.

| Form | Student signs | Witness | Notary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Health care power of attorney | Yes | One adult witness, 18 or older | Not required |
| Power of attorney for property | Yes | One witness | Required |
| HIPAA release | Yes | Check the provider's own form | Usually not |

The witness cannot be the agent or a successor agent, and cannot be the student's attending physician or certain other care providers. The same limits apply to relatives of the agent in some cases (755 ILCS 45/4-5.1 for health care; the property witness list is similar). The practical rule is to ask someone with no stake: a family friend, a coworker, or a staff member at the lawyer's office.

A common mistake our attorneys see is the older sibling or the other parent signing as witness on the same page that names them as agent. The form can then be refused at the very moment a hospital reads it. Use a neutral adult.

### Steps for a family doing this at home

1. Talk first. The student chooses the agent and a backup. Many pick a parent, but a sibling, grandparent or partner is fine.
2. Fill in the health care form, including the choices about life-sustaining treatment. Parents should leave the student to answer them.
3. Fill in the property form. Initial only the powers the student wants to grant. Banking, taxes and government benefits cover most college needs.
4. Choose when the property power starts. Immediate is simpler for a bank; a delayed start can require proof of incapacity, which slows things down. The comparison is in [springing versus immediate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).
5. Sign in front of the right people, with a notary for the property form.
6. Scan everything and give the agents copies. Tell the campus health center who the agent is.

## What should a student limit or hold back?

A young adult can give a parent less than everything. The statutory property form lists the powers in categories, and the student initials each one granted. Gifts, changes to beneficiaries and changes to a trust are not allowed unless the form specifically says so (755 ILCS 45/3-4), so a standard form already holds back the most worrying powers.

Some students want the parent to see bills and arrange payments but not read months of medical notes. The HIPAA release can be narrow: name particular providers, or limit the time period, or exclude certain categories. Other students prefer a release that covers everything, because in an emergency nobody has time to sort which hospital is covered. Both choices belong to the student.

For concerns about trust inside a family, our guide to [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) and the article on [limiting an agent's powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/limiting-an-agents-powers-and-adding-safeguards-in-a-power-of-attorney) apply here too. The student can also revoke at any time while competent (755 ILCS 45/2-5 for property; 755 ILCS 45/4-6 for health care).

## Do Illinois forms work for a student at school in another state?

Mostly, but expect friction. A federal HIPAA release is recognized wherever the provider is covered by the rule. A health care power of attorney signed in Illinois is generally honored in other states, though a hospital in another state may ask questions or want to speak with the agent. The article on [powers of attorney across state lines or abroad](/learn/power-of-attorney/is-a-power-of-attorney-valid-in-another-state-or-abroad) covers the cases. For students leaving the country, see [advance directives when you travel or live abroad](/learn/healthcare-directives/advance-directive-when-you-travel-or-live-abroad).

Banks are the other friction point. Some will ask for their own form even though Illinois has a statutory one. If the student has an account at a national bank, send them a copy of the property power before school starts and ask what they will accept. [When a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) lists next steps.

## What if nothing is signed and the student cannot sign now?

If an adult child has lost capacity and has no forms, the family has two paths. One is the surrogate list for medical choices, which does not reach finances. The other is a guardianship case in the Illinois courts, where a judge decides whether the young adult is disabled and appoints a guardian of the person, the estate, or both. It takes weeks or months, costs money and puts private health facts in a court record. Our [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) guide explains the process, and [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains why a signed form is the easier route.

This is also the reason to sign early. A form can be made only by a person who understands what they are signing. After a car accident or a psychiatric crisis, that window may be closed.

## What does a family with a disabled young adult do differently?

The forms above assume the student has capacity to sign. If your child has an intellectual or developmental disability, the questions are different: supported decision-making, a representative payee and sometimes guardianship. Start with [what to do when a child with a disability turns 18](/learn/special-needs/what-to-do-when-a-child-with-a-disability-turns-18) and [Illinois special needs planning](/learn/illinois/special-needs-planning-in-illinois-for-a-disabled-child).
