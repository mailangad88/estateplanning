Your mother is 81, and the family is starting to talk about a nursing home. One friend says Medicaid will take her house. Another says to put it in your name right away. The wrong move is hard to undo.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), takes the house alone and walks through each choice a family usually considers: leaving it alone, a spouse keeping it, gifting it, the allowed transfers, a transfer on death instrument, a trust and selling. For each one it asks two questions, because the house faces two separate tests. If a parent's care is already being discussed, a [consultation about a parent's house](/book?from=learn-protecting-your-house-from-a-nursing-home-in-illinois&topic=parent) can check the timing before anyone signs.

## What are the two tests the house has to pass?

The first test is eligibility, while the person is alive. When someone applies for long-term care Medicaid, Illinois reviews transfers made in the 60 months before the application, and a transfer for less than fair market value can cause a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay. The rules for applying are on the page about [qualifying for Medicaid nursing home care in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning).

The second test is estate recovery, after death. The Department of Healthcare and Family Services (HFS) can file a claim against the probate estate of a person who received long-term care benefits. The full rules, including the hardship waiver, are on the page about [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery).

Most bad outcomes come from passing one test and failing the other.

## Does the house count while someone still lives in it?

Generally, a primary home is treated differently from savings while the owner or a spouse lives there, though the state's rules for the home are technical. The national page on [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) explains the usual home exemption and the idea of intending to return. In Illinois, ask the caseworker or an attorney how the home is being treated in your parent's application, rather than assuming it is safe or unsafe.

The house is rarely what stops eligibility. It becomes a problem when someone moves it, sells it or dies owning it.

## Is the house safer if a spouse still lives there?

Yes, for as long as that spouse is alive. Two rules work together. Transfers to the community spouse, meaning the spouse who is not in the nursing home, are generally allowable. And after the nursing home spouse dies, HFS does not seek recovery while a spouse is still living.

That protection ends with the second spouse. If the house is in the healthy spouse's name and that spouse dies owning it with no plan, it goes through probate and may later be part of a claim if that spouse also received benefits. The page on [what happens to Medicaid when the healthy spouse dies first](/learn/elder-care/what-happens-to-medicaid-when-the-healthy-spouse-dies-first) covers that turn, and [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples) explains the spousal allowances.

## What happens if a parent simply gives the house to a child?

This is the move that hurts most families. A gift of the house to an adult child is a transfer for less than fair market value, so it falls inside the look-back if an application comes within 60 months. HFS describes the penalty as starting on the later of the transfer date or the date the person is in a nursing home and found otherwise eligible. The clock does not run quietly in the background while the parent is healthy.

Undoing it is also hard. HFS states that partial returns no longer reduce a penalty, so the whole transfer has to come back. The child's creditors and divorce can also reach the house, and the step-up in basis is generally lost. The page on [adding a child to your deed in Illinois](/learn/illinois/should-i-add-my-child-to-my-deed-illinois) lays those out, and the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) shows where the 60-month window falls.

## Which transfers of the house are allowed?

Illinois lists certain transfers of the home as allowable, meaning they do not cause a penalty. As the eligibility page describes them, they are a transfer of the home to:

- a spouse,
- a child under 21,
- a blind or disabled child, or
- certain siblings or a caregiving child, in the cases the state lists.

The last group is where families most often guess wrong. "My son helped Mom for years" does not by itself meet the state's technical conditions. Before deeding the house to a caregiving child, have an attorney check the facts against current HFS rules. If a child is being paid for care instead, the page on [paying a family caregiver in Illinois](/learn/illinois/paying-a-family-caregiver) explains how to keep those payments from looking like gifts.

## Does a TODI or a living trust protect the house?

Each one helps with probate. Neither is a dependable shield.

A [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) gives the beneficiary nothing while the owner is alive, and the owner can sell or revoke. For that reason it is generally not treated like a lifetime gift for the look-back. After death, though, the Act makes the beneficiary answerable for claims against the estate in the same way as a revocable trust beneficiary (755 ILCS 27/85), and how HFS treats a TODI house in a recovery case is not settled on its plain-language pages. The page on [whether creditors or Medicaid can reach a TODI house](/learn/illinois/todi-creditors-and-medicaid-in-illinois) works through that question. One timing point matters here: an agent under a power of attorney cannot create or revoke a TODI, so it has to be signed while the owner can sign.

A revocable living trust does even less for Medicaid. Because the owner can take the house back, the trust's assets still count for eligibility. For recovery, the same open question applies as for a TODI. The page on [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house) compares the two for passing the house.

## What does an irrevocable trust do?

An irrevocable trust can change what counts, because the owner gives up the house for good. The transfer into the trust is itself a gift that the look-back reaches, so it helps only if it is done well before care is needed and the owner can pay privately in the meantime. A trust that leaves the owner too much control can make the house countable again. The national page on the [Medicaid asset protection trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) explains the drafting limits, and [life estate deed vs irrevocable trust](/learn/elder-care/life-estate-deed-vs-irrevocable-trust-for-medicaid) compares the two tools that both start the 60-month clock.

## Should the house be sold?

Sometimes, but plan the proceeds first. A sale at full value is not a gift, so it does not cause a look-back penalty. The problem is what comes next: the house becomes cash, and cash generally counts toward the asset limit. A sale to a relative below market value is generally treated as a transfer of the difference. The page on [capital gains when you sell a home to pay for nursing home care](/learn/elder-care/capital-gains-when-you-sell-a-home-to-pay-for-nursing-home-care) covers the tax side, including the main-home exclusion.

Keeping the house can be the right answer. New liens on real property have been barred since Public Act 102-1037 took effect on June 2, 2022, and the claim waits until after death.

## How do the moves compare?

| Move | Look-back risk | Estate recovery effect |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the house in the parent's name | None | In the probate estate; claim possible after death unless a spouse or protected child survives |
| Spouse keeps or receives the house | Generally allowable | No recovery while a spouse is living; revisit at the second death |
| Gift to an adult child who did not provide care | Penalty if within 60 months of applying | Out of the estate, but the child's creditors and divorce can reach it |
| Transfer to a child under 21, a blind or disabled child, or a listed caregiving child or sibling | Allowable if the conditions are met | Out of the parent's estate |
| TODI naming the children | Generally not a lifetime gift | Avoids probate; whether HFS can reach it is unsettled |
| Revocable living trust | Not a transfer, and the house still counts | Avoids probate; confirm the recovery treatment |
| Irrevocable trust | A gift that starts the 60-month clock | Depends on the trust terms and timing |
| Sale at full value | None | The house is gone, and the cash generally counts |

## What do the deferrals mean if nothing is done?

If the house is simply left in the parent's name, several rules soften recovery. HFS does not recover while a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child survives. For deaths on or after July 1, 2022, the first $25,000 of estate value is protected. Funeral costs, legal costs and a home mortgage are paid before the claim. Heirs are not personally liable, and they may pay the claim from other money to keep the house. HFS does not offer payment plans, but a hardship waiver can be requested with proof.

## What does this look like for one family?

Example, with invented names. Dorothy, 79, a widow in Rockford, owns a house worth $210,000. Her son Paul lives with her and has done most of her daily care for several years. Her daughter Nina lives in Texas. Dorothy's doctor thinks a nursing home is likely within two years.

Nina suggests Dorothy deed the house to both children now. Inside two years, that gift would almost certainly fall within the look-back and lead to a penalty once Dorothy is in care and otherwise eligible. Paul's caregiving might fit the caregiving child transfer, but only if the facts meet the state's conditions, so the family asks an attorney to check before signing anything. In the meantime Dorothy signs a property power of attorney naming Paul, so someone can act if she cannot. Both children are told what is decided, because a transfer to one child changes what the other inherits.

## What should the family do, and in what order?

1. Write down who lives in the house and who has given care, with dates.
2. List every transfer in the last 60 months, including any deed already signed.
3. Make sure a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) is in place while your parent can sign. If not, see [how to get power of attorney for a parent](/learn/illinois/how-to-get-power-of-attorney-for-a-parent-in-illinois).
4. Do not sign a deed, a trust or a sales contract until an attorney has checked the look-back and recovery effect together.
5. If the house will be left to one child, read [how to leave the house to one child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-leave-the-house-to-one-child-in-illinois).
