You have just learned that you, or you and your brothers and sisters, are getting a house in Illinois. Before anything else, you need to know three things: who legally owns it right now, what is owed on it, and who is paying the bills until that is settled.

This page covers inheriting a house in Illinois from the first week to the sale or move-in. It is general education, not legal advice. The wider picture is in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), and the general national checklist is in [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house).

## How does a house pass to you in Illinois?

It depends on how the owner held title, and the deed or trust document tells you which path you are on.

| How title was held | Who can act first | Probate needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Owner's name alone, with a will | The executor, after the court admits the will and issues letters | Yes |
| Owner's name alone, no will | An administrator the court appoints | Yes |
| Recorded transfer on death instrument | The named beneficiary, after filing the right paperwork | No |
| Living trust or land trust | The successor trustee | No |
| Joint tenancy with survivorship | The surviving owner | No |
| Tenancy by the entirety | The surviving spouse | No |

Illinois is easy to misread here. A deed naming two people with no extra words makes them tenants in common, not joint tenants, so a half share can end up in probate. Our article on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains the wording that creates survivorship. If the owner recorded a transfer on death instrument, [how a TODI works](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) lists the recording rules and what the beneficiary must do after the death.

For a house in one name alone, the executor needs court authority before selling or deeding it. The [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) and [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois) cover that stretch. One Illinois detail catches people: if the will leaves the house to a named person, the executor cannot sell or mortgage it without that person's written consent (755 ILCS 5/28-8).

## What should you do in the first month?

Protect the house and keep it paid for. You do not have to decide anything big yet.

1. Secure it. Change locks if several people hold keys, and remove cash, firearms, medicine and papers.
2. Call the homeowner's insurer. Say the owner has died and ask how long a vacant house stays covered. Many policies restrict coverage after 30 to 60 days of vacancy.
3. Find the latest property tax bill, the deed, and the most recent mortgage statement. Put all three in one folder.
4. Keep paying the mortgage, utilities and taxes from estate funds if the executor has access, or document what you pay yourself so you can be reimbursed.
5. Do not give anyone a key, a lease or a promise to buy. Only the person with legal authority can.

Dana's mother died in Joliet in March. The house was in her mother's name only. Dana paid the March and April mortgage payments from her own account, kept every receipt, and then asked the executor, her uncle, to reimburse her from the estate once letters were issued. That small habit saved an argument later. ## What happens to the mortgage on an inherited house?

The loan stays with the house, and it does not disappear or automatically come due. The estate owes it first, since the signer's debt becomes a claim against the estate. The lender also holds a lien on the property no matter who ends up with the keys. If you never signed the note, you are not personally liable just because you inherit. If you co-signed, you are. Our guide to [the mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) lays out the options.

Illinois adds a simple practical point. The mortgage payoff comes out of the sale price at closing, and the title company pays the lender directly. Here is an example with invented numbers. An estate sells a house for $310,000 with a $92,000 payoff. The payoff and closing costs come out first, and the rest goes into the estate account, where it sits until the creditor claims period passes. Heirs do not receive a check at closing. For why, see [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate).

If a reverse mortgage is involved, call the servicer right away, because those loans run on short clocks.

## Do property taxes change when the owner dies?

The tax bill keeps coming in the same way, and someone has to pay it. Illinois property taxes are billed by the county on the assessed value, and unpaid bills can lead to a tax sale. A death does not by itself trigger a new assessment the way a sale can, but an exemption that belonged to the owner, such as a senior or homeowner exemption, may need to be applied for again in the new owner's name. Ask the county assessor or clerk where the house sits, because counties apply these rules and deadlines in their own way.

A separate protection sometimes helps a surviving family. The homestead exemption of $15,000 survives the owner's death for a surviving spouse who keeps living there, and for children until the youngest turns 18 (735 ILCS 5/12-902). It is small and only protects against creditors. Read [the Illinois homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death) before assuming it covers more. For a comparison of reassessment rules across states, see [property tax reassessment when a house passes to family](/learn/property-and-assets/property-tax-reassessment-and-transfer-tax-when-a-house-passes-to-family).

## Will you owe tax when you inherit or sell?

Probably little. Illinois has no inheritance tax. It does have an estate tax on estates over $4 million, paid by the estate, as explained in [the Illinois estate tax page](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). Federal estate tax applies only above $15 million per person in 2026.

For income tax, most inherited property gets a stepped-up basis. Your tax basis becomes the house's value on the date of death, not what the owner paid decades ago. If a house was bought for $60,000 in 1985 and was worth $240,000 at death, and you sell it for $245,000, your gain is $5,000, not $185,000. Keep the date-of-death appraisal. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) and [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property).

## Should you sell or keep it?

Sell if you do not want to be a landlord, cannot carry the costs, or share the house with people you do not agree with. Keep it if you will live there, or the numbers work as a rental. A vacant house costs real money every month. Add up taxes, insurance, utilities, upkeep and any loan, then compare that to what you would get after a sale. Our worksheet on [whether to keep or sell an inherited house](/learn/property-and-assets/should-you-keep-or-sell-an-inherited-house-a-carrying-cost-and-timing-worksheet) walks through it, and [vacant inherited house insurance](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters) covers the risks of waiting.

A common mistake our attorneys see: an heir moves in, stops paying rent anywhere else, and assumes the house is theirs. Meanwhile the will leaves it to three people. Living in the house is not ownership.

## What if siblings inherit the house together?

Co-heirs own it as co-owners, each with an undivided share, and nobody can sell the whole house alone. Illinois courts can order a partition when co-owners cannot agree, and for one house that almost always means a sale with the money split by shares. Before it gets that far, there are three practical routes.

- **A buyout.** One sibling pays the others their shares based on a recent appraisal. Settle who pays for the appraisal first.
- **A sale by agreement.** Everyone signs the listing and contract, and the proceeds are split.
- **A written co-ownership agreement.** It sets who pays what and how a sibling can exit, if the group wants to keep the house for a while.

Siblings who fight usually fight about money spent, not about the house. The page on [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) and the guide to [buying out a sibling or forcing a partition](/learn/property-and-assets/buying-out-a-sibling-or-co-owner-of-an-inherited-house-or-forcing-a-partition) cover the options.

## What if the owner is still alive and you are planning ahead?

Then you have the best chance to avoid every problem above. A recorded transfer on death instrument, a living trust, or a clear will plus a written plan among siblings can save months.
