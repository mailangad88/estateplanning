You have a baby, or one on the way, and you have realized that nothing on paper says who would raise your child or who would manage the money if something happened to you. This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), lays out the whole first plan for an Illinois couple with a baby or young children: which documents each parent signs, how they fit together, and what to do first if the due date is close.

It is the Illinois companion to the general article on [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents), and each section links to the page that covers that piece in full. When you have a guardian and a backup in mind, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-for-new-parents-in-illinois&topic=kids) to turn the list into signed documents.

## What is in a first estate plan for Illinois parents?

Six pieces, most signed once by each parent. The signing rules come from the [Illinois estate planning checklist](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-checklist-illinois).

| Piece | What it settles | Who signs | Illinois signing rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will with guardian and backup | Who raises the child if both parents are gone | Each parent | Two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3); guardian nomination under 755 ILCS 5/11-5 |
| Trust for the children inside each will | Who manages the money, and until what age | Each parent, as part of the will | Same as the will |
| Beneficiary forms | Where life insurance and retirement money goes | Each account owner | Set by each plan; a 401(k) may need spousal consent |
| Short-term guardian paper | Who can act for the child while you are alive but unavailable | Each parent, with the appointee | Two adult witnesses, no court (755 ILCS 5/11-5.4) |
| Health care power of attorney | Who makes medical decisions for you | Each parent | One adult witness, no notary (755 ILCS 45/4-10) |
| Power of attorney for property | Who pays bills and handles accounts in your name | Each parent | One witness plus a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3) |

The first three rows protect your child if you die. The last three cover the more likely event: you are alive but hurt, sick or away.

## Who raises the baby, and why name a backup?

The will is where you nominate a guardian for your child. Under 755 ILCS 5/11-5 a parent can designate a guardian in a will or other signed writing with two credible witnesses, and a judge still approves the choice. If one parent dies, the other generally keeps raising the child, so the nomination matters most when both of you are gone.

Name a backup too. A sister who says yes today may move or fall ill by the time your child is in grade school, and without a second name the court is back to hearing from whichever relatives file. Both wills should name the same people in the same order.

Choosing the person has its own page: [naming a guardian for your children in Illinois](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children) covers how to decide and what to do when spouses disagree. A short [letter of intent for your children's guardian](/learn/guardianship/letter-of-intent-for-your-childrens-guardian) passes along routines and values the will cannot hold.

## Where should the money go so a court does not have to manage it?

To a trust or a custodian you named, not to the child. New parents most often get this wrong on a beneficiary form filled out in a hurry during benefits enrollment.

When a baby is named directly, the insurer or plan will not pay. Unless the form names a custodian or trustee, someone must petition the probate court to be appointed guardian of the child's estate. That guardian manages the money under court supervision, may need a bond, files reports, and hands whatever is left to the child at 18. The page on [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) walks through the court process, and [guardian of the person vs. guardian of the estate](/learn/illinois/guardian-of-the-person-vs-guardian-of-the-estate-in-illinois) shows how Illinois splits the care role from the money role.

Parents have three ways to avoid that:

| Option | Who controls the money | When the child gets it | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Child named directly | A court-appointed guardian of the estate | At 18, all at once | Almost no one |
| UTMA custodian | The custodian you name | At 21 for transfers by will or gift (760 ILCS 20/21) | Smaller sums |
| Trust in each parent's will | The trustee you name | At ages you set, such as 25 and 30 | Life insurance and retirement money |

For most young families the trust is the answer, because life insurance turns a modest estate into a large one overnight. It is a [testamentary trust for minor children](/learn/guardianship/testamentary-trust-for-minor-children), and the trustee pays the guardian for the child's needs under rules you set. For the custodial route, read [Illinois UTMA accounts and gifts to minors](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) and [UTMA account vs. trust for a minor child](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child).

## How should the beneficiary forms read?

Each parent usually names the other as primary beneficiary and the children's trust as contingent beneficiary. If both of you die, the money lands with your trustee instead of in a court guardianship. The form controls the account, not the will, so a good will cannot fix a form that still names your baby or your college roommate.

Two Illinois points come up with new parents:

- **A 401(k) and your spouse.** For most employer plans, federal law makes your spouse the beneficiary unless the spouse signs a notarized or plan-witnessed waiver. That matters only if you want the trust as primary. The page on [401(k) and IRA beneficiaries and spousal consent in Illinois](/learn/illinois/retirement-account-beneficiaries-and-spousal-consent-in-illinois) covers it.
- **The wording of the contingent line.** Ask the insurer or plan how to name a trust created under your will. A form that just says "my children" is the direct-to-minor problem again.

The general rules for policies are in [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries), and naming a minor is covered in [naming a minor as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary). The [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you list every account in one place.

## What covers an emergency while you are both alive?

A will only speaks after a death. If both of you are hurt in the same accident, someone needs authority to make medical and school decisions for your child now. Illinois gives parents a short-term guardian paper for that. It needs two adult witnesses who are not the appointee, the appointee's signature, and no court. It lasts 365 days unless the paper sets a shorter period, and it can be signed for a child who has not yet been born (755 ILCS 5/11-5.4). A standby guardian under 755 ILCS 5/11-5.3 is the heavier tool for a serious illness, and that person must petition the court. Both are explained in [standby and short-term guardians for children in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children).

Many couples name the same person here and in the will, so the child does not move twice.

## Why does each parent need both powers of attorney?

Because each power of attorney speaks for one person, and a spouse does not automatically control the other spouse's accounts. A 401(k), a paycheck or a car loan is often in one name only. If that parent is unconscious for weeks, the other may not be able to act on it without a court case.

- **Power of attorney for property.** The Illinois statutory short form needs your signature, one witness who is not your agent, and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3). See [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
- **Health care power of attorney.** One adult witness, no notary (755 ILCS 45/4-10). See [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney). Add a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so the other parent and a backup can talk to doctors.

Without a health care agent, Illinois doctors turn to the Health Care Surrogate Act list, which puts a spouse right after a court guardian (755 ILCS 40/25). That helps until both spouses are in the same accident. Then the list skips past adult children, which new parents do not have, to your parents and siblings, who may disagree. See [who decides under the Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides). A backup agent on each form avoids that.

## What should you do first if time is short?

Finish what you can this week, then book the pieces that need formal signing:

1. **Today: fix the beneficiary forms.** Most can be changed online. Name each other first and remove anyone who no longer belongs. If a contingent line names your baby, switch it to the trust once step 5 is done.
2. **This week: agree on a guardian and a backup, and ask them.** Nothing else can be drafted until you have names.
3. **This week: sign health care powers of attorney.** One adult witness each, no notary.
4. **Before the birth or a planned absence: sign a short-term guardian paper.** It can cover an unborn child.
5. **Within a month: sign the wills, the children's trust and the property powers of attorney** with an Illinois estate planning attorney. Then go back and name the trust as contingent beneficiary on every form.

A will signed before the baby still works. Illinois gives a child born after a will an intestate share unless the will provides for or disinherits that child (755 ILCS 5/4-10); see [children born after an Illinois will](/learn/illinois/children-born-after-an-illinois-will-and-lapsed-gifts). That default adds no guardian and no trust, so update the will. The [new parents kit](/free/new-parents-kit) puts the guardian, money and medical decisions on a few pages you can fill in together.

## Example: how one Illinois couple put it together

This is an invented example. Andre and Mei live in Evanston with a four-month-old daughter, Lila. They own their condo jointly. Andre has a 401(k) and a term life policy of $500,000. Mei has group life insurance through work worth twice her salary. Neither has a will.

Andre's policy still names his brother from before the marriage, and Mei's names "my children" as contingent beneficiary. If both died, Lila's share would need a court guardian of the estate and would be hers outright at 18.

They choose Mei's sister in Naperville as guardian and Andre's close friend as backup, and they ask both. They sign health care powers of attorney that weekend, naming each other first and Mei's sister second, and a short-term guardian paper naming the sister. Three weeks later each signs a will naming the same guardians and creating a trust for Lila, with Andre's cousin, an accountant, as trustee and distributions at 25 and 30. They then change both policies and the 401(k) contingent line to the trust. They used the [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) to check the insurance amount.
