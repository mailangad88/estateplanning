You signed a will years ago, and something no longer fits. The executor you named has moved away, a grandchild has arrived, you remarried, or you want the house to go to a different child. You want to know the least you can do that will still hold up in an Illinois probate court.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), starts from the change you want and shows what it takes. The statutory rules on revocation and revival have their own page, [changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois). If you would rather go through your current will with someone, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-to-change-a-will-in-illinois&topic=will) and bring the original.

## What are the ways to change a will in Illinois?

There are three. Anything else, such as a note, an email to your executor or a talk with your family, leaves the old will in force.

1. **A codicil.** A short amendment that names the will by date, says what it changes and leaves the rest standing.
2. **A new will.** A complete replacement that says it revokes all earlier wills and codicils.
3. **Revocation without a replacement.** Destroying the original on purpose, or signing an attested revocation, as 755 ILCS 5/4-7 allows. With nothing in its place, your probate estate then passes under the intestacy rules in 755 ILCS 5/2-1, explained in [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

The first two are signed the same way as any Illinois will: in writing, signed by you, and attested in your presence by at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3). The general comparison of codicils and new wills, for any state, is in [changing a will: codicils, new wills and revocation](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils).

## Which change needs what?

| The change you want | What it takes in Illinois | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Replace the executor or add a backup | Codicil or new will | Name a backup too, so the court does not have to pick one |
| Name a different guardian for minor children | Codicil or new will | Name a second choice in case the first cannot serve |
| Add a gift to a grandchild, friend or charity | Codicil or new will | The witnesses must not be that person or their spouse (755 ILCS 5/4-6) |
| Fix a misspelled name or wrong address | Codicil | Never fix it in pen on the original |
| Remove a beneficiary or cut a gift | New will, usually | A clean document is easier to defend if someone objects later |
| Change who gets the house or the residue | New will | Check the deed, any transfer on death instrument and any trust |
| Several changes, or a codicil already exists | New will with a revocation clause | Read the old will and every codicil first |
| You married | New will | Your spouse can renounce the will (755 ILCS 5/2-8) |
| You divorced | New will | The statute removes only the former spouse (755 ILCS 5/4-7(b)) |
| A child was born after you signed | Codicil or new will | Without one, 755 ILCS 5/4-10 may give the child an intestate share |
| Cancel the will completely | New will, attested revocation, or destroying the original | Destroying a copy does nothing |
| Change who gets an IRA, life insurance or POD account | The company's beneficiary form | The will cannot override the form |

The last row catches people every year. See [which controls: will, trust, joint account or beneficiary form](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form) and use the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) to list every form.

## Can you cross things out or write on your will?

It will not do what you hoped, and it can do something you did not intend. Illinois lets a will be revoked by burning, cancelling, tearing or obliterating it (755 ILCS 5/4-7). A heavy line through a paragraph can therefore be argued as a partial cancellation of that paragraph. Words you write in the margin were never signed in front of two witnesses, so they add nothing.

Put those together and a pen edit often produces the worst of both outcomes. Suppose you strike out "my brother Ray" as executor and write "my daughter Nora" above it. The court may find that Ray's nomination was cancelled, while Nora's name has no legal effect. Now no executor is named, and the court appoints one. A relative may also argue that someone else made the marks.

An original last in your hands that turns up altered, or not at all, also invites the presumption of revocation explained in [when the original will cannot be found in Illinois](/learn/illinois/lost-original-will-in-illinois). Leave the original clean. [Handwritten wills in Illinois](/learn/illinois/handwritten-wills-in-illinois) explains why handwriting never replaces the witnesses.

## Does a codicil need witnesses in Illinois?

Yes. A codicil is signed and witnessed with the same formalities as a will, so it needs your signature and two credible witnesses who watch you sign and then sign in your presence (755 ILCS 5/4-3). An unwitnessed codicil is just a note, and a notary does not replace a witness.

Four points apply when you sign a change:

- **Pick witnesses who gain nothing,** especially when the codicil adds someone. A gift to a witness, or to a witness's spouse, can be void unless the will has enough other disinterested witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-6).
- **Add an attestation clause.** A short statement the witnesses sign lets the court admit the document without their live testimony (755 ILCS 5/6-4). See the [self-proving affidavit](/glossary/self-proving-affidavit) entry.
- **You can sign remotely in some cases.** The Electronic Wills and Remote Witnesses Act (755 ILCS 6) allows video witnessing and fully electronic wills if its steps are followed, including naming Illinois as the place of signing. See [how to sign a will or trust remotely in Illinois](/learn/illinois/sign-will-or-trust-remotely-in-illinois).
- **Capacity is judged on the day you sign the change.** The person signing needs to be of sound mind and memory, just as for the original (755 ILCS 5/4-1). If health is a concern, read [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).

The full witness rules are on our page on [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements), and the free [Illinois signing requirements cheat sheet](/free/illinois-signing-requirements-cheat-sheet) fits on one page.

## Example: one change, then several

This is an invented example. Gloria, 67, a widow in Peoria, signed a will in 2015. It names her brother Ray as executor, leaves her house to her son Ben and daughter Nora equally, and splits everything else between them.

**First change.** Ray is now 80 and lives out of state, so Gloria wants Nora as executor with Ben as backup. That is one clean edit, so she signs a one-page codicil that replaces the executor paragraph and confirms the rest of the 2015 will. Two neighbors who inherit nothing watch her sign and sign an attestation clause. She keeps the codicil with the original.

**What almost went wrong.** Before the appointment, Gloria had written "and my late husband's coins go to my grandson Eli" in the margin of the original. That line has no effect, because nobody witnessed it. Her attorney puts the coin gift in the codicil instead and notes the stray line in the file.

**Second round.** Two years later Gloria marries Walt. She wants Walt to live in the house for life, the house to go to Ben and Nora after that, and a new guardian named for Eli's trust share. Three changes on top of a codicil, plus a marriage, call for a new will that revokes the 2015 will and the codicil. It also has to account for Walt's right to renounce the will and take a statutory share (755 ILCS 5/2-8), so couples like Gloria and Walt often add a trust or a written agreement. See [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death).

## How do you revoke an Illinois will completely?

Sign a new will that revokes it, sign an attested revocation, or destroy the original yourself or at your direction in your presence (755 ILCS 5/4-7). Tearing up a copy does nothing, and revoking with no replacement leaves your estate to intestacy. Revival rules are on [changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois).

## What happens to your Illinois will after marriage, divorce or a new baby?

- **Marriage.** An Illinois will is not revoked by a change in marital status (755 ILCS 5/4-7(b)). A new spouse who is left out can renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if you leave descendants, or one-half if you do not, by filing within 7 months after the will is admitted (755 ILCS 5/2-8). For blended families, see [what happens if you remarry and never update your will](/learn/blended-families/remarried-without-updating-your-will).
- **Divorce.** After the judgment, gifts to the former spouse and nominations of the former spouse as executor or trustee are treated as if the ex died before you (755 ILCS 5/4-7(b)). Gifts to the ex's relatives stand. See [how divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan), and the free [divorce and remarriage beneficiary checklist](/free/illinois-divorce-and-remarriage-beneficiary-checklist) covers the forms.
- **A new child.** A child born after you sign takes an intestate share unless the will provides for that child or shows an intent to disinherit (755 ILCS 5/4-10). See [children born after an Illinois will](/learn/illinois/children-born-after-an-illinois-will-and-lapsed-gifts).

## What should you do with the old will and the codicil afterward?

Keep the codicil with the original will, and tell your executor where both are. After a death, whoever holds the will must file it with the circuit court clerk (755 ILCS 5/6-1), and a codicil in a different drawer can be missed. See [filing the original will with the circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk).

Do not destroy an old original until the new will is signed and stored. Many people leave it with their attorney, marked as revoked. Two signed wills with no clear revocation can end up in court, as described in [two wills that conflict](/learn/what-if/two-wills-that-conflict).

## What steps should you follow to change your will?

1. **Find the signed original** and any codicils, and read them in order.
2. **Write the result you want,** one sentence per person or asset.
3. **Use the table above** to decide between a codicil and a new will. The free [codicil or new will decision guide](/free/codicil-vs-new-will-decision-guide) helps.
4. **Check the papers outside the will:** deeds, any trust, and every beneficiary form.
5. **Sign with two disinterested witnesses** and an attestation clause. The [will signing day checklist](/free/will-signing-day-checklist) lists what to bring.
6. **Store the new papers together** and tell your executor the location. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
7. **Set a review date.** The [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) can prompt you each year or after a life event.

For whether these steps need a lawyer, see [do I need a lawyer for a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/do-i-need-a-lawyer-for-a-will-in-illinois).
