You have heard that a living trust "avoids probate," and you have also heard that a simple will is all most people need. Both can be true in Illinois, depending on what you own and who depends on you. What makes the Illinois answer different is a third option that sits between the two: a will paired with a recorded transfer on death instrument for the house and up-to-date beneficiary forms on your accounts.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), sorts common Illinois situations into those three options, explains when a will is enough, and shows what a trust adds for children. The general comparison of wills and trusts is on [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust), so this page sticks to the Illinois rules that change the answer. If you would rather talk your own situation through, you can [book a consultation about a will or a trust](/book?from=learn-will-vs-trust-in-illinois&topic=will-or-trust).

## Does a will avoid probate in Illinois?

No. A will is the set of instructions the probate court admits and carries out, so anything that passes under it goes through the court. The page on [how to avoid probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-avoid-probate-in-illinois) makes the same point asset by asset.

There is one narrow exception. If what you leave in your own name is personal property of $150,000 or less, not counting registered vehicles, and nobody has asked for letters of office, an heir can collect it with a sworn [small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) given to the bank (755 ILCS 5/25-1). The affidavit cannot transfer real estate. So a will-only plan for someone who owns a house in their own name almost always means an Illinois probate case.

The person holding your original will must deliver it to the circuit clerk within 30 days after the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). After the court admits it, creditors have until the later of six months from first publication or three months from mailed notice to file claims (755 ILCS 5/18-3), and anyone with an interest has six months to contest the will (755 ILCS 5/8-1). Those periods mean even a simple estate rarely closes much sooner than six months. Timing is covered in [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois), and the cost items are listed in [what probate costs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs).

## What is the middle option between a will and a trust?

It is a will that handles only the leftovers, while each large asset has its own route around the court:

- **The house:** a [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) (TODI), signed with two credible witnesses and a notary, and recorded with the county recorder before you die (755 ILCS 27/40). An unrecorded TODI is void.
- **Bank accounts:** a payable-on-death designation filed with the bank (205 ILCS 625/4). The page on [payable-on-death and joint bank accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) explains why a POD is usually safer than adding a child as a joint owner.
- **Retirement accounts and life insurance:** the beneficiary form, with a backup named on every form. See [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).
- **Everything else:** the will, and if the remainder is small and contains no land, the small estate affidavit.

This middle option avoids probate for most estates without a trust. Its limits show up in three places. A TODI does nothing while you are alive, and an agent under a power of attorney cannot create or revoke one for you. It and the beneficiary forms pay adults outright and pay minors into a court process. And they do not shield the house from your creditors or a surviving spouse's statutory claims (755 ILCS 27/85 and 27/66). The side-by-side detail for the house alone is on [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house).

## Which Illinois situations fit which plan?

Find the row closest to your family. "Fits" means the option usually does the job; "Gaps" means it leaves a problem you would want to solve another way.

| Your situation | Will alone | Will plus TODI and beneficiary forms | Living trust with pour-over will |
|---|---|---|---|
| Renter, savings and a 401(k), adult heirs | Fits if forms are current | Fits; POD on the bank account | Usually more than you need |
| Paid-off house, one or two adult children who get along | Gaps: the house goes to probate | Fits | Works, at more setup effort |
| Parents of children under 18 | Gaps: names a guardian, but money for kids goes through court | Gaps: a minor cannot take a house or a payout directly | Fits; holds money to the ages you pick |
| A child or heir who receives SSI or Medicaid | Gaps | Gaps: a direct gift can affect benefits | Fits, with a special needs trust |
| Second marriage, children from a first marriage | Gaps | Gaps: no way to give a spouse lifetime use first | Fits |
| Worried about dementia or a long illness | Gaps: a will does nothing during life | Gaps: needs a strong power of attorney | Fits, with a power of attorney for the rest |
| Real estate in another state too | Gaps: a second probate there | Depends on that state's rules | Fits if the deed is recorded there |
| Couple near the Illinois $4 million estate tax line | Gaps | Gaps | Fits only with tax language added |

Two rows deserve a note. For the estate tax row, Illinois tests the whole estate against $4 million and allows no portability between spouses, and a revocable trust does not reduce that tax by itself; see [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). For the blended family row, the page on [blended family inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/blended-family-inheritance) explains how a trust can give a spouse the right to live in the house before it passes to your children.

## When is a will enough in Illinois?

A will by itself is enough when nothing large is left in your name alone. That usually means you rent, your accounts already name living adult beneficiaries with backups, and your heirs are adults who can handle money. In that case, whatever the will controls may be small enough for the affidavit, and the will's main jobs are naming an executor and covering stray property.

A will plus a TODI is enough for many homeowners whose children are grown. The [do I need a will](/learn/illinois/do-i-need-a-will) page covers who needs a will at all, and [how to make a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-make-a-will-in-illinois) walks through signing it with two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3).

## What does a trust do for kids in Illinois?

It keeps a child's inheritance out of court and lets you decide when the child receives it. Without a trust or custodian, a minor who inherits generally needs a court-appointed guardian of the estate, who reports to the court and may need a bond, and the child receives whatever is left at 18, all at once. The page on [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) describes that process. A UTMA custodian avoids the court but turns property over at 21 (760 ILCS 20/21), as [Illinois UTMA accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) explains.

A living trust can instead hold the money with a successor trustee you chose, pay for school and health, and release shares at ages like 25 and 30. The beneficiary forms on life insurance and retirement accounts can name the trust, so those payouts land in the same place.

One job stays with the will. Illinois lets a parent nominate a guardian for a minor child in a will or other writing signed with two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/11-5), and a trust cannot do it. A trust-based plan therefore includes a [pour-over will](/learn/illinois/pour-over-wills-in-illinois) that names the guardian and sends leftovers to the trustee (755 ILCS 5/4-4). See [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children) and, for young families generally, [estate planning for new parents in Illinois](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-for-new-parents-in-illinois).

## What does a trust require in Illinois?

It needs a written document under the Illinois Trust Code (760 ILCS 3) that says expressly it is revocable (760 ILCS 3/602), and it needs funding. The trust controls only property titled to the trustee or paid to it by a beneficiary form, so the house needs a recorded deed into the trust, or it stays in probate; see [unfunded living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/unfunded-living-trust-in-illinois). The rules are on [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts), and [can I make my own living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-i-make-my-own-living-trust-in-illinois) covers kits.

## Worked example: the Okafor family in Joliet

This is an invented example. Chidi, 41, and Grace, 39, own a house in joint tenancy in Joliet. They have a checking account, two 401(k) plans, term life insurance, and two children, ages 6 and 3.

**Option one, will alone.** Each signs a will leaving everything to the other, then to the children, and nominating Grace's sister as guardian. If both die together, the house ends up in the last survivor's name and goes to probate, and the insurance and 401(k) forms name the children as backups. A guardian of each child's estate is appointed, and each child receives the remainder at 18.

**Option two, will plus TODI and forms.** They record a TODI and add POD designations. Probate shrinks, but the TODI names two minors, so the house and the insurance money still land in a guardianship.

**Option three, trust.** They sign a joint revocable trust, deed the house to themselves as trustees, and change the backup beneficiary on the life insurance and 401(k) forms to the trust. Their pour-over wills nominate Grace's sister as guardian of the children and Chidi's brother as successor trustee. If both die, the brother manages one fund for both children and pays out shares at 25 and 30. If Chidi has a stroke at 50, Grace keeps running the trust assets without going to court.

For this family, the trust does what the other options cannot. Ten years from now, with the children grown and steady, a will and a TODI might be enough again.

## How do you decide for your own family?

1. **List each asset and how it is titled today.** Note anything in your name alone.
2. **Check every beneficiary form** for a living adult primary and a backup.
3. **Ask the four trust questions:** Is an heir under 25, on benefits, or not good with money? Is there a second marriage? Would an illness leave no one able to manage the house? Do you own land in another state?
4. **If every answer is no,** a will plus a recorded TODI and current forms is usually a sound Illinois plan.
5. **If any answer is yes,** price a trust and plan to fund it the same month you sign it, using [funding a living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/funding-a-living-trust-in-illinois).
6. **Either way,** sign a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) and revisit the choice after a birth, a purchase, a marriage or a death.
