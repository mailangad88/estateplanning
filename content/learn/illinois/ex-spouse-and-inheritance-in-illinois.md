Divorce ends a marriage, but the paperwork behind it may stay in place for years. A will still names the ex as executor. A policy still lists the ex as beneficiary. A joint account still has both names. This page explains what Illinois law does automatically after a divorce and what stays in place unless you fix it.

For a wider look at divorce and planning, read [how divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan). This page is the narrower question: who can still collect, from which document. Our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) lists other topics.

## Does an ex-spouse inherit in Illinois if there is no will?

No. A former spouse is not a spouse for intestacy. The Probate Act's list of heirs starts with a surviving spouse (755 ILCS 5/2-1), and a person whose marriage was ended by a judgment is no longer one. The estate goes to children, then parents and siblings, as in [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). A former spouse also has no right to renounce a will or to claim a spouse's award, which are covered in [a surviving spouse's rights](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death).

Children from the marriage are heirs in their own right. They may hold shares in the estate that an ex-spouse then manages as their guardian if they are minors. That is one reason to name a guardian of the estate in your will.

## What does a divorce do to a will and a trust?

The Illinois Probate Act says that a judgment of dissolution of marriage revokes the provisions of a will that benefit the former spouse, including any appointment as executor, and the will is read as if the former spouse had died (755 ILCS 5/4-7). Illinois has a similar rule for revocable trusts. As we understand it, the Trusts and Dissolutions of Marriage Act (760 ILCS 35) treats provisions benefiting an ex-spouse in a trust as revoked after dissolution, unless the trust or the judgment says otherwise.

The word "unless" is common. A remarriage to the same person can restore a gift. A document may state a different intent. A pending divorce does not trigger the revocation at all, because the person is still a spouse.

## What happens to beneficiary forms after a divorce?

It depends on the type of account. Illinois has a statute that revokes a former spouse's designation on certain life insurance policies, annuities and retirement accounts when the divorce judgment is entered (750 ILCS 5/503). It does not apply when the judgment orders otherwise, and it cannot override federal rules for employer plans. The U.S. Supreme Court has held that plans under ERISA generally pay the person named on the form. Our page on [an ex-spouse still named as beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) explains the idea.

| Document | Does Illinois cancel the ex? | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Yes, gifts and executor role revoked | Remarriage or contrary wording can change it |
| Revocable trust | Generally yes under 760 ILCS 35 | Check the trust and the judgment |
| Life insurance, individual policy | Often yes under 750 ILCS 5/503 | Judgment can require keeping the ex |
| Employer 401(k) or pension under ERISA | Not reliably | The plan pays the named person |
| IRA | Often yes, depending on the account type | Confirm with the custodian |
| Joint bank account | No automatic change | Survivor may keep it unless changed |
| Property in joint tenancy | Divorce usually severs; check judgment | Fix the title |

## What should you do after your divorce is final?

Do not wait for the statute to save you. Work through the list in order.

1. Get a certified copy of the judgment and read it for any requirement to keep life insurance or a retirement beneficiary.
2. Sign a new will and, if you have one, a new trust that names new people.
3. Replace the executor, trustee, health care agent and property agent. See [revoking a power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney).
4. File new beneficiary forms for every life insurance policy, IRA, 401(k) and annuity. Keep the confirmation.
5. Update payable on death accounts and any transfer on death instrument. See [payable on death and joint accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts).
6. Retitle the house and any joint accounts to match the judgment.
7. Run the [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) and mark a date to check again.

## Example: Marcus and the old 401(k) form

Marcus, 52, divorced Dana in 2019. His will, signed in 2010, names Dana as executor and gives her everything. His employer 401(k), worth $180,000, still names Dana. Marcus dies in 2026. The will's gifts and executor appointment to Dana are revoked under 755 ILCS 5/4-7, so his two children take through the will. But the 401(k) is governed by federal law, and the plan administrator pays Dana because the form was never changed. Marcus's children can only seek recovery from Dana if the divorce judgment or another document supports it. A five-minute form would have avoided this.

## What if a divorce is pending?

While the case is open, the other spouse is still a spouse. If you die, the spouse may inherit under intestacy, renounce the will or claim the award. Illinois courts may enter orders that bar changes to insurance or beneficiaries while a case is pending, so ask an attorney before signing new forms. See [death during a divorce](/learn/what-if/death-during-divorce).

## What mistakes cause an ex to collect anyway?

Most cases involve forms that were never updated. An old beneficiary form on an employer plan, an IRA custodian that used a paper form from years ago, or a life insurance policy that was changed on paper but not in the insurer's system can all pay the wrong person. People also forget that a divorce judgment may require them to keep an ex as beneficiary for a time, to secure support. Removing the ex without checking the judgment can lead to a claim against the estate. Another mistake is changing only the will, which does not reach accounts with beneficiaries.

Keep a one-page list of every account, the beneficiary on file and the date you last confirmed it. Ask for written confirmation after each change. Review it each year, and again after any marriage, divorce, birth or death in the family.
