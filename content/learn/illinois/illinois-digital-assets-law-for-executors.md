Your mother's phone is locked, her email holds the utility bills, and the photos you want are in a cloud account that nobody remembers setting up. You are the executor, you have your letters of office, and you assume that is enough. With online accounts, it often is not.

Illinois has a statute for exactly this, found in 755 ILCS 70, and it draws a line between the content of messages and everything else. This page explains the line, the paperwork a company can ask for and what you can do now so your own executor is not stuck. Our national guide to [fiduciary access laws](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained) gives the wider background, and the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) lists related topics.

## The short version

- Message content (what was said in the emails and texts) is harder to get than the account list.
- Your consent in a will, trust or power of attorney, or a setting in an online tool, matters a great deal.
- Expect to send a certified death certificate and a certified copy of your letters of office.
- A court order is the fallback when a company will not cooperate or nobody consented.

## What does the Illinois law actually say?

We read two sections of the Illinois statute that apply to a deceased user's personal representative. They sit side by side because the law treats two kinds of information differently.

| | Section 7: content of communications | Section 8: other digital assets |
|---|---|---|
| What it covers | The content of electronic communications the user sent or received | A catalogue of those communications (without content) and the user's other digital assets |
| When a company must release it | Only if the user consented or a court directs disclosure | Unless the user prohibited disclosure or a court directs otherwise |
| Who asks | The personal representative | The personal representative |

The text of section 8 shows it came from Public Act 99-775, effective August 12, 2016. The two sections are 755 ILCS 70/7 and 755 ILCS 70/8.

## Can an Illinois executor read a dead person's email?

Not automatically. Section 7 says disclosure of the content of electronic communications applies only if the deceased user consented to disclosure or a court directs it (755 ILCS 70/7). If you never said anything, and nobody has a court order, the email provider can refuse.

The reasoning is privacy, and it protects the people who wrote to the user as well. A letter in a filing cabinet belongs to the estate, while an email sits on a company's server under federal privacy law and the company's own terms.

Consent can take several forms. The statute asks for a copy of a record showing the user's consent, such as a will, trust or power of attorney. That requirement drops away if the user gave direction through an online tool (755 ILCS 70/7). Think of a setting inside an account that names someone who may see content after death.

## What can the executor get without that consent?

Quite a lot. Section 8 says that unless the user prohibited disclosure or a court directs otherwise, the custodian must give the representative a catalogue of the electronic communications the user sent or received, and the user's other digital assets (755 ILCS 70/8). The catalogue and the assets exclude the content of the communications.

In plain terms, you can learn that your father exchanged forty messages with a bank last spring and when they were sent, without reading them. For an executor trying to find a missing account or a forgotten subscription, that list can be the clue that matters most.

## What paperwork does the company expect?

For content, the personal representative gives the custodian these items (755 ILCS 70/7):

1. A written request for disclosure, on paper or electronically.
2. A certified copy of the death certificate.
3. A certified copy of the letter of appointment, or a court order.
4. A copy of a record showing the user's consent, such as a will, trust or power of attorney. This is not needed if the user used an online tool.
5. If the custodian asks, an account identifier, evidence linking the account to the user, or a court finding.

The court finding the custodian can ask for says the user had a specific account, that disclosure would not violate federal communications privacy laws or other law, that the user consented (unless the user used an online tool), or that disclosure is permitted and reasonably necessary to administer the estate.

For the catalogue and other assets, the list is shorter: a written request, the certified death certificate and the certified letters or court order (755 ILCS 70/8). A custodian may also ask for an identifier, evidence linking the account to the user, an affidavit that disclosure is reasonably necessary for administration of the estate, or a court finding.

Our [letters of office article](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) explains how to get certified copies, and the guide on [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need) helps with counting.

## What if a company says no?

Ask what exactly it needs, in writing, and answer each point. Many refusals come from missing paperwork. If the company still refuses, or consent is missing and you need the content to settle the estate, the statute points to the court. A judge can direct disclosure, and the court finding in section 7 is the document that custodians tend to want.

That means a probate petition on this issue. Go in with the account identifier and a short reason why the content is reasonably necessary, such as finding a business contract or proof of a payment. Consider this for a person like Gerald, 71, whose estate includes a small consulting business run through a personal email address. His daughter, the executor, needs the invoices. Without his consent, a court finding that disclosure is reasonably necessary to administer the estate is the route.

## What should you do now so your executor has an easier time?

Four small steps do most of the work.

1. Add a clause to your will and your [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) that consents to disclosure of the content of your electronic communications to your executor, agent or a named person. Write it plainly.
2. Use any account-level tool that lets you name someone, and keep that choice in line with your will.
3. Keep a list of accounts and where the passwords or keys are stored, without writing passwords in the will itself, because a will becomes a court record. Our [online accounts after death checklist](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) gives a format.
4. Decide whether someone should be named specifically for digital matters. The [digital executor guide](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) explains how that role fits with the executor.

If you do not want the executor reading something, say so. Section 8 allows the user to prohibit disclosure of the catalogue and other assets, and section 7 turns on consent, so silence is not the same as permission.

## Where does this law stop?

It governs access for a personal representative to online accounts held by a custodian. It does not decide who owns a cryptocurrency wallet that you control with a key, and it does not erase a company's own terms of service in every case. For crypto, read [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance). For the executor's overall job, see [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties). We did not read the other sections of the Illinois statute for this page, including those for agents and trustees, so treat this as a starting point.
