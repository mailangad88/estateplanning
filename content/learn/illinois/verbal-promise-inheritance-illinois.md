Maybe your mother said for twenty years that the house is yours, because you were the one who drove her to appointments. Maybe two brothers shook hands on who keeps the family home. Maybe your father said he did not need a will because the kids would work it out. Then someone dies, and a sibling, a bank or a title company asks for paper.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), covers four things. It explains why a spoken promise usually does not decide who inherits. It shows what the law does instead. It describes the narrow claims a relative may bring. And it shows how to put the promise on paper while the person who made it is still here. If your family is already at that point, a [consultation](/book?from=learn-verbal-promise-inheritance-illinois&topic=will) can sort out where you stand.

## Does a verbal promise count as a will in Illinois?

No. Illinois requires a will to be in writing, signed by the person making it (or by someone else at their direction and in their presence), and attested in the signer's presence by at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-1 and 4-3). Our page on [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) covers who can witness and how a will becomes self-proving. A recorded voice message or video does not fix the problem, because the statute asks for a writing.

Illinois also does not have a general rule that a handwritten will without witnesses is valid. The family often discovers this too late, when a note in a drawer says "the house goes to Denise" and nobody watched it being signed. The general rule about notes and spoken wishes is explained in [do notes, letters and spoken wishes bind your family](/learn/wills/notes-letters-and-spoken-wishes-do-they-bind-your-family).

## Why does a house need more than a promise?

Real estate is transferred by signed paperwork, and agreements about land are generally required to be in writing. A bank moves money because of an account title, a beneficiary form or a court order. A county recorder records a deed, a recorded transfer on death instrument or a court order. None of them records a memory.

Here is how the pieces compare.

| What the family says | What the paperwork shows | Who gets it |
|---|---|---|
| "Mom always said the house is mine" | House titled in Mom's name alone, no will | Mom's heirs under 755 ILCS 5/2-1 |
| "Dad promised me the savings" | Account in Dad's name, no beneficiary | Dad's estate, then heirs by statute |
| "We agreed Lena would keep the house" | Both siblings listed as heirs | Shared among the heirs |
| "Grandpa said the farm stays with me" | Signed, witnessed will naming the grandchild | The grandchild, after probate |
| "Mom said the account is yours" | You are named payable on death | You, directly, with a death certificate |

The last two rows show that paper beats talk in both directions. A beneficiary form on an account also beats a will. Our page on [Illinois payable on death and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) explains that rule.

## What happens instead when there is no will?

Illinois treats the person as intestate and divides the probate estate by statute (755 ILCS 5/2-1). With a spouse and children, the spouse takes one-half and the children share the other half. With no spouse, the children share everything equally. A child who died earlier is replaced by that child's own children. The full order, with examples, is in [who inherits if you die without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

Example. Carol, 79, owns a house in Cicero in her name alone. For years she tells her daughter Rosa, who lives with her, that the house will be Rosa's. Carol dies without a will. She also has a son, Martin, and a second daughter, Elena. The statute gives each of the three children one-third of the house. Rosa's years of caregiving earn her no extra share unless the family agrees to one.

Two points follow. First, the house is in Carol's name, so someone must be appointed by the court to handle the estate before anyone can deed it. Our page on [what to do when a parent dies with the house in their name](/learn/illinois/parent-died-house-in-their-name) lays out the steps, and [heirship affidavit versus probate](/learn/illinois/heirship-affidavit-vs-probate) explains why an affidavit alone often does not do the job. Second, if the siblings do agree that Rosa should keep the house, they can carry that out in writing during the estate, for example through deeds or a buyout. That is a family decision made after death, not a promise enforced by the court.

## Can a family member ever make a claim?

Sometimes, but it is the exception and it is hard. Two kinds of claim come up, described here in general terms.

- **A claim for repayment.** A sibling who paid a parent's bills, taxes or repairs, or who provided care under an understanding that they would be paid, may file a claim against the estate. Illinois has a claims process and time limits for claims against an estate, and unfiled claims are barred two years after death at the outside (755 ILCS 5/18-12). The claimant needs proof of the arrangement and the amounts.
- **A claim based on an agreement.** A person who gave up a job, moved in, or spent money because a parent made a promise may ask a court to enforce it or to award money instead. Courts look at whether there is a clear agreement, whether the person really relied on it, and whether the agreement meets the writing rules. A vague "everyone knew" does not do it.

All three are litigation, with legal costs, delay and strained relationships. They depend on evidence that rarely survives informally: receipts, bank records, emails, letters. A claim that rests only on what the person who died supposedly said is the weakest kind. Talk it through with an Illinois estate planning attorney before spending money on a lawsuit. A challenge to an existing will is a different fight; see [the six-month deadline to contest an Illinois will](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline).

## What is the heirs' property problem?

When a house passes to several relatives without a probate, ownership can spread across generations while the deed stays in the name of someone who died long ago. This is called [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property), and it is common in older neighborhoods where houses stayed in families for decades because "everybody knew whose house it was."

How it grows. Teresa dies in 1998 and leaves a house on her own. No estate is opened. Her three children each hold a one-third share. One child dies in 2015, and her two children inherit that share. Now four people own the house: two at one-third each and two at one-sixth each. If one of those four dies without a will, the count grows again. Nobody holds a deed in their own name.

The consequences are practical:

- A buyer or title company will not close without the signatures of every owner or a court order.
- A lender will not make a mortgage or home equity loan on unclear title.
- One co-owner can generally ask a court to divide or sell the property, which is called partition. The free [co-owners and partition guide](/free/illinois-inherited-house-co-owners-and-partition-guide) goes through the options.
- Property tax bills still arrive, and whoever pays them has no written agreement to be repaid.

Clearing title means opening an estate for each owner who died, in order, and proving who the heirs are. See [proving heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois) and the checklist in [clearing title to inherited Illinois property](/free/illinois-inherited-property-clear-title-guide). When the owners disagree about what to do with the house, [what to do when siblings disagree about an inherited house](/learn/illinois/siblings-disagree-inherited-house) covers the next steps.

## How do you turn the promise into paper now?

If you are the parent, or the person who made the promise, this takes less time than most people expect. If you are the child who was promised, the useful move is to raise it gently and offer to help with the paperwork. Do not ask a parent with memory problems to sign anything on your own; a neutral attorney should be in the room. Our page on [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure) explains why.

1. **Decide what the promise really is.** "The house goes to Rosa" and "Rosa lives in the house, then it is split three ways" are different plans. Write the sentence the way you mean it, including what happens if Rosa dies first.
2. **Look at the title.** Find out how the house is held and whether there is a mortgage. A deed copy is available from the county recorder.
3. **Choose the document.** A signed and witnessed will gives the house to a named person through probate. A [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) passes a house outside probate if it is signed, witnessed, notarized and recorded before the owner's death. A [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) works when you want to avoid probate and spell out conditions, such as a sibling's right to live there.
4. **Think about the other children.** A house to one child with nothing for the others is a classic cause of lawsuits. Many families balance it with life insurance, an account or a payout from the child who keeps the house. See [can you disinherit a child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-illinois) for the legal side.
5. **Sign with the formalities.** Two witnesses who gain nothing under the document, and a notary for a transfer on death instrument.
6. **Keep it findable.** Tell the executor where the original is. Illinois requires whoever holds a will to file it with the circuit court clerk after a death (755 ILCS 5/6-1).
7. **Review after any change.** A sale of the house, a divorce, or the death of the named child makes the document out of date.

If the promise was between siblings, write it down together. A short signed agreement about who gets the house, who pays what and what happens if one of you dies is far better than a handshake. It does not replace a will, and a parent's own plan controls the parent's property, but it records what you each expect.
