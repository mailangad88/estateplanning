You have noticed missing money, a new "friend" who answers the phone, or a parent who suddenly cannot explain where the checks went. In Illinois the first official call is usually to Adult Protective Services, known as APS, which investigates reports of financial exploitation of older adults.

This page covers who qualifies, how to report, what happens afterward, and the planning steps that make exploitation harder. For warning signs and scam types, see our general article on [protecting older adults from financial exploitation](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse). This is general education, not legal advice, and part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## How do you report elder financial abuse in Illinois?

Call the Adult Protective Services hotline at 1-866-800-1409, or 1-888-206-1327 for TTY. Anyone who suspects abuse, abandonment, neglect, financial exploitation or self-neglect may report to the Department on Aging or a designated provider agency (320 ILCS 20/4). You do not need proof. You need a reasonable concern and the facts you have.

Before you call, write down:

1. The older adult's name, age, address and phone number.
2. Who you suspect and how they are connected to the person.
3. What you saw: dates, amounts, checks, withdrawals, a new will or deed, a changed power of attorney.
4. Whether the person has dementia or another condition that affects their judgment.
5. Whether anyone is in immediate danger. If so, call 911 first.

Keep copies of what you describe. If there is a clear theft, also file a police report, because APS refers criminal conduct to law enforcement but is not itself a police agency.

## Who counts as an eligible adult?

APS serves an "eligible adult," which the Act defines as an adult with disabilities aged 18 to 59, or a person 60 or older, who lives in a domestic living situation and is abused, abandoned, neglected or financially exploited, or who neglects themselves (320 ILCS 20/2). Some adults in certain licensed facilities qualify under limited conditions. If the person lives in a nursing home or assisted living, call the hotline anyway and let intake staff decide where the report belongs.

### What counts as financial exploitation?

The Act defines it as using an eligible adult's resources to that adult's disadvantage or for someone else's benefit. In practice that covers:

- An agent under a power of attorney paying their own bills from the principal's account.
- A caregiver or relative taking cash, adding themselves to an account, or running up the person's credit cards.
- Pressure to sign a deed, a loan or a new will.
- A "romance" or "friend" relationship that keeps draining savings.

Not everything is covered. The Act's definition of abuse leaves out disputes such as telemarketing scams, consumer fraud, home repair disputes and landlord-tenant conflicts. A stranger's phone scam is better reported to the bank and the police.

## What happens after you make a report?

The path below follows the Adult Protective Services Act, though local practice varies.

1. **Intake.** The provider agency receives the report and screens it. Where there is a risk of injury or death, agencies must be able to respond 24 hours a day (320 ILCS 20/3).
2. **Assessment.** The agency makes a face-to-face assessment, including a home visit, and talks to people who know the person. Those interviews generally need the adult's consent (320 ILCS 20/5).
3. **Finding.** The agency decides whether the report is substantiated, meaning there is reason to believe exploitation occurred. A final report is written whether or not it is substantiated (320 ILCS 20/7.1).
4. **Service plan.** If substantiated, the agency offers the least restrictive services that meet the adult's needs. Services are reviewed at least quarterly for up to a year (320 ILCS 20/7).
5. **Law enforcement.** Evidence of a crime goes to law enforcement. A suspicious death goes immediately to law enforcement and the coroner or medical examiner.

The adult stays in charge. Services are provided only with the adult's consent, and if the adult refuses, they stop (320 ILCS 20/9). Reporters should know that the Act does not promise feedback. With the adult's consent, notice of the finding can go to the adult, the person accused and the reporter when that would not create a risk of harm (320 ILCS 20/7.1).

### Can a court step in?

Yes, in two situations. If an emergency exists and the adult lacks capacity to consent, the Department or agency can ask the circuit court for an emergency order. That order expires within 15 days unless the agency petitions for a guardian (320 ILCS 20/9). And if financial exploitation is substantiated and the agency reasonably believes the adult will be irreparably harmed, it can petition to freeze assets. Such an order bars selling, transferring or wasting assets without court permission, and cannot last more than 2 years (320 ILCS 20/13 and 13.5).

If the person suspected of abuse is also the adult's guardian or agent under a power of attorney and withdraws consent, the Department, the agency or the Attorney General can ask the court for an order, including removal of the guardian (320 ILCS 20/9). For the court side, see our [Illinois guardianship article](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship).

## What if you are a banker, advisor or other mandated reporter?

Then the clock is short. Mandated reporters must report within 24 hours after developing a reasonable belief that an eligible adult who cannot seek help because of a disability or other condition was abused, neglected or exploited within the previous 12 months (320 ILCS 20/4). The list includes investment advisors, many health professionals, social workers and law enforcement. Good-faith reporters are immune, and good faith is presumed. Employers may not retaliate against an employee who reports in good faith.

## How can a power of attorney or trust prevent exploitation?

Documents cannot stop a determined thief, but they can make theft slower to carry out and faster to spot.

**Choose the agent with care.** Agents who misuse their authority are often people who were trusted because they lived nearby. Our article on [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers what to look for.

**Keep gift powers off the form unless you mean them.** An Illinois agent cannot make gifts, appoint property to others or change a beneficiary unless the form gives specific authority (755 ILCS 45/3-4). Leaving that power out removes the easiest route for self-dealing. If you do allow gifts, cap them.

**Name a second set of eyes.** A successor agent can be told to receive copies of statements. A co-trustee or a professional trustee can serve in a trust. Our [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) page lists safeguards that can go into the document.

**Know how to revoke.** While the principal has capacity, a power of attorney can be revoked. See [revoking an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney). After incapacity, only a court can step in. That is why timing matters.

**Use a trust for large assets.** Property in a living trust is managed by a trustee who owes duties to the beneficiaries. Our [Illinois trustee duty to inform](/learn/illinois/illinois-trustee-duty-to-inform-beneficiaries) page explains what beneficiaries are entitled to see.

**Lock down the basics.** [Freezing a parent's credit and adding safeguards to accounts](/learn/elder-care/how-to-freeze-a-parents-credit-and-put-safeguards-on-their-accounts) stops many scams outright.

### A common mistake our attorneys see

Families wait for proof. Dana, 52, noticed her father's checking balance dropping by about $2,000 a month and told herself it was none of her business until she was sure. By the time she called, $30,000 was gone and her father no longer had the capacity to revoke the power of attorney he had signed. An example like this is illustrative, not a prediction, but the lesson is real: report early, because reports made while the older adult can still act give the most options.
