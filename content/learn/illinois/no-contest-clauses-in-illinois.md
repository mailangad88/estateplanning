Ruth's father left her $50,000 and left her brother $400,000. His will says anyone who contests it loses everything they receive. Ruth thinks her brother pushed their father to change it. She wonders whether suing would cost her the $50,000.

That is a fair question, and the answer depends on facts and wording. This page covers how Illinois treats no-contest clauses, the choices that follow for both the person who writes one and the person who faces one, and a table to help weigh the risk. For the filing deadline see the page on the [Illinois six-month will contest rule](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline), and see the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) for the full set of topics.

## What does a no-contest clause do?

It adds a price to challenging a document. A clause might say: "If any beneficiary contests this will or any trust created by me, that person's gift is revoked and passes as if they had died before me." The clause is also called an in terrorem clause. See the glossary entry for [no-contest clause](/glossary/no-contest-clause), and our general article on [no-contest clauses](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) for a multi-state view.

The threat works only if the person can lose something. A child who is left $1 has little to lose. A child left $200,000 has plenty.

## Are no-contest clauses enforced in Illinois?

Generally yes, but courts read them with care. A forfeiture takes away a gift the maker chose to give, and courts try to avoid that result unless the language clearly demands it. So the court asks two things: what the clause says counts as a contest, and what the person did. Illinois decisions on this are fact-specific, and we have not confirmed a rule that makes every clause enforceable in every case. Have an attorney read your clause and recent decisions before acting.

A few points tend to hold. A clause cannot move the deadline: the 6-month period in 755 ILCS 5/8-1 runs either way. It also cannot stop the court from hearing a question about whether a will is valid, if someone files. It only decides what the filer loses.

## Who does the clause reach?

| Person | Does the clause reach them? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A child left a large share who sues for undue influence | Yes, if the wording covers it | They stand to lose the share |
| A child left nothing | No practical effect | No gift to forfeit |
| A beneficiary who asks the executor for an accounting | Usually not | Not an attack on validity, but check the wording |
| A trustee or executor who asks the court for instructions | Usually not | Asking for guidance is not a contest |
| A beneficiary who sues on a different claim, such as a debt owed by the estate | Depends | Many clauses name only will or trust challenges |
| An heir who disclaims instead of suing | No | See [disclaiming an inheritance](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois) |

## How should a beneficiary decide whether to contest?

Use these steps.

1. Read the exact clause, not a summary. Note what it says counts as a contest and which documents it covers.
2. Write down what you would receive if the will stands, and what you would receive if it falls. Check the earlier will and the intestacy rules in 755 ILCS 5/2-1.
3. Subtract the cost of a case from any gain. Court cases take months or years.
4. Ask a lawyer how Illinois courts have treated that kind of wording and what actions are safe, such as asking for the will and an accounting.
5. Mark the 6-month deadline and decide a safe filing date.
6. If you settle, get a written agreement that the clause will not be enforced.

In Ruth's case, she weighs a $50,000 gift against a possible gain of $150,000 if the earlier will is restored. A lawyer helps her see whether the claim is strong enough to risk the smaller gift.

## How do you write a clause that works?

Be specific. Say which documents it covers, what counts as a contest, and what happens to the forfeited gift. Add it to both the will and the trust. Give each person enough to make the threat real, but ask yourself whether you want that fight. Our article on [disinheriting a child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-illinois) explains that a clause is a poor fit for a person who gets nothing.

## How does a clause work with trusts and pour-over wills?

Many Illinois plans use a revocable trust with a pour-over will. A clause in only one document leaves a gap. If the clause sits in the will but the main gifts are in the trust, a beneficiary might attack the trust without triggering it. Better drafting repeats the clause in both documents and says it covers any challenge to either one. Note also that after a death, a trust contest is a separate matter with its own deadlines, as described in our page on [contesting a trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/contesting-a-trust-in-illinois-who-can-sue-grounds-and-deadlines). The Illinois Trust Code, 760 ILCS 3, governs trusts, and the Probate Act governs wills.

Consider Marcus, who leaves a $600,000 trust equally to two sons, with a clause. One son sues, claiming the other pushed their father. If the clause covers the claim and the court enforces it, the suing son risks his $300,000 share. If he wins, he may gain more. If he loses, he may pay costs and lose the share. That is the weighing a clause is meant to force, and the reason a lawyer should read the wording before anyone files.

## What works better than a clause?

A plan that is hard to attack. Meet an independent lawyer alone, record the reasons for unequal shares, keep medical notes near the signing and tell your family while you can. See [testamentary capacity in Illinois](/learn/illinois/testamentary-capacity-in-illinois) and the page on [undue influence](/learn/illinois/undue-influence-will-challenges-in-illinois). A trust with a careful trustee also reduces surprise. For trust challenges, see [contesting a trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/contesting-a-trust-in-illinois-who-can-sue-grounds-and-deadlines).
