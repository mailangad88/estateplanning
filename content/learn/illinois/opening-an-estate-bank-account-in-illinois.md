You have your letters of office from the circuit court, and now Aunt Ruth's bank statements are on your kitchen table. Her checking account holds $22,000. The bills keep coming. You need a place to put the money that is not your own checking account.

That place is an estate account, and in Illinois the order of steps matters: letters first, EIN second, bank third. This page walks through each, shows what a bank usually asks for, and covers the mistakes that cause trouble later. For the general national version, see [how to open an estate bank account as an executor](/learn/probate/how-to-open-an-estate-bank-account). The wider process is in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What do you need before you go to the bank?

You need three things before a branch will do anything: letters, an EIN and the death certificate. Everything else is detail.

1. **Letters of office.** The circuit court in the county where the estate is open issues them after it admits the will and appoints you. The clerk can certify copies. Our article on [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) explains the bond, which often has to be in place before letters issue.
2. **The estate's EIN.** The estate gets its own federal tax number. More on that below.
3. **Certified death certificate.** The county clerk and the state health department both issue them. See [Illinois death certificates](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph) for who can order.
4. **Your photo ID.** You sign as the fiduciary.
5. **A list of accounts.** Bring statements if you have them, so the banker can see what to retitle or close.

Banks often want letters issued recently, and some set their own cutoff, so ask. Order more certified copies than you think you need. Each bank, brokerage and insurer will want its own.

## How do you get an EIN for an Illinois estate?

Apply online with the IRS, which issues the number for free. The IRS says you can get an EIN directly from it in minutes, and that the applicant must name a responsible party, the person in charge of the entity and its assets. It also says nominees are not authorized to apply. For an estate, that means the executor, not a relative helping out.

Do not pay a website to apply for you. The IRS application is free. Our article on [IRS paperwork for an executor](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810) covers Form 56 and the other forms that come with it.

The estate usually keeps one EIN for its life. The IRS says an estate needs a new one if it creates a trust with estate funds, or if it runs a sole proprietorship after the owner's death. Changing the executor's address does not call for a new one.

The EIN matters because the interest the account earns is reported under it. If the estate earns enough, it files [Illinois Form IL-1041](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-il-1041-estate-and-trust-income-tax-return). A bank that opens an account under the dead person's Social Security number will send the interest to the wrong place.

## What will the bank ask you for?

Expect a short stack of requests. Banks differ, so treat this as a typical list and not a rule.

| Item | Why the bank wants it |
|---|---|
| Certified letters of office | Proves you are the court-appointed representative |
| Death certificate | Confirms the owner died |
| Estate EIN notice from the IRS | Used to title and report the account |
| Your photo ID | Identifies the signer |
| Its own account-opening and fiduciary forms | Bank policy |
| A copy of the will, sometimes | Some banks ask, though the letters should be enough |

Title the account in the estate's name and your role, for example "Estate of Ruth Alvarez, Daniel Alvarez, Executor." Ask three questions at the desk. Is there a monthly fee? Does it give you images of cancelled checks, which you will want for the accounting? And will it take a check payable to the estate without a hold?

## What money belongs in the estate account?

Only assets that were in the dead person's sole name and have no beneficiary. That is the probate estate. A joint account with survivorship, a payable-on-death account and an account with a named beneficiary go straight to the survivor or the beneficiary. Our article on [Illinois POD and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) explains each, including the case where the beneficiary died first and the money falls back into the estate.

Example only: Ruth's sole-name checking holds $22,000 and her savings holds $14,500. A joint account with her daughter holds $9,000, and an IRA names her son as beneficiary. Daniel moves the $36,500 into the estate account. The joint money stays with his sister, and the IRA goes to the son. Putting all of it in the estate account would have been wrong, and the other heirs would have had a fair complaint.

If the whole estate is small, you may not need an account at all. The [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) lets a bank release personal property up to $150,000 as of 2026, without letters, if no court case is open.

## What mistakes do executors make with estate money?

The same handful, again and again.

- **Using a personal account "just for now."** Even a few days of mixing makes the records unreliable and gives an unhappy heir something to point to. Pay with estate checks.
- **Depositing estate refunds in your own account.** A refund check made out to the dead person or the estate belongs in the estate account.
- **Paying beneficiaries early.** Illinois sets a claims deadline: the later of six months after the first publication or three months after mailing notice. The money for valid claims has to be there. Early distribution to an heir is possible only with court permission and a bond of double the share. Read [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) before you cut any check to a family member.
- **Paying a creditor out of order.** If the estate cannot cover every debt, the Probate Act ranks them. Funeral and administration costs come first. Our [credit card debt article](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois) shows how the classes work.
- **Taking a fee with no record.** Executor pay needs a paper trail. See [how much an executor gets paid in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-much-does-an-executor-get-paid-in-illinois).
- **Closing the account too soon.** A late bill, a tax refund or a creditor with an unbarred claim shows up after the account is gone. Keep it open until the closing report and discharge. The steps are in [closing an Illinois probate estate](/learn/illinois/closing-an-illinois-probate-estate-final-account-and-discharge).
- **Paying the funeral and forgetting the receipt.** Funeral costs sit at the top of the payment order, and you can reimburse yourself if you kept the proof. See [paying for a funeral from an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/paying-for-a-funeral-from-an-illinois-estate).

## What records should you keep?

Keep a ledger with date, payee, purpose, amount and check number. Staple the receipt to the statement line. Interested persons can ask for an accounting, and the closing report states that notices and inventory went out and each claim was resolved. A clean ledger turns that report from a weekend of detective work into an afternoon. The [handling an Illinois estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer) page lists the filings that go with it.
