The person who died is gone, but the estate is now a taxpayer in its own right. If it earns interest, rent or dividends while probate runs, or if a living trust turns irrevocable and keeps earning, someone has to decide whether Illinois wants a return. That return is Form IL-1041.

This page explains who must file it, when it is due, how it connects to the federal Form 1041, and the errors that cost executors and trustees money. The personal return for the year of death is a different filing, covered in our page on the [final Illinois income tax return for someone who died](/learn/illinois/final-illinois-income-tax-return-for-someone-who-died). Everything on the Illinois side of an estate is gathered in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). Confirm forms and rates on tax.illinois.gov, because they change.

## Who has to file Form IL-1041 in Illinois?

The fiduciary of a trust or estate files it when any one of three tests applies, as the Illinois Department of Revenue describes them:

- The trust or estate has net income or loss as defined under the Illinois Income Tax Act, even if it deducts distributions to beneficiaries.
- It is an Illinois resident, is required to file, or files a federal income tax return, whatever its net income or loss.
- It is a nonresident with Illinois-source income that was not reported as pass-through withholding. A nonresident filer also completes Schedule NR.

Two groups are excluded. Grantor trusts do not file Form IL-1041. Certain charitable organizations exempt under section 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code do not file it either, though they report unrelated business income on a different form.

In plain terms: if the estate had to file a federal Form 1041, plan on an Illinois return as well. Our page on [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) explains the federal side, including the gross income of $600 or more that triggers a federal filing for an estate.

| Situation | Likely Illinois filing |
|---|---|
| Illinois estate earning interest on an estate account, files federal 1041 | IL-1041 |
| Estate with no income at all and no federal return | Usually none, confirm with a preparer |
| Living trust while the maker is alive | None, it is a grantor trust |
| Living trust after the maker dies, still holding investments | IL-1041 and a trust EIN |
| Non-Illinois estate with Illinois rental income | IL-1041 with Schedule NR |
| Final return for the person who died | IL-1040, not IL-1041 |

## What is the difference between the estate's return and the final return?

The final IL-1040 covers January 1 through the date of death. The IL-1041 covers what the estate or trust earns afterward. Think of two meters. Wages, pension payments and interest received before death belong on the final personal return. Interest on the estate checking account, rent from a house held during probate and gain on a sale after death belong on the estate's return, unless the income passes straight to a beneficiary.

Take Walter, who died on June 9 in Rockford. His final IL-1040 reports income through June 9. His daughter Joyce, the executor, opens an estate account, rents out his duplex for the next fourteen months and sells it. That rent and the gain run through the estate's IL-1041, not through Walter's final return. If the sale price is close to the date-of-death value, the gain tends to be small because of the step-up in basis, which our page on [inherited stocks and brokerage accounts](/learn/after-a-death/inherited-stocks-and-brokerage-accounts-selling-retitling-and-tax) explains in a related setting.

## When is Form IL-1041 due?

On or before the 15th day of the fourth month after the tax year closes. For a calendar-year estate or trust, that is April 15. An estate that chose a fiscal year ending on the last day of January, for example, would file by May 15. The return is tied to the estate's tax year, and our page on [IRS paperwork for an executor](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810) explains how that choice is made and why a fiscal year can help the first year.

A numbered path through the first year:

1. Get letters of office and the estate's EIN.
2. Choose the tax year with your preparer before the first income arrives.
3. Open the estate account and keep every deposit labeled by source.
4. At year end, prepare the federal Form 1041 first, then the IL-1041, which starts from federal figures.
5. Pay any tax by the original due date.
6. File the return, then keep a copy for the closing report.

## Can you get an extension?

Yes, and automatically. Illinois grants a six-month extension to file without any form. The extension does not stretch the time to pay. If tax is expected, the estimated amount is due by the original date, or interest and penalties begin. This trips up executors who assume that "extended" means paid later. Treat the original date as the payment deadline and the extended date as the filing deadline.

## How is the Illinois tax calculated?

The state starts from federal taxable income and makes Illinois adjustments, such as for state and municipal bond interest and U.S. Treasury interest, to reach base income. It then multiplies net income by a flat rate. We do not print the rate here, since it can change; the Department of Revenue's tax rate database lists the current figure for trusts and estates. Trusts and estates pay a business income tax, and trusts also pay a replacement tax.

Beneficiaries then report their share of estate or trust income on their own returns, so a distribution of income is not taxed twice. Distributions of principal, the inheritance itself, are generally not income. Making that split correctly is why most executors with real income hire a preparer.

## What if the estate or trust has beneficiaries who live out of state?

The state requires S corporations, partnerships and trusts to make Illinois tax payments for nonresident owners, beneficiaries or partners, and trusts must notify nonresident beneficiaries of the amounts paid for them. If those payments cover the beneficiary's Illinois tax, the beneficiary may not need to file in Illinois. A beneficiary who does file claims credit for the payment. If you are a trustee with children in several states, expect extra letters at year end.

## What mistakes do executors make with Form IL-1041?

Our attorneys see the same few errors:

- **Skipping the return because "the estate is small."** The test turns on whether a federal return is filed, not on how large the estate feels.
- **Treating the extension as an extension to pay.** Penalties run from the original date.
- **Putting estate income on the final IL-1040.** Income received after death usually belongs to the estate.
- **Filing a second IL-1041 to correct the first.** Use Form IL-1041-X, one per tax year.
- **Amending too fast after an IRS change.** Wait until the IRS finalizes the federal change.
- **Closing the estate before the last return is filed.** The closing report in Illinois lists what the estate paid, and unpaid taxes can come back to the executor. See [closing an Illinois probate estate](/learn/illinois/closing-an-illinois-probate-estate-final-account-and-discharge).

## How do you file and pay?

Electronically, in the usual case. Form IL-1041 can be filed through the IRS Modernized e-File program using approved software. Payments can be made through MyTax Illinois, through software with ACH debit, or by ACH credit. A business filer registers with the Department of Revenue first, using Form REG-1 if it is not registered. Paper filing instructions are in the form's own instructions.
