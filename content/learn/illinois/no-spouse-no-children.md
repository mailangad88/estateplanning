You are single, you have no children, and you are not sure what happens to your home, your savings and your say over your own care. The honest answer is that Illinois has a list for both, and the list probably is not the one you would write.

This page walks through the two lists, who they put in charge, and the short set of documents that replaces them with your own choices. It is part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), and a [consultation about a will](/book?from=learn-no-spouse-no-children&topic=will) is the quickest way to put your choices on paper.

## Who inherits in Illinois if I have no spouse and no children?

Your parents and your brothers and sisters inherit first, in equal parts, and a surviving parent takes a double share (755 ILCS 5/2-1). This applies only to property in your name alone that passes through probate, and only when you have no valid will.

The statute works down a waterfall. The first group with a living member takes the estate, and no one below it receives anything.

| Step | Who takes | How it splits |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Your parents and siblings | Equal parts, with a surviving parent counted double. A deceased sibling's children share that sibling's part |
| 2 | Your grandparents and their descendants | Half to the mother's side, half to the father's side. If one side is empty, the other side takes all |
| 3 | Great-grandparents and their descendants | Split the same way by side |
| 4 | Nearest kin by degree | Equal shares |
| 5 | No kin at all | The county or the state |

For the full order, including families with a spouse or children, see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). Here we stay with the case of someone on their own.

### How does the split work in real numbers?

Here is an example. Marcy, 61, is single with no children. She owns a $300,000 condo and savings in her own name. Her father has died. Her mother and two brothers are alive. The estate divides into four parts: two for her mother and one for each brother. Her mother receives $150,000 and each brother $75,000.

Now change one fact. Suppose her mother has also died and one brother has died, leaving two daughters. The surviving brother takes half, and the two nieces split the other half. Her closest friend of thirty years, who drove her to every appointment, receives nothing.

## Who is left out?

Anyone who is not a blood relative or an adopted child. That includes:

- A partner you never married. A civil union partner is treated like a spouse under Illinois law, which our page on [civil union partners](/learn/illinois/civil-union-partners-and-illinois-estate-law) explains.
- Close friends and the people who care for you.
- A charity, a church or your alma mater.
- A sibling's partner, even if they raised your nieces with you.

If any of these should receive something, the answer is a will or a trust. Beneficiary forms do the same job for accounts that have them.

## Which assets skip the intestacy list altogether?

The list controls only your probate estate. These pass by their own rules, whatever a will or the statute says:

- Retirement accounts and life insurance, to the person named on the form.
- Bank accounts with a payable-on-death beneficiary, as described in [Illinois payable-on-death and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts).
- Property held in joint tenancy with survivorship, such as a house you share with a sibling.
- Real estate with a recorded transfer on death instrument. See [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).

Single people often have more of these than they realize, and not always correctly filled out. A form that still names a late parent, or no one, sends the money into the estate and back to the waterfall above. Pull each form once a year.

## Who makes medical decisions for me if I am single?

If you cannot decide and have no health care agent, Illinois law ranks the people who may speak for you. For someone with no spouse or children, the order runs to a parent, then an adult brother or sister, then an adult grandchild, then a close friend (755 ILCS 40/25). That list comes from the Health Care Surrogate Act, and our page on [who decides under the Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides) covers the whole order, including how disagreements are settled.

<!-- visual: whatif id=hospital-wont-talk -->

Two things stand out for a single person.

1. **A partner is not on the list.** At best, a partner might qualify as a close friend, which ranks below every relative. If your sister is available and willing, she outranks him.
2. **The person at the top may not be the person you would choose.** A parent in their eighties or a sibling you rarely speak to may be first in line.

A health care power of attorney replaces the list. In Illinois the statutory form is signed with one adult witness, and no notary is needed (755 ILCS 45/4-10). Your attending physician and other treating providers cannot be your agent or your witness. Our [Illinois health care power of attorney guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) has the steps, and [how to choose a health care agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) will help you pick a person who will follow your wishes.

## Who handles my money if I get sick?

No one, unless you sign a property power of attorney. A spouse could often step in and sign. You have no automatic substitute, so a bank may ask a court to appoint a guardian, which is public, slow and costly. The Illinois statutory form for property needs a witness and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3). Our page on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) shows what the agent can and cannot do, and [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) describes the court route you are trying to avoid.

## What should I sign?

Start with these four, in this order.

1. **A will.** In Illinois it must be in writing, signed by an adult of sound mind and witnessed by two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3). It names your heirs, an executor and a backup. See [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) for the signing rules.
2. **A health care power of attorney,** with a backup agent who lives within reach.
3. **A property power of attorney,** with a backup agent and clear limits.
4. **Fresh beneficiary forms** on every account that has one, matching the will.

Add a living trust if you own real estate and want a trustee ready to step in. See [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) for what a trust avoids and what it does not. Our general guide to [estate planning if you are single](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people) covers aging alone, long-term care and professional fiduciaries.

## What do single people usually get wrong?

- **Naming a parent as the only agent.** Parents age. Name a sibling or friend as backup.
- **Leaving everything to "my family" without saying who.** A will should name people, not groups.
- **Forgetting the executor.** The person must be willing and able to serve. Our guide to [who can be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-can-be-an-executor-in-illinois) lists the qualifications.
- **Assuming a joint account with a friend is a gift.** It may belong to the survivor, or it may be fought over.
- **Waiting for a diagnosis.** Capacity is required to sign, and it is hard to document after a stroke.
