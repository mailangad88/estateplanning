When someone dies, the family usually learns about the accounts they knew of. The ones nobody remembered are different. A checking account closed years ago with a few hundred dollars left in it, a refund check that was never cashed, a life insurance policy the company could not deliver. In Illinois, money like that often ends up with the State Treasurer.

This page explains how to look for it, what the claim involves, and who is entitled to collect. It sits alongside our general guide to [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) and the rest of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What is unclaimed property in Illinois?

It is money or property that a holder, such as a bank, insurer or employer, has been unable to return to its owner and has turned over to the Illinois State Treasurer. The Treasurer's I-Cash program lists the items. The office describes the typical categories as forgotten bank accounts, uncashed rebate checks, unpaid life insurance benefits and the contents of abandoned safe deposit boxes. The program has run since the 1960s.

The Treasurer holds the property for the owner. According to the office, it is legally required to return it to owners or their heirs. For families, that last phrase is the important one: a person's death does not end the claim.

## Who can claim for someone who has died?

The owner's heirs can claim, and so can the person administering the estate. In practice that sorts out like this:

| Your role | What you probably need |
|---|---|
| Executor or administrator with letters of office | A death certificate, your letters, and your identification |
| Heir where a probate case is open but you are not the representative | Ask the executor to claim, or ask the Treasurer what it requires |
| Heir where no probate is open | Proof of death and of your relationship, which may include a small estate affidavit if the estate qualifies |
| Beneficiary of a life insurance policy | The policy details and proof of death |

The exact document list is on the I-Cash site and varies with the claim, so confirm it there before collecting papers. Our pages on [proving heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois) and [how to prove you are an heir](/learn/probate/how-to-prove-you-are-an-heir) explain what the court and agencies usually accept. For smaller estates, see the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit).

One point of order: unclaimed property belongs to the estate, and it is owed to whoever the will or the intestacy rules say. Finding $3,000 does not make it yours. If you are not the executor, tell the executor, because the money may be needed to pay debts first.

## How do you search I-Cash?

The site is icash.illinoistreasurer.gov, run by the Illinois State Treasurer (illinoistreasurer.gov). Searching is free and open at all hours. A sensible search works like this.

1. **Start with the full legal name.** Try the common version too. Margaret Ann Sullivan may be listed as Peg Sullivan or M. A. Sullivan.
2. **Add maiden and married names.** Accounts often predate a marriage.
3. **Search every address.** Make a list of cities and streets where the person lived over the last 20 years or so.
4. **Search businesses.** If the person owned a company, a trust or a nonprofit, search those names too.
5. **Search the spouse and parents,** since property can sit in the name of someone who died before.
6. **Repeat in six months.** The Treasurer recommends checking at least a couple of times a year, because companies keep turning property over.

Keep a log of what you searched and the result. If the estate goes through court, an executor who can show a systematic search is in a better position than one who checked once.

The Treasurer's site also has a feature for sharing a find with relatives. And for the searching of other places, such as other states, see our overview of [after-death asset searches](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets). Property held in another state is handled by that state's program.

## How do you file a claim?

Claims can be filed electronically through I-Cash. The flow is the same in outline each time, but the screens and the document list change, so follow the site as it stands.

1. Locate the property and add it to your claim.
2. Say whether you are the owner's executor, an heir, or a beneficiary.
3. Provide proof of identity, proof of the death and proof of your connection to the owner.
4. Wait for review. The Treasurer may ask for more documents.
5. Receive payment, then record it with the estate.

The Treasurer says it returns property at no cost with the proper identification. That is also the answer to the pitches from companies that offer to find money for a share. You never need to pay a stranger to do something you can do for free.

Some property is returned automatically. The office runs an Enhanced Money Match program that returns money after certain information is verified electronically, so a claim is not always required.

### An example

Joan's father, Walter, died in March. The family knew of his checking account and pension. Six months later, Joan searches I-Cash under her father's name and finds a $4,100 listing from an old utility deposit and an insurance refund. She is the executor, so she files a claim with the death certificate and her letters. When the check comes, it goes into the estate account and is listed on the final accounting. Without the search, the money would have stayed with the Treasurer.

## Why bother searching if the estate looks small?

Because small items add up and because families rarely know everything. A parent who moved twice, changed banks and worked for three employers can easily have four or five forgotten balances. None of them is large. Together they might pay the funeral home bill. There is also the matter of duty. An executor is expected to collect the estate's assets, and a documented I-Cash search is one inexpensive way to show that the job was done carefully.

It also helps to remember that a search is not a one-time event. Companies turn property over to the Treasurer on their own schedule, so something absent in April may appear by the fall. Put a reminder on the estate calendar to repeat the search before you close the estate, and again if you reopen it for any reason.

## What if you find property in a safe deposit box or a life insurance policy?

These need more care. Life insurance can be claimed by the named beneficiary directly, and our guide to [claiming life insurance and survivor benefits](/learn/after-a-death/claiming-life-insurance-pension-and-va-survivor-benefits) covers the process. Abandoned safe deposit box contents may be sold or held, and a claim is made through the Treasurer. Ask the office what is listed before assuming the box is gone.
