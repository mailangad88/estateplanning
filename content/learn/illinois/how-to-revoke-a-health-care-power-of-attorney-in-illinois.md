You named someone to make medical decisions for you, and now that choice no longer fits. Your sister lives three states away, or your son and you have stopped speaking, or your husband is now ill himself. Or you are the adult child of a parent in assisted living who keeps saying she wants a different person in charge. This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), explains how Illinois lets you end a health care power of attorney and how to make sure the hospital actually stops calling the old agent.

The signing rules for the form itself are on the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) page. If you would like someone to look over what you signed before you change it, you can [book a review](/book?from=learn-how-to-revoke-a-health-care-power-of-attorney-in-illinois&topic=review).

## The short version

- Illinois allows three methods of revocation under 755 ILCS 45/4-6. Use a signed paper even if you also use another method.
- Look at your old form for the 30-day delay option before you rely on an immediate change.
- Notify the agent, every doctor and hospital, and any facility where you live.
- If you still want an agent, sign a new form that states it revokes earlier health care powers.
- Revoking a health care power leaves your property power of attorney in place.

## How do you revoke a health care power of attorney in Illinois?

You use one of the three methods in section 4-6 of the Power of Attorney Act, while you still have capacity. The section lets you revoke by:

1. **Destroying the document.** Tearing up or shredding the signed form.
2. **Signing a written revocation.** A short, dated statement that the form is cancelled.
3. **Saying so aloud in front of a witness who is 18 or older.** A spoken revocation counts when an adult hears it.

Each method is valid, but they leave very different evidence. Shredding your own copy says nothing to the clinic that scanned the form into your record two years ago. A spoken revocation depends on someone remembering it and writing it down. A signed revocation can be copied, mailed and placed in a chart, which is why most people should use it, alone or alongside one of the others.

A written revocation does not need a particular form or a notary. Make it clear enough that a nurse reading it at 2 a.m. understands it:

- Your full name and date of birth, which is how hospitals match records.
- The date you signed the health care power of attorney being revoked.
- The names of the agent and any [successor agents](/glossary/successor-agent).
- One sentence: "I revoke my health care power of attorney dated [date] in full."
- The name and phone number of your new agent, if you have signed a new form.
- Your signature and the date.

### What about the 30-day delay option?

The Illinois statutory health care form offers an option to delay a revocation for 30 days. Some people choose it to protect against a decision made in a frightening moment, such as in the middle of a hospital stay or under pressure from a relative. If you checked that option, a revocation may not take effect right away. Find your signed copy and read the box before you assume the old agent is out. If the wording is unclear, the attorney you book with can tell you how it applies.

## Who do you tell when you revoke?

Everyone who could act on the old form. Under the Act, a revocation does not affect the agent until the agent actually knows of it (755 ILCS 45/2-7), and a provider that has only the old form in its file will keep using it. Health care powers live in more places than people expect: hospital charts, every doctor's office, patient portals and the front desk at an assisted living building.

### Health care revocation checklist

| Who to notify | Why | What to give them |
|---|---|---|
| The old agent and each successor agent | Their role ends when they know | The signed revocation, by certified mail or hand delivery with a witness; ask for their copies back |
| Primary care doctor | The chart copy is the one used in a crisis | The revocation and any new form; ask that the old form be marked revoked |
| Other doctors you see regularly | Each office keeps its own file | The same papers |
| Every hospital you have used | Hospital records are separate from clinic records | Send to the medical records or patient advocate office |
| Nursing home, assisted living or memory care | Staff call whoever is listed in the file | The revocation, the new form and the new agent's phone number |
| Practitioner who signed a POLST | The POLST may name a representative | Tell them; the POLST is changed separately |
| Patient portals and any registry you used | Uploaded copies stay unless replaced | Upload the new form and delete the old one |
| County recorder | Usually not needed; a health care power is rarely recorded | Nothing, unless a property power was recorded (see below) |
| Family members who knew the old agent | Avoids a call to the wrong person | A short note or call |

If someone was also given a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) at the same time, revoke that in writing too, or the old agent may still be able to get your records. A POLST follows its own steps, explained in [Illinois POLST and DNR orders](/learn/illinois/illinois-polst-and-dnr-orders).

## Does a new health care form cancel the old one?

Do not count on it. The Act's general rule is that a new power of attorney does not revoke an earlier one unless it expressly says so or revokes all other powers of attorney (755 ILCS 45/2-5). Two signed health care forms in two different charts can leave a hospital unsure whom to call.

So do both things. Put a sentence in the new form revoking all earlier health care powers of attorney, and send the old agent and your providers a separate signed revocation. The new form needs one adult witness and no notary (755 ILCS 45/4-10), and the witness cannot be the agent or certain providers; the details are on the page about [who can witness an Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/witness-rules-for-an-illinois-health-care-power-of-attorney). Remember too that your attending physician cannot be your agent (755 ILCS 45/4-5).

## Can a parent with dementia revoke a health care power of attorney?

Sometimes. A diagnosis does not end the right to revoke. What matters is whether, on the day, your parent understands that the form lets someone decide medical care, who that person is, and what ending it means. Capacity can come and go with dementia, which is why an attorney who meets your parent alone, often with a recent doctor's note, should judge it, as explained in [dementia and no power of attorney in Illinois](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

Watch for pressure. A revocation that one child arranged to push out a sibling may be questioned later, and a parent who no longer understands the document cannot fix things with a new signature. When that point has passed, family who believe the agent is not acting for the parent generally have to go to court. The Act lets interested people, such as a spouse, a descendant or an adult protective services provider, ask the court to review an agent once it finds the principal lacks capacity to control or revoke the agency (755 ILCS 45/2-10). A guardianship of the person is the broader route, described in [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship).

## What if the old agent will not step aside?

The agent's agreement is not needed, but an agent who keeps turning up at the bedside creates real confusion. Take these steps.

1. **Deliver the revocation again** by a second method you can prove, such as certified mail plus email.
2. **Make sure the hospital and facility have it.** Ask the patient advocate or social worker to confirm the chart now lists the new agent.
3. **Tell staff directly** if you are a patient or resident. While you can speak for yourself, your own decisions control.
4. **Keep a short log** of calls and visits by the old agent after notice.
5. **Get advice** if the old agent is interfering with care or making threats. The family conflict steps in [challenging or removing an agent when siblings disagree](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-challenge-or-remove-a-power-of-attorney-agent-when-siblings-disagree) apply here too.

## What about a property power of attorney?

That is a separate document, and revoking the health care form leaves it alone. A property power is revoked by communicating the revocation to the agent or to others the agency deals with (755 ILCS 45/2-5), and if it was recorded with a deed, the revocation may need recording too. The full steps, and what to do about an agent misusing money, are on [revoking an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney).

## A worked example

Example. Dorothy, 81, lives in an assisted living building in Rockford. In 2020 she signed an Illinois health care power of attorney naming her son Greg, with no successor. Greg now works overseas for months at a time, and last spring the hospital could not reach him during a fall. Dorothy wants her daughter Lena as agent and her nephew Sam as successor. She follows conversations easily, and her doctor's notes say so.

- **Week 1, Monday.** An Illinois estate planning attorney meets Dorothy alone and confirms she understands the change. She signs a new statutory health care form naming Lena and Sam, with a neighbor as the one adult witness. The new form revokes all earlier health care powers. She did not choose the 30-day delay on either form.
- **Monday.** She also signs a one-page revocation of the 2020 form naming Greg.
- **Tuesday.** The revocation goes to Greg by email and international mail. He replies that he understands.
- **Wednesday.** Lena gives copies to the building's nurse, Dorothy's primary doctor and her cardiologist, and sends them to the medical records office of the hospital where Dorothy was treated.
- **Week 2.** Dorothy's portal shows the new form. The building's file lists Lena's phone number. Dorothy's property power of attorney, which already named Lena, is unchanged.
