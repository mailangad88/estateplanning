You retired in Illinois, you have a plan, and now you are looking at a condo in Florida or Arizona. You are wondering whether the will and trust you paid for still work, and whether Illinois will still tax the house in Naperville that you are not ready to sell. 

This page answers those two questions from the Illinois side only. It covers what happens to your Illinois documents, what the Illinois estate tax does to Illinois real estate you keep, and a before-you-move checklist. What your new state requires is that state's business: have a lawyer licensed there review your plan. For the reverse trip, see our page on [an out-of-state will after you move to Illinois](/learn/illinois/is-an-out-of-state-will-valid-in-illinois). To go through your own situation, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-moving-out-of-illinois&topic=review) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Is my Illinois estate plan still good if I move to Florida or Arizona?

The documents still exist, but they may not do everything you expect. A will or trust signed properly in Illinois is generally still honored in other states, and moving does not cancel it. What changes is the law applied around it: the state where you are domiciled usually decides which court handles your probate, what a spouse can claim and how your estate is run. Our general piece on [whether a will survives a move](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) explains the national rule.

Here is how the pieces usually sort out.

| Piece of your plan | What usually happens after you move | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Illinois will | Generally still valid, but your new state's rules govern probate and spouse's rights | Have a lawyer licensed in the new state review it |
| Revocable living trust | Usually travels with you | Keep it, update it, and make sure new property is titled in the trust |
| Illinois power of attorney for property | Often accepted, but a bank may refuse an unfamiliar form | Sign new forms in your new state, and keep the Illinois ones for Illinois assets |
| Health care directive | Hospitals in the new state may not know the Illinois form | Sign the new state's form as well |
| Illinois real estate you keep | Stays under Illinois law because the land is in Illinois | Review the deed and the estate tax picture (below) |
| Beneficiary forms | Follow their own forms, not your will | Re-check every one after the move |

The Illinois statutory short form for property needs your signature, one witness and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3), and an Illinois bank that refuses a valid power of attorney without reasonable cause can be liable for the damages that follow (755 ILCS 45/2-8). Those rules help you in Illinois. They do not control a bank in another state. For the Illinois forms, see the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).

## Does Illinois estate tax still apply to my Illinois real estate if I move?

It can. Illinois has its own estate tax, and the Illinois Attorney General collects it on the estates of Illinois residents and on Illinois property of some nonresidents. Our page on [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) covers the rules in full. These are the points that matter for someone leaving.

- **The filing test uses your whole estate.** The Attorney General says any estate with a gross value over $4,000,000 must file Illinois Form 700 whether or not a federal return is required, and that this applies to residents and nonresidents. That means a retiree with a $5 million estate and a $500,000 Illinois condo may have a filing duty, even though most assets are in another state.
- **The amount is spread by location.** Illinois figures the tax as if everything were in Illinois, then multiplies by the share of the estate located in Illinois. The same method applies to nonresidents.
- **The $4 million is a threshold, not a credit.** An estate at or under it generally owes nothing to Illinois. Over it, the interrelated calculation can produce a large number.
- **Nothing carries between spouses.** Illinois does not allow portability, so a late spouse's unused amount cannot be added to yours.
- **Timing.** The return and the tax are due nine months after death, and an extension must be requested within that time.

### A worked example

Example only, using the Attorney General's published illustration. For an estate of $5,000,000, all in Illinois, the Attorney General shows an Illinois estate tax of $285,714. Suppose Walt, 71, moves to Arizona and dies with a $5,000,000 gross estate, of which $1,000,000 is a Lake County, Illinois condo he kept. That is 20% in Illinois. Applying the share method, the Illinois tax would be about $57,000 (20% of $285,714). If Walt had sold the condo before moving, the Illinois share would be zero. If his gross estate were $3.8 million, no Illinois return would be needed at all.

The figures are for illustration only. The Attorney General's online calculator is the way to test a real estate, and the destination state may have its own tax. A move to a state with no estate tax removes the Illinois tax on assets outside Illinois, but not on Illinois land you decide to keep.

## What happens to Illinois real estate I keep after I move?

If it is in your sole name, your family generally needs a probate case in Illinois, because land is controlled by the state where it sits. This is ancillary probate. It adds a second lawyer, second court fees and a second timeline even when everything else is handled in your new state. Our page for the opposite situation, an [Illinois resident with property in another state](/learn/illinois/illinois-resident-owning-property-in-another-state), explains the mechanics, and the main article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) covers the general rule.

You have four common choices:

1. **Sell before you go.** Simplest. It removes the Illinois estate tax connection and the second case, though you should look at capital gains tax with a tax professional.
2. **Move the property into a revocable living trust.** The trust owns the deed, so there is no Illinois probate estate for that property. It does not by itself reduce the Illinois estate tax, which is explained on our [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) page.
3. **Use an Illinois transfer on death instrument.** It covers Illinois real estate and passes it at death to the person you name. See the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument). Ask an Illinois attorney to confirm it fits your ownership and the property type.
4. **Leave it as is and accept the second case.** Reasonable for a low-value property if your family understands the cost. Do it knowingly.

If your Illinois property is held in a land trust, read [what happens to an Illinois land trust when the owner dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-land-trust-when-the-owner-dies) first, because the interest passes differently from a deed. Most condos and houses are not in a land trust, but check before assuming.

## Before-the-move checklist for an Illinois retiree

1. List every Illinois asset: real estate, bank accounts, business interests, tax-deferred accounts, life insurance. Note how each is titled.
2. Add up your gross estate, including life insurance and retirement accounts. Compare it to $4 million and use the Attorney General's calculator.
3. Decide what happens to each Illinois property: sell, trust, transfer on death instrument or keep.
4. Keep copies of your Illinois will, trust, power of attorney and health care directive, and tell your agents and executor where they are.
5. Book a lawyer licensed in your destination state to review and re-sign the documents there. Do not rely on an Illinois attorney for that state's law.
6. Re-check beneficiary forms on retirement accounts and insurance.
7. Make the domicile move real and consistent: license, voter registration, tax filings, address on legal papers, time spent.
8. If an Illinois executor is named, ask whether a local backup should be added, since a non-resident executor can be required to post a bond (755 ILCS 5/6-13).

## What about my spouse and my children?

Both depend on your new state's rules for spouses and probate, which you should confirm with a lawyer there. On the Illinois side, remember that a surviving spouse's rights under an Illinois will are covered in [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death), and that Illinois has no inheritance tax for children. A plan that leaves everything to a spouse outright can waste the first spouse's $4 million Illinois exclusion if you keep significant Illinois property.
