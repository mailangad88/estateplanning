Your mother is 78, lives alone in her Illinois home, and can no longer manage bathing or cooking safely. You want her to stay put. Someone at the hospital mentioned the Community Care Program, and now you are wondering what it pays for, whether her savings disqualify her, and whether the state will come after the house later.

This page covers the Illinois Community Care Program: who qualifies, how the asset limit works, how the Medicaid application fits in, and what recovery can look like afterward. Nursing home Medicaid has its own rules, which our page on [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) owns. This is general education, not legal advice, and the limits are reset by the state, so confirm current numbers before acting.

## What is the Illinois Community Care Program?

It is a state program run by the Illinois Department on Aging to help older adults who might otherwise need a nursing home stay in their own homes. The Department says it began in 1979 under Public Act 81-202 and describes it as a cost-effective alternative to nursing home placement. The statute behind it is Section 4.02 of the Illinois Act on the Aging (20 ILCS 105/4.02), which states the goal as preventing unnecessary institutionalization of people 60 and older who need long term care.

Services are coordinated locally through Care Coordination Units. The Department lists these services:

| Service | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Care coordination | Assessment and a care plan |
| In-home service | Cleaning, meal preparation, laundry, shopping, errands, and personal care such as bathing, dressing and grooming |
| Emergency home response | A wearable button linked to a 24-hour support center, for people with documented health and safety needs and mobility limits |
| Adult day service | Supervised daytime care with health and medication oversight, meals and activities, which also gives family caregivers a break |
| Automated medication dispenser | A device that dispenses or reminds, tracks missed doses and alerts caregivers |

## Who qualifies for the Community Care Program?

Five conditions, according to the Department on Aging. The person must be 60 or older, a U.S. citizen or an eligible non-citizen, and an Illinois resident. They must have non-exempt assets of $17,500 or less. And they must have an assessed need for long-term care, meaning they are at risk of nursing facility placement as measured by the Determination of Need (DON) assessment. The Department's page does not publish scoring details, so the Care Coordination Unit is where you learn whether a particular score qualifies.

The program is open to anyone who asks and meets the current requirements. That is worth saying plainly, because many families assume it is a waiting list like some Medicaid waivers. Our page on [Medicaid home care waivers](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver) explains why those programs often have waiting lists.

## How do the asset rules work?

The $17,500 limit applies to non-exempt assets only. The Department says the home, a car and personal furnishings are exempt. So the house does not push Mom over the line, but the savings account, a CD, brokerage holdings or a second property can.

A married applicant gets some help. Section 4.02 says that, in determining services, the Department does not give consideration to the value of assets held in the spouse's name under certain marital property agreements, as long as the spouse's share is not made available to the applicant. That is a narrow rule with conditions, so a married couple should not assume it applies to them without checking.

Here is an example, labelled as one. Ruth, 81, has a $210,000 house, a 2016 sedan, $9,000 in checking and $6,000 in a savings account. Her countable assets are $15,000, because the house and car are exempt. She is under the $17,500 limit. If she had inherited another $5,000 last spring and kept it in the bank, she would be at $20,000 and over. The usual mistake our attorneys see is paying for the Care Coordination Unit's assessment, learning the family is a few thousand dollars over, and then making hasty gifts. A gift is a transfer, and transfers are exactly what Medicaid reviews. Talk to an attorney before moving money.

## Why does Community Care require a Medicaid application?

Because the statute says so. Section 4.02 requires, as a condition of eligibility, that new financially eligible applicants apply for and enroll in medical assistance. The same section lets the Department authorize CCP services until the Medicaid decision comes in.

This is the part that surprises families. CCP is not a separate world from Medicaid. If your parent files a Medicaid application, the state looks at her finances and her transfers. The look-back the HFS uses for long-term care Medicaid is 60 months, and our page on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains how penalties work. Whether a particular gift would affect a particular CCP applicant depends on which Medicaid category the application falls under, which this page cannot answer for you. Raise any gift in the last five years with the attorney before the application, not after.

## What does it cost?

The statute lets the Department set a copayment system for recipients. The copayment is based on the person's ability to pay and can never exceed the actual cost of the services. Income at or below the federal poverty standard is left out when the copayment is set, and the level adjusts when that standard changes. The Department's current rate schedule is on its own site, so ask your Care Coordination Unit for the figure rather than relying on a number from an article.

## Can the state recover CCP costs from the estate?

In limited cases, yes. Section 4.02 allows the Department to recover money spent on CCP services through a claim against the person's estate, or the surviving spouse's estate. The statute then narrows it:

1. No recovery until after the death of the surviving spouse, if there is one.
2. No recovery while a surviving child is under 21, blind, or permanently and totally disabled.
3. Recovery cannot be enforced against real estate while it is occupied as a homestead by a surviving spouse or another dependent, if no other creditors have filed claims.
4. If a spouse dies first, payments made for the person can be claimed against the deceased spouse's estate.

Compare that to the HFS rules on Medicaid long-term care recovery, which have their own protected-family list and a $25,000 estate threshold for recent deaths. Our page on [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) covers those. They are separate rules from separate agencies, so a claim from one does not tell you what the other will do.

Recovery follows the probate estate, so how assets are titled matters. A home that passed outside probate is a different conversation from one that did not. Our guide to [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains why. If your parent has not signed a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property), do that while she can, because the agent will handle applications and paperwork.

## How do you apply, step by step?

1. Find your local Care Coordination Unit using the Department on Aging's map tool or the Find a Provider search.
2. Ask for an assessment. The DON measures the need for nursing-facility-level care.
3. Gather statements for every non-exempt account, so you can show the $17,500 total.
4. Apply for Medicaid when asked. The agent under a power of attorney can sign if the applicant cannot.
5. Review the care plan and confirm which services you chose, how often, and who provides them.
6. Ask about the copayment in writing before services start.
7. Keep every notice. If the situation changes, such as a move to a facility or an inheritance, tell the coordinator.

## When is a different program the better fit?

When the need outgrows a few hours a week. CCP is designed around keeping someone at home with support, and it ends at the point of nursing home admission. For full-time care, a spouse at home, or assets well above the limit, the comparison to [nursing home Medicaid](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) and to [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) matters more than the program itself. Many families use CCP for two or three years and then face a nursing home decision, so planning for the second step during the first is sensible.
