Hiring a probate lawyer can feel like a bill on top of a loss, and plenty of Illinois families wonder whether the executor can simply do the paperwork. Sometimes yes, often partly, and in some counties not for the first step.

This page is about doing it yourself in Illinois: which estates fit, what the court expects of you, and the steps where people stall. It sits in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). For the national version of the question, see [do I need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer).

## The short version

- A small estate with only personal property can be collected with an affidavit and no court case.
- A full probate case has more steps than the forms suggest, and Cook County expects an attorney to open it.
- You can do much of the work yourself and still pay a lawyer for the court filings.
- Paying heirs too early is the costliest error.

## Can you do probate yourself in Illinois?

For a small estate, yes. For a full case, it depends on the county and on how simple the estate is. The Illinois Probate Act does not make you hire a lawyer to serve as executor, but a clerk's office can still set its own practice for filings.

The Cook County Clerk of the Circuit Court says on its probate page that to open a probate estate, with or without a will, you need to hire an attorney, who completes the court forms and files them electronically. It lists a $479 filing fee and says filing a will with the clerk costs nothing. Fees change, so confirm the amount with your clerk. Other Illinois counties publish their own rules and fee schedules, and some courts see more self-represented filers than others. Phone the circuit clerk in the county where the person lived and ask two questions: can I petition without counsel, and do you have forms?

The affidavit route is covered in detail in our [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) article. It requires that no letters of office be outstanding and no petition be pending, and it cannot transfer real estate. The person who signs must pay valid debts first.

If the personal estate is below the limit but a house is in the dead person's name, the affidavit does not reach the house. That is the most common reason a "small" estate ends up in court. A transfer on death instrument, if the person recorded one, can sidestep it. See [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).

## What does the court expect from an executor?

A lot more than filing the will. Here is the sequence a self-handling executor still has to complete, using the same Illinois steps our probate page lists:

1. File the original will with the circuit court clerk right after the death.
2. Petition to admit the will and appoint the executor, and attend the hearing.
3. Receive letters of office, which say independent or supervised, and sign an oath. Some executors must post a surety bond.
4. Mail written notice to heirs and legatees within 14 days after the order admitting the will, with the required explanation of their rights (755 ILCS 5/6-10).
5. Publish a claims notice once a week for three weeks and mail it to every known creditor (755 ILCS 5/18-3).
6. Open an estate bank account, collect assets, file an inventory, and pay claims in the statutory order.
7. File the final tax returns.
8. Wait for the claims deadline, distribute, and close with a verified report that gives interested people 42 days to object (755 ILCS 5/28-11).

The six-month contest window for the will and the claims period make a first-time executor's calendar long. Our [probate process article](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) gives the full details, and [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) explains why even a clean case runs past six months.

## Where do people get stuck?

Four spots, in the order they usually appear.

**The petition and hearing.** The clerk can answer where to file but not what to say. A wrong heir list or missing information in the petition can hold up letters. Proving who the heirs are, especially without a will, has its own procedure: see [proving heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois).

**The bond.** An administrator with no will should expect a surety bond, sized to the personal estate. An executor can be excused from bond by the will, though the court may require one anyway for a nonresident. [Letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) covers the numbers.

**The notices.** Missed or late notices to heirs, or publishing in the wrong newspaper or county, create problems that surface months later. Creditor notice is where our attorneys see the most avoidable errors. The creditor timeline is walked through in [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate).

**Selling the house.** Even in an independent estate, a title company wants letters, the will and a death certificate. In a supervised estate, the court has to approve the sale. See [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois).

## What mistake costs the most?

Distributing before the claims deadline. Take Brian, executor for his father. The will leaves everything equally to Brian and his sister. Eager to be fair, Brian divides $40,000 in January. In April, a hospital claim for $18,000 arrives inside the claims period. Brian has to ask his sister to return her share of it, and she has already spent it. Illinois lets a court order a refund and holds a distributee to the extent needed to pay a valid claim, but the executor is the one explaining. Wait for the bar date unless the court approves an early distribution.

Second place goes to paying a bill that belongs lower in the order. Funeral and administration costs sit in class 1, and credit cards in the last class. The table is on [credit card debt after death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois).

## What is the middle path?

Many families split the work. You gather statements, make the list of assets, talk to the heirs, handle the funeral and organize the house. A lawyer prepares the petition, appears at the hearing, drafts the notices and reviews the final report. This is sometimes called limited-scope help, and you should agree on exactly which tasks are covered in writing. Our [probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) article lists the fees in an Illinois case, and the [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) puts numbers on it.

Compare that cost with the cost of an error. An executor who gets a step wrong can be held personally accountable, and heirs who feel in the dark can petition a judge. See [what heirs can do about a slow Illinois executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor) for how that looks from the other side, and the [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) for the whole job.
