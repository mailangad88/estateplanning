Your uncle died without a will. His house sits in his name. The title company wants proof of who his heirs are before it will insure a sale, and nobody can agree what to hand over. A family tree on a napkin will not satisfy anyone.

Illinois has a procedure for exactly this. The Probate Act lets the court determine and declare who the heirs are, and it allows that proof to be made two ways. This article explains both, what each one does, and where families get stuck. It is general education and not legal advice. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the wider picture. For who the heirs usually are, start with [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

## The short version

- Heirship is a factual question: who are the people the law says inherit, given this family?
- You prove it by sworn affidavit or by recorded testimony, and the court can then declare it by order.
- The order is strong evidence, but it is not the only proof.
- If the family is simple and the estate small, a different tool, the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit), may do the job.

## What does "heirship" mean, and when does it come up?

An heir is someone entitled by law to inherit when there is no will, or whose share is fixed by law. Heirship comes up when someone dies intestate, when a will does not cover all the property, when title to real estate must be cleared, or when a person is not sure they are in line. Our glossary entry on [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession) gives the vocabulary.

Take Dolores, who dies a widow with two sons, one of whom died years ago leaving two children. Who inherits? The living son, and the grandchildren, probably. But a bank or a buyer will not take a family member's word. They want a document.

## How can heirship be proved under Illinois law?

Section 5-3 of the Probate Act gives the court power to ascertain and declare heirship, and sets two ways to prove it (755 ILCS 5/5-3):

1. **An affidavit.** Any person may sign an affidavit stating the facts from which heirship can be determined.
2. **Testimony.** Evidence taken in narrative form or by question and answer, reduced to writing and certified by the court.

During administration of the estate, the court rules on heirship as part of the case. If administration has not been granted, the court sets the notice and the manner.

### What does the affidavit have to say?

It states facts, not conclusions. The affiant says who the decedent was, when and where the person died, whether the decedent married and when, the names and dates of any children, whether any child died earlier and who survived that child, and whether anyone was adopted or born outside marriage. Add the affiant's relationship and how they know. A sworn statement from someone who knew Dolores for 40 years carries more weight than a stranger's.

### Who can sign it, and how is it sworn?

The statute says any person may sign. It does not list special qualifications. Practically, it should be someone with real knowledge of the family. The affidavit must be sworn or affirmed before a notary or a judge of a court of record in the United States or its possessions or territories, certified by that court's clerk when the judge takes it. It may also be sworn before certain U.S. consular or military officers, whose seals and designations are sufficient proof of identity and capacity. The clerk files the affidavit or transcript, and it becomes part of the case file.

## What is an order declaring heirship?

It is the court's finding, entered after the evidence, of who the decedent's heirs are. Under section 5-3, an order declaring heirship is prima facie evidence of heirship. Prima facie means it stands as proof unless someone presents evidence to the contrary.

Two points are worth noting. First, the statute says an interested person may use any other legal method of proving heirship, in any place or court where the question arises. The order is not the only road. Second, where there is no contrary evidence, the court may presume that the decedent, and any person through whom heirship is traced, were not parents of a child born outside marriage. For male persons, the court may also presume that no such child was filiated, acknowledged or legitimated. If you know of such a child, say so in the affidavit.

## How do you move from family tree to court order?

A typical path looks like this.

1. Collect the facts: marriage and death certificates, birth records for children, and records of any adoption.
2. Draw the family tree back to the decedent's spouse, children and, for any child who died first, that child's descendants.
3. Decide who can swear to the facts. Often it is two relatives who did not share the same side of the family.
4. Open the estate, usually by petition, because the court declares heirship during administration. See [how probate works in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) and [letters of office](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration).
5. File the affidavit or present the testimony, and give notice as the court directs.
6. Ask the court to enter its order declaring heirship, then give certified copies to the bank, title company or transfer agent.

The order matters most for real estate. If your uncle's house is in his name, the heirs cannot sell it with a family tree. Our article on [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) covers what comes next.

## When is heirship hard?

Heirship is routine when the family is small and records are complete. It gets harder in these cases:

| Situation | Why it complicates proof |
|---|---|
| Unknown or missing relatives | You may need records or searches to find everyone |
| A child born outside marriage | The presumption can be overcome with evidence |
| Adoption, step-relatives | Whether they inherit depends on the legal relationship |
| A predeceased child with descendants | Their children must be identified |
| Disputed family stories | Competing affidavits may require a hearing |

If relatives dispute the facts, a sworn affidavit may not be enough, and testimony before the court becomes the better route.

## How is this different from a small estate affidavit?

They solve different problems. An affidavit of heirship proves who the family is. The [small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) lets people collect certain property of a small estate without opening a full case, and it is a separate tool with its own requirements. If the estate qualifies, you may not need a court order declaring heirship at all. If real estate or a dispute is involved, you probably will.

## A common mistake

Families often assume a funeral home death certificate, plus a signed list of relatives, will satisfy a bank. It rarely does for real estate or larger accounts. Another common slip is leaving out a person because everyone "knows" they were estranged. Heirship is about legal relationship, not closeness. If an estranged child is an heir and is left out, the title problem returns years later, when you try to sell.

The cleaner approach is to list every person the law might count, state the facts about each, and let the court sort it out. Where you truly do not know whether an heir exists, say so in the affidavit instead of guessing. A careful affidavit that admits a gap is more useful than a confident one that is wrong.

Also keep the records. Certified copies of the order cost little and are handy when a bank, a county recorder or a retirement plan each asks for their own.
