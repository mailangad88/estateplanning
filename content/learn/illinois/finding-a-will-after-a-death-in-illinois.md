Your uncle Tom died on a Tuesday. By Friday, three relatives have told you three different things about his will: it is in a drawer, it is with a lawyer in Peoria, or he never made one. Nobody knows. Meanwhile his mail is piling up and the house is empty.

This page walks through where a will is usually found, what Illinois law says about who has to file it, how a bank handles a safe deposit box, and who gets to read it once it is in court. For the rest of the process, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What does Illinois law say about a will once the person dies?

Whoever has the will must file it. Section 6-1 of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/6-1) says that anyone holding a testator's will must file it with the clerk of the proper county court immediately after the death. If the holder fails or refuses, the court can act on its own or on a petition from an interested person, and may issue an attachment to compel production of the will.

The same section makes it a crime to willfully alter or destroy a will without the testator's direction, or to willfully hide it for 30 days after learning of the death. A conviction is punishable as theft of property classified as a Class 3 felony. That should settle one common family argument: a will found in a drawer is not the finder's to sit on while the relatives "talk it over."

Filing a will is not the same as starting probate. The filing puts the document with the court. The probate case itself begins with a petition, which our [probate process guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) describes step by step.

## Where do you look for a will?

Start close and work outward. People keep wills in more places than you would expect.

1. **The home.** A fireproof box, a desk drawer, a file marked "important," or a folder in a closet. Look for a copy even if you cannot find the original.
2. **The lawyer who drafted it.** Call the firms the person mentioned. Attorneys often keep originals, and a letter on the firm's stationery in the mail pile is a clue.
3. **A safe deposit box.** Look for a key, a rental statement or a bank envelope in the mail.
4. **Family and fiduciaries.** A named executor, a trustee, an accountant or a sibling may hold a copy or the original.
5. **The court.** Check whether the clerk's office in the county where the person lived has a file already open. Our [county probate guides](/estate-planning/illinois) show where each Illinois clerk's office is.
6. **The Secretary of State.** Section 6-1 refers to Section 5.15 of the Secretary of State Act, which deals with a will on deposit with that office. Ask the office whether one exists and what it requires of you.

If the original cannot be found, a copy is not automatically useless, but proving a lost will takes more work. Read our article on a [lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will) before assuming the worst.

## How does a safe deposit box get opened after a death?

Illinois has a statute written for exactly this problem, the Safe Deposit Box Opening Act (755 ILCS 15/1). The point is to let a family look for a will or burial papers without waiting weeks for a court.

The process works like this:

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Proof of death | The bank receives proof that the sole renter, or the last surviving co-renter, has died. |
| Interested person appears | A person with an interest signs an affidavit saying they want to file a will or arrange burial and believe the box may hold those papers. |
| Box is opened | The bank opens it in the interested person's presence. |
| Will is removed | The bank takes out any apparent will or codicil and delivers it to the circuit court clerk in the renter's county of residence, if known. |
| Burial papers | The bank may give burial documents to the interested person. |
| Nothing else | No other contents may be removed under this Act. |

Who counts as interested? Someone who had deputy access to the box before the death, an executor named in a copy of a purported will, or the renter's spouse, adult descendant, parent or sibling. The bank may in its discretion accept another person with a legitimate interest.

The bank does not have to proceed if someone objects, or if it expects an objection, or if the key or combination is missing. It must not proceed under this Act if it has received letters of office, another court order or a small estate affidavit. In those cases, the representative or the affidavit's designee opens the box and removes the contents. So the quick route is for the search for a will. For jewelry and deeds in the box, you will need the executor with letters, or a court order.

## When is a will public?

A will is private until it is filed. After that, it is part of a court file. The petition for probate lists the testator's name and residence, the date and place of death, the date of the will, the estimated value of the Illinois estate, and the names and mailing addresses of all heirs and legatees, along with the executor (755 ILCS 5/6-2). A file with that much information is something anyone can ask the clerk about.

Assets that pass outside probate, such as an account with a beneficiary, do not appear in the will. Our general article on [whether probate is public](/learn/probate/is-probate-public) covers what strangers can see and how families limit it. If privacy matters to you, a trust is the usual answer, and our [Illinois living trusts article](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) covers how the Illinois Trust Code works.

## What happens after a will is filed?

Within 14 days after the court admits or denies the will or appoints a representative, the representative or the petitioner must mail a copy of the petition and the order to each heir and legatee named in the petition (755 ILCS 5/6-10). If an heir's name or address is missing, notice goes in a newspaper once a week for three successive weeks. The notice tells the reader about the right to ask for formal proof of the will and the right to contest.

That formal proof request has a 42-day window, counted from the effective date of the order admitting the will (755 ILCS 5/6-21). Filing it does not extend the separate deadline to contest. If you suspect forgery, undue influence or a missing codicil, call an attorney the day you get the notice. Our overview of [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains the usual grounds.

## Example: finding Tom's will

Tom's niece Lena found a bank key in his kitchen drawer. The bank's records showed Tom as the only renter. Lena brought his death certificate and her ID, signed the affidavit as his niece, and watched a bank officer open the box. Inside were two envelopes. One held a will signed in 2014, the other a burial plot deed. The bank mailed the will to the circuit court clerk and handed Lena the deed. She never touched the will. A week later the clerk's office had it on file. Tom's named executor, a friend, filed the petition within the month.

## What if there is no will?

If you search and find nothing, the estate passes by the intestacy rules. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) sets out the order of heirs. The search for a will deserves a few weeks of effort, though, because a later-found will can reorder everything.

If you are the person who will leave a will, tell someone where it is. Our guide to [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) covers the practical options.
