You have been told that the judge will appoint you guardian once you "get a bond." That sounds like a bank loan or a deposit, and the next question is who will give you one. A bond is closer to an insurance policy that protects the person whose money you will manage.

This page focuses on the guardian bond for an adult under Article XIa of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/11a). For the same idea in an estate after a death, see [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond). For the whole picture, see the [Illinois guardianship overview](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship).

## What is a guardian bond, and who does it protect?

A guardian bond is a written promise, backed by a surety company, to pay the estate if the guardian's mistakes or dishonesty cause a loss. The guardian signs an oath to do the job faithfully, and the surety backs the money. If the surety pays, it can pursue the guardian to recover. The person under guardianship is the protected party.

The general rules on bonds for court-appointed representatives are in Article XII of the Probate Act, including sections 12-2 and 12-5. The court has the final say on what security it accepts.

## When does the court require one?

A bond applies to a guardian of the estate, because that role handles money and property. A guardian of the person generally does not post a bond. A judge can require security in a particular case if there is concern about the guardian or the size of the estate, and can set conditions. Because practice varies, ask the clerk for the local rule and the form of order.

## How big is the bond?

Under section 12-5 (755 ILCS 5/12-5) the bond must be at least one and a half times the personal estate when a surety company signs, and at least double when individuals act as sureties. If the guardian will manage real estate income, the court may increase the amount. The amount is not what you pay. It is the limit of the surety's promise, and the premium is a small slice of it.

Example math: Elena's father has $80,000 in personal property. With a surety company, the bond would be at least $120,000. The premium is a percentage of that figure, which depends on the surety and the guardian's credit. Ask for quotes before the hearing.

## How can the bond be reduced?

The size follows the property the guardian can reach. In many counties, a judge will lower the bond if the guardian places most funds in a restricted account at a financial institution. The institution agrees not to release money without a court order. That reduces the amount the surety must cover. Details differ by county, so ask whether your court uses such orders and what forms it needs.

Other ways to keep the bond small:

- Do not keep large cash balances in the guardian's control.
- Ask whether benefits paid to a representative payee are counted. Social Security payments handled by a payee are overseen separately by the agency.
- Treat real estate with care, because increased bond amounts can follow.

## What does it cost, and who pays?

The premium is a fraction of the bond amount. We do not list a rate, because surety companies price by the guardian's credit, the bond amount and the type of case. The premium is usually paid again each year or term the bond remains in force. With court approval it comes out of the person's estate, and the guardian keeps the receipt. Compare it with other costs in [what adult guardianship costs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/cost-of-guardianship-in-illinois).

## What are the steps to get a bond?

1. Ask the clerk whether the court will require a bond and in what amount.
2. Gather the order or draft order, your identification and basic financial information.
3. Ask an insurance agent who writes court bonds for a quote.
4. Complete the application, which may include a credit check.
5. File the bond with the clerk and ask the court to approve it.
6. Receive letters of office once the bond is approved.
7. Calendar the renewal date and keep the premium receipt for your accounts.

## How do bond choices compare?

| | Surety bond, full amount | Bond with restricted account | Individual sureties |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum amount | 1.5 times personal estate | Reduced where court allows | 2 times personal estate |
| Who signs | Surety company | Surety company plus bank order | Two individuals |
| Access to funds | Guardian, within court rules | Court order needed to withdraw | Guardian |
| Cost | Annual premium | Lower premium | Often impractical |
| Fits | Active management of funds | Savings left mostly untouched | Rare |

## When does the bond end?

The bond stays in place until the court discharges the guardian after a final account, or a successor takes over. Your accounts must be in order. See [annual reports and accountings for an Illinois guardian](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardian-reports-and-accountings) and [ending or changing an Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/ending-or-changing-an-illinois-guardianship).

Guardians for minors who inherit money meet the same bond rule. Our article on [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) and the national piece on [guardian versus trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) show how a trust can reduce the need for a bond.

## What happens if the guardian cannot pay the premium?

Tell the court and the surety before the bond lapses. A lapsed bond can stop the guardian from acting, and the judge may name a successor. If cash is short because funds are tied up, the court can approve payment of the premium from the estate. Keep invoices so your account shows the expense.

## Are bonds needed for minors too?

Often yes, when a child's money is large enough to need a guardian of the estate. The same Article XII rules apply, and a UTMA account or a trust can sometimes avoid the need. See [Illinois UTMA accounts and gifts to minors](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) and the national piece on [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).
