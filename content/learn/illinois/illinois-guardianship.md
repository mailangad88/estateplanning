Guardianship in Illinois covers two very different situations. One is an adult who can no longer manage their own health or money. The other is a child whose parents have died or cannot care for them. The courts, the forms, and the rules differ for each.

This article explains both, starting with the adult process in Article XIa of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/11a). It is general education, not legal advice. See our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) for the wider plan.

## What is guardianship of an adult in Illinois?

It is a court order that gives another person authority over the personal decisions, the property, or both, of an adult with a disability. A guardian of the person handles care, such as health and living arrangements. A guardian of the estate handles money and property. The court may appoint one person for both roles.

Illinois law defines a person with a disability as someone 18 or older who, because of mental deterioration or physical incapacity, is not fully able to manage their person or estate. The court must find this by clear and convincing evidence (755 ILCS 5/11a-3). A bad decision alone is not enough.

## Does the court give the guardian full control?

Not necessarily. The statute says guardianship should be used only as necessary to promote the person's well-being and ordered only to the extent the person's actual mental, physical, and adaptive limitations require (755 ILCS 5/11a-3). This means the court can tailor an order, called a limited guardianship, instead of taking all decisions away.

Take Hal, 81, who has memory loss but still handles his daily routine. A court might give a guardian power over his finances only and leave his personal choices alone.

## How does an adult guardianship case work?

The steps below give the general path. Local court practice varies by county.

1. A petition is filed by a reputable person or by the person themselves.
2. A doctor evaluates the person, and the report is filed within 3 months of the petition.
3. The person receives the petition and summons at least 14 days before the hearing.
4. The court appoints a guardian ad litem, who investigates for the court. The person has the right to a lawyer and may ask for a jury.
5. At the hearing, the court looks at the person's intellectual and physical functioning and decides if guardianship is needed.
6. If so, the court enters a written order that states the facts it found and the guardian's duties and powers.

### What about emergencies?

A court can appoint a temporary guardian for the immediate welfare and protection of the person. A temporary guardianship expires within 60 days after appointment, or sooner if a regular guardian is appointed (755 ILCS 5/11a-4). Families use this when a hospital discharge or a financial scam cannot wait.

## What does a guardian have to do?

A guardian of the person must provide for the ward's support, care, comfort, health, education, and maintenance (755 ILCS 5/11a-17). A guardian of the estate manages the estate frugally and uses income and principal for the ward's support (755 ILCS 5/11a-18). Guardians answer to the court and report to it. The court may require a bond, so ask the clerk or your attorney about the current rules.

The ward keeps the right to ask the court to change or end the guardianship. The court must tell the ward about this right in writing (755 ILCS 5/11a-19). It may terminate the guardianship if the ward's capacity is shown by clear and convincing evidence (755 ILCS 5/11a-20).

## How does guardianship work for a minor child?

For a child under 18, Article XI of the Probate Act applies. A guardian must be at least 18, live in the United States, and be of sound mind, among other qualifications (755 ILCS 5/11-3). A parent can designate a guardian in writing, including in a will, with at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/11-5). Some details turn on the wording, so have an attorney review.

The court may appoint a guardian of the person, the estate, or both when it serves the child's best interest. If a parent is willing and able to make and carry out day-to-day child care decisions, the court generally lacks jurisdiction unless that parent consents or does not object. A minor who is 14 or older may nominate a guardian. The guardianship ends when the child turns 18, or earlier if a parent proves a material change in circumstances (755 ILCS 5/11-14.1).
