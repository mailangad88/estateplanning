If your father or aunt lived in Galesburg, Knoxville, Abingdon or anywhere else in Knox County, the estate is tied to the Knox County court. The county sits in the 9th Judicial Circuit. That circuit also covers Fulton, Hancock, Henderson, McDonough and Warren counties. This page is part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

Below you will find the courthouse and clerk, how the will is filed, how remote hearings work, what self-help exists and how to look up a case. It then takes up a worry many families have: what to do when the original will cannot be found. To talk about your own estate, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-probate-works-in-knox-county-illinois&topic=probate) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Where is the Knox County Courthouse and Circuit Clerk?

The Knox County Courthouse is at 200 South Cherry Street, Galesburg, IL 61401. The 9th Circuit's Knox County page and the Illinois Courts directory give that address. The state's list of circuit clerks puts the Knox County Circuit Clerk at the same street address.

Both court sources list weekday hours of 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. The numbers are less tidy. The 9th Circuit page shows one number for the clerk, the Illinois Courts directory shows another for the court, and the state's clerk list shows a third. Rather than guess, call the courthouse switchboard and ask for the Circuit Clerk.

| Place | Where | What a probate family uses it for |
|---|---|---|
| Circuit Clerk | 200 South Cherry Street, Galesburg | Filing the will and the estate case |
| E-filing kiosk | First floor, next to the civil division | Sending documents electronically |
| Self-Help Center and law library | Basement | Forms help and legal reference |
| Courthouse lot | South side, plus three sides of the building | Free parking, per the state directory |

The same directory lists a copier, a printer, lockers and a waiting room on the second floor. It also says cameras are allowed in a courtroom only if a motion is filed and granted.

## What should the family gather before going to Galesburg?

Start with the will and a rough list of what the person owned. [Finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) explains where wills turn up, from a desk drawer to an attorney's vault.

Then sort the list into two piles. Accounts with a named beneficiary and property held jointly usually pass outside court. A house in the deceased person's name alone usually points toward probate. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) shows where each item tends to land.

If the person also owned land in another county, read [which county handles probate when property is in more than one county](/learn/illinois/probate-venue-property-in-two-illinois-counties). For a small estate, a [small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) may mean no court case at all.

## How does an estate open in Knox County, and where does the will go?

The filing starts with the person who holds the will. Illinois expects that person to deliver the original to the circuit court clerk after a death, as covered in [filing the original will with the Illinois circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk). In Knox County that means the Circuit Clerk in Galesburg.

After the will is on file, someone asks the court to open the estate and to appoint the person who will run it. When there is a will, that person is usually the named executor. When there is not, it is an administrator. The steps are laid out in [how to open a probate estate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-open-a-probate-estate-in-illinois), and the whole path is in the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process).

<!-- visual: diagram name=ProbateTimeline -->

The Knox County and 9th Circuit pages do not describe a probate-specific checklist, a will-deposit window or what identification the clerk wants. Those are questions for the clerk. A short list helps you arrive ready:

1. Bring the original will, flat and unstapled, and a photocopy for yourself.
2. Write down the full legal name and the date of death.
3. Note the names and addresses of the relatives who would inherit if there were no will.
4. Ask whether the case must be e-filed, and whether the kiosk on the first floor can be used.
5. Keep any receipt or stamped copy with the estate papers.

## Can I attend a Knox County hearing by Zoom?

Often, yes, but the court sets the terms. The 9th Circuit's page on attending court by Zoom lists a separate link for each courtroom, organized by county on the circuit's home page. The meeting ID belongs to the courtroom, not to your case.

The page tells you to start early so you are in the waiting room when court begins. You enter the meeting ID or link name, and a password if the link has one. You then join with video and computer audio, and wait to be admitted. Your audio stays muted until your case is called.

Use your legal name as your display name. The page says the court may punish recording, photographing or sharing the Zoom or YouTube feeds as contempt. That rule comes from Illinois Supreme Court Rule 68(A)(8).

The Zoom page does not say how to ask for a remote hearing, or how far ahead. For that, check the circuit's court rules PDF or ask the clerk. An attorney handling the case will know the local practice.

## What self-help does the Knox County court offer?

The Illinois Courts directory says the Self-Help Center is in the courthouse basement, staffed by a JusticeCorps Fellow, with occasional volunteers from Knox College. A law library is also in the basement.

The 9th Circuit's resource page says Knox County JusticeCorps helps with civil paperwork, forms, e-filing and resources, and that it does not provide legal advice. It lists no probate or estate resources, so ask the center directly what it can help with before you make the trip.

The same page points to Illinois Legal Aid for finding a legal aid attorney. If you want to handle an estate without hiring anyone, read [handling an Illinois probate estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer) first. For court costs, see [the Illinois probate fee waiver](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-fee-waiver).

## How do I look up a Knox County case?

The 9th Circuit's Knox County page links to a Judici daily docket and a Judici case search for the county. That page describes the search as running by name, ticket number or case number, and it speaks mainly about traffic and payments.

It does not say whether estate files appear there or how much of a file is visible. If you are an heir and are not sure an estate was ever opened, [how to find out if you are named in a will](/learn/illinois/how-to-find-out-if-you-are-named-in-a-will-in-illinois) explains the next steps. Calling the clerk is the fallback.

## What if the original will cannot be found?

This is the problem that stops many Knox County families before they reach the clerk. Someone remembers a will. The house has been searched. The paper is gone. Illinois treats this carefully, because a person can revoke a will by destroying it.

Under [lost original wills in Illinois](/learn/illinois/lost-original-will-in-illinois), a will last held by the maker and not found at death is presumed revoked. The family can answer that presumption with clear and convincing evidence. That means showing the will was validly signed, that it was lost without the maker's intent to revoke, and what it said.

Where the paper was kept changes the picture a great deal.

| Where the original was last kept | How the court is likely to see it | What helps the family |
|---|---|---|
| In the maker's own desk or closet | Presumption of revocation applies | Proof that others had access, or a fire, flood or move |
| In a safe deposit box only the maker used | Presumption usually applies | Bank records and who else held a key |
| In a drafting attorney's vault | Presumption is much weaker | The attorney's receipt and vault records |
| Filed with a court clerk during life | Presumption is much weaker | The clerk's receipt |

If the copy is not admitted, the estate passes under an earlier will or under the intestacy rules in 755 ILCS 5/2-1. The page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) gives that order. The search steps are in the [lost or missing will guide](/free/illinois-lost-or-missing-will-guide), and the broader page on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) covers prevention.

Act early. A will contest in Illinois has a short deadline, covered in the [six-month will contest rule](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline).

## Example: the Lindqvist family in Galesburg

This is an invented example. Walter, 83, lived in Galesburg and died in November. His daughter Anya remembers that he signed a will years ago leaving the house to her and her brother Tomas equally. The family searches the house and finds no will. No bank box exists.

Anya calls the lawyer who prepared it. The attorney's file has a signed copy, a note of the signing date and the names of two witnesses. Walter kept the original at home in a metal box, which was in the basement during a flood two winters ago.

Anya's attorney explains the presumption and says the flood, the water-stained box and the file copy are the evidence to gather. If the court does not admit the copy, Anya and Tomas would still split the estate equally under the intestacy rules, which was the plan anyway. A charity named in the will, though, would receive nothing, so the attorney asks whether it will take part in a settlement. Tomas can join the first hearing by Zoom from Denver if the court allows.
