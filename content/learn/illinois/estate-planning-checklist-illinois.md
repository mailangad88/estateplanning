## What goes on an Illinois estate planning checklist?

Nine items cover most Illinois adults. Not everyone needs all of them, but you should be able to say yes, no or not yet to each.

| # | Item | Illinois signing rule | Read more |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Will | Signed, two credible witnesses, no notary (755 ILCS 5/4-3) | [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) |
| 2 | House: TODI or living trust | TODI: two witnesses, notary, recorded before death (755 ILCS 27) | [TODI vs living trust](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house) |
| 3 | Power of attorney for property | Statutory short form: one witness who is not the agent, plus a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3) | [Property power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) |

## Item 1: Do you have a will that meets Illinois rules?

Check that your will is in writing, signed by you, and signed by at least two credible witnesses who watched you sign. No notary is required. A witness who inherits under the will can lose the gift, so pick witnesses who receive nothing.

What to check off:

- [ ] The will names an executor and at least one backup. See [who can be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-can-be-an-executor-in-illinois).
- [ ] It was signed after your last marriage, divorce or child, or you have confirmed it still fits. [Changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois) covers the ways to update it.
- [ ] If it is handwritten or from a website, you have checked it against [is my will valid in Illinois](/learn/illinois/is-my-will-valid).

If you plan to use a living trust, the will becomes a short backup that sends stray assets into the trust. That is a [pour-over will](/learn/illinois/pour-over-wills-in-illinois).

## Item 2: How will your house pass, by TODI, trust, or neither?

The house is often the one asset that would otherwise go through probate. Three common answers:

- **Transfer on death instrument (TODI).** A recorded document that passes Illinois real estate at death to the person you name. It needs two credible witnesses and a notary, and it must be recorded in the county where the land sits before you die. An unrecorded one does not work (755 ILCS 27/40). See [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) and [recording an Illinois TODI](/learn/illinois/recording-an-illinois-todi).
- **Revocable living trust.** You deed the house to the trust and name a successor trustee, who can also manage it if you become incapacitated. The trust only controls what is actually retitled into it. Read [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) and [how to put your house in a living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-put-your-house-in-a-living-trust-in-illinois).
- **Joint ownership with a spouse.** This handles the first death but not the second. See [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).

Adding a child to your deed carries real risks, explained in [should I add my child to my deed](/learn/illinois/should-i-add-my-child-to-my-deed-illinois).

## Item 3: Have you signed the statutory short form power of attorney for property?

This is the document that lets someone pay your bills, deal with your bank and handle real estate if you cannot. Illinois publishes a statutory short form. It is not valid until you sign it in front of one witness who is not your agent and a notary.

- [ ] You initialed the powers you want to grant and struck any you do not.
- [ ] You named at least one successor agent. The short form allows successors but not co-agents.
- [ ] You chose when it takes effect: right away, or on a date or event you describe.
- [ ] You thought about whether your agent may make gifts or change beneficiaries. Those powers need express wording; see [gifting and beneficiary change powers](/learn/illinois/illinois-poa-gifting-and-beneficiary-change-powers).

The line-by-line walkthrough is in [filling out the Illinois statutory short form](/learn/illinois/filling-out-the-illinois-statutory-short-form-poa-for-property).

## Item 4: Who makes medical decisions, and who can see your records?

Sign a health care power of attorney and a HIPAA release. The health care form needs one adult witness and no notary. Your agent, your successor agents and your attending physician cannot be the witness; [witness rules for an Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/witness-rules-for-an-illinois-health-care-power-of-attorney) lists everyone else who is excluded.

The [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) is a separate page that lets named people see records and talk to your doctors. If you skip the health care form, Illinois falls back on a ranked list of relatives, which may not be who you would choose. See [who decides under the Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).

## Item 5: Do you want a living will, a POLST, or both?

A living will is optional, and a POLST is for people who are already seriously ill or frail. Know which one fits before you sign anything.

The Illinois living will under 755 ILCS 35 tells doctors to withhold or withdraw death-delaying procedures if you have a terminal condition. It is witnessed by two adults. It only applies once your attending physician has diagnosed a terminal condition in writing, and it steps aside while a health care agent is available to decide. Many people rely on their agent and written instructions instead.

A POLST is a medical order you complete with a practitioner. It travels with you, and hospitals, nursing homes and EMS must honor a qualifying form that is apparent and available. See [Illinois POLST and DNR orders](/learn/illinois/illinois-polst-and-dnr-orders).

## Item 6: Do your beneficiary forms match your plan?

Pull every form: 401(k), IRA, pension, life insurance, annuity, and any payable on death or transfer on death account. The form controls the account whatever the will says.

- [ ] Each form names a primary and a [contingent beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).
- [ ] No form names an ex-spouse, a person who has died, or a minor outright.
- [ ] If you are married and want someone other than your spouse on a 401(k), your spouse signed the plan's written waiver.

The Illinois rules on bank accounts are in [payable on death and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts). The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you list them.

## Item 7: If you have children under 18, have you named a guardian?

Name a guardian of the person in your will or another signed writing with two credible witnesses. A judge still decides what serves the child, but your written choice carries weight. [Naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children) covers how to choose. For travel or a hospital stay, a [standby or short-term guardian](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children) fills the gap while you are alive. Money left to a minor needs its own plan; see [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois).

## Item 8: Can anyone reach your digital accounts?

Under 755 ILCS 70, an executor can get the content of your emails and messages only if you consented or a court directs it. You can give that consent in your will, trust or power of attorney, or through a company's own online setting. Add a clause and keep a list of accounts and devices. [The Illinois digital assets law for executors](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors) explains what each kind of request needs, and [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan) covers passwords and crypto.

## Item 9: Where will the originals be kept?

Choose a place your executor can reach. After a death, whoever holds the original will must file it with the circuit clerk, and a photocopy cannot be admitted in its place. A will lost in a drawer can cause a long detour, described in [a lost original will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/lost-original-will-in-illinois).

- [ ] Originals are in a fireproof box at home or with the attorney who prepared them.
- [ ] Your executor, agents and trustee know where, and have copies of the papers that name them.
- [ ] You did not lock the only original in a safe deposit box. Illinois lets family open a box to look for a will, but taking other things out needs court authority; see [the safe deposit box after a death](/learn/illinois/safe-deposit-box-after-a-death-in-illinois).

For organizing the binder itself, see [how to organize your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents).

## What should you gather before meeting an Illinois attorney?

Bring a short packet, not a full inventory.

1. A list of assets and how each is titled: the house (with a copy of the deed), bank and brokerage accounts, retirement accounts, life insurance, vehicles, any business interest, and any property in another state.
2. A rough total. Illinois taxes estates over $4 million, and spouses cannot share that exclusion, so a household near that number needs to say so. See [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).
3. Names and contact details for your executor, agents, guardian and backups.
4. Copies of any existing will, trust, powers of attorney and beneficiary confirmations.
5. Family facts that change the plan: a prior marriage, stepchildren, a child with a disability, a prenuptial agreement.
6. Your questions, written down.

More detail is in [what to bring to an estate planning consultation](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation). Our [Illinois estate planning checklist download](/free/illinois-estate-planning-checklist) gives you fill-in lines for the same items.

## Example: one Illinois household works through the list

This is an invented example. Dev and Hannah, both 44, live in Naperville with two children, 8 and 11. They own their house jointly, each has a 401(k), Dev has term life insurance, and their wills are from before the younger child was born.

Their wills name Hannah's brother as guardian, but he has moved away, so the guardian clause changes first. The joint house passes to the surviving spouse, but they want the second death covered and someone to manage money for the children, so they choose a living trust over a TODI. Neither has a property power of attorney, and Dev's 401(k) still lists his mother as beneficiary from his first job. They add health care forms and HIPAA releases, skip the living will, and add a digital assets clause.

Before the meeting they gather the deed, beneficiary confirmations, old wills and an account list. Their total is well under $4 million, so tax is not a focus.
