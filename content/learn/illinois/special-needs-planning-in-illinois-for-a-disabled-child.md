If you are the parent of a child with a disability in Illinois, the main question is simple: what happens to your child's care, benefits and money when you can no longer manage them? A good plan answers that with a few documents, done in the right order.

This page is a working sequence for Illinois families. It covers the trust, the ABLE account and the alternatives to guardianship, and it points to the guides that own each topic. It is general education, not legal advice. The trust rules themselves are in [special needs trust rules in Illinois](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-rules-in-illinois), and the broader subject is in our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## Why does an Illinois special needs plan look different?

Because money and decisions are separate problems, and the usual estate plan fixes neither. SSI allows a single person no more than $2,000 in countable resources, and Medicaid has similar tests. An inheritance paid directly to a child on those benefits can push them over the line. And at 18 the law treats your child as an adult, whatever their abilities, so you have no automatic right to make medical or financial decisions.

Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the rest of the state picture. Illinois adds local machinery: the circuit court in your county decides guardianship, the Illinois Trust Code governs the trustee, and state agencies run the waiver programs. The federal benefit rules stay the same everywhere. Our overview of [first-party and third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) explains why the type of trust matters before anything else.

## What order should you do things in?

Start with the item that carries the nearest deadline. For most families that is the 18th birthday, then the trust.

1. **Before age 18.** Sign HIPAA releases and school consents. Talk with a doctor about decision-making ability. See [what to do when a child with a disability turns 18](/learn/special-needs/what-to-do-when-a-child-with-a-disability-turns-18).
2. **At 18.** Decide between powers of attorney, a representative payee, and a limited guardianship. Apply for SSI as an adult if eligible.
3. **Register on PUNS.** Illinois uses the PUNS list to track needs for developmental disability services. Register and update it every year.
4. **Rewrite your will or living trust.** Replace "to my children equally" with a third-party supplemental needs trust for the child with a disability.
5. **Open an ABLE account** for small, regular spending money.
6. **Write a letter of intent.** Our [letter of intent guide](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) shows what to include.
7. **Review beneficiary forms** for IRAs, life insurance and 401(k)s so they name the trust, not the child.

## How does a supplemental needs trust protect an Illinois child?

It holds the money for your child without making it theirs. The trustee decides what to pay for, and the child cannot demand cash, so the trust is not counted as the child's resource. In a third-party trust funded with your own money, nothing is owed back to Medicaid when the child dies. The trust names who receives what remains, such as siblings.

Take Linda, 62, a widow in Peoria with two adult children. Her son Marcus has an intellectual disability and receives SSI and Medicaid. Her daughter Alicia does not. Linda's will originally split everything 50/50. Our attorneys moved Marcus's half into a third-party trust with Alicia as trustee and a bank as co-trustee. They also changed the IRA beneficiary and the life insurance, which had passed by form, to the trust. If Linda had skipped that step, the IRA would have gone to Marcus outright, and his SSI would have been at risk.

| Money source | Where it should go | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Your will or living trust share | Third-party supplemental needs trust | No Medicaid payback |
| IRA or 401(k) | Trust named as beneficiary | See [special needs trust as IRA beneficiary](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-as-ira-beneficiary) |
| Life insurance | Trust named as beneficiary | Avoids a direct payment to the child |
| Child's own settlement or back pay | First-party or pooled trust | Payback rules apply |
| Small monthly spending | ABLE account | Easy access for the child |

## Where does the Illinois ABLE account fit?

It is the checkbook, while the trust is the vault. An ABLE account lets a person whose disability began before age 46 save money that SSI generally does not count up to $100,000. Anyone can contribute, up to the annual gift tax exclusion of $19,000 in 2026. The money can pay disability-related expenses, which include housing.

The limit and the fees matter. Read [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) and [choosing and funding an ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/choosing-and-funding-an-able-account-state-plans-fees-and-529-rollovers) before opening one. Illinois Medicaid can make a claim against what remains in an ABLE account at death, so large savings belong in the trust, not the ABLE account. The trustee can move modest amounts across for daily bills.

## What are the alternatives to guardianship in Illinois?

There are several, and many families need only the first two or three. The ladder runs from the least court involvement to the most.

- **Releases and consents.** HIPAA and school forms that let providers speak with you.
- **Power of attorney for property and health care.** The child signs while able to understand them. See the Illinois [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) and [health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).
- **Representative payee.** Social Security appoints you to manage benefits without a court.
- **Joint or ABLE accounts.** Shared access to small sums.
- **Limited guardianship.** The court gives a guardian only the powers the person needs.
- **Plenary guardianship.** Broad authority, used only when nothing smaller works.

The [alternatives to guardianship decision map](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) compares these in more detail.

## When is guardianship the right choice in Illinois?

When your adult child cannot understand or sign even simple documents, and the risk of harm is real. Illinois defines a person with a disability as someone 18 or older who, because of mental deterioration or physical incapacity, is not fully able to manage their person or estate. The court must find this by clear and convincing evidence, and guardianship should be used only as necessary and only to the extent the limitations require (755 ILCS 5/11a-3). A bad decision alone is not enough.

The path: a petition, a physician's report within 3 months, notice to the person at least 14 days before the hearing, a guardian ad litem, and a hearing. The person can ask for a lawyer and a jury. Our article on [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) walks through the process and the guardian's duties. A guardian answers to the court, and the ward may ask the court to change or end the order.

## Who steps in when you cannot?

Name people now, and name backups. A trustee and a guardian do different jobs, and they do not have to be the same person. Our guide on [who should be trustee of a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/who-should-be-trustee-of-a-special-needs-trust) covers the choice, and [who cares for a person with a disability after parents die](/learn/special-needs/who-cares-for-a-person-with-a-disability-after-parents-die) covers the care side. If you are a grandparent who wants to help, read [grandparents leaving money to a grandchild with special needs](/learn/special-needs/grandparents-leaving-money-to-a-grandchild-with-special-needs) first.

## What mistakes do our attorneys see most?

Three. Parents leave an IRA to the disabled child directly while the will says "trust". Relatives hand the child cash, which can reduce SSI. And families wait to register on PUNS until there is a crisis. A one-hour review of your beneficiary forms often finds the first problem.
