A grandmother once said she was "taking care of" you. An aunt's lawyer called the family after her funeral, but not you. Or you simply heard that a relative died and wonder whether your name is on any paper at all. The question "am I a beneficiary?" has more than one answer, because a will is only one of several places your name could be.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is written from the side of the person who might inherit. It shows where your name could appear, how each place becomes visible after a death, and what to do if you suspect you were left out or that a will is being kept from the court. If you already know an estate is open and something feels wrong, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-to-find-out-if-you-are-named-in-a-will-in-illinois&topic=probate) to have the file reviewed.

## The short version

- While the person is alive, there is nothing to find.
- After the death, the court file is the place to look.
- If you are an heir or a [legatee](/glossary/legatee), you should get a mailed notice once a will is admitted.
- Trusts, beneficiary forms and recorded deeds pass outside the will and are checked separately.
- The six-month window to contest a will starts when the will is admitted, not when you hear about it.

## Can I find out if I am in someone's will while they are alive?

No, not unless they choose to tell you. A will does nothing until death. The person who signed it can revoke or replace it whenever they like, as long as they follow the methods in [changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois). Their lawyer owes confidentiality to them, not to the family, and no court has anything on file yet.

The same goes for beneficiary forms. A payable on death beneficiary has no rights in the account while the owner is alive, and the owner can change the name or empty the account. The page on [Illinois payable on death and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) explains that rule.

If a parent or grandparent is still living, the honest route is a conversation, not a search.

## Where could my name be after a death?

The will covers only what was in the person's sole name with no beneficiary, so there are several places to check.

| Where your name might be | How it becomes visible | How you check |
|---|---|---|
| A will | Filed with the circuit clerk after the death | Ask the clerk in the county where the person lived for the court file |
| The probate petition | Lists heirs and legatees with addresses | Same court file, and the mailed notice if you are listed |
| A living trust | Stays private, but the trustee owes notice to qualified beneficiaries | Ask the successor trustee in writing for the notice and a copy |
| A payable on death or joint account | Paid by the bank to the named person | The bank will tell a named beneficiary who brings a death certificate |
| Life insurance or a retirement account | Paid to the beneficiary on the company's form | Contact the insurer or plan, or use a policy locator search |
| A transfer on death instrument for a house | Recorded with the county before the death | Search the county land records for the property |
| Unclaimed property | Held by the Illinois State Treasurer | Search I-Cash under the person's name |

A beneficiary form generally controls over the will for that asset, so someone "left nothing in the will" can still receive a large check.

## How do I see the will once the person has died?

Go to the court file. Illinois requires anyone holding the original will to file it with the circuit clerk of the proper county immediately after the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). The rules for the person holding it are in [filing the original will with the Illinois circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk), and the search for a will that has not turned up is in [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois).

From your side, the steps are simple:

1. **Identify the county.** It is usually the county where the person lived at death.
2. **Contact the circuit clerk.** Ask whether a will has been filed or a probate case opened under the person's full name. Many clerks have an online case search.
3. **Read the petition, not only the will.** The petition for probate lists the heirs and legatees and their mailing addresses (755 ILCS 5/6-2). If you are on that list, you are owed notice.
4. **Note the admission date.** Write down the date of the order admitting the will. Two of the deadlines below run from it.
5. **Check again later.** A file that shows only a deposited will may show a petition a few weeks on.

If the clerk has nothing, no one may have filed yet, or there was no will. Without one, [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) shows who takes. The free [who-inherits tool](/tools/who-inherits) lets you check where you would fall.

## Who gets notice in Illinois probate?

Heirs and legatees named in the petition. Within 14 days after the court admits the will, the executor or petitioner must mail each of them a copy of the petition and the order (755 ILCS 5/6-10). An heir is someone who would inherit if there were no will, so a child left out of the will still receives notice. The page on [notice to heirs and legatees in Illinois probate](/learn/illinois/notice-to-heirs-and-legatees-in-illinois-probate) covers the packet and what happens when an address is unknown.

The notice is useful, but do not wait for it. Addresses go stale and relatives get left off lists. The contest deadline runs from admission, and the statute says that a failure to notify an heir or legatee does not extend it (755 ILCS 5/8-1). Checking the court file yourself is the safer habit.

## What about a trust?

A trust is different, because it never goes to the clerk. But beneficiaries are not kept in the dark. A trustee must notify each qualified beneficiary within 90 days after the trust becomes irrevocable, tell them they may request a copy of the trust instrument, and say whether they may receive accountings (760 ILCS 3/813.1). The page on [what an Illinois trustee must tell beneficiaries](/learn/illinois/illinois-trustee-duty-to-inform-beneficiaries) has the details.

Clues that a trust exists include a will in the court file leaving everything "to the trustee of my trust" or a relative who calls themselves the successor trustee. Write to that person and ask. The national picture is in [what trust beneficiaries can see and when](/learn/trusts/what-trust-beneficiaries-can-see-and-when).

## How do I find accounts, policies and forgotten money?

These sit outside the court file, so each holder has to be asked.

- **Bank accounts.** A payable on death beneficiary usually collects with a certified death certificate, identification and the bank's claim form.
- **Life insurance.** If you think a policy exists but cannot find it, the National Association of Insurance Commissioners' policy locator sends a request to participating insurers. See [life insurance with no living beneficiary in Illinois](/learn/illinois/life-insurance-with-no-living-beneficiary-in-illinois) for what happens when the named person has died.
- **Retirement accounts.** These follow the plan's beneficiary form. The page on [retirement account beneficiaries in Illinois](/learn/illinois/retirement-account-beneficiaries-and-spousal-consent-in-illinois) explains when a spouse must consent.
- **A house.** An Illinois [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) must be recorded before the owner dies, so it shows in the county land records.
- **Unclaimed property.** The Illinois State Treasurer's I-Cash site lists forgotten accounts and uncashed benefits, and claiming directly is free. The page on [finding unclaimed property for an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/finding-unclaimed-property-for-an-illinois-estate) walks through the search.

The free [claim an account as beneficiary checklist](/free/claim-an-account-as-beneficiary-checklist) tracks each claim, and the general steps are in [how to claim an account as a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-to-claim-an-account-as-a-beneficiary).

## Example: Dana checks what her aunt left

This is an invented example. Dana, 41, lives in Springfield. Her aunt Lorraine, a widow with no children, dies in Rockford. Lorraine's only brother, Dana's father, died years earlier. A cousin says everything went to Lorraine's church.

Dana asks the Winnebago County circuit clerk for any case under Lorraine's name. The file shows a will admitted on May 2 and a petition listing Dana and her brother Mark as heirs, since they would take their father's share if there were no will. The petition gives Dana's address as an apartment she left three years ago, which is why the notice never reached her. She was owed notice, and the six-month contest window still closes on November 2.

She also searches I-Cash under Lorraine's name and finds an old utility deposit, which she reports to the executor. In June, well inside the window, an attorney tells her the will looks properly signed and a gift to a church is not a ground to challenge it. She decides not to contest, and she sends the executor her current address for the rest of the case.

## What if I think I was left out or a will is being hidden?

Act on the court file, not family rumor.

1. **Get the court file.** Confirm whether a will was filed, whether it was admitted and the date of the order.
2. **Calendar two dates.** An interested person may ask for [formal proof of the will](/glossary/formal-proof-of-will) within 42 days after admission (755 ILCS 5/6-21). A will contest must be filed within six months after admission (755 ILCS 5/8-1). The rules are in [the six-month will contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline).
3. **If you believe someone has the will and is not filing it,** know that the court can order a person to produce a will, and willfully hiding one for 30 days after learning of the death is a felony under 755 ILCS 5/6-1.
4. **If only a copy is circulating,** read [what to do when the original will is lost in Illinois](/learn/illinois/lost-original-will-in-illinois).
5. **If a trust is involved,** the contest limit for a trust that was revocable at death is the earlier of two years after the death or six months after the trustee sends the required copy and notice (760 ILCS 3/604). See [contesting a trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/contesting-a-trust-in-illinois-who-can-sue-grounds-and-deadlines).
6. **If the estate is open but stalled,** see [what heirs can do about a slow Illinois executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor).
7. **Talk to an attorney early.** Being left out is not, by itself, a reason a court will set a will aside; see [can you disinherit a child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-illinois). An attorney can tell you whether you have a real ground.
