A brokerage account is often the biggest asset a person owns outside a house. If it has no beneficiary, it goes through probate, even if the account was opened long ago for the children's benefit. Illinois offers a simple fix: register the securities in "transfer on death" form.

This page explains how that works for Illinois investors. For bank accounts, see our article on [payable-on-death and joint accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts). For the national picture, see [POD and TOD accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts). The wider set is in our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois). This is general education, not legal advice.

## What does TOD registration do?

It tells the firm that holds your securities to re-register them in your beneficiary's name when you die. Illinois adopted the Uniform TOD Security Registration Act (815 ILCS 10). Under that Act, a registration in beneficiary form is not a will, so the securities pass without probate. The firm is not required to offer it, and each firm sets its own forms and options.

During your life the beneficiary has no rights. You can buy, sell, borrow against the account or change the beneficiary. The account is also not a gift, so you do not file a gift tax return for naming someone.

## Which assets can have a TOD registration?

| Asset | TOD available? | Where you set it up |
|---|---|---|
| Brokerage account (stocks, bonds, ETFs) | Usually | Broker's account form |
| Mutual fund held with the fund company | Often | Fund company's TOD form |
| Shares held directly with a transfer agent | Sometimes | Transfer agent's form |
| Bank checking or savings | Not TOD; POD instead | Bank form |
| IRA or 401(k) | Uses a beneficiary designation | Custodian or plan form |
| Real estate | Not under this Act | Separate [TODI](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) |

## How do you set up a TOD account step by step?

1. Ask the firm whether it offers TOD registration on your account type.
2. Request the TOD or beneficiary form, or find it in the firm's online account settings.
3. Name each beneficiary by full legal name, with date of birth or tax ID if the form asks.
4. State the shares as percentages that add to 100, if there is more than one.
5. Ask whether the firm allows contingent beneficiaries and a per stirpes option. If the beneficiary dies first, this is what keeps the account out of probate.
6. Sign and return the form, and get written confirmation.
7. Check the account statement or online profile to see that the TOD beneficiary shows.
8. Repeat the process for each account, since a new account does not copy the old one's beneficiary.

## What happens at death?

The beneficiary contacts the firm's estate or deceased-client unit. The firm normally asks for a certified death certificate, a claim form and ID. Securities are re-registered in the beneficiary's name or moved to a new account, and the beneficiary may sell. If there are several beneficiaries, each one takes a share. A beneficiary who is not alive when the owner dies is treated according to the registration and the firm's rules, which is why a backup matters. Our article on [claiming an account as a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-to-claim-an-account-as-a-beneficiary) walks through the process.

## Does the beneficiary pay income tax on the stock?

Generally the securities receive a step-up in basis to date-of-death value, so gains that built up during the owner's life are not taxed when the beneficiary sells right away. Dividends and gains after death are the beneficiary's. Our article on [inherited stocks and brokerage accounts](/learn/after-a-death/inherited-stocks-and-brokerage-accounts-selling-retitling-and-tax) covers the tax steps, and [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) is defined in our glossary.

## A worked example

Henry, 77, has a brokerage account worth $210,000 that he bought for $90,000 over thirty years. He also has a $40,000 savings account and a house. His will leaves everything equally to his daughters Joan and Priya. Years ago he registered the brokerage account TOD to Joan only, because she helped him set it up.

At his death Joan receives the $210,000 account directly. The will cannot override it. Priya and Joan split the savings and house, but Priya's total is far less. Joan's basis is $210,000, so a sale at that price creates no gain. The family realizes the plan was lopsided only afterward. If Henry had wanted equal shares, he could have named both daughters, 50 percent each. The simplest fix for an old account is to read the current registration. ## When is TOD not the right tool?

TOD is poor for minors, for adults who receive means-tested benefits, and for owners who want rules about how and when the money is spent. A beneficiary who is a minor needs a custodian or trust. See [UTMA accounts and gifts to minors in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors). A living trust may also be the better fit if you have many accounts. See Illinois living trusts.

## Can creditors reach a TOD account?

Passing outside probate does not make the account immune. If the probate estate cannot pay valid claims, funeral costs or taxes, Illinois law can reach assets that passed to beneficiaries. See how creditor claims work. A TOD account is also still an asset for Medicaid eligibility during life.

## What are common problems with TOD accounts?

A few patterns come up often.

- **Old accounts with no form.** An account opened decades ago may have never had a beneficiary added.
- **Rollovers and transfers.** Moving an account to a new firm often resets the registration, so the TOD form must be filed again.
- **Split accounts.** A joint account with a spouse generally passes to the survivor first. TOD matters after the second death, so ask the firm how it handles that.
- **Beneficiary named as a minor.** The firm may require a court-appointed representative or a custodian before it releases the securities.
- **Stale names.** A beneficiary who has married, divorced or changed name may need the firm to match the current legal name.
- **Unequal results.** A TOD to one child can undo an equal-shares will. See [leaving unequal shares with beneficiary forms](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-unequal-shares-with-beneficiary-forms).

Take a snapshot of each registration page once a year and keep it with your estate papers. The page costs nothing and can save your family a probate case.
