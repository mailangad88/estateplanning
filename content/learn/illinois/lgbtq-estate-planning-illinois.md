If you are an LGBTQ couple or family in Illinois, the short version is this: marriage and civil unions give you strong default rights, and a good plan fills the gaps they leave. Those gaps are usually about people, not paperwork. A parent whose name is on no court order. A partner whose relatives never accepted the relationship. A second marriage made in your seventies.

This page covers three problems in order: protecting a non-biological parent's tie to a child, keeping hostile relatives from overriding your wishes, and planning when you married late. Each section points to the page in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) that explains the rule in full. It is general education, not legal advice, and parentage in particular is a question to take to an attorney. If you would rather talk it through, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-lgbtq-estate-planning-illinois&topic=will) at any point.

## What does LGBTQ estate planning in Illinois cover?

It covers the same documents everyone needs, set up around the people you actually rely on. Marriage is recognized nationwide since the 2015 Obergefell decision, and Illinois treats a civil union partner as a spouse for state-law purposes (750 ILCS 75/20). Our general page on [estate planning for same-sex couples and LGBTQ families](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-same-sex-couples-and-lgbtq-families) covers the national picture, and [civil union partners and Illinois estate law](/learn/illinois/civil-union-partners-and-illinois-estate-law) covers that status.

Here is where the Illinois defaults help and where they stop.

| Your situation | What Illinois law gives you by default | What you add |
|---|---|---|
| Married spouse or civil union partner | Intestacy share, ranks near the top for medical decisions, can renounce a will | Will or trust, powers of attorney, beneficiary check |
| Unmarried partner | Nothing at death; "close friend" in a hospital | Everything, in signed documents |
| Non-biological parent | Depends on how the child was born and what court papers exist | Parentage judgment or adoption, then a will |
| Chosen family | No rights | Documents naming them for each role |

## How do you protect a non-biological parent's relationship with the child?

Get the strongest legal tie you can while everyone is healthy. A court judgment of parentage or an adoption gives a non-biological parent the firmest footing, because it is a court order that follows the family to other states and into schools, hospitals and insurance files. A name on a birth certificate helps. It is not the same as an order, and it is the weaker proof when a dispute arises.

Which route applies depends on how your child came to your family: birth within the marriage, a donor, a surrogate, an earlier relationship, or an adoption. Parentage law is its own field, and the right step differs for each. A family law attorney can tell you whether a judgment, a second-parent adoption or a stepparent adoption fits.

Why does an estate plan care? Because rights run through the legal parent. Suppose Elena and Priya are married, and their daughter Noor was born to Elena. Priya raised Noor from day one but has never been adjudicated a parent. If Elena dies, Priya's claim to keep Noor rests on how a court reads her status, and Elena's brother might file his own petition. If Priya is a legal parent, she is the surviving parent and the question mostly disappears. Noor also inherits from a legal parent, which she may not do from a person with no legal tie.

A will fills part of the gap. A legal parent can name a guardian in a will with two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/11-5), and our page on [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children) shows how. Treat that nomination as a backstop. It tells a judge what you wanted. It does not make someone a parent.

For a gap while you are alive, such as surgery or a long trip, see [Illinois standby and short-term guardians](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children). Our page on [guardianship, custody and adoption](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-vs-custody-vs-adoption) explains how the three legal tracks differ. Keep any court order, adoption decree and donor or surrogate agreement with the will.

## How do you keep hostile relatives from overriding your wishes?

Name your own people on signed forms, because Illinois defaults run through family. There are four places where relatives usually step in, and each has its own fix.

| Moment | Who decides if you sign nothing | What overrides the default |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital, you cannot speak | Guardian, spouse, adult children, parents, siblings, grandchildren, then close friend | Health care power of attorney naming your agent |
| Funeral and burial | Spouse, adult children, parents, nearest kin | Notarized agent form or written directions |
| Money and bills | Nobody, until a court appoints a guardian | Power of attorney for property |
| Inheritance | Intestacy list of blood relatives and spouse | Will or trust, beneficiary forms |

### What happens at the hospital?

If you cannot decide for yourself and named no agent, the Health Care Surrogate Act ranks who speaks for you: guardian, spouse, adult son or daughter, parent, sibling, grandchild, and only then a close friend (755 ILCS 40/25). A partner who is not a legal spouse or civil union partner is that close friend. The detail is in [who decides under the Illinois Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).

The fix is a health care power of attorney. Illinois's statutory form needs one adult witness and no notary, and the witness cannot be your agent or a provider caring for you (755 ILCS 45/4-10). Pair it with a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so your agent can get records, and give copies to your doctor and the hospital where you receive care. See the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) page for who can serve as agent.

### Who controls your funeral?

Under the Disposition of Remains Act, a person named in a qualifying written instrument comes first, an executor following written instructions in a will comes next, and then the spouse, adult children, parents and next of kin (755 ILCS 65/5). A partner or friend is on the list only if you put them there. The named-agent form is signed by you and the agent, with your signature notarized. A homemade version that the agent never signs does not work. Details are in the [Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act) page.

This is the painful one, because it happens within hours of a death and cremation cannot be undone. Put the form in the hands of the person named, not in a drawer.

### Can relatives contest your will?

They can try, and the ones who can are the people who would inherit if the will failed. Under Illinois law an interested person has six months after the will is admitted to probate to file a contest (755 ILCS 5/8-1). The usual grounds are lack of capacity, [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) and defective signing. Our page on the [six-month will contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) explains the process.

Disliking your partner is not a ground, but even a failed contest costs time and money. These habits make a successful challenge harder:

1. Sign with the formalities in [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements), and choose witnesses who are not beneficiaries.
2. Put the home and main accounts in a [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts), so less depends on a probate hearing. Trusts can be challenged too, and the rules are in [contesting a trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/contesting-a-trust-in-illinois-who-can-sue-grounds-and-deadlines).
3. Write a short letter explaining your choices, in your own voice, while your thinking is clear.
4. Meet the attorney alone, so nobody can later say your partner steered the plan.
5. Keep the plan current, since a recent plan is harder to call a product of confusion.

No document can promise to stop a lawsuit. A well-built one makes it harder to win.

## What if you married late in life?

A late marriage changes who has rights over your estate the day you sign the certificate. Illinois treats the spouse like any other spouse, so the older plan may no longer say what you meant. Walt, 74, and Frank, 71, married last spring after twenty years together. As an example: Walt's will, signed years ago, leaves everything to his sister. Walt owns a $380,000 house and savings in his own name. If Walt dies with that will, Frank can renounce it and claim one-half, because Walt has no descendants (755 ILCS 5/2-8). If Walt dies with no will at all, Frank takes the whole probate estate, because there are no descendants (755 ILCS 5/2-1). Neither result is what Walt chose. A current plan lets him choose.

Four things to check after a late marriage:

- **Renunciation.** A spouse can renounce a will and take one-third of the estate if the decedent left descendants, or one-half if not, filed within 7 months after the will is admitted. There is also a spouse's award of at least $20,000 (755 ILCS 5/15-1). Our page on [a surviving spouse's rights](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) has the details.
- **Prenuptial or postnuptial agreements.** A signed agreement can waive some of those rights. If children from earlier relationships are the heirs, read [Illinois prenup and inheritance rights](/learn/illinois/illinois-prenup-and-inheritance-rights).
- **Beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts, life insurance and pensions go to the person on the form, not the will. An old form naming a former partner or a sibling still controls. See [Illinois pension survivor benefits](/learn/illinois/illinois-pension-survivor-benefits-imrf-trs-surs-sers).
- **Estate tax.** Illinois taxes estates above $4 million, and a surviving spouse cannot add the first spouse's unused amount to their own. See [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).

If adult children or nieces expect to inherit, consider a trust that gives the surviving spouse the home for life and the rest to your chosen heirs. [Blended family inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/blended-family-inheritance) goes deeper on this.

## What should you sign first?

Start with the documents that protect you while you are alive, then the ones for death. For an unmarried couple, see also [unmarried partner inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/unmarried-partners).

1. Health care power of attorney and HIPAA authorization.
2. Power of attorney for property, named to someone you trust to follow your wishes. See [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
3. Disposition of remains agent form, notarized.
4. Will, with a guardian nomination if you have minor children, or revocable trust with a pour-over will.
5. Beneficiary and deed review, so the forms and the title match the plan.
6. Parentage judgment or adoption paperwork, with an attorney, if a child has only one legal parent.
