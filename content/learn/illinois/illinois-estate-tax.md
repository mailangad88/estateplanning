Most families hear that the federal estate tax only affects the very wealthy. In 2026 the federal exclusion is $15 million per person, so that is mostly true. Illinois is different. The state has its own estate tax that starts at $4 million, and a family with a paid-off house, retirement accounts and life insurance can cross that line.

This page explains how the Illinois estate tax works in plain terms. It covers the $4 million exclusion, why spouses cannot share it, who files the return, and what planning can do. The rules come from the Illinois Estate and Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax Act (35 ILCS 405) and from the Illinois Attorney General, who runs the tax. For the larger picture, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Does Illinois have an estate tax?

Yes. Illinois imposes its own estate tax on the estates of people who die as Illinois residents, and on Illinois property of some nonresidents. The tax is separate from the federal estate tax, and an estate can owe Illinois tax while owing nothing to the IRS. The Illinois Attorney General collects the return, and payment goes to the Illinois State Treasurer.

Illinois has no inheritance tax. The estate pays, not the heirs. For how other states handle this, read our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## How does the $4 million exclusion work?

The Illinois exclusion amount is $4,000,000 for people who die on or after January 1, 2013 (35 ILCS 405/2). The Attorney General describes it as "a taxable threshold and not a credit against tax."

That wording matters. A credit would take a fixed amount off the bill. A threshold works like a gate. If the estate is at or below $4 million, there is no Illinois tax and no return. If it is above $4 million, a return is required and tax is figured under a special formula.

The value that is tested is the gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts. The gross estate includes more than the probate estate. It generally includes life insurance you own, retirement accounts, jointly owned property and the value of a revocable trust. A home, a 401(k) and a life insurance policy can add up fast.

### Why can the tax jump just above $4 million?

Illinois uses what the Attorney General calls an "interrelated calculation." In plain terms, the tax and the amount being taxed depend on each other, so the answer cannot be read off a simple rate table. The Attorney General offers an online calculator for the math.

The Attorney General's own example shows the effect. For an estate of $5,000,000, all in Illinois, the Illinois estate tax is $285,714 and the federal tax is $0. That is about 28.6 cents for each dollar above $4 million. Tax at that level on the first dollars over the line is why even a modest overage deserves a close look. These figures are the Attorney General's example, so use the calculator for a real estate.

## Is the exclusion portable between spouses?

No. Illinois does not allow portability. The Attorney General's fact sheet says "the portability and carry-over of the unused federal exemption to the surviving spouse is inapplicable to the computation and assessment of the Illinois Estate Tax."

Federal law works the other way. A surviving spouse can add the late spouse's unused federal exclusion if an election is made on a federal return. You can read about it in our article on [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples). Illinois ignores that election.

Here is an example. Maria and Tom are married and live in Illinois. Each of them owns $3 million, for a combined $6 million. Tom dies first and leaves everything to Maria outright. No tax is due at Tom's death. Maria now owns $6 million, and she has only her own $4 million exclusion. Tom's $4 million was never used, and Illinois will not let her claim any of it. Her estate is over the threshold and must file.

### How do couples use both exclusions?

A common plan is for the first spouse to leave an amount up to the exclusion to a trust for the family, sometimes called a credit shelter or bypass trust, instead of leaving everything outright to the spouse. The survivor can still benefit from the trust. That amount is not counted in the survivor's estate later, so the first spouse's exclusion is used.

Illinois also allows a separate state-only election for a [QTIP trust](/glossary/qtip-trust). The statute says an Illinois election can be made apart from the federal one (35 ILCS 405/2). That gives planners room to build a trust that avoids federal waste and Illinois tax. Our attorneys can explain how a [marital trust](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) fits a particular family.

Because the federal exclusion is $15 million, a trust that holds the full federal amount could be taxed in Illinois at the first death. The amount placed in the trust has to be chosen with the state limit in mind.

## Who has to file, and when?

The Attorney General says any estate with a gross value over $4,000,000 must file Illinois Form 700, "whether or not a Federal Return is required." This applies to both residents and nonresidents. The person who is responsible for the federal return also files the Illinois one (35 ILCS 405/6).

Here are the main steps in the process.

1. Add up all assets, including non-probate assets and lifetime taxable gifts, at date-of-death values.
2. If the total is over $4 million, prepare Form 700. If the estate has assets outside Illinois, add the Form 700-Addendum.
3. Attach a federal Form 706 or the same information in another format if no federal return is required.
4. Send the return to the Attorney General. Cook, DuPage, Lake and McHenry County estates go to the Chicago office. All other counties go to the Springfield office.
5. Pay the tax to the Illinois State Treasurer. The tax is due nine months after death.
6. If more time is needed, request an extension within the nine months.

The nine-month date is the same as the federal due date. The Attorney General says extensions are available on request or by recognizing a federal extension. Late payment can lead to interest, so confirm the current rate and rules on the Attorney General's site.

## What about property in other states?

The Illinois tax is figured as if everything were in Illinois. It is then multiplied by the share of the estate that is located in Illinois. The Attorney General's second example is a $5,000,000 estate with half in Illinois and half in Florida. The Illinois tax is $142,857, even though Florida has no estate tax.

The same method applies to nonresidents. A person who lives elsewhere but owns Illinois real estate files based on the percentage of the estate that is in Illinois.

## How can a family lower or avoid Illinois estate tax?

Some common tools exist, and the right one depends on the family. None of them works in every case, and each has a cost.

- **Use both spouses' exclusions.** This is the most common step for married couples, as shown above.
- **Marital deduction.** Property that passes to a surviving spouse in a qualifying way can generally be deducted. It delays the tax rather than removing it, because the property is in the survivor's estate later.
- **Charitable gifts.** Gifts to charity are deductible.
- **Lifetime gifts.** Gifts can move value out of the estate, but taxable gifts are added back in the Illinois test.
- **Life insurance in a trust.** An irrevocable trust can keep policy proceeds out of the taxable estate if set up correctly.

A living trust alone does not reduce the tax. See our article on [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) for why. ## Will the $4 million figure change?

The statute sets the exclusion at $4,000,000 for deaths on or after January 1, 2013. Bills to raise it have been filed in recent sessions of the Illinois General Assembly, but we have not seen one become law. The rules that apply are the ones in force on the date of death, so confirm the current figure with the Attorney General or an attorney before relying on this page.
