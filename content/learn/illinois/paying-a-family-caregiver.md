You moved your father into your spare room last year. You cook, drive him to appointments, manage his pills and get up at night. He wants to pay you. Your brother in Naperville has said, more than once, that he hopes you are not "taking money from Dad."

Both worries are fair. This page covers how a parent in Illinois can pay a family caregiver without turning the payments into a Medicaid problem, and how to keep the other siblings from feeling cheated. The general rules are on our broader page about [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver). Here we focus on how it plays out in Illinois. If your parent has savings and may need long-term care, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-paying-a-family-caregiver&topic=parent) with an Illinois estate planning attorney before any money moves.

## Why does Medicaid care who gets paid?

Because Medicaid cannot tell the difference between pay and a gift unless the paperwork shows it. When someone applies for Medicaid nursing home care in Illinois, the state reviews transfers made in the 60 months before the application. A transfer for less than fair market value can lead to a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay for care. The penalty clock generally starts only once the person is in a nursing home and otherwise eligible. Our page on [qualifying for Medicaid nursing home care in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) explains the details.

A parent who pays a child $1,000 a month with nothing in writing leaves an investigator with two stories. One is wages. The other is a gift to a child, with a convenient label attached. The first story needs evidence, and you need to create it as you go, not afterwards.

## What makes a care payment look like pay?

Four things, and all four matter.

1. **A written agreement made before payment starts.** It names the parent and the caregiver, lists the services, states the rate and the schedule, and is signed and dated. Our [personal care agreement guide](/free/personal-care-agreement-guide) has an outline an attorney can adapt.
2. **A reasonable rate.** The rate should look like what a local home care agency or aide would charge for the same tasks. A rate far above that reads as a disguised gift.
3. **A time log.** Dates, hours and tasks, kept as you go. A simple notebook works. Our [caregiver daily log template](/free/caregiver-daily-log-template) shows the idea.
4. **Payments from the parent's own account, in the same way each time.** Check or transfer, never cash, with the memo naming the pay period.

Add a fifth if the parent is already close to needing Medicaid: have an attorney read the agreement first. Illinois practice on how caseworkers treat these agreements is something to verify rather than assume.

## Which arrangements cause trouble?

| Arrangement | Why it is risky |
|---|---|
| Paying for care already given, with nothing signed beforehand | Looks like a reward or a gift, not a wage |
| Paying years of care in a lump sum up front | The unused part can be called a transfer |
| A rate well above agency rates | The excess looks like a gift |
| Cash payments with no record | Nothing to show an investigator |
| Care that is only "being there" | Companionship is hard to price; list concrete tasks |
| Agent pays himself under a power of attorney | The agent is on both sides of the contract |

## Example: Priya, her father and her brother

Priya's father, Anand, 81, has about enough savings to pay for a few years of care and is starting to need help bathing and dressing. Priya will give about 20 hours a week. The local agency in their area charges a certain hourly rate for an aide. Priya and Anand, with their attorney, set her rate at or slightly below that agency rate and write the agreement for the next 12 months. Anand pays her every two weeks by check from his own account. Priya keeps a log.

Here is the arithmetic, using invented figures for illustration only. At 20 hours a week and an example rate of $22 an hour, Priya would earn $440 a week, or $1,760 over four weeks. Her brother, Vikram, receives a copy of the agreement and the log summary each quarter. He may not like the arrangement, but he cannot say he was kept in the dark.

The calculator idea is straightforward: hours per week, times a rate, times the weeks. What matters is that the rate is defensible and that the actual hours match what the log shows. If the hours rise, update the agreement in writing before the pay rises.

## What about taxes and payroll?

Pay to a family caregiver is income to the caregiver, and a parent who pays a child to work in the home may be a household employer. Whether payroll taxes apply depends on how much is paid and on the relationship, and the rules are federal and technical. Our page on [payroll and taxes for a paid family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/payroll-and-taxes-when-a-family-member-is-your-paid-caregiver) walks through it, and the [pay and taxes guide](/free/family-caregiver-pay-and-taxes-guide) gives a checklist to bring to a tax professional. Payroll records also help with Medicaid, because they show the money was a wage.

## What about the house and other Medicaid rules?

Paying a caregiver is separate from what happens to the house. Illinois lists certain transfers of a home, including to a caregiving child, as allowable in listed cases, but the conditions are technical. Do not deed the house to a child "for taking care of me" without advice. The same applies to promises of the house in exchange for care. After the parent dies, the state may also make a claim against the estate, a topic we cover on [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery).

If your parent does not have the money to pay you, Illinois has programs that may pay for in-home help. See our page on the [Illinois Community Care Program](/learn/illinois/illinois-community-care-program-in-home-care-for-older-adults).

## How do you keep the siblings from resenting it?

Resentment usually comes from secrecy, not from the dollar amount. A few habits help.

- Tell your siblings before the first payment, and show them the agreement.
- Send a short quarterly summary of hours and amounts. It takes ten minutes.
- Invite the others to take specific tasks, such as Sunday visits or paying bills, so the work is visibly shared.
- Do not treat the pay as an advance on the inheritance unless the will says so. Whether it should be counted at all is a decision for the parent, put in writing.
- If a sibling is also giving substantial care, use the [sibling caregiving agreement worksheet](/free/sibling-caregiving-agreement-worksheet) to put both on the same footing.

The harder case is a parent whose capacity is slipping. If the parent signs an agreement while confused, a sibling can argue it was the result of undue influence. A doctor's note close to the signing date helps. Our page on [what to do when a parent has dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney) covers the situation when the window has already closed.

## A short checklist before the first payment

- Parent can sign with capacity, with a doctor's note if in doubt.
- Written agreement with services, rate, hours and start date.
- Rate checked against local agency pricing.
- Separate log, started on day one.
- Payment from the parent's own account by check or transfer.
- Siblings told and given a copy.
- Tax and payroll question answered.
- An attorney has read it if Medicaid may be needed within five years.
