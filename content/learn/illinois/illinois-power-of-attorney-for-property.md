If you get sick or hurt, someone has to pay your bills, manage your accounts, and sign papers for you. Without a legal document, that person may have to ask a court for guardianship. In Illinois, the usual tool is the statutory short form power of attorney for property.

This article explains what the form is, how to sign it correctly, what your agent can and cannot do, and when it ends. It is general education, not legal advice. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the other documents that go with it.

## What is an Illinois power of attorney for property?

It is a signed document that lets you (the principal) name an agent to handle your property and money if you cannot, or simply do not want to. The Illinois Power of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45) sets out a statutory short form. Using the form gives banks, brokers, and title companies a familiar document to rely on.

The form is "durable" when it says it survives your incapacity. Our general article on the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) explains the idea in every state. In Illinois, the form is also separate from the health care form, which has different rules (see below).

## How do you sign it so it is valid?

The statute says every property power must bear the signature of a witness to the signing and must be notarized (755 ILCS 45/3-3). Follow these steps.

1. Choose your agent and at least one successor agent.
2. Read the form's notice to the agent, which lists the agent's duties.
3. Initial the powers you want to give and cross out any you do not.
4. Add any limits, such as a gift power or a future start date.
5. Sign the form in front of one witness and a notary public.
6. Have the witness sign. Make sure the witness is not on the excluded list.
7. Keep the original safe and give copies to your agent and your bank.

### Who cannot be your witness?

According to the statute, the witness cannot be your agent or a successor agent. The list also includes your attending physician or mental health provider, the owner or operator of a health care facility where you live, and certain relatives. These include a parent, sibling, or descendant of you or any agent, or the spouse of one of them. Pick someone with no stake in the document, such as a coworker or neighbor.

## What can your agent do under the form?

The statutory form lists powers in categories. You initial the ones you grant. As of 2026, the form covers these categories.

| Category | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Real estate | Buying, selling, leasing, and managing property |
| Financial institutions | Banking and accounts |
| Stocks and bonds | Brokerage accounts |
| Tangible personal property | Cars, furniture, and similar items |
| Safe deposit box | Access to the box |
| Insurance and annuities | Policies and claims |
| Retirement plans | Accounts and distributions |
| Government benefits | Social Security, employment, and military benefits |
| Tax matters | Returns and audits |
| Claims and litigation | Lawsuits and settlements |
| Commodity and option transactions | Futures and options |
| Business operations | Running a business |
| Borrowing | Loans and credit |
| Estate transactions | Dealings with an estate where you are a beneficiary |
| All other property | Anything not listed |

You can grant all of them or only a few. A narrow power can fit a short-term need, such as selling one house while you are abroad.

## Can the agent make gifts or change my beneficiaries?

No, not unless the document says so. The statute provides that the agent has no power under the listed categories to make gifts, to appoint property to others, or to change a beneficiary, unless specific authority is given (755 ILCS 45/3-4). The same section says an agent cannot make or change your will, and cannot revoke or amend a trust unless the form gives that power and refers to the trust.

This protects you, but it can also block useful planning. Suppose Priya, 79, wants her agent to move savings into a trust or make annual gifts to her grandchildren for tax reasons. She would need to write that authority into the form with clear limits.

## What duties does the agent have?

The form's notice tells the agent to act in good faith, for the principal's best interest, using due care, competence, and diligence. It also tells the agent to keep a complete record of receipts, disbursements, and significant actions. The notice says the agent must not create a conflict of interest, commingle the principal's money with the agent's own, or borrow from the principal unless the form allows it.

An agent who breaks these duties can be made to restore the property. Courts can order an agent to give an accounting. Adult protective services and certain others can also ask for the agent's records. This is one reason to choose someone organized and honest, as our article on [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) explains.

## When does the power start, and when does it end?

You can make the power start now or at a future date or event. Many people choose "effective now" so the agent can act without proof of incapacity. The [springing versus immediate](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) comparison covers the tradeoffs.

The power ends when you die, when you revoke it, when a stated end date arrives, or when no agent is left to serve. Illinois law lets you revoke at any time while you have capacity, in any manner communicated to the agent or to others involved (755 ILCS 45/2-4). Revoke in writing, and tell your bank and the agent.

If you lose capacity and have no valid power, your family may need to ask the court for a guardian. Our article on [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) explains that process. A property power does not give anyone authority over medical decisions. For that you need the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## What if the bank will not accept it?

Some banks ask for their own forms or question older documents. Our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) lists next steps. Using the statutory form and signing it with a witness and notary removes many objections. Sending your bank a copy early can also help.
