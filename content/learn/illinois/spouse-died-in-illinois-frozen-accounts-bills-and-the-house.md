Your spouse died, you went to the bank, and the teller said the account was frozen. Now the bills keep arriving and you are not sure whether the house is safe. This page covers the first few weeks in Illinois, in the order the worries usually come.

If you want to talk it through with someone, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-spouse-died-in-illinois-frozen-accounts-bills-and-the-house&topic=probate) whenever you are ready. Nothing here has to happen this week. A free printable list is also available: the [surviving spouse checklist](/free/surviving-spouse-checklist).

## What does it mean when an account is frozen?

A frozen account is one the bank has restricted after learning of the death. It applies to accounts titled in your spouse's name alone. Nobody can write checks on it until a court names an executor or administrator, who then shows the bank letters of office and opens an estate account. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) lists every step. Our page on [opening an estate bank account in Illinois](/learn/illinois/opening-an-estate-bank-account-in-illinois) walks through that step.

Three kinds of money behave differently:

| Type of account | What usually happens | Court needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Joint account with you | Illinois law lets a bank pay any holder, even after another dies (765 ILCS 1005/2). You keep access. | No |
| Payable-on-death (POD) account naming you | Passes to you with a death certificate and the bank's form (205 ILCS 625/4). | No |
| Account in your spouse's name only | Frozen until an executor or administrator has letters of office, or until the affidavit route is available. | Often |

Sort your accounts into those three piles before you panic. Many widows and widowers discover that their day-to-day money was joint all along. The page on [payable-on-death and joint bank accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) explains the rules and the exceptions.

## What do you do this week?

Keep it short. Four things matter in week one:

1. **Order certified death certificates.** The funeral director files the record. Plan on about eight to ten certified copies, one for each bank, insurer and agency. See [Illinois death certificates](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph).
2. **Find the will.** Look at home, ask the attorney who drafted it, and check any safe deposit box. A will must be filed with the circuit court clerk after a death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). Our page on [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) covers the details.
3. **Leave the sole-name accounts alone.** Do not try to move the money, and do not write checks from a frozen account. Banks can reverse them.
4. **Keep a notebook.** Write down every call, who you spoke to, and what they said.

Call Social Security if the funeral home has not, and ask about survivor benefits. Those do not start by themselves; you apply. The [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) page and the [Social Security and pension notification kit](/free/social-security-and-pension-notification-kit) list who to call.

## Who pays the bills?

The estate pays them, not you. A creditor files a claim, and the executor pays valid claims in the order the Probate Act sets: funeral and administration costs first, then the spouse's award, and later the general unsecured debts such as credit cards (755 ILCS 5/18-10). If the estate runs short, most unsecured debt simply goes unpaid. The page on [credit card debt after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois) explains the order, and [how creditor claims work](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) covers the calendar.

There are exceptions to know about:

- A card or loan you co-signed, or an account with both names, is yours too.
- A mortgage or car loan is secured by the property. The lender can take the collateral if payments stop, so keep paying while you decide what to do.
- Do not "acknowledge" an old debt on the phone or promise to pay. Ask the collector to put the claim in writing and send it to the executor.

Margaret's husband Dale died with $14,000 in card balances in his name only and a joint checking account. The cards were estate claims. Margaret kept the joint account, paid the funeral from it, and left the cards for the estate to sort out. She was not asked to pay them.

## Can anyone take the house?

Not because of the death alone. What happens to the house depends on how title was held, and that is a deed question, not a bank question.

- **Joint tenancy or tenancy by the entirety.** The house usually passes to you automatically. The page on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains how to tell which you have.
- **Sole name of your spouse.** The house is a probate asset. It does not move to you until the will is admitted or an administrator is appointed and the title is dealt with. Read about [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois).
- **Land trust or living trust.** The trust document controls. Find it first.

Illinois also has a homestead exemption of $15,000 that continues after death for a surviving spouse who stays in the home (735 ILCS 5/12-902). It is a shield against creditors, not a right to keep the house, and it does not stop a mortgage lender. See [the homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death).

Keep paying the mortgage, taxes and insurance. Do not sign a deed, a quitclaim or a sale contract in the first months. Your property tax breaks may also need attention; [exemptions after a spouse dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies) covers that.

## Do I need probate, and what is the spouse's award?

You need probate only if there are assets in your spouse's name alone that cannot pass another way. When the personal property is $150,000 or less, not counting registered vehicles, an [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) can skip the court case, though it cannot transfer real estate. Otherwise the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) applies.

If probate opens, you may claim the spouse's award, a support payment for the nine months after death. It cannot be less than $20,000, plus at least $10,000 for each minor child of the decedent who lived with you (755 ILCS 5/15-1). Confirm the current figures when you file, because the legislature can change them. The award sits high in the payment order, ahead of credit card companies. The full rules, including renouncing a will, are on the page about [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death). An award is not automatic; someone has to ask the court for it.

## What can wait for a few months?

| Do now | Can wait |
|---|---|
| Death certificates, Social Security call, life insurance claims | Selling the house or downsizing |
| Keep the mortgage and utilities paid | Paying off the mortgage with savings |
| Find the will and the deed | Changing your own will and beneficiaries |
| Sort accounts into joint, POD and sole name | Distributing belongings and gifts |

Your own plan does need updating, though not this week. If your spouse was your executor, your agent or your beneficiary, those documents now point at nobody.
