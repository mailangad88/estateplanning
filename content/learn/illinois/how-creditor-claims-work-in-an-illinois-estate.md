A bill addressed to your late father shows up, then a letter from a law office, then a form that says "proof of claim." If you are the executor, you want to know what you are supposed to do with each of them. If you are the person owed money, you want to know how to get on the list before a deadline closes the door.

This page follows a single claim from the day it is sent to the day it is allowed, disputed or barred under the Illinois Probate Act. It covers the mechanics. For what a card issuer or collector can and cannot ask of the family, see our page on [credit card debt after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois), which owns the questions about relatives and personal liability. This is general education, not legal advice.

## The short version

- The executor starts the clock by publishing a notice and mailing it to known creditors. The notice sets the bar date.
- A creditor files a written claim. The executor then pays it, consents to it, disputes it, or lets the court decide.
- Late claims are barred, but a claim sent to the executor and then disallowed has its own extra deadline.
- Distributing the estate before the bar date, without court approval, is the mistake that causes the most trouble.

## How does the claims period start?

It starts when the executor publishes a notice once a week for three weeks in a newspaper in the county where the estate is open, then files proof of publication with the clerk. At the same time the executor mails or delivers the notice to each creditor whose name and address are known or reasonably ascertainable (755 ILCS 5/18-3).

The notice has to say that the person died, give the name and address of the executor and of the executor's attorney, state a date for filing claims, and warn that later claims are barred. That date must be at least six months after the first publication or three months after mailing, whichever is later. If you want the full timeline, [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) shows where the claims period sits in the whole case.

## How does a creditor file a claim?

A claim can be filed with the executor, with the court clerk, or with both (755 ILCS 5/18-1). It can rest on a contract, an injury, a custodial-care claim or anything else. Two details matter.

First, form. The claim has to be in writing and say enough for the executor to understand what it is about (755 ILCS 5/18-2). There is no special court form that the statute requires, but the circuit clerk in many counties offers one, and using it saves argument. Attach the proof: the signed agreement, the statement, the invoice, the dates of service.

Second, copies. If the claim is filed in court, the claimant must mail or deliver a copy to the executor and the executor's attorney within 10 days, and file proof that this was done. Missing that step does not invalidate the filing, the statute says, but it invites a fight about whether the executor knew.

If you only send the claim to the executor, the executor is not required to file it with the court. That sounds harmless until the next section.

## What can the executor do with a claim?

There are three choices, and the statute spells out two of them (755 ILCS 5/18-11).

1. **Allow it.** The executor can pay all or part of the claim, or consent to it in writing, when the claim is not barred, has not been disallowed by the court and appears valid. Payment or written consent counts as allowance and binds the estate. If the claim was filed in court, the executor files a notice of allowance.
2. **Disallow it.** For a claim that has not been filed with the court, the executor mails or delivers a notice of disallowance to the claimant, and to the claimant's attorney if known. The notice must say the claim will be lost unless it is filed with the court by a stated date, and that date must be at least two months after the notice.
3. **Leave it for the judge.** If the claim is already in court and the executor will not agree to it, the dispute goes to the court to decide.

Any interested person can ask the executor to justify an allowance. That is the heirs' protection against an executor who is too quick to pay a friend's invoice.

### A short example

Marcus is executor for his mother, Helen. A home health agency mails a claim for $4,800 in unpaid bills from her last year. Marcus checks the agency's records against Helen's checkbook and finds she had paid about half of it. He writes to the agency and disallows the half that was already paid, and states in the notice that the agency has two months or more to file with the court if it disagrees. The agency never files. The disputed portion is treated as barred, and Marcus pays the rest.

## In what order are claims paid?

When the estate has enough money, order barely matters. When it does not, the Probate Act ranks claims in classes (755 ILCS 5/18-10). In general terms, funeral, burial and administration costs sit at the top, and the spouse's or child's award comes next. Debts to the federal government come after that, then the final year of medical and nursing home bills plus a capped amount of recent wages, then untraceable trust money, then debts to the State and local governments. Everything else, including most credit and personal loans, shares what is left. The full table is on the [credit card debt page](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois), so it is not repeated here.

The practical rule for an executor is simple. Do not pay a lower class while a higher class is unpaid, and keep a ledger showing which class each bill is in. The family's [spouse rights](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) can matter here because the award ranks high.

## What about a mortgage or a car loan?

A debt that is secured by property is a different animal from a credit card. The lender can look to the house or the car, not only to a claim against the estate. The claim classes in the Probate Act do not rank a secured loan the way they rank unsecured bills. What happens to a mortgage when the estate is short is a question for the attorney handling the estate. The loan documents and who lives in the home both matter. Keep paying the loan if the estate can afford to while the family decides what to do with the property.

## What happens if the executor distributes too early?

The bar date is the executor's protection. After it passes, claims that were not filed in time are barred, and an executor who gave proper notice and used reasonable care to find creditors is generally protected from personal liability to the ones who missed the notice (755 ILCS 5/18-12). Before it passes, distributing to heirs is risky.

If the estate has enough assets to cover every claim, the court can permit an early distribution to someone entitled to a share before the claims period ends. The recipient must post a bond for double the value of the share, which secures a refund of their share of any valid claim that appears later (755 ILCS 5/24-4). If a distribution later has to be returned, any interested person may ask the court for a refund order, and a distributee who refuses within 60 days of an order and demand is in breach of that bond.

Section 18-12 also sets an outer limit. Claims that could have been barred are barred two years after the death, whether or not an estate was ever opened. Unbarred claims can still be asserted against the estate's undistributed assets, and against a distributee who is not a creditor up to the amount needed so that person does not end up with less than they would have received had the claim been paid. Claims covered by liability insurance are treated differently, because they reach the insurance. ## If you are the executor, a checklist

| Task | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Publish for three weeks and file proof | Starts the bar date |
| Mail notice to every known creditor | The statute requires it for creditors you know of |
| Log each claim with date and amount | Heirs can demand an explanation |
| Check the claim against bank and card records | Estates often receive claims for bills already paid |
| Send written allowance or disallowance | Starts or ends the dispute |
| Pay by class, not by who calls most | Protects you from personal exposure |
| Wait for the bar date before final distribution | Avoids refund demands from heirs |
