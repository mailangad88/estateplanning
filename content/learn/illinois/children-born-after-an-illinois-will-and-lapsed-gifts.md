A will is a snapshot. Illinois will law has to deal with the fact that families keep changing after the photo is taken: a baby arrives, a daughter dies before her mother, a friend who was supposed to inherit the cabin is gone. Two sections of the Probate Act, 4-10 and 4-11, supply the default answers.

This page explains both, with examples, and shows where a plain sentence in your will beats the default. For other Illinois pages, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). If you are changing a will rather than reading one, start with [changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois).

## What happens to a child born after you sign your will?

Section 4-10 is one sentence long. A child born after the will was signed takes the share of your estate that the child would have received if you had died without a will. There are two ways out. The rule does not apply if the will makes provision for a child born after its signing, or if the will shows an intention to disinherit that child. When the later child does take a share, the other gifts in the will are reduced proportionally to cover it.

The statute is protective rather than punishing. It assumes most people would not have meant to leave out a child they had not yet had. A short example shows how it plays out.

Tom signs a will in 2019 leaving his entire estate to his wife, Priya, and their one son. In 2022 a daughter is born. Tom never updates the will and dies in 2026. The daughter can claim the share she would have taken under the intestacy rules, and the gifts to Priya and the son shrink to pay for it. To see the share that applies in each family arrangement, read [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

The share is calculated as if no will existed, but only for that child. The rest of the will still works. That is why a pretermitted-child claim can leave a family with a will that nobody quite knows how to read.

### How do you avoid the default?

You have three real options.

1. **Provide for future children.** A sentence such as "I leave my estate to my children in equal shares, including any children born or adopted after I sign this will" does the job and costs nothing.
2. **Name the child.** When the child exists, name them in the will, even if the gift is small.
3. **Disinherit expressly.** If you truly mean to leave someone out, say so in words. Do not count on silence to do it for you. Our page on [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) covers how to do it without inviting a fight.

The best fix is not clever drafting, it is a regular review. New child, new will. A baby is the clearest signal that a plan needs attention, and the usual advice for [new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) starts there.

## What happens when a gift lapses in Illinois?

A gift lapses when the person it was meant for cannot take it, most often because they died before you did. Section 4-11 sets the default rules, and the opening words matter: they apply unless the will expressly provides otherwise.

| Who the gift was for | If that person dies first or fails to take | Default under 4-11 |
|---|---|---|
| Your child, grandchild or other descendant | Their living descendants take | Divided by family branch (per stirpes) |
| A class (for example "my nieces and nephews") | Surviving class members take the share | If the missing member was your descendant, that member's descendants take it |
| Anyone else, such as a friend, sibling or charity | The gift fails | It falls into the residue |

Section 4-11 also contains an exception for a future interest that becomes indefeasibly vested at or after your death, before it takes effect. That is technical and mostly affects wills that create gifts in later stages, such as "to my wife for life, then to my son."

### An example with a descendant

Rosa leaves her house to her son Carlos. Carlos dies two years before Rosa, leaving two children. If Rosa never changed her will, the house goes to Carlos's two children in equal shares rather than failing. The substitute gift follows from the statute, not from anything Rosa wrote.

Now suppose Rosa leaves $20,000 to her friend Helen, who dies first. Helen is not Rosa's descendant, so the gift lapses. Under 4-11(c) it becomes part of the residue and passes to whoever takes the residue, in proportion to their interests there. If Helen's daughter was the one Rosa really cared about, the will needed to say so.

### Why the residuary clause matters

Lapsed gifts end up in the residue, so the residuary clause is where a will either works or breaks. If the residue goes to one person and that person has died, nobody takes it by the will, and the court falls back on the intestacy rules. Our page on [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) explains how these gift types interact, and [lapsed gifts and simultaneous death](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death) covers the general rules across states.

## What do lawyers see go wrong?

The common failure is not a statute, it is a stale will. A couple signs mirror wills when they have one child, leaving everything to each other and then to that child. Two more children arrive. Nobody revisits the document. If both parents die, the later children have a claim under 4-10 and the family discovers it only when a lawyer reads the will. The result is usually workable, but it is a different outcome from the one the parents imagined, and it can strain the relationship between the older and younger siblings.

The lapse side has its own pattern. A parent leaves a house to a daughter and the rest to a son. The daughter dies first, leaving a young child. The parent intends the grandchild to receive something but never says so, and the default sends the house to the grandchild, which may or may not match what the parent would have chosen. Grandchildren who are minors then raise a second question, how to hold the property for them, which our page on [Illinois UTMA accounts and gifts to minors](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) addresses.

## How can you control what happens?

A will is allowed to override the defaults, and a good one does. Practical steps:

- Name a backup for every significant gift, preferably an individual and not "my heirs."
- Say whether a gift to a child is meant to pass to that child's children if the child dies first.
- Write a survival requirement if you want to prevent a gift passing through someone who dies days after you.
- Name a final backup for the residue, such as a charity or a named relative.
- Review the will after each birth, death, marriage and divorce.

Gifts outside the will need the same care. A beneficiary form that names only one person fails the same way, and the fix there is a contingent beneficiary on the form. See the [beneficiary audit](/learn/beneficiary-designations) for how to check each account.
