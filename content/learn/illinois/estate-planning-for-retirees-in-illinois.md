You have stopped working, or you are about to. The plan you signed when the kids were young was built around a paycheck and a mortgage. Now your money sits in a 401(k), an IRA or an Illinois pension, the house may be paid off, and the question is less "who raises the children" and more "who handles things if I cannot, and what is left for the family."

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is organized by the decisions that change at retirement for someone who lives here. Each section names the decision, gives the Illinois rule that shapes it, and links the page that covers it in depth. For the general version that applies in any state, see [estate planning in retirement](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-retirees). If you want to go through your own documents with someone, you can [book a plan review](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-for-retirees-in-illinois&topic=review) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Who should inherit your retirement accounts and pension?

Decide this account by account, because each one follows its own form, not your will. Retirement is often when a 401(k) gets rolled into an IRA, and that rollover is a decision point many people miss. In a private-sector 401(k), federal rules generally make your spouse the beneficiary unless your spouse signs a waiver witnessed by a notary or a plan representative. Once the money is in an IRA, there is no federal spousal consent rule, and the custodian's form controls. The full comparison, including the waiver steps, is on our page about [401(k) and IRA beneficiaries for Illinois married couples](/learn/illinois/retirement-account-beneficiaries-and-spousal-consent-in-illinois).

So a rollover can quietly change who has a say. Talk it through with your spouse before you sign the new IRA form.

Public employees face a different set of choices. IMRF, TRS, SURS and SERS each have their own survivor rules, and the form on file beats the will. Two details matter most at retirement. IMRF says a spouse must be the only primary beneficiary on the member's form to qualify for a surviving spouse pension when an active member dies. TRS warns that when a member names a new spouse after a divorce, children from the earlier marriage are not eligible for monthly survivor benefits. Both are explained on our page on [Illinois pension survivor benefits](/learn/illinois/illinois-pension-survivor-benefits-imrf-trs-surs-sers), and the free [guide for teachers and public employees](/free/estate-planning-for-teachers-and-public-employees) has a worksheet for the forms.

One more reason to act now rather than later: IMRF says it will not accept a beneficiary change signed by an agent, conservator, trustee or guardian. If your memory fails before you update the form, the old form usually stays.

On taxes, the federal rules decide most of the result for your heirs. A spouse can usually roll the account into their own, while most adult children must empty it within 10 years, as our page on the [inherited IRA 10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) explains. Illinois subtracts most federally taxed retirement distributions, which our page on [Illinois income tax on an inherited IRA or 401(k)](/learn/illinois/do-you-pay-illinois-income-tax-on-an-inherited-ira-or-401k) covers.

## How will you pay for long-term care, and what happens to what is left?

Plan early, because the Illinois rules reward early choices. Medicare generally does not pay for long-term custodial care. The usual sources are your own savings, long-term care insurance, family help and, once assets are low enough, Medicaid. Our page on [long-term care insurance](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance) covers the private option.

Two Illinois rules shape the Medicaid side.

1. **The look-back.** When someone applies for long-term care Medicaid, Illinois reviews transfers made in the 60 months before the application. A gift inside that window can cause a penalty period. Transfers to a spouse are generally allowed. Our page on [qualifying for Medicaid nursing home care in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) lists the exceptions, and the [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) finds your window.
2. **Estate recovery.** After a Medicaid recipient dies, the state can claim against the probate estate. Recovery waits while a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child survives. For deaths on or after July 1, 2022, the first $25,000 of estate value is protected. HFS says retirement accounts and pension plans that pass to a named beneficiary are generally outside the estate it claims against. The details are on our page on [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery).

The retirement-age mistake is giving the house to the children "to protect it." That gift starts the look-back clock, can cost the children the step-up in basis, and puts the house within reach of their creditors. The free [Illinois Medicaid long-term care guide](/free/illinois-medicaid-long-term-care-guide) walks through the safer questions to ask first.

## What should happen to the house?

Decide two things: how it passes at death, and how you will carry it while you live there.

For passing it, the usual Illinois choice is between a transfer on death instrument and a revocable living trust. A TODI under 755 ILCS 27 is signed, witnessed by two credible witnesses, notarized and recorded before death, and it passes the house without probate. A trust costs more but can also manage the house if you become incapacitated. An agent under a power of attorney cannot create or revoke a TODI for you, so if you want one, sign it while you can. Our side-by-side page on [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house) helps you choose, and [can creditors or Medicaid reach a TODI house](/learn/illinois/todi-creditors-and-medicaid-in-illinois) covers the long-term care angle.

For carrying it, look at the property tax tools for older owners. The senior exemption applies to owners 65 or older who live in the home. The senior freeze adds an income test and must be filed every year. Our page on [Illinois property tax exemptions](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies) explains how each behaves. Separately, the Senior Citizens Real Estate Tax Deferral Program lets eligible owners 65 or older defer part of the tax as a loan from the state, at 3% simple interest for recent tax years, secured by a lien on the house. That lien must be repaid when the house is sold or the owner dies, so it reduces what heirs receive. Read [the deferral program and what heirs repay](/learn/illinois/illinois-senior-citizens-property-tax-deferral-program-and-what-heirs-repay) before applying. A TODI or trust does not remove the lien.

## Who will act for you if you cannot?

Name people who can actually do the job in your seventies and eighties. The agent you picked at 45 may be your spouse, who is now your age, or a sibling who has moved away.

- **Property.** The Illinois statutory short form for property is signed in front of one witness and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3). Add a younger successor agent. Our page on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) explains the form, and [co-agents and successor agents](/learn/illinois/co-agents-and-successor-agents-in-illinois) covers backups.
- **Health care.** The statutory health care power of attorney needs one adult witness and no notary (755 ILCS 45/4-10). See the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).
- **End of life.** A living will covers terminal conditions and needs two witnesses. A POLST is a doctor's order for when health declines; see [Illinois POLST and DNR orders](/learn/illinois/illinois-polst-and-dnr-orders).

Without these, a family may need a court guardianship. Retirees with early memory changes should read [planning for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) while they can still sign.

## Will your estate owe Illinois estate tax?

Add it up once, because retirees cross the line more often than they expect. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026, but Illinois taxes estates over $4 million, counts retirement accounts and life insurance you own, and has no portability between spouses. A couple can each be under the line and still leave a taxable estate at the second death if everything passes outright to the survivor. Run the numbers with our [Illinois estate tax worksheet](/learn/illinois/will-my-estate-owe-illinois-estate-tax), and see [QTIP elections and credit shelter trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-qtip-election-and-credit-shelter-trusts) for the drafting fix.

## What if you retire somewhere else?

Moving does not cancel your Illinois documents, but your new state then controls much of your estate. Illinois real estate you keep can still need an Illinois probate and can still be counted for Illinois estate tax if your total is over $4 million. Our page on [moving out of Illinois in retirement](/learn/illinois/moving-out-of-illinois) has the before-you-move checklist. A lawyer licensed in the new state should review the plan there.

## A worked example: Gail and Victor

Example only, with invented names and round numbers. Gail, 64, is retiring from a private company with a $700,000 401(k). Victor, 66, retired from teaching and draws a TRS pension. They own a $380,000 house in Bloomington and $250,000 in savings. Their wills, signed in 2005, name Victor's brother as executor.

- **Accounts.** Gail plans to roll her 401(k) into an IRA. Before she does, they agree in writing that Victor stays primary beneficiary and her two children from a first marriage are contingent, so the rollover does not quietly change the plan.
- **Pension.** Victor checks his TRS form and finds it still names his late mother as a beneficiary. He updates it now, while he can sign.
- **Long-term care.** They price a long-term care policy and decide not to gift the house to the children.
- **House.** With adult children who get along and no minors, they sign a TODI naming each other and then the children. They file for the senior exemption and decide against the deferral loan.
- **Incapacity.** They sign new property and health care powers of attorney naming each other and then Gail's daughter, who lives in Normal.
- **Tax.** Their combined total is about $1.33 million, well under $4 million, so they note it and recheck in a few years.
