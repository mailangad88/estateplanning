You are probably the person everyone calls. A child needs a ride, a parent needs a doctor's appointment, and somewhere in the week you are supposed to be working. Estate planning feels like the thing that belongs on a later list, and meanwhile both ends of the family depend on documents that may not exist.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), puts the two plans into one checklist, ordered by what hurts most if it is missing. It points to the longer Illinois pages on each piece so you can go deeper only where you need to. If you would rather work through it with someone, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-sandwich-generation-estate-planning-illinois&topic=parent).

## Why plan for both generations at once?

Because the same few documents show up on both sides, and because a crisis at one end tends to land on the other. A parent's fall in February can pull you out of town just when your children need you at home. If you cannot act for your parent, or your children's guardian is unnamed, the gap lands on whoever is closest.

Doing the two plans together also saves effort. You gather the same kinds of information twice: account lists, insurance, a list of doctors, the names of people you trust. Our [sandwich generation planning kit](/free/estate-planning-for-gen-x-sandwich-generation-kit) has a one-page map for this, and the [aging parent conversation kit](/free/aging-parent-conversation-kit) helps with the awkward first talk.

## The combined checklist, in order of urgency

Work down the list. Items 1 and 2 cannot wait if a parent has any memory trouble.

| Order | Who | What | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Parent | Power of attorney for property and for health care | Must be signed while the parent understands it |
| 2 | Parent | HIPAA release and a list of doctors, medications, accounts | Lets you talk to doctors and find things in an emergency |
| 3 | You | Guardian named for your children, in a signed will | Without it, relatives have to ask a court |
| 4 | You | Your own health care and property powers of attorney | You are the one holding the family together |
| 5 | You and parent | Beneficiary forms on retirement accounts and life insurance | Forms override wills, and old ones cause the worst surprises |
| 6 | Parent | Will, or a trust if the house or assets warrant it | Decides who inherits and who serves as executor |
| 7 | You | Life insurance and a guardian fund for the kids | Money to raise the children if you are gone |
| 8 | Family | Caregiving agreement, siblings' roles, backup caregiver | Keeps money and effort fair, and Medicaid-safe |
| 9 | Parent | Long-term care and Medicaid plan | Matters most when care is coming within a few years |

## 1 and 2: Your parent's powers of attorney and medical access

If your parent has any decline, this is first. In Illinois, a parent can sign a power of attorney if they understand what it does and whom they are naming on the day they sign. A diagnosis of dementia does not by itself decide that. If they cannot, nobody can sign for them, and the family has to petition for guardianship under 755 ILCS 5/11a. Our page on a [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney) explains how capacity is judged.

Two documents matter. The power of attorney for property lets the agent handle bills, accounts and the house. The statutory form needs the parent's signature, one witness who is not the agent, and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3). The health care power of attorney names who speaks for the parent in medical decisions and needs one witness but no notary (755 ILCS 45/4-10). See the pages on the [property power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) and the [health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

Choose the agent for fit, not birth order. The sibling who lives closest and is organised often beats the oldest. Name a successor. For more, see [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent).

Without a health care agent, hospital decisions fall to a ranked list of relatives under the Health Care Surrogate Act. That works for treatment but not for money. The page on [hospital paperwork with no power of attorney](/learn/illinois/hospital-no-power-of-attorney) covers what you may be asked to sign.

## 3 and 4: Your children's guardian and your own documents

Naming a guardian is the single most important document for a parent of minor children. In Illinois you can name one in a signed will with two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/11-5), and a judge still decides based on the child's best interest. Read [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children) for choosing between candidates, and add a short-term guardian paper if you travel for a parent's care often. The [standby and short-term guardians](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children) page explains how.

Then do your own powers of attorney. People who care for a parent often skip these on the theory that they are the healthy one. Yet if your spouse is also stretched, nobody may be positioned to step in for you.

## 5 to 7: Forms, wills and money

Check beneficiary forms for yourself and for your parent. An ex-spouse, a deceased sibling or no one at all are the common problems, and the form controls the account no matter what the will says. See [how often to review beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-often-to-review-beneficiary-designations).

If you have minor children, think about how a guardian would pay for them. A term life insurance policy is the usual answer, and the [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) gives a rough size. The [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) is the companion.

## 8 and 9: Caregiving money and long-term care

Money for a parent's care raises its own risks. A parent can pay a child for care, but Illinois reviews transfers made in the 60 months before a Medicaid application, and an undocumented payment can look like a gift. A written agreement signed before payment, a fair rate and a log of hours are the starting points, all laid out in [paying a family caregiver](/learn/illinois/paying-a-family-caregiver). Also read [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility and planning](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) before moving any assets.

Do not pay for a parent's care out of your own retirement account without running the numbers first, and do not assume that moving your parent in will solve things. The article on [moving a parent in with you](/learn/elder-care/moving-a-parent-in-with-you-or-to-another-state) lists what to settle first.

## A realistic week: Priya and her mother

Priya, 52, lives in Oak Park with two children, 9 and 14. Her mother, Lena, 79, lives alone and has started missing bills. Priya's own will is eleven years old, from before her second child, and names her brother as guardian when he has since moved overseas.

She sequences it. This month, Lena signs both powers of attorney with Priya as agent and Priya's brother as successor, and gets a HIPAA release at her next doctor visit. Next month, Priya and her husband update their wills with a new guardian and backups, and they check the beneficiary on Priya's work 401(k), which still lists a former roommate. The caregiving agreement waits until paid help starts, but she opens a notebook to log hours now.

The order matters more than the speed. The parent's documents came first because the window might close.
