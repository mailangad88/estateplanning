When a parent dies in Ottawa, Peru, LaSalle, Streator or Mendota, the estate is a LaSalle County, Illinois matter. The county sits in the 13th Judicial Circuit with Bureau and Grundy counties. This page belongs to the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

It covers where the courthouse and clerk are, how an estate opens, what to do with the original will, remote hearings, self-help and case lookup. It also covers a question rural families raise: a parent who owned a river cabin, a boat and a few vehicles. To talk it through with an Illinois estate planning attorney, [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-probate-works-in-lasalle-county&topic=probate).

This is general education drawn from official court and county pages read in November 2026, not legal advice. Rooms, phone numbers and rules change, so confirm them with the clerk before you travel.

## Where is the LaSalle County Courthouse and the Circuit Clerk?

The courthouse is at 119 West Madison Street, Ottawa, IL 61350. The Illinois Courts directory lists it in the 13th Judicial Circuit, open Monday through Friday, 8:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., with phone numbers 815-434-8671 and 815-433-9198. The 13th Circuit's own location page gives the same address and hours.

The county's contact page is where the probate details are. It lists 815-434-8671 for the Civil and Small Claims Division, divorce, immigration and probate. A separate line, 815-434-8271, is listed for the Traffic and Criminal Division and for wills.

Another office, on Etna Road in Ottawa, houses the Traffic and Criminal Division. The pages do not say which building takes a will, so ask on the phone before you drive.

| Office or service | What the official pages list | What a probate family uses it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Circuit Clerk, civil side | 119 West Madison Street; 815-434-8671 | Petitions, orders and probate questions |
| Wills line | 815-434-8271 | Asking how an original will is delivered |
| Clerk's Office, Room 201 | E-filing kiosk and Self-Help Center | Filing help and forms without a lawyer |

## How does an estate open, and what do you do with the original will?

An estate opens when someone files a petition with the Circuit Clerk in the county where the person lived. A named executor asks the court to admit the will and issue letters of office. With no will, a relative asks to be appointed administrator. The longer walk-through is in [how to open a probate estate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-open-a-probate-estate-in-illinois).

The original will comes first. Under 755 ILCS 5/6-1, anyone holding the original must file it with the clerk right after the death. The page on [filing the original will with the Illinois circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk) explains that duty and the 30-day clocks in section 6-3.

In practice:

1. Keep the original flat and unstapled, and do not write on it.
2. Photograph every page so the family has a record.
3. Call the clerk and ask where and how to deliver the original.
4. Ask for a receipt showing the date and any case or will number.
5. Tell the named executor where the original now sits.

Filing the will preserves it. It does not give anyone authority over an account, a house or a truck. That comes from the court's order and letters of office after the petition is heard. The overall order of events is in [the Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process).

## Can you e-file in LaSalle County?

The 13th Circuit's home page links to e-File Illinois, the statewide portal. The Circuit Clerk's site says the office has put in place the state-required e-filing and e-appeals, and the Illinois Courts directory places an e-filing kiosk in Room 201.

The pages do not say whether a probate petition must be e-filed, or how a will is handled. Treat that as a question for the clerk. Whatever the answer, the original will is the paper a scan cannot replace.

## Can you attend a LaSalle County hearing by Zoom?

Sometimes. The 13th Circuit links a page titled How to Attend Court by Zoom, and the LaSalle clerk's site has a Zoom Court Rules page. That page links two court administrative orders and tells you to acknowledge Administrative Order 23-13 before joining through the courtroom live stream.

The location page lists Zoom courtrooms by number. The pages do not say which hearings qualify or whether probate is treated differently. The judge decides whether you may appear remotely. A person living in another state should ask the clerk or the attorney on the case before relying on it.

## What self-help does the LaSalle County court list?

Three resources appear on official pages. The Illinois Courts directory says the Self-Help Center is in the Circuit Clerk's Office, Room 201. The clerk's site links Illinois Legal Aid's LaSalle County page and Illinois Court Help.

The clerk's court forms page has civil and traffic or criminal categories and a link to the Illinois Supreme Court's standardized forms. It does not mention probate or wills. For a sense of what a do-it-yourself executor takes on, read [handling an Illinois probate estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer).

## How do you look up a LaSalle County case?

The 13th Circuit's home page links a Search Cases page. The clerk's online court records page links a search application and a Fee Schedule page. It also offers text or email alerts for court appearance dates.

Neither page says what the search shows for estates. A missing entry online is not proof that nothing was filed, so call 815-434-8671. For how estate files become public, see [is probate public](/learn/probate/is-probate-public) and [how to look up a probate case](/learn/probate/how-to-look-up-a-probate-case).

## Does a small estate need the court at all?

Sometimes not. Illinois caps the personal property a small estate affidavit can cover at $150,000 as of 2026, not counting registered vehicles, as the page on the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) explains. The affidavit cannot move real estate.

A family with a house or cabin, or with more than the cap, usually needs a petition. See [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) for how the court then oversees the executor.

## What happens to a parent's river cabin, boat and vehicles?

Each asset follows its own paper, and in a rural family they often differ. The cabin is real estate, held by a deed. The boat, the truck and the trailer are held by titles. A will can name who gets all of them, but the route to put the new name on each is different.

A cabin passes by deed, will or intestacy, and an affidavit cannot move it. If the cabin was in the parent's name alone, expect a petition and letters of office. The pages on [taking care of a deceased parent's house](/learn/illinois/taking-care-of-a-deceased-parents-house-in-illinois) and [who inherits without a will](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) cover the next steps.

Vehicles are easier. The page on [transferring a car title after death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/transfer-car-title-after-death-in-illinois) explains the three routes:

| How the vehicle is titled | Who usually takes it | Court needed? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two owners with survivorship | The surviving owner | No |
| One owner, small estate, heirs agree | The heir who signs a small estate affidavit | No |
| One owner, estate in probate | The executor, with letters of office | Yes, already open |

Registered vehicles sit outside the $150,000 cap, so a modest estate can often retitle a truck without probate. A loan stays with the vehicle, and the lender must release its lien or be paid. Keep the insurance active while the title is sorted out.

The page on [transferring a car, boat or RV](/learn/property-and-assets/transfer-a-car-boat-or-rv-after-death) adds that a boat may be state-registered or documented with the U.S. Coast Guard, and the papers show which. Both need the hull number, proof of death and proof of authority. The [vehicle transfer checklist](/free/vehicle-transfer-after-death-checklist) keeps the paper trail in order. For the death certificates each office will want, see [how many to order](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need), and for debts see [car loans after death](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-car-loan-and-student-loans-after-death).

## Example: the Kowalski family near Utica

This is an invented example. Walter Kowalski, 81, lived in Ottawa and died in October. He owned a small cabin on the Illinois River, a 14-foot fishing boat, a 2012 pickup and a car he shared on title with his wife, Irene. His short will named his daughter Beth as executor and left the cabin to Beth and her brother Paul.

Irene, as surviving owner, retitled the shared car with a death certificate. Beth called the clerk's office, learned where the original will goes and filed it. She then asked an attorney whether a petition was needed. Because the cabin was in Walter's name alone, it was.

The pickup and the boat were titled to Walter only. Once the court issued letters, Beth could sign for both. Paul wanted the boat, Beth wanted the truck, and they put the swap in writing. The loan on the truck was paid from the estate's account so the lender released its lien. The cabin took the longest, because the deed work came after letters. Beth kept the insurance on every vehicle in force until the titles were done.

## What mistakes should a LaSalle County family avoid?

- Selling the boat or truck before anyone has authority to sign the title.
- Assuming the small estate affidavit can move the cabin.
- Letting vehicle insurance lapse while the family decides.
- Relying on an online search to prove a document was or was not filed.
- Keeping the original will at home while everyone agrees on who goes first.
