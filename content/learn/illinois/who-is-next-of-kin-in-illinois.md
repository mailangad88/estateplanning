A nurse asks who the next of kin is. A funeral director asks the same thing two days later, and then a bank, and then the probate clerk. Most families assume each one is asking the same question. In Illinois they are not. The phrase sounds like a single ranking, but state law uses four different lists depending on what has to be decided.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), puts those four lists side by side: medical decisions, inheritance, funeral and remains, and who can serve as administrator of the estate. It also answers the questions that tend to come next, about unmarried partners and about debts, and walks through one family to show how the lists split apart. If your family's situation does not fit neatly, a [consultation](/book?from=learn-who-is-next-of-kin-in-illinois&topic=other) can sort out who speaks for whom.

## Is there one legal definition of next of kin in Illinois?

No. Illinois does not keep one master list. Each statute that needs a decision-maker writes its own order. The Health Care Surrogate Act ranks who decides medical care (755 ILCS 40/25). The Probate Act ranks who inherits without a will (755 ILCS 5/2-1) and, separately, who has preference to be appointed administrator (755 ILCS 5/9-3). The Disposition of Remains Act ranks who controls burial or cremation (755 ILCS 65/5).

The lists overlap at the top. A spouse, and a civil union partner, who Illinois treats like a spouse (750 ILCS 75/20), comes first or nearly first on all four. Below the spouse, the orders drift apart. That drift is where families get surprised.

### Where do the lists disagree?

Three gaps cause most of the confusion.

- **Parents and siblings.** For medical and funeral decisions, a parent outranks a brother or sister. For inheritance, parents and siblings share the estate in equal parts, with a double share for a surviving parent when the other parent has died.
- **Grandchildren.** A grandchild whose parent has died steps into that parent's inheritance share, per stirpes. For appointment as administrator, grandchildren rank above the decedent's parents. On the medical list, adult grandchildren rank below adult siblings.
- **Friends.** A close friend appears on the medical list. Friends do not appear on the inheritance or administrator lists, and appear on the funeral list only through the catch-all for anyone willing to take legal and financial responsibility, after every relative.

## Who decides medical care when there is no health care power of attorney?

The Health Care Surrogate Act decides, but only after the hospital confirms there is no health care agent and the patient cannot decide. The provider then works down the list in the table, starting with a guardian of the person and then the spouse (755 ILCS 40/25). Adult children at the same rank decide by majority. A child in the minority who wants to change the outcome has to start a guardianship case.

Stopping life-sustaining treatment carries extra safeguards, explained on the [Health Care Surrogate Act page](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides), and the [Illinois health care surrogate order chart](/free/illinois-health-care-surrogate-order-chart) is a printable copy of the list. A signed [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) puts your own choice ahead of the whole list.

## Who inherits when there is no will?

The intestacy statute, 755 ILCS 5/2-1, sets fixed shares rather than a ranking of decision-makers. With a spouse and descendants, the spouse takes one-half and the descendants share one-half. With descendants and no spouse, the descendants take everything. The table on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) covers every step down to distant cousins, and the [Illinois intestacy chart](/free/illinois-intestacy-chart) lets you map your own family. When the line runs out entirely, [dying with no heirs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/dying-with-no-heirs-in-illinois) explains how property passes to the county or state.

Being an heir and proving it are different steps. Banks and title companies often want proof of who the heirs are, which the Probate Act allows by affidavit or testimony and a court order declaring heirship (755 ILCS 5/5-3). See [how to prove heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois) and, for a house, [heirship affidavit versus probate](/learn/illinois/heirship-affidavit-vs-probate).

## Who controls the funeral and the remains?

The Disposition of Remains Act gives control first to a person named in a qualifying written instrument, then to an executor carrying out written instructions in a will, then to the spouse, adult children, parents and nearest adult kin (755 ILCS 65/5). Several adult children decide by majority. A dispute between people on the list goes to court, and the Act calls for a ruling within 30 days of filing (755 ILCS 65/50).

The funeral list does not wait for probate. The [Disposition of Remains Act page](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act) covers the notarized agent form, and [paying for a funeral from an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/paying-for-a-funeral-from-an-illinois-estate) covers the bill.

## Who can serve as administrator of the estate?

When there is no will, the court issues letters of administration, and Section 9-3 of the Probate Act gives preference to the surviving spouse or the spouse's nominee, then to children, grandchildren, parents, siblings and other kin (755 ILCS 5/9-3). The person must also qualify under Section 9-1: at least 18, a United States resident, of sound mind, and generally without a felony conviction. Expect a bond in an intestate case, because only a will can excuse one. The [letters of administration page](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) has the full order and the bond rules.

When there is a will, the person named as executor serves instead, if they qualify. That test is on [who can be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-can-be-an-executor-in-illinois). When nobody with preference is willing, the court can appoint the county Public Administrator (755 ILCS 5/13-4).

## Is an unmarried partner next of kin in Illinois?

No, for most purposes. Illinois does not recognize common-law marriage, so a partner without a marriage or civil union is not a spouse on any of the four lists. A partner inherits nothing without a will, has no funeral role without a signed agent form, has no preference to serve as administrator, and can make medical decisions only as a close friend, below every relative.

Each gap closes with a document: a will or trust, a health care power of attorney, a notarized funeral agent form, and beneficiary designations. The page on [unmarried partners in Illinois](/learn/illinois/unmarried-partners) lists what to sign, and [civil union partners and Illinois estate law](/learn/illinois/civil-union-partners-and-illinois-estate-law) explains when a civil union changes the picture.

## Does next of kin have to pay the dead person's debts?

No, not because of the relationship. Debts are claims against the estate, and the estate pays valid claims in the order set by 755 ILCS 5/18-10, with funeral and administration costs near the front and credit cards near the back. A relative owes personally only on an account they signed for or shared, or in limited cases after receiving estate property. Unfiled claims are barred two years after death at the outside (755 ILCS 5/18-12).

The person who serves as administrator handles the claims with estate money, not their own. See [credit card debt after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois) and [do you inherit your parents' debt in Illinois](/learn/illinois/do-you-inherit-your-parents-debt-in-illinois) for the details.

## Worked example: one family, four answers

This is an invented example to show how the lists differ.

Gary, 67, is divorced and lives in Joliet with his partner of eleven years, Diane. They never married. Gary has an adult daughter, Kara. His son, Neil, died three years ago and left a son, Eli, who is 24. Gary's mother, June, is 90, and his brother, Sam, lives nearby. Gary signed no will, no health care power of attorney and no funeral form.

**In the hospital.** Gary has a stroke and cannot speak. There is no agent, no guardian and no spouse, so the surrogate is his adult child, Kara. June, Sam and Eli rank below her. Diane could act only as a close friend, and only if no one above her is available.

**At the funeral home.** Gary dies a week later. With no written instrument and no spouse, his adult children control. Kara is the only surviving adult child, so she signs. Her grandmother June, who ranks next, has no say while Kara is willing.

**In the estate.** Gary has no spouse, so his descendants take everything, per stirpes. Kara takes one-half. Eli takes the one-half his father Neil would have taken. June and Sam inherit nothing, and neither does Diane.

**At the courthouse.** Kara, as a child, has first preference for letters of administration. Eli, a grandchild, comes next. Kara could nominate Diane to serve if Diane qualifies, but Diane has no right of her own.

**With the creditors.** Gary's credit card bills are paid from his estate in the statutory order. Kara and Eli do not owe them from their own pockets.

Now change one fact. If Gary and Diane had entered a civil union, Diane would be surrogate, would control the funeral, would inherit one-half with Kara and Eli sharing the other half, and would have first preference to serve.
