Your mother's house passed to you by a transfer on death instrument. The funeral is over, and the bank wants "proof the house is yours." The paper that gives them that proof is called the notice of death affidavit.

This page is the beneficiary's side of an Illinois TODI. The owner's steps are covered in our overview of the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) and in [recording an Illinois TODI](/learn/illinois/recording-an-illinois-todi). The full set of Illinois articles starts at our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois). This is general education, not legal advice.

## What is a notice of death affidavit?

It is a sworn statement, recorded in the county land records, that tells the public a TODI has taken effect because the owner died. Under 755 ILCS 27/75 it identifies the beneficiary, the legal description, the address and parcel number, the recording details of the TODI, and the date and place of the owner's death.

The Act says recording it is not a condition of the transfer. It is the document that makes the transfer easy to prove. Without it, a buyer or lender has to piece the history together from a death certificate and the old instrument. Because the Act has changed over the years and the statute may carry timing rules, check the current text of 755 ILCS 27/75 or ask the recorder before you assume there is no deadline.

## What do you need before you start?

Gather these papers.

- The recorded TODI, or at least its document number and recording date.
- A certified death certificate. See [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need).
- The legal description and parcel number from the TODI or the tax bill.
- Your government ID and access to a notary.
- Contact information for the other beneficiaries, if any.

## How do you record it step by step?

1. Confirm that the TODI was recorded before the owner died by searching the county index.
2. Order certified copies of the death certificate.
3. Complete the affidavit with the facts the statute lists, using the exact legal description.
4. Sign before a notary. If there is more than one beneficiary, each signs for their interest.
5. Record the affidavit with the county recorder, or the Cook County Clerk if the land is in Cook County.
6. Ask for a stamped copy and later confirm the record appears in the index.
7. Notify the homeowner's insurer, the mortgage servicer and the county assessor of the change in ownership.
8. Update the property tax mailing address. Our article on [property tax exemptions after a spouse dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies) covers exemptions for surviving spouses.

## Who holds the house at that point, and what claims remain?

The beneficiary holds the house, but the transfer is not a shield. The Act subjects TODI beneficiaries to creditor claims, funeral costs and certain statutory claims to the same extent as beneficiaries of a revocable trust (755 ILCS 27/85). A surviving spouse may renounce and claim a share (755 ILCS 27/66). A contest must be filed by the earlier of two years after death or six months after letters of office are issued (755 ILCS 27/90). Our article on [creditors and Medicaid with a TODI](/learn/illinois/todi-creditors-and-medicaid-in-illinois) goes into the claims.

If there is a mortgage, it stays. The beneficiary can keep making payments or refinance. Our article on [mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) explains what the lender can and cannot do.

## What should you compare before choosing to keep or sell?

| Choice | What it means | What to check first |
|---|---|---|
| Keep and live there | Update insurance, taxes, utilities | Whether estate claims could force a sale |
| Keep as a rental | New landlord duties and insurance | Mortgage terms and landlord rules |
| Sell soon | Stepped-up basis often limits taxable gain | Title company requirements and open claims |
| Refuse the gift | Disclaimer before acting as owner | Timing and who takes next |

The date-of-death value generally becomes the starting point for gain when the house is sold. Our article on [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) covers the tax side.

## A worked example

Hector, 61, is the sole beneficiary of his mother Lucia's TODI for a house worth $240,000. The mortgage balance is $55,000. He records the notice of death affidavit three weeks after the funeral. Two months later he lists the house for $240,000. The title company asks for the death certificate, the recorded TODI, the notice of death affidavit and confirmation that no probate case is open. Because no claims are filed and he is the sole beneficiary, the sale closes. Hector pays off the $55,000 mortgage and receives about $169,000 after $16,000 in selling costs.

If Lucia had also owed a $30,000 medical bill and her other assets were small, the creditor could have looked to the house under the Act, and a title company may have asked for more time. Early legal advice would have helped.

## What if there is a will or probate case too?

The TODI still controls the house if it was valid and recorded, even if the will says something different. A will cannot revoke a TODI. Executors still need to know about the house, since creditor claims may reach it. Our article on [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) explains how.

## What if the TODI was never recorded, or a document is missing?

Start by searching the county index under the owner's name and the parcel number. If a recorded instrument turns up, you can proceed with the affidavit. If none is found, the Act treats an unrecorded instrument as void and ineffective (755 ILCS 27/40), and the house is handled as part of the owner's estate. That usually means probate, under the will or the intestacy rules. See [the Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) and who inherits without a will.

If the instrument was recorded but you cannot find your copy, order a certified copy from the recorder. If the legal description in the instrument seems wrong, do not record the affidavit yet. A title company or lawyer should compare it with the vesting deed, because an affidavit that repeats an error can carry the problem forward.

Siblings sometimes disagree about whether the owner was able to sign. The Act sets limits on contests, as noted above, and our article on contesting a trust in Illinois describes similar grounds for capacity and undue influence. Do not wait to get advice if a challenge seems likely.
