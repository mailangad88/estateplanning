Sometimes a neighbor, a retired teacher or a long-time tenant dies, and no one steps forward. The county wants to know who is in charge of the house and the bank account. Illinois has a procedure for that, and it also has a final rule for the rare case where no relative exists.

This page follows the process from the first call to the last step, including who looks for heirs, what is paid first and what happens to leftover money. For the usual order of heirs, see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). The wider question of what happens in any state is in [our article on missing heirs](/learn/what-if/heir-cannot-be-found-or-no-family-survives).

## How far does the Illinois heir list go?

Further than most people expect. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-1, after a spouse, descendants, parents and siblings come grandparents and their descendants, then great-grandparents and their descendants, and then the nearest kin by degree. Aunts, uncles and cousins are therefore heirs, and in some cases second cousins and beyond. A person with no known family may still have a distant cousin out of state.

| Step | Who acts | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Neighbor, landlord, hospital or bank notices a death | Coroner or county is told; remains are held |
| 2 | Someone petitions the circuit court | A public administrator or other administrator is appointed |
| 3 | Administrator searches for a will and heirs | Notice to creditors and heirs; records search |
| 4 | Debts, taxes and costs are paid | Only what is left is distributed |
| 5 | Heir found | Estate is distributed to the heir |
| 6 | No heir found | Real estate to the county, other property to county or state |

## Who takes charge when no one steps forward?

The probate court of the county where the person lived appoints an administrator. When no one is entitled or willing to serve, the court can appoint the county's public administrator (755 ILCS 5/13-4). Public administrators are county officers. They post bond, take control of the assets, secure a house and look for a will. They are paid from the estate within court limits.

If you are a landlord, bank or friend holding the person's property, do not clear it out. Contact the county coroner or medical examiner for the body and the circuit clerk for the probate process. The estate can reimburse reasonable funeral costs if there are assets. Our guide to the [Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act) shows who has authority over burial.

## What steps does an administrator follow?

1. Gets letters of administration from the court. See [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond).
2. Searches the home, safe deposit box, mail and computer for a will and for family names.
3. Secures the property, insures the house and stops unneeded accounts.
4. Publishes and mails notice to creditors and to any known or unknown heirs.
5. Pays the claims in order. See [how creditor claims work](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate).
6. Hires a professional heir finder or genealogist if the court allows it, or asks the court for help.
7. Reports to the court. If an heir is found, distributes. If not, requests an order to deliver the net estate to the county or the State Treasurer.

## What gets paid before the county or state?

Almost everything else. Funeral and burial costs, the costs of administration, taxes and valid claims come ahead of any escheat. A house may be sold. A person with no heirs and a $90,000 estate might leave $55,000 after bills. Only that remainder is escheated. If the debts exceed the assets, nothing goes anywhere, and creditors take a reduced share in the legal order.

## What happens in the final step?

The statute directs real estate to the county where it is located. Personal property goes to the county where the decedent lived. Other property goes to the state, and the State Treasurer takes charge of it (755 ILCS 5/2-1). Unclaimed items, like uncashed checks, may go through the Illinois unclaimed property program instead. Our page on [finding unclaimed property for an estate](/learn/illinois/finding-unclaimed-property-for-an-illinois-estate) covers searches. A relative who appears later should speak with an attorney and the court about reopening the matter, since time limits can apply.

## What if you are a possible heir who has just heard?

Contact the circuit clerk in the county where the person lived and ask whether an estate is open. Bring a family tree with names, dates of birth and death, and copies of records that show how you are related. Our page on [proving heirship](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois) lists what courts accept. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) can help list what is likely part of the estate.

## How can a person with no family avoid all this?

Sign a will. A will can name a friend, a caregiver, a charity or a distant cousin, and an executor of the person's choice. Beneficiary forms and a transfer on death instrument can also direct accounts and a house. See [estate planning with no heirs or family nearby](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-with-no-heirs-or-family-nearby) and [what a single person should sign](/learn/illinois/no-spouse-no-children).

## What are common mistakes when someone dies with no apparent family?

Neighbors sometimes remove belongings or sell a car to "help." That can expose them to claims later, since the property belongs to the estate. Landlords sometimes dispose of a tenant's things before the court is involved. Banks may freeze accounts until they see letters from the court. The better course is to secure the home, keep a list of what is there, take photos and call the county coroner and circuit clerk. Keep receipts if you pay for the burial, because the estate can repay reasonable funeral costs when there are assets.

Also avoid assuming that no family means no heirs. A cousin in another state may be an heir, and a court may take months to find the person. Patience is part of the process, and the court will require notice before any property is sent to the county or state.

If the person left a pet, a vehicle or perishable items, tell the administrator or the county right away. Small steps like feeding a pet and moving a car off a street can prevent costs that the estate would otherwise have to cover.
