Your father died last month. Since then a hospital bill, two card statements and a collection letter have arrived, and one caller said the family "needs to resolve this balance." You wonder whether his debts are now yours.

For most Illinois families the answer is reassuring. This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), goes through a parent's debts one type at a time. For each, it says who owes the money after the death, and it covers the few situations where a child really does become liable. If your situation is already tangled, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-do-you-inherit-your-parents-debt-in-illinois&topic=probate) to go through the bills with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## The short version

- Your parent's debts belong to your parent's estate, not to you.
- The estate pays valid claims in the order Illinois law sets (755 ILCS 5/18-10). If the money runs out, most remaining creditors go unpaid.
- You owe personally only for a debt you signed for yourself, a joint account, a nursing home promise you made from your own funds, or estate property you took before creditors were dealt with.
- A mortgage or car loan stays with the property. You can keep the property by keeping up the payments, but you are not personally liable for the loan.
- When a collector calls, give the executor's name and say you are not responsible for the account.

## Why don't children inherit a parent's debt?

Because a debt is a promise the borrower made, and you did not make it. When your parent dies, everything they owned in their own name becomes their estate. The executor (or administrator, if there was no will) gathers that property, notifies creditors, and pays valid claims before anyone inherits. Our page on [credit card debt after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois) shows the full payment order and the notice rules, and [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) walks through how a claim is filed, allowed or disputed.

What heirs receive is what is left after that. If the debts are larger than the estate, the estate is insolvent, and the heirs receive nothing, but they are not asked to make up the gap. Unpaid claims are cut off two years after the death at the outside, whether or not an estate was ever opened (755 ILCS 5/18-12).

Serving as executor does not change this. An executor risks personal liability only for their own mistakes, such as paying debts out of order; see [executor personal liability in Illinois](/learn/illinois/executor-personal-liability-in-illinois).

## Debt by debt: who owes it after death?

Here is how the common debts sort out for an adult child.

| Debt | Who owes it after death | When a child can owe it personally |
|---|---|---|
| Credit card in the parent's name | The estate, in the last payment class | The child was a joint account holder or co-signer |
| Credit card where the child was an authorized user | The estate | Usually never, as long as the child stops using the card |
| Hospital and doctor bills | The estate; the final year of medical bills ranks fourth (755 ILCS 5/18-10) | The child signed a personal promise to pay |
| Nursing home bill | The estate, in the same medical class | The child signed as guarantor or as personally liable |
| Medicaid estate recovery (HFS) | The probate estate only | HFS says heirs are not personally liable |
| Mortgage | Secured by the house | Only if the child co-signed or later takes over the loan; otherwise the house, not the child, answers |
| Car loan | Secured by the car | Only if the child co-signed |
| Federal student loans | Discharged at the borrower's death | Not applicable |
| Private student loan the parent co-signed for someone else | Depends on the loan contract; may be a claim against the estate | Not the child, unless the child also signed |

The mortgage surprises people most. It stays attached to the house but does not become your personal debt. The details, including the federal rule that generally stops a lender from calling the loan just because a house passed to a child, are on [what happens to a mortgage, car loan and student loans after death](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-car-loan-and-student-loans-after-death).

## When can a child end up owing?

In a few situations, each turning on something you did yourself.

**You co-signed.** A co-signer promised to repay the loan, and that promise survives the borrower's death. The lender can come to you for the full balance.

**You shared the account.** A joint credit card or joint line of credit makes each holder responsible for the balance. Shared bank accounts work differently: Illinois lets a bank pay a joint deposit to a surviving holder (765 ILCS 1005/2), so the money may come to you. Whether a creditor of your parent can reach that money is a separate, fact-specific question. Our page on [payable-on-death and joint bank accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) explains how those accounts pass.

**You signed a nursing home agreement as a guarantor.** Federal rules generally bar a nursing home that takes Medicare or Medicaid from requiring a family member to personally promise payment as a condition of admission. That rule does not undo a promise you chose to sign. If the form said you were "personally liable," "jointly and severally liable" or a "guarantor," the facility may pursue you. Signing only as a contact or as your parent's representative is different. Our page on the [nursing home 'responsible party' signature](/learn/illinois/hospital-no-power-of-attorney) explains the wording, and [can a nursing home make my children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) covers the federal rule and the broader subject of state support laws.

**You took estate property before the creditors were dealt with.** If you moved your parent's cash into your account or collected under a small estate affidavit and handed the money out, creditors may still reach it. Under 755 ILCS 5/18-12, a creditor with a claim that is not barred can reach a person who received a distribution, but only up to the amount needed so that person ends up with what they would have received had the claim been paid. Read the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) page before signing one, because the person who signs takes on duties to the estate's creditors.

## What about Medicaid estate recovery?

If your parent received Medicaid long-term care, the Illinois Department of Healthcare and Family Services (HFS) may file a claim against the estate. According to HFS, heirs are not expected to pay that claim from their own money, and if the estate cannot cover it, family members do not owe the rest. Recovery is deferred while a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child survives, and for deaths on or after July 1, 2022, the first $25,000 of the estate is protected.

HFS treats the estate as property subject to probate, and it does not collect against life insurance with a named beneficiary or accounts payable on death to someone else. The full rules, including the hardship waiver, are on our [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) page.

A letter from HFS is about your parent's estate, not about you. Paying the claim from other money to keep the house is a choice, not an obligation.

## What should you say to a debt collector?

Say little, and do not agree that you owe anything. A collector may contact relatives to find out who handles the estate, but federal rules limit what collectors can say to family members, and a collector should not suggest you owe a debt you do not. A short script works:

> "My father has died. I am not responsible for his debts. Please send any claim in writing to the executor of his estate, [name], at [address]. Do not call me about this account again."

Then:

1. **Log every call:** date, company, caller, amount.
2. **Ask for the claim in writing**, including the original creditor, the account number and any document you supposedly signed.
3. **Do not pay anything, even a small "good faith" amount,** until you know whether the debt is yours. A payment from your own money is generally hard to get back.
4. **If the collector says you co-signed,** ask for the signed agreement, and talk to an attorney before negotiating.
5. **Send estate debts to the executor.** The executor decides which claims are valid and pays them in order. The free [letter to creditors after a death template](/free/letter-to-creditors-after-a-death-template) gives the executor a ready form.

The national overview in [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) has more on insolvent estates.

## A worked example

Example only. Lorraine, 84, of Joliet, dies a widow. She leaves a house worth $210,000 with a $60,000 mortgage, a checking account with $7,000, a credit card balance of $14,000, a $9,500 hospital bill from her last month, and an unpaid nursing home balance of $6,000. Her two children are Paul and Anita. Years ago Anita was a joint holder on a second card, which now carries $3,200.

Here is who owes what:

- **The $14,000 card in Lorraine's name.** Her estate. Paul and Anita owe nothing.
- **The $3,200 joint card.** Anita owes it in her own right. The issuer can also file against the estate.
- **The hospital and nursing home bills.** Her estate, in the medical class, ahead of the credit cards. When Lorraine moved into the facility, Paul signed the admission packet. He signed as "representative, resident's funds to pay," not as a guarantor, so he does not owe the $6,000 personally.
- **The mortgage.** It stays on the house. If Paul and Anita want to keep the house, the estate or they must keep paying, or they refinance. If they sell, the loan is paid from the sale.

The executor sells the house, the sale pays off the mortgage, the claims are paid by class, and Paul and Anita split what remains. Apart from Anita's joint card, neither spends their own money on their mother's debts. Our page on [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois) covers the choices about the house itself.

## What can parents do now to protect their children?

Most of the risk comes from signatures. A parent who needs help with bills can sign a power of attorney for property instead of adding a child to accounts; see [how to get power of attorney for a parent in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-get-power-of-attorney-for-a-parent-in-illinois) for the steps. Beneficiary designations on life insurance and accounts pass outside the probate estate, which is why [what creditors can take from a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits) is worth reading before a parent moves money around.
