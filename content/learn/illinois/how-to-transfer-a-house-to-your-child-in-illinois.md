You want your house to end up with your son or daughter. Someone suggests a quitclaim deed, a neighbor swears by a trust, and your will already names the kids. Each works, but at different times and with different side effects.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), lines up the six common ways to transfer a house to a child in Illinois and compares them on the same five points: when the child gets title, who controls the house, the tax basis, creditor and Medicaid risk, and probate. If you would like an Illinois estate planning attorney to look at your deed and family before you pick one, you can [book a consultation about a will or trust](/book?from=learn-how-to-transfer-a-house-to-your-child-in-illinois&topic=will-or-trust).

## What are the ways to transfer a house to a child in Illinois?

There are six, and they sort into two groups by timing.

**Transfers that happen now:**

1. **Deed the house to your child.** You record a new deed, often a quitclaim, naming your child as owner.
2. **Add your child as a joint tenant.** You and your child both own the house, and the survivor takes it all. In Illinois the deed must say "not in tenancy in common but in joint tenancy" or the survivorship feature does not apply (765 ILCS 1005/1), as the page on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains.
3. **Sign a life estate deed.** You keep the right to live in the house for life, and your child receives the remainder, which becomes theirs when you die.

**Transfers that happen at your death:**

4. **A transfer on death instrument (TODI).** You record a document naming your child, and title passes at your death (755 ILCS 27).
5. **A living trust.** You deed the house to yourself as trustee, and the successor trustee transfers it after your death.
6. **A will.** Your will leaves the house to your child through probate.

## How do the six options compare?

| Option | When the child gets title | Who controls the house | Child's tax basis | Creditor and Medicaid risk | Probate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deed now | When the deed is recorded | The child | Your original basis carries over | Child's creditors and divorce can reach it; a gift in the 60-month look-back | None for the house |
| Add as joint tenant | A share now, the rest at your death | Both of you; selling needs the child's signature | Carryover on the gifted share; step-up on your share | Child's share exposed; a gift in the look-back | None if the deed has survivorship wording |
| TODI | At your death | You, fully | Generally stepped up | Not a lifetime gift; answers to estate claims after death (755 ILCS 27/85) | None if recorded before death |
| Living trust | When the trustee transfers it after your death | You as trustee, then the successor trustee | Generally stepped up | Counts for Medicaid eligibility; trust answers to claims | None if the house is deeded in |
| Will | After probate | You, then the executor | Generally stepped up | In the probate estate, where creditors and HFS can claim | Yes |
| Life estate | Remainder now, possession at your death | You live there; a sale needs every remainder owner | Generally stepped up | Child's remainder exposed; a gift in the look-back | None for the house |

The life estate row comes from the general rules described in [life estate deed vs irrevocable trust](/learn/elder-care/life-estate-deed-vs-irrevocable-trust-for-medicaid). Illinois's Medicaid agency has not settled on its plain-language pages how it treats a life estate in estate recovery, so ask an attorney about your specific deed.

## Should I deed it now or at death?

At death, in most families. The death-time tools let you keep, sell or redirect the house. The lifetime tools hand your child ownership you generally cannot take back without the child's signature.

A deed now is a gift. In 2026 the federal gift tax annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient, so a house or a share worth more than that usually means filing Form 709, generally with no tax due because the gift counts against the $15,000,000 lifetime exclusion. The page on [Form 709 and gift tax returns](/learn/estate-tax/form-709-gift-tax-return-gift-splitting-and-net-gifts) covers the filing. The bigger costs are the basis and Medicaid effects below, which [giving your house to your child now](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs) also walks through.

Among the death-time tools, a TODI is one recorded document, while a trust can also manage the house if you become ill. The page on [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house) compares those two, and [will vs trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/will-vs-trust-in-illinois) covers the will-plus-TODI middle route. If the house should go to only one of your children, read [how to leave the house to one child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-leave-the-house-to-one-child-in-illinois).

## What about capital gains?

Capital gains tax applies when the child sells, and it is measured from the child's basis. A house received at death generally takes a basis equal to its value on the date of death, which is the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). That applies to a house passing by will, by TODI or from a revocable trust, and generally to a house subject to a life estate you kept.

A lifetime gift does not step up: your child takes your original cost plus improvements. With joint tenancy, only your share steps up at death. These are federal rules that Illinois law does not change.

## Can I keep living there?

Yes with five of the six options, but on different footing.

- **TODI, living trust or will:** you still own or control the house.
- **Life estate:** you keep a legal right to live in or rent the house for life.
- **Joint tenancy:** you remain an owner, sharing the house with your child and their creditors.
- **Deed now:** you no longer own it. You live there with your child's permission, and a judgment or divorce against your child can affect your home.

## What does a quitclaim do?

A quitclaim deed passes whatever interest the signer holds, without any promise that the title is good. It is a form of deed, not a separate strategy. A quitclaim from you to your child is a "deed now" transfer with every consequence in that row. A quitclaim from you to yourself as trustee is the usual way to fund a living trust, and it changes nothing about who benefits. The page on [types of deeds](/learn/property-and-assets/types-of-deeds-quitclaim-warranty-grant-and-trustee-deeds) compares quitclaim, warranty and trustee deeds.

Any deed is signed, notarized and recorded with the county recorder, which may ask for a transfer declaration or exemption statement. The trust deed steps are in [how to put your house in a living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-put-your-house-in-a-living-trust-in-illinois).

## Does it affect Medicaid?

It can, in two ways. First, eligibility: Illinois reviews transfers made in the 60 months before a long-term care Medicaid application. A deed to an adult child, a joint tenancy share or a life estate remainder is generally a gift that can cause a penalty, and the penalty starts only once the parent is in care and otherwise eligible. Transfers of the home to a spouse, a child under 21, a blind or disabled child, or a caregiving child or sibling in listed cases are allowable. The page on [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility and planning](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) lists them, and the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) shows where the window falls.

Second, estate recovery after death. HFS can claim against the probate estate, so a house passing by will is within reach unless a spouse, a child under 21 or a blind or disabled child survives. A TODI and a revocable trust are generally not lifetime gifts, but a revocable trust still counts for eligibility, and how HFS treats either one in recovery is not settled. See [whether creditors or Medicaid can reach a TODI house](/learn/illinois/todi-creditors-and-medicaid-in-illinois), [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) and [how to protect your house from a nursing home in Illinois](/learn/illinois/protecting-your-house-from-a-nursing-home-in-illinois).

## Worked example: one house, six outcomes

This is an invented example with illustrative numbers. Walter, 74, a widower in Springfield, bought his house in 1991 for $70,000, and it is worth about $300,000 today. He wants it to go to his daughter Renee. He has modest savings and no current health crisis. Assume he dies in six years, when the house is worth $330,000, and Renee sells it soon after for that price.

- **Deed now.** Renee owns the house today and takes Walter's $70,000 basis, so her gain on the sale is about $260,000. Walter likely files a gift tax return, a Medicaid application within 60 months would review the gift, and a divorce in year three would pull the house into her case.
- **Joint tenancy.** Renee's gifted half keeps a $35,000 basis. Walter's half steps up to $165,000 at his death. Her total basis is about $200,000, so her gain is about $130,000. She owns the whole house at his death without probate.
- **Life estate.** Walter lives there for life, and Renee's remainder becomes full ownership at his death, generally with a basis near $330,000. But her creditors can reach the remainder, the deed starts the look-back, and Walter cannot sell without her.
- **TODI.** Walter records a TODI naming Renee, with her son as a backup. He keeps full ownership. At his death Renee's basis is about $330,000, so her gain on the sale is close to zero, and no probate is needed.
- **Living trust.** The same tax result as the TODI, and Renee as successor trustee can also manage the house if Walter becomes ill.
- **Will.** The same basis as the TODI, but the house goes through an Illinois probate case, with a creditor claims period and a six-month contest window (755 ILCS 5/18-3, 5/8-1).

Walter picks the TODI plus a [property power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) naming Renee, because he wants the step-up and control and needs no trust terms.

## What steps should you take before you transfer the house?

1. **Pull your current deed** to see how the house is titled now.
2. **Decide the timing goal:** help now, or a clean transfer at death.
3. **Estimate the basis difference** with a tax preparer.
4. **Check the Medicaid window.** If long-term care within five years is possible, talk to an attorney before signing any deed.
5. **Name backups.** A TODI or trust should say who takes the house if your child dies first; see [TODI beneficiaries and contingent beneficiaries](/learn/illinois/todi-beneficiaries-and-contingent-beneficiaries-in-illinois).
6. **Sign and record the right document,** and confirm your property tax exemptions with the county assessor afterward.
