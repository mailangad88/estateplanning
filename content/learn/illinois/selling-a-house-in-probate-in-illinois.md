Your mother's house is in her name, the lawn needs mowing, and a neighbor has already asked if it is for sale. You are the executor, or about to be, and you want to know whether you can sell it now or whether the court has to bless every step. In Illinois the answer turns on one choice made at the start of the case.

This page covers the sale itself: who signs, when a judge must approve, the executor's deed and what a title company will want to see. For the whole case from filing to closing, read [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process). The rest of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the surrounding topics. For the broader question of heirs and houses, see [can heirs sell the house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate).

## Who can sell a house in probate in Illinois?

Only the representative the court appoints. Until letters of office issue, the house is titled in a dead person's name and nobody can sign a contract or a deed for it. A named executor does have some power before letters, but it is narrow: carrying out the burial, paying necessary funeral charges and preserving the estate (755 ILCS 5/6-14). Selling the house is not on that list.

Once the will is admitted, the letters say whether the executor is independent or supervised. That label decides what happens next.

| | Independent administration | Supervised administration |
|---|---|---|
| Authority to sell | Executor may sell at public or private sale, for cash or on credit (755 ILCS 5/28-8) | Executor needs court permission, and the sale must be necessary for proper administration (755 ILCS 5/20-4) |
| Court filing before the sale | None required by the statute | Petition to the court that issued letters, with the proposed contract attached (755 ILCS 5/20-5) |
| Court approval of the price | No | Yes, by order, or after a verified report of sale (755 ILCS 5/20-9) |
| Deed | Signed by the executor | Delivered within 30 days after approval (755 ILCS 5/20-10) |

Most Illinois estates are independent, so the left column is the usual one. Our page on [independent vs. supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains how an estate ends up in each column and how an unhappy heir can push it from one to the other.

## When can an independent executor sell without asking the court?

Almost always, with two exceptions. Section 28-8 lets an independent representative exercise the estate's powers without a court order if the representative acts reasonably and in the estate's best interests. The will can limit those powers, so read it for any language about real estate.

The second exception matters more often. If the will leaves the house to a named person, the executor cannot sell or mortgage it without that person's written consent (755 ILCS 5/28-8). Take Gloria, who leaves her bungalow to her daughter Tanya and everything else to her son. The estate has $6,000 in the bank and $31,000 in debts. Tanya's gift may still be reached to pay claims, but the executor needs her signature or a court order first. Without either, a buyer's title company will balk.

## When does a judge have to approve the sale?

In a supervised estate, every time. The representative petitions the court, describes the property, its approximate value and known liens, and attaches the sale contract. People whose rights are affected, such as lienholders, are named as defendants and served, and the court appoints a guardian ad litem for a minor or a person with a disability who has no guardian (755 ILCS 5/20-5).

The court then sets the terms. It can approve a private sale or order a public one, and a public sale has a notice schedule of its own. If the court orders an appraisal, the sale cannot go below two-thirds of the appraised value (755 ILCS 5/20-8). If the representative sold under a contract the court already authorized, no separate report is needed. Otherwise a verified report of sale is filed, notice goes to those who have appeared, and the judge confirms the sale or orders a resale (755 ILCS 5/20-9).

Even in an independent estate, a judge can end up involved. An heir can ask for a hearing on one sale under Section 28-5, and the executor can ask for instructions. If either happens, the court decides the point.

## What does the executor's deed do?

It moves title from the estate to the buyer. The executor signs in that capacity, and the deed identifies the estate and the court case. For the buyer, the comfort is Section 28-9. A person dealing with an independent representative has no duty to investigate the representative's powers or track the money. A good-faith purchaser who pays value takes title free of the rights of others interested in the estate, even if the representative acted improperly.

Section 28-8 also lets the representative record and deliver a release of the estate's interest to confirm an heir's or legatee's title. That is the tool used when the house is not sold but passes to the heir named in the will.

In Cook County, the clerk's office describes the last step this way: the attorney submits an order of release for the judge to sign, and you take the signed order and the letters to the Recorder of Deeds, who changes the name on the deed. Other counties have their own recording offices and habits.

## What will the title company ask for?

Expect a request list before closing day. Practices vary by company, and the statute does not prescribe the list, but these items come up again and again:

1. A certified death certificate.
2. Letters of office, often certified within a recent window.
3. The will, or a copy of the order admitting it.
4. Proof the estate is independent, or the court order approving a supervised sale.
5. Payoff letters for the mortgage and any liens.
6. A signed consent from the legatee if the house was specifically left to someone.

Ask the title officer for the list the day you sign the listing agreement. A surprise at closing, such as letters that are too old, can cost a week.

## Does the homestead affect the sale?

Sometimes. If the decedent's spouse or young children are entitled to a homestead interest, the court can set it off or sell the whole property and pay the homestead value to the person entitled to it. Our page on the [Illinois homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death) covers the $15,000 figure and the exceptions. A surviving spouse's award and other rights are in [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death).

## Where does the money go after closing?

Not to the heirs yet. Here is an example with invented numbers, for illustration only. Dennis, the independent executor for his father, sells the family house for $310,000. The mortgage payoff is $92,000. The title company pays off the lender and the closing costs from the proceeds, and wires the remainder to the estate account Dennis opened. Funeral and administration costs sit in the first class of claims, ahead of credit cards. Dennis waits for the claims deadline set by the published notice before dividing what is left. Our page on [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) explains that deadline, and [closing the estate](/learn/illinois/closing-an-illinois-probate-estate-final-account-and-discharge) explains the last report.

The tax picture is usually kinder than families fear, because most inherited property takes a new tax basis at death. See [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) and the guide on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## Mistakes our attorneys see

- Signing a purchase contract as an heir before letters issue. The buyer's lender or title company finds out and the deal stalls.
- Forgetting the mortgage. Payments continue during probate, and the lender must be told. See [what happens to the mortgage when the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).
- Selling a house the will left to a named person, without that person's written consent.
- Leaving the house empty without telling the insurer.
