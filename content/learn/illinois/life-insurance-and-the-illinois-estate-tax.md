Life insurance feels like the opposite of a tax problem. It pays a family tax-free income when someone dies. That is true for income tax. For the estate tax, a large policy that you own can be the amount that takes an Illinois estate from comfortable to taxable.

This page looks at when a policy matters for the $4 million Illinois line, who should own it, and what to do if you already hold one. It is general education. For a policy-by-policy look at trusts, see our guide to [irrevocable life insurance trusts](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust). The overall [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) links the other taxes.

## Why does life insurance count toward the Illinois estate tax?

Because the Illinois tax starts from the federal gross estate, and federal law includes life insurance on your life when you hold any "incidents of ownership" at death (26 U.S.C. 2042). Illinois follows the federal definition in 35 ILCS 405. An incident of ownership is the right to change the beneficiary, borrow against the policy, cancel it, or assign it. Owning the policy outright is the clearest case.

The death benefit is the number that counts, not the cash value. A term policy with no cash value and a $1 million benefit adds $1 million to the estate if you own it.

## Whose estate does the policy land in?

| Who owns the policy | Who is the beneficiary | In the insured's Illinois estate? |
|---|---|---|
| The insured | Spouse | Yes, but the marital deduction usually offsets it at that death |
| The insured | Children | Yes, taxable |
| The insured's spouse | Children | No, but it may be in the spouse's estate if the spouse dies first |
| An adult child | The child or another family member | No |
| An irrevocable trust, created more than three years before death | Trust beneficiaries | No |
| An irrevocable trust, created less than three years before death by transfer of an existing policy | Trust beneficiaries | Yes, under the three-year rule (26 U.S.C. 2035) |
| Employer group term, rights not assigned | Family | Yes |

The three-year rule only applies to a transfer of an existing policy. When the trust buys a new policy on the insured directly, the proceeds generally stay out, though the details need an attorney.

## How much can a policy cost in Illinois estate tax?

Take Leon, 63, a widowed engineer in Schaumburg. His assets are a house worth $700,000, retirement accounts of $1.8 million and brokerage accounts of $900,000. That makes $3.4 million. His employer-paid term policy pays $1 million, and he can name the beneficiary. Leon's estate is $4.4 million.

The estate is his alone, so the estimate is about $114,000 of Illinois tax: $400,000 over the line times roughly 28.6 percent. This is our own arithmetic, so use the Attorney General's calculator for an actual number. Our page of [Illinois estate tax worked examples](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax-worked-examples) shows the full formula.

If Leon moves the policy into an irrevocable trust and survives three years, the estate returns to $3.4 million and the tax is zero. If he dies in year two, the policy is pulled back in.

## What steps should you take if you own a large policy?

1. Add up your estate, including every policy where you can name or change the beneficiary.
2. Compare the total with $4,000,000. If you are comfortably under, leave it alone.
3. If you are near or over, find out whether the policy can be given to an irrevocable trust or to an adult family member.
4. If you transfer an existing policy, record the date and calendar the three-year anniversary.
5. Fund the trust's premiums with gifts, and follow the trust's notice rules. Gifts under the annual exclusion of $19,000 per beneficiary in 2026 are not taxable gifts.
6. Update your beneficiary forms so nothing still points to you or your estate.

Our guide to [transferring an existing life insurance policy to an ILIT](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-transfer-an-existing-life-insurance-policy-to-an-ilit) goes through the paperwork. Check that the trust, not you, is the owner and beneficiary on the insurer's own records. Many failed plans have a signed trust and a policy that was never actually re-titled.

## Is a trust always needed?

No. A smaller step can be enough. An adult child can own a policy on a parent, as long as the parent has no control, and premium gifts are made with care. A policy can also be dropped if the need has passed. People sometimes buy $1 million of coverage for a mortgage that has since been paid off, and the policy becomes a tax cost with no purpose. A review of the [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) can help you decide whether the amount is still right.

Married couples should also think about a policy on the first spouse's life that is owned by the survivor, or a second-to-die policy owned by a trust. Both can be used to pay an estate tax bill at the second death. Remember that the surviving spouse's estate sits alone against the $4 million threshold, as explained in [credit shelter trusts and the Illinois QTIP election](/learn/illinois/illinois-qtip-election-and-credit-shelter-trusts).

## What about beneficiaries and income tax?

Life insurance paid to a named beneficiary is generally not income taxable to the beneficiary, and it passes outside probate, so Illinois courts are not involved. Read [do beneficiaries pay taxes on what they receive](/learn/beneficiary-designations/do-beneficiaries-pay-taxes-on-what-they-receive) for the income tax picture. The estate tax is a separate tax on the same money, paid by the estate, usually before the beneficiaries see the payout. Who bears it depends on the will or trust, as covered in [who pays the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/who-pays-the-estate-tax-executor-or-heirs).

One practical risk: if the estate owes tax and the insurance is paid directly to a child, the child gets the money and the executor may have no cash to pay the tax. Plan where the liquid funds will come from.

## What if you cannot or do not want to give up the policy?

Some people need to keep control of a policy, for example a permanent policy that funds a business buy-sell agreement or a policy with a loan against it. In that case, the planning moves to the other side of the equation. Options include paying down the estate with lifetime gifts, using a credit shelter trust for a married couple, leaving a larger share to charity, or accepting the tax and making sure there is cash to pay it.

Cash matters. The Illinois tax is due in nine months, and a family that owns a house, a farm and a policy paid straight to a child may have nothing liquid in the estate. Some families own a second-to-die policy in a trust to pay the tax bill at the survivor's death. That puts the money where it is needed, but it only works if the trust owns the policy from the start. See [Illinois Form 700](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-700-estate-tax-return) for the payment deadline the executor faces.
