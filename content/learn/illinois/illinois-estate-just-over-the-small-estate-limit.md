Your father left about $162,000. The family hoped to use the small estate affidavit and skip court. Then the bank statement and the brokerage statement added up to a little more than the limit. This page is about that frustrating gap.

The rules for the affidavit itself are in our [Illinois small estate affidavit overview](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit). Here we focus on near-limit estates: how to count honestly, what the numbers can legitimately change, and what to do when the estate is truly over. The full set of Illinois pages starts at our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois). This is general education, not legal advice.

## Is the limit really $150,000?

As of 2026 the personal estate must not exceed $150,000 under 755 ILCS 5/25-1, not counting motor vehicles registered with the Secretary of State. Amendments in 2025 changed the limit, and one later act restates the figure with a January 1, 2027 effective date. Check the current text of the statute or ask the circuit clerk before relying on a number. The [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) gives a quick estimate.

## What counts toward the limit, and what does not?

Count the assets that would go through probate: accounts in the decedent's name alone with no beneficiary, individual stocks, the final paycheck, tax refunds, tools, furniture and similar property. Do not count these.

- Accounts with a payable-on-death beneficiary or a living joint owner. See [POD and joint accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts).
- Life insurance and retirement accounts that name a living beneficiary.
- A house passing by a recorded transfer on death instrument or joint tenancy. See [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).
- Registered motor vehicles, which the statute sets apart.

Our article on [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains why. Many "over the limit" estates are actually under it once these items come out.

## How do I check the numbers step by step?

1. List every asset with the title shown on the statement.
2. Mark each as probate or non-probate, using the title and any beneficiary form.
3. Take the value on the date of death, not today's value.
4. Add up only the probate items, leaving out registered vehicles.
5. Compare the total with the current limit.
6. If you are within about 10 percent of the limit, ask a lawyer to review before you sign anything.

Use the [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) for steps 1 to 2.

## What if the estate is over the limit?

Then the affidavit is off the table. Here is how the main options compare.

| Option | When it fits | Main downside |
|---|---|---|
| Small estate affidavit | Personal estate within limit, no real estate, no dispute | Not available once over |
| Independent administration | Over the limit, heirs agree, no big dispute | Court case, fees, creditor notice |
| Supervised administration | Disputes, minors with large shares, or a court-ordered need | More hearings and cost |
| Wait on a disputed asset | A single asset's value is truly uncertain | Delays the whole matter |

Most families land on independent administration. Our article on [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains the difference, and our [probate costs article](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) puts dollar figures on it.

## Can you lawfully lower the estate before the affidavit?

Only by real, earlier planning, not by moves after the death. Before death, the owner could have named a POD beneficiary or recorded a transfer on death instrument. After death, no one may change how an asset is owned to fit under the limit. Splitting accounts between heirs, moving money to a child's name or pricing an asset below its worth turns a minor annoyance into possible personal liability for the signer.

Legitimate ways the number moves: a payable-on-death form that turns out to exist, a joint owner who turns out to be on the account, a car that is handled separately, or a debt that reduces the amount of a particular account. If you can document it, it counts.

## A worked example

Gloria, 58, lists her mother's assets. The checking account shows $61,000, a brokerage account shows $88,500, and a final tax refund is $2,300. That is $151,800, just $1,800 over. A closer look shows the brokerage account was opened with a transfer on death registration to Gloria and her brother. It is not a probate asset at all. Without it, the personal estate is $63,300, and the affidavit works for the rest.

Now change one fact. Suppose the brokerage account had no beneficiary. The estate is $151,800, over the limit by $1,800. Gloria cannot sign. She and her brother ask a lawyer about independent administration, which keeps the court's role small.

## What about the spouse's and children's awards?

A court estate also lets the surviving spouse and minor children claim awards for support. The family allowance article in our probate cluster, [family allowance and homestead in probate](/learn/probate/family-allowance-and-homestead-in-probate), explains how that works. Those awards are one reason a court case, though slower, can help a surviving spouse.

## What should you ask a lawyer when the numbers are close?

A short meeting is worth it when the total falls within about $15,000 of the limit in either direction. Bring the date-of-death statements, the titles and beneficiary forms for each account, and the funeral bill. Ask these questions.

- Which items are truly probate assets, based on how each is titled?
- Are any values uncertain, and would a written appraisal settle them?
- If the estate is over, is independent administration open to us, or does a minor heir or a dispute call for supervision?
- Will a court case allow the spouse's or children's awards that the affidavit does not?
- What would the court route cost, and how long until an heir sees money?

Write down the answers. If the lawyer concludes the estate is under the limit, ask for that opinion in a short letter you can show a bank that doubts the numbers. If the lawyer concludes it is over, you have saved the family from signing a sworn statement that could later be called false.

## What records should you keep either way?

Whether you use the affidavit or go to court, keep a one-page schedule of assets with the date-of-death value, the source of each value, and the title of each account. Attach the statements. If anyone later asks why you chose one route, the schedule answers it. If a creditor or an heir questions a figure, you can show your work in minutes instead of weeks.
