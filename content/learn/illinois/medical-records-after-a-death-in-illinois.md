After a death, families often have questions that only the medical chart can answer. What was the diagnosis? Was a treatment delayed? Is there a record of a fall that may matter for an estate claim? You may also need records for life insurance, a will contest or an insurance dispute.

Privacy rules do not end at death. This page explains who can ask for the records in Illinois, what to send, and what to do when no estate has been opened yet. The [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links the court steps that give someone authority.

## Does medical privacy end at death?

No. Under HIPAA, a deceased person's health information stays protected for 50 years after death. The federal rule treats the executor, administrator or another person with authority under state law to act for the deceased or the estate as the "personal representative" (45 CFR 164.502(g)(4)). That person generally has the same access that the patient had.

Others may get limited information. A provider can share information with family members involved in the patient's care, relevant to that involvement, unless the patient objected earlier. That helps with questions, but it is not a right to a full copy.

## Who can request the records in Illinois?

| Person | Likely access | What the provider may ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Executor named in a will, with letters | Full chart | Death certificate, letters of office, ID |
| Administrator appointed by the court | Full chart | Death certificate, letters, ID |
| Heir, no estate open | Varies by provider | Proof of relationship; some ask for an affidavit |
| Person who signed the small estate affidavit | Varies | Copy of the affidavit and ID |
| Spouse or child who helped with care | Conversation, limited summary | Identification; a full copy is not assured |
| Insurance company, with a signed release | Specific records | Release signed by the personal representative |
| Attorney for the estate | Full chart on the estate's behalf | Letters and engagement letter |

Letters of office are issued by the circuit court in a probate case. See [Illinois letters of administration](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) and our [probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process). Whether a small estate affidavit is enough for a medical records request depends on the provider. The article on the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) explains what it can and cannot do.

## How do you request the records, step by step?

1. List every provider: hospital, primary doctor, each doctor's office, nursing home, hospice, home health agency, pharmacy and lab.
2. Order several certified copies of the death certificate. The article on [Illinois death certificates](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph) explains where.
3. Call each records office, often called health information management, and ask what they require from the executor or administrator.
4. Prepare a written request that names the patient, date of birth, dates of care and the records wanted, such as the full chart, imaging and billing.
5. Attach a copy of your ID, the death certificate and the letters of office, or the document the provider accepts.
6. Say how you want the records sent: paper, disk or secure portal.
7. Keep a log with the date sent, the name of the person who received it and the 30-day mark.
8. Pay a reasonable copying fee if asked, and request an itemized receipt.
9. Follow up in writing if you do not hear back. Escalate to the privacy officer if needed.

## What if no estate has been opened?

Ask the provider what it accepts. Many hospitals will release records to a next of kin who signs an affidavit and shows ID, but they are not required to. If the main reason for the records is a claim against a provider, a dispute over the will or a life insurance claim, talk to an attorney before spending money on copies. Opening a probate case may be needed, and it also gives authority to deal with banks, as described in [how to find a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets).

If the patient signed a HIPAA release before death, it generally ends with the patient's death. See our article on [HIPAA authorizations](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization), which explains the "what happens after death" question.

## Which records are harder to get?

Mental health records are covered by the Illinois Mental Health and Developmental Disabilities Confidentiality Act (740 ILCS 110), which has its own consent rules. Substance use disorder treatment records can also carry extra federal protection. Ask the provider what consent it needs after death. An attorney can help if the provider refuses.

Autopsy and coroner records follow a different path. In Illinois these are held by the county coroner or medical examiner, and the release rules differ by county. The [Illinois death certificate guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph) covers the official cause of death record.

## What can providers charge?

Illinois law, 735 ILCS 5/8-2001, governs copies and fees for health care records. HIPAA also limits what a provider may charge for access to a reasonable, cost-based fee. Ask for the fee schedule before you pay. Electronic copies are often cheaper.

## Example: Denise requests her mother's records

Denise, 54, is the executor of her mother Alma's estate in Kane County. Alma, 86, died after four months in a nursing home following a hospital stay. Denise gets six certified death certificates and the letters of office from the circuit court. She sends one request to the hospital, one to the nursing home and one to the hospice. Each letter lists the dates of care. The hospital sends the chart in 22 days for a $38 fee. The nursing home takes 31 days and Denise reminds the privacy officer in writing. She needed the records to answer a Medicare billing question, and the [Medicare and medical bills article](/learn/after-a-death/what-happens-to-medicare-and-medical-bills-when-someone-dies) helped her sort it out. You can read about [Kane County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/kane) for local estate steps.

## Why might you need the records?

Knowing the reason helps you ask for the right pages and avoid paying for a full chart you will not read.

- **Insurance claims.** A life insurer may ask for records about the cause of death or a recent diagnosis. Our article on [claiming life insurance, pension and VA survivor benefits](/learn/after-a-death/claiming-life-insurance-pension-and-va-survivor-benefits) describes those claims.
- **Medical bills.** An itemized bill and the records behind it can help you check a charge before the estate pays.
- **Family health history.** Children may want diagnoses and test results that affect their own care.
- **Estate disputes.** In a will contest or capacity question, the records of the months before death can matter. A person with that concern should speak to an attorney before records are requested, because deadlines such as the [six-month will contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) run from the admission of the will.
- **Possible claims.** Wrongful death and survival actions have their own time limits, described in [Illinois wrongful death and survival claims for an estate](/learn/illinois/illinois-wrongful-death-and-survival-claims-for-an-estate).

Ask for specific record types when you can, such as discharge summary, lab results and nursing notes, to speed the response.
