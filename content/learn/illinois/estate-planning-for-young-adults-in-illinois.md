You have a job with a 401(k), a lease or a first condo, maybe a dog, maybe a partner. Estate planning sounds like something for your parents. But Illinois's default rules for a single adult with no children rarely pick the people you would pick.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is for working adults roughly 22 to 35, with no kids or a new partner. If you already know you want help putting it on paper, you can [book a consultation about a will](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-for-young-adults-in-illinois&topic=will). If you are a parent of a teenager about to turn 18, the page you want is [documents an 18-year-old needs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/documents-an-18-year-old-needs-in-illinois).

## What is the short answer for someone in their 20s or 30s?

Four steps cover most people this age, roughly in this order:

- Sign a health care power of attorney and a HIPAA release, so someone you chose can speak to doctors.
- Sign a power of attorney for property, so someone can pay rent, deal with your bank and file your taxes if you cannot.
- Open every beneficiary form you have at work and at your bank, and make sure each one names the right person plus a backup.
- Sign a will once there is a partner, a pet, a cause or property you would not want split between your parents and siblings.

## Do I need a will if I have no kids?

Not always first, but usually sooner than you think. A will controls only property in your name alone with no beneficiary or co-owner: the car, a checking account without a payable on death name, furniture, a final paycheck, maybe a condo.

A will also does three things no beneficiary form can. It names an [executor](/glossary/executor) to close out your affairs. It says who takes your pet. And it can leave property to people the law ignores, such as a partner, a godchild or a friend. Illinois requires the will to be in writing, signed by you as an adult of sound mind, and witnessed by two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-1 and 4-3); the signing steps are on our page about [how to make a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-make-a-will-in-illinois).

If your property is small, your family may skip a full probate case with a [small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit), available when the personal estate, not counting registered vehicles, is $150,000 or less (755 ILCS 5/25-1). It does not change who inherits.

## Who inherits if I am single and die without a will in Illinois?

Your parents and your brothers and sisters, in equal shares, with a surviving parent counted twice (755 ILCS 5/2-1). If a sibling has died, that sibling's children take the sibling's share. A partner you have not married, a roommate, a friend and a charity are not on the list at all.

With two living parents and one sister, the estate splits three ways; with one parent, that parent and the sister take half each. Our page on [having no spouse and no children in Illinois](/learn/illinois/no-spouse-no-children) walks through the rest of the order, and [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) covers every family shape.

Many people this age are fine with their parents inheriting. The trouble is the gaps: no one is named to handle things, and a partner who shares the rent has no claim.

## Do I need a power of attorney in my 20s?

Yes, and for most young adults it matters more than a will. A car crash or serious illness can leave you alive but unable to decide for weeks, and as an adult nobody, including your parents, has automatic authority to sign for you.

Illinois uses two separate statutory forms under the Powers of Attorney Act:

| Form | What it covers | How it is signed | Read more |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Health care power of attorney | Medical decisions when you cannot make them | One adult witness, no notary (755 ILCS 45/4-10) | [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) |
| Power of attorney for property | Bank accounts, bills, taxes, a lease, a car title | A witness and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3) | [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) |
| HIPAA release | Lets named people get your medical information now | Usually the provider's form or your attorney's | [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) |

Without a health care agent, an Illinois hospital uses the Health Care Surrogate Act list. For a single adult with no children, it starts with a parent, then an adult sibling, and reaches a close friend only after every available relative (755 ILCS 40/25); see [who decides under the Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides). For money there is no list at all, and the family may need a guardianship case.

The standard property form does not let your agent make gifts or change beneficiaries unless you grant those powers (755 ILCS 45/3-4).

## Who gets my 401(k) if I die?

Whoever is named on the plan's beneficiary form, not whoever your will names. The same goes for group life insurance, an IRA and a health savings account: the plan pays the person on file.

This is where people this age most often get caught. You named your mom during onboarding at 23 and never looked again. Or you left it blank, and the plan's default sends the money to your estate and through probate. Our article on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) explains how payouts work, including the federal rule that most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited account within 10 years.

Two points matter at this age:

1. **Marriage changes the 401(k) rules.** Under federal law for most private employer plans, a spouse is the beneficiary unless the spouse signs a written waiver witnessed by a notary or plan representative. Our page on [401(k) beneficiaries and spousal consent in Illinois](/learn/illinois/retirement-account-beneficiaries-and-spousal-consent-in-illinois) has the details. An IRA has no federal consent rule.
2. **Old jobs leave old forms.** A 401(k) left at a former employer keeps whatever form you signed there.

The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) walks through each account, and our guide to [changing the beneficiary on a 401(k) or life insurance policy](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-to-change-the-beneficiary-on-a-401k-ira-or-life-insurance-policy) shows the mechanics. Always fill in the [contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary) line as well.

## What about my partner if we are not married?

Without paper, an unmarried partner in Illinois inherits nothing, ranks as a close friend at the hospital, and has no say over the funeral. Illinois does not recognize common-law marriage, so living together for years does not change that. A civil union is treated like marriage under state law (750 ILCS 75/20); see [civil union partners and Illinois estate law](/learn/illinois/civil-union-partners-and-illinois-estate-law).

<!-- visual: whatif id=unmarried-partner-no-will -->

For a partner, the fix is a set of forms that each name them:

- The beneficiary form on your 401(k), life insurance and any payable on death bank account.
- A health care power of attorney and HIPAA release naming your partner as agent.
- A property power of attorney, if you want your partner to handle money for you.
- A will leaving your partner the car, the dog or whatever else you choose.
- A notarized funeral agent form under the Disposition of Remains Act, if you want your partner to decide burial or cremation (755 ILCS 65/5). See the [Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act).

Our page on [unmarried partners in Illinois](/learn/illinois/unmarried-partners) covers deeds and the longer list for established couples. If you are about to marry, read [estate planning for newlyweds in Illinois](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-for-newlyweds-in-illinois), because a spouse changes the defaults.

## How does it play out for one person?

Example. Jordan, 29, rents an apartment in Logan Square with his girlfriend Sam, 30. They have been together three years and share a dog. Jordan owns a paid-off car, a checking account in his name alone, and a 401(k) worth about $48,000. He also has group life insurance through work. At onboarding six years ago he named his mother on both forms. He has no will and no powers of attorney. His father and mother are both living, and he has one brother.

**If Jordan is hurt and in intensive care.** The hospital turns to his parents first, then his brother. Sam counts only as a close friend, at the bottom of the list. No one can reach his bank account to pay his share of the rent.

**If Jordan dies.** The 401(k) and the life insurance go to his mother, as the forms say. The car and checking account are probate property. With no will, they split equally among his mother, father and brother. The family can likely use a small estate affidavit. Sam receives nothing, not even the dog, unless the family agrees.

**What Jordan signs instead.** A health care power of attorney naming Sam, with his brother as backup, plus a HIPAA release. A property power of attorney naming Sam. New beneficiary forms splitting the life insurance between Sam and his mother, with Sam on the 401(k). A short will leaving the car and the dog to Sam and naming his brother as executor. If Jordan and Sam later marry, he revisits all of it.

## What should go on a starter checklist?

- [ ] Health care power of attorney, with a primary and a backup agent
- [ ] HIPAA release naming the same people
- [ ] Power of attorney for property, with only the powers you want to grant
- [ ] 401(k) and any old employer plans: primary and contingent beneficiaries checked online
- [ ] Group life insurance and any personal policy: beneficiaries checked
- [ ] Bank and brokerage accounts: payable on death names added where wanted, as described in [Illinois payable on death and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts)
- [ ] A will naming an executor, a backup, and who takes your pet
- [ ] A short list of accounts and where your passwords live; Illinois lets executors see message content only with your consent or a court order (755 ILCS 70/7), as explained in [Illinois digital assets law](/learn/illinois/illinois-digital-assets-law-for-executors)
- [ ] Copies given to your agents, with the originals somewhere they can find

## What about student loans and other debts?

Debts are paid from what you leave, not by your family. Federal student loans are discharged at death, and heirs do not become personally liable for your debts. A cosigned private loan can be different. See [what happens to a mortgage, car loan and student loans after death](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-car-loan-and-student-loans-after-death) and [do you inherit your parents' debt in Illinois](/learn/illinois/do-you-inherit-your-parents-debt-in-illinois).
