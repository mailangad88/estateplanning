You have been named executor, you expect to give up evenings and weekends for a year, and you want to know what the job pays. The short answer for Illinois is that the pay is "reasonable," and no table tells you what that means in dollars. That leaves room for a fair arrangement, and room for a family argument, so the details are worth getting right.

This page covers how reasonable pay is judged, how to record your work, what the tax cost is, and how heirs can push back. For the full picture of what an Illinois estate costs, see our page on [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs), and for the whole subject in one place, start with our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## How much does an executor get paid in Illinois?

There is no standard number. An Illinois executor may receive reasonable compensation for the services performed, and the amount depends on the facts. We do not list a percentage for Illinois because the state does not set one in the way California or Florida do for their probate fees. A small estate with one bank account and a clear will might justify a modest sum. A larger estate with a business, rental property and an unhappy sibling can justify much more.

Courts and heirs usually look at the same handful of things:

| What is looked at | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Hours and tasks actually done | Paperwork, phone calls, property care, sales, tax filings |
| Size and kinds of assets | A house to sell takes more time than a checking account |
| Difficulty and risk | Disputes, creditor claims, litigation, a complicated tax return |
| Skill the work needed | Running a rental or business is not the same as closing an account |
| Who else was paid for the same work | An attorney or accountant already handled it |
| What the will says | A will may set a fee or say the executor serves without pay |

If the will names a fee, that is the starting point. If the will is silent, the executor and the heirs work from reasonableness, and a court can step in when they cannot agree.

## Is executor pay worth taking?

Often it is not, for a simple tax reason. Executor compensation is taxable income to the executor. An inheritance is generally not income tax to the person who receives it. So when the executor is also an heir, taking $10,000 of pay can cost that person federal income tax and Illinois income tax on the $10,000, while the same $10,000 left in the estate and passed to them as an heir normally arrives without income tax. The estate can usually deduct the pay, but that rarely offsets the loss unless the executor is the only heir in a lower bracket than the estate.

Here is an example with invented numbers. Dolores, 62, is executor of her mother's estate in Peoria. She and her brother Gene split the residue equally. Dolores keeps a time log and reaches 95 hours at the end. If she charges $50 an hour, she would be owed $4,750 and would owe tax on it. The estate pays the amount, and Gene's half of the residue shrinks by $2,375. Dolores keeps part of the net after tax. If instead she waives, the full amount stays in the pot, each sibling receives it as an inheritance, and Dolores has no extra tax. Many families decide the cleaner route is to waive and give the executor a gift outside the estate, or to take the fee because Dolores did far more work than Gene did. Either choice is legitimate as long as it is open.

Pay makes more sense when the executor is not an heir, when the work is large and falls on one person, or when a sibling lives out of state and does nothing. Put a family conversation ahead of the paperwork.

## How do you keep a record that will hold up?

A written log made at the time is the best protection. Do not rebuild it from memory in the last week. Keep the following:

1. **A running time log.** Date, minutes spent, and a plain description: "Called county recorder about deed, 25 min." Use a notebook or a shared spreadsheet.
2. **Receipts for out-of-pocket costs.** Mileage, postage, certified copies and storage. These are reimbursed, not pay. See what [an executor may take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate).
3. **A short note of what was delegated.** If you hired an accountant or a house cleaner, record what they did so no one says you charged for it too.
4. **A proposed number and the reason, sent in writing to the heirs before you pay yourself.** A two-line email works.

## Can an executor also be paid for work that an attorney does?

Not for the same task. Illinois estates commonly have two professionals on the payroll: the attorney and, sometimes, the executor. If a lawyer prepares the inventory, the closing report and the notices, an executor who also bills hours for those items invites an objection. The honest line is that an executor is paid for the executor's own work, such as gathering records, meeting the appraiser, managing a vacant house and signing. A lawyer is paid for legal work. When the same person holds both roles, a written breakdown that separates them is the minimum.

Our page on [how attorney fees are set in Illinois probate](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) covers the other half of the bill.

## What can heirs do if they think the pay is too high?

They can ask questions early and object in the closing process. In an independent administration, the executor mails the heirs an accounting and, later, a closing report. If anyone entitled to notice has not signed a receipt or waiver, the executor mails the report and waits 42 days for an objection before the court discharges the executor (see [closing an Illinois probate estate](/learn/illinois/closing-an-illinois-probate-estate-final-account-and-discharge)). An heir who thinks the fee is excessive should object in that window rather than sign a receipt. Heirs who sign a full receipt, with approval of the fees, give up their right to notice, which is the reason to read before signing.

If the case is supervised, the court is closer to the money and the judge reviews the accounts. The page on [independent and supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains the difference. And if you are an heir who is already worried about how the estate is being handled, [what heirs can do about a slow Illinois executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor) lists steps that apply here too.

A practical point from the table: nearly every fee dispute our attorneys see began with surprise, not with the amount. An executor who tells the family the number and the reasons before taking a dime rarely ends up before a judge.

## What if the executor is a bank or professional?

A bank or trust company that serves as executor charges under its own published fee schedule, and an individual professional, such as an attorney named as executor, generally charges by time. Both are still bound by the reasonable standard. Ask for the schedule in writing before you name an institution in your will. A fee that looks small as a percentage can be large in dollars on a big estate.

## How can you set executor pay in your own will?

You can do it directly. A will can say your executor serves without pay, receives a stated sum, or receives reasonable compensation. Some people set a modest fixed amount for a child who will do the work, and say the others agree. That prevents a dispute later. If you are choosing an executor, our guide to [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) covers who to ask and who to name as a backup, and the [executor duties checklist](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) shows the workload so you can set a fair figure.

## What does the executor do for the pay?

A quick sense of the work helps. In an Illinois formal estate, the executor opens the case, publishes the creditor notice, inventories assets, pays valid claims, files tax returns, sells or distributes property and closes the case. Our page on the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) walks through each stage and [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) shows why a year of part-time work is typical.
