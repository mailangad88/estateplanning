Marcia, 74, died in October. Her daughter searched the house, the safe deposit box and the bank. There was no will. But Marcia's lawyer still had a signed copy in his file, showing the house going to her two children equally and $20,000 to a church. The original was never found. The family asks whether the copy counts.

Illinois has a particular approach to this problem, and it starts with an assumption many families do not expect. This page explains the presumption of revocation, how a family can answer it, and what to do before and after a death. For the general search steps, see [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois). Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the wider setting.

## Why does Illinois presume a lost will was revoked?

Because a person can revoke a will by destroying it. Under 755 ILCS 5/4-7, a will can be revoked by burning, cancelling, tearing or obliterating it, by the maker or by another person at the maker's direction and in their presence. If the original was last in the maker's hands and cannot be found after death, Illinois courts infer that the maker destroyed it on purpose.

The presumption can be answered. Illinois treats a lost will as revoked unless the family offers clear and convincing evidence that it was not. That is a higher bar than an ordinary civil case. Courts still allow lost wills to be probated, but the family has to work for it. The Illinois [Supreme Court case law](https://www.illinoiscourts.gov/) and the Probate Act's rules on proving wills (755 ILCS 5/6-4) set the framework, so an attorney should confirm the current standard.

## How does the place the will was kept change the result?

Where the original was kept matters more than anything else. Here are the common cases.

| Where the original was last kept | Presumption of revocation | What the family should show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In the maker's own home or desk | Applies | Others had access, fire, flood, move, theft, or a long record of confirming the plan |
| In a bank safe deposit box the maker alone used | Usually applies | Bank access log, who else held a key |
| In the drafting attorney's vault | Weak or absent | The firm's receipt, the vault record, and how it was lost |
| Filed with a court clerk during life | Weak or absent | The clerk's receipt and records |
| With a named executor or relative | Depends | The holder's testimony and a chain of custody |

If you are the family, the question to answer is: who had the paper, and who could have thrown it away?

## What does the family have to prove to admit a copy?

Three things, in this order.

1. The will was validly signed and witnessed under 755 ILCS 5/4-3.
2. The original is lost or destroyed, and the maker did not revoke it. This answers the presumption.
3. The contents, in full, as the maker wrote them.

Strong sources for the first point are the two witnesses and the attestation clause. For the contents, the drafting lawyer's file copy is usually the key. The petition goes in the circuit court where the estate will be opened. Courts have said this proof needs to be clear and the contents specific. If the file copy omits a page or has gaps, the court may admit only what is shown.

## What steps should the family take after a death?

1. Search in the order we describe in our page on [finding a will](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois), including the lawyer, the county clerk and the safe deposit box.
2. Ask the drafting attorney in writing for the file copy, the date of signing and the names of the witnesses.
3. Write down every place the original might have been, who visited the home in the last months and any move or repair.
4. Gather statements from the maker's friends and advisers about whether they said the will was still right.
5. Talk to an Illinois probate attorney before the third month, because a contest by someone who wants intestacy can follow the 6-month rule in 755 ILCS 5/8-1.
6. File the petition to admit the copy, with supporting affidavits and a list of the heirs.

## What happens if the copy is not admitted?

The estate passes as if the lost will did not exist. If an earlier valid will exists, it may govern. Otherwise the intestacy rules apply (755 ILCS 5/2-1). In Marcia's case, if the church gift disappears, her two children would split everything and the church receives nothing. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) gives the order. Heirs can also agree to carry out the lost will's terms in a family settlement. A church or charity named in the lost will would not be bound by that agreement unless it takes part, so this works well only when everyone affected is willing.

## What if the will was lost in a fire, flood or move?

Say so, and prove it. An event that destroyed the paper without the maker's choice is the clearest answer to the presumption. Fire department reports, insurance claims, storm records and a landlord's notice about a flooded storage unit are all useful. The same is true if the maker moved to assisted living and relatives cleared the old home. In that case, statements from the people who packed and discarded papers can show that the will left the maker's hands by accident. Courts still expect proof of the contents, so the attorney's copy remains the key document.

If there are several copies, tell the court about all of them. Differences between a signed copy and a later draft can create doubt about which version the maker signed. The more complete the story, the easier it is for the judge to find that the will was valid and not revoked.

## How can you prevent this problem?

Put the original with your attorney, get a written receipt, and tell your executor which firm. Note the location in your estate-planning documents list. Keep a signed copy with your papers, and update it each time you sign a new will. Our guide on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) lists the options, and the [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) can prompt a yearly check. If you rewrite the will, destroy the old original only when you have the new one signed, and see [changing or revoking a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois).
