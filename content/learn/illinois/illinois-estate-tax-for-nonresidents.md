You may have left Illinois years ago, but kept a condo in the city, a lake cabin, a rental building, or a piece of family farmland. Now you wonder whether Illinois still has a claim on your estate. For many people the honest answer is no, and for some it is yes. The size of your total estate decides which.

This page covers how Illinois treats a nonresident, which assets are tied to the state, how the tax is divided, and what planning is possible. If you are an Illinois resident with property elsewhere, read [Illinois residents with out-of-state property](/learn/illinois/illinois-resident-owning-property-in-another-state) instead. The base rules are in [the Illinois estate tax guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).

## Can Illinois tax an estate if the owner lived in another state?

Yes, in a limited way. The Illinois Estate and Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax Act (35 ILCS 405) applies to property with a tax situs in Illinois. For a nonresident, the Attorney General's instructions say that is real estate and tangible personal property located in Illinois, including such property held in a trust.

Tangible personal property means things you can touch: furniture in the cabin, a vehicle kept in a Chicago garage, equipment on a farm. Cash, brokerage accounts and bonds are intangible, and they are generally treated as located where the owner lived.

## Does the $4 million test look at all the assets or only Illinois assets?

It looks at all of them. A nonresident's estate is tested on its total value. If the total gross estate, with adjusted taxable gifts, is $4,000,000 or less, Illinois does not tax it, even if the whole estate is an Illinois building.

That feature surprises people on both sides. A retiree in Arizona with a $3.5 million estate, including a $3 million Illinois rental building, owes Illinois nothing. A retiree in Arizona with a $9 million estate that includes a $1 million Illinois condo owes Illinois tax, but only a fraction of what an Illinois resident would owe.

## How is the tax split?

The Form 700 instructions describe two steps. First, compute a preliminary tax as if every asset were in Illinois. Second, multiply it by the ratio of Illinois property to the total estate. Here is an example with invented numbers.

Gloria, 77, lived in Scottsdale, Arizona. Her estate was $6 million: a $1.2 million Chicago condo and $4.8 million in Arizona real estate and accounts. Arizona has no estate tax.

1. Preliminary Illinois tax on $6 million, as if all in Illinois: about $456,000.
2. Illinois share: $1.2 million divided by $6 million is 20 percent.
3. Apportioned Illinois tax: 20 percent of $456,000, or about $91,000.

Compare the same $6 million estate with the condo sold before death. Gloria's estate would have been $6 million with no Illinois property, and Illinois would have collected nothing. Selling has income tax and basis effects, and we cover those in [the step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). The number above is our own arithmetic, so run the Attorney General's calculator for an actual case.

## Which assets are Illinois property?

| Asset | Tied to Illinois for a nonresident? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Illinois house, condo or land | Yes | Taxed where it sits |
| Illinois rental building | Yes | Same rule, even if income goes elsewhere |
| Furniture, art or a car kept in Illinois | Generally yes | Tangible property in the state |
| Illinois bank account | Generally no | Follows the owner's home state |
| Stock in an Illinois company | Generally no | Intangible |
| Interest in an LLC that owns Illinois land | Depends on facts | The entity form is reviewed, so confirm with an attorney |
| Illinois land trust beneficial interest | Depends on facts | Illinois law treats it as personal property for some purposes. Review it. |

The last two rows deserve care. People often put property into an LLC or a land trust to keep it private or limit liability, and then assume it is outside the Illinois estate tax. Treatment turns on the facts and the statute, not on the label. Our page on [Illinois land trusts when the owner dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-land-trust-when-the-owner-dies) covers the title side.

## What steps should a nonresident owner take?

1. Add up your total estate, including home-state property, retirement accounts and life insurance.
2. List the Illinois real estate and tangible property with current values.
3. If the total is well under $4 million, you are likely done with the estate tax, but keep the record.
4. If the total is near or over $4 million, ask an attorney to run the apportioned Illinois tax for your numbers.
5. Decide how to hold Illinois property. A revocable trust avoids a separate Illinois probate, though it does not change the tax.
6. Update your documents, so an executor in your home state knows an Illinois return may be needed within nine months.

Doing step 5 can save heirs an extra court case. A living trust that owns the Illinois property avoids [ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate), as we explain in [living trusts and owning property in another state](/learn/trusts/living-trusts-and-moving-or-owning-property-in-another-state).

## What about your home state's tax?

Your home state may have its own estate or inheritance tax. Several states do, and the thresholds are often lower than the federal level. If yours does, it may also tax the Illinois property, with a credit or deduction for tax paid to Illinois. Rules vary a great deal, so read our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and check yours. Try the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) for a first look.

## What happens when the owner dies?

The executor in the home state usually has to coordinate with an Illinois filing. That means a Form 700 sent to the Attorney General within nine months of death, details in [Illinois Form 700](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-700-estate-tax-return), and possibly a probate case in the Illinois county where the land sits. Counties such as [Cook](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/cook) and [Lake](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/lake) each have their own court procedures for foreign estates.

Ordering an updated appraisal of the Illinois property early helps. Executors who start in month eight often find the nine-month date is too close.
