Maybe you are widowed, your house is your largest asset, and you want your daughter to get it without a court proceeding. Maybe your son helps with the bills and you want the bank to talk to him. A friend says: "Just put his name on the deed." It is simple to do and hard to undo, and its side effects can take years to show up.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), explains why parents add a child, what it can cost, and what Illinois offers instead. If you are weighing it for a specific house, a [consultation](/book?from=learn-should-i-add-my-child-to-my-deed-illinois&topic=trust) can look at your deed, your mortgage and your family before you sign.

## Why do parents add a child to the deed?

Two reasons come up again and again. The first is to avoid probate. A parent hears that a house owned jointly passes to the survivor automatically, and concludes that adding a child fixes the problem. The second is to help with the house: a child who lives nearby can handle repairs, deal with the insurer and sign for the parent in a pinch.

Both goals are reasonable. The trouble is that a deed does much more than either goal needs. A recorded deed to you and your child makes the child an owner today. For how the common forms of ownership work in Illinois, see [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) and [the risks of joint tenancy](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks). Adding a spouse is a different, much safer move, covered in [how to add your spouse to your deed](/learn/property-and-assets/how-to-add-your-spouse-to-your-deed).

## What are the risks of putting a child on the deed?

The risks fall into seven groups. The first four are general principles that apply in most states. The Medicaid and property tax points come from the Illinois pages linked below.

**1. The child's creditors can reach the child's share.** Once your child owns part of the house, a creditor with a judgment against your child can generally try to collect from that share. That includes lawsuits, unpaid taxes, medical bills or business debts. A forced sale of the child's interest could leave you co-owning with a stranger.

**2. A divorce can pull the house into the settlement.** If your child's marriage ends, a divorce court may treat the child's share as property to be considered. Your house becomes part of someone else's legal fight.

**3. You lose the step-up in basis on the gifted share.** Basis is the starting number used to measure taxable profit when a house is sold. When someone inherits property, the basis is generally reset to its value at death, which is the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). A lifetime gift generally carries over your old basis instead. If you bought the house for $60,000 decades ago and it is worth $320,000, the child who received a gifted half may face tax on gain measured from your low number. Federal law drives this rule. State law does not change it.

**4. A gift tax return may be required.** Adding a child gives away a share of the house. In 2026 the federal annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient. A share worth more than that generally means filing Form 709, usually with no tax actually due, because the amount counts against your lifetime exclusion. See [Form 709 and gift tax returns](/learn/estate-tax/form-709-gift-tax-return-gift-splitting-and-net-gifts) and the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).

**5. Medicaid can treat the deed as a penalized gift.** Illinois reviews transfers made in the 60 months before an application for long-term care Medicaid, and a transfer for less than fair market value can lead to a penalty period. A few transfers are allowed, such as a home to a spouse, a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child, and some caregiver child situations. The page on [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility and planning](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) explains the test, and [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) covers why simply signing the house over to a child often backfires.

**6. Property tax exemptions may be affected.** Illinois exemptions such as the homeowner and senior exemptions depend on who owns the home and lives in it. The County Assessor's records follow the owner of record. The page on [Illinois property tax exemptions after a spouse dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies) shows why a mismatch between the deed and the person living there causes trouble. Ask your county assessor how a new co-owner who lives elsewhere would show up on your parcel. Separately, the Illinois homestead exemption protects only a small amount of equity, as described in [the homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death), so do not count on it to shield a child's share.

**7. You cannot undo it without the child's signature.** After the deed is recorded, taking your child off needs a new deed that your child signs. Selling or refinancing the house generally needs the child's signature too. If your child dies before you, their share can pass to their own heirs, who may be a spouse you barely know.

## What are the Illinois alternatives?

The aims behind a child on the deed are probate avoidance and practical help. Illinois law offers a separate tool for each.

**Transfer on death instrument.** Under the Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument Act (755 ILCS 27), you sign a record-ready instrument naming a beneficiary. It takes effect only at your death. While you are alive the beneficiary has no ownership interest, and you can sell, borrow against or revoke. To be valid, it must be signed in front of two witnesses and a notary, and recorded before you die (755 ILCS 27/40). The page on the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) goes through the steps. Because the child owns nothing until your death, the creditor, divorce and Medicaid look-back concerns above do not attach to a TODI in the same way, although the Act says beneficiaries remain subject to certain creditor claims after death (755 ILCS 27/85). Property passing at death also generally gets the step-up.

**Living trust.** You move the house into your own revocable trust and name your child as successor trustee and beneficiary. You stay in charge. The trust can say what happens if a child dies first, and who steps in if you cannot manage things. The trade-off is the cost of setting it up and the need to fund it with a deed. See [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts), and [land trust vs. living trust](/learn/illinois/land-trust-vs-living-trust) if someone mentioned a land trust.

**Property power of attorney.** For the "help with the bills" goal, the Illinois statutory short form (755 ILCS 45) lets you name an agent who handles your money and property if you cannot. It is signed in front of a witness and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3), and your child owns nothing. Gifts and changes to a trust need specific written authority (755 ILCS 45/3-4). The page on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) explains it.

## How do the options compare?

| | Add child to deed | TOD instrument | Living trust | Property power of attorney |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Child owns part of the house now | Yes | No | No | No |
| Avoids probate for the house | Mostly | Yes, if recorded | Yes, if funded | No, it ends at death |
| Child's creditors or divorce can reach it | Yes | Not during your life | Not during your life | No |
| Likely tax basis for the child | Carryover on the gifted share | Generally stepped up | Generally stepped up | Not applicable |
| Medicaid look-back gift risk | Yes | Not as a lifetime gift | Revocable trust assets still count | Not a transfer |
| Can you change your mind alone | No | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Helps if you are ill | Only for a co-owner's signature | No | Yes | Yes |

Many Illinois families use a combination, such as a power of attorney for the practical help and a TODI or trust for the house. The free [Illinois child-on-the-deed risk worksheet](/free/illinois-child-on-deed-risk-worksheet) lays the same trade-offs out as a worksheet you can fill in.

## What does this look like in a real family?

Here is an invented example. Linda, 76, is a widow in Joliet. She bought her house in 1988 for $60,000, and it is now worth about $320,000. Her son Mark helps her with repairs and wants to be her contact with the bank. Her daughter Dana lives in another state. A friend tells Linda to add Mark to the deed.

If Linda deeds Mark a half interest, the first problem is that the house now belongs half to Mark, and Dana gets nothing from that half. The second is a gift of about $160,000, which is far above the $19,000 annual exclusion, so a gift tax return is likely. The third is basis: Mark's half carries about $30,000 of Linda's original cost. If Linda later needs nursing home care, the deed starts the 60-month look-back question. And if Mark is sued after a car accident in year three, his half is in play.

Instead, Linda signs a property power of attorney naming Mark as agent and Dana as successor, and a transfer on death instrument naming Mark and Dana in equal shares. Both are recorded or signed in front of the right people. Mark can talk to the bank. Linda still owns the whole house and can sell it. At her death, the house passes to both children without a probate for that asset, and both generally get a stepped-up basis. The numbers are illustrative only; a real house needs its facts checked.

## What should you do before signing a deed?

1. **Write down the goal.** "Avoid probate" and "let Mark pay bills" are different goals with different tools.
2. **Get a copy of your current deed and mortgage.** The county recorder has the deed. Check the mortgage for transfer terms and ask your title insurer whether a deed to a child affects your policy.
3. **Ask how a gift would be taxed.** Have a CPA or attorney estimate the gift tax return requirement and your child's likely tax basis.
4. **Check Medicaid timing.** If a nursing home move is possible in five years, talk to an attorney first. The [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) can help you plan the timeline.
5. **Talk with every child.** An added name for one child often feels unfair to the others, and a TODI or trust can address that.
6. **Choose the tool and record what needs recording.**
