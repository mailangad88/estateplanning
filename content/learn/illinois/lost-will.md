Your father died last month. You have a clean photocopy of his will in a folder, with his signature, two witness signatures and the name of the lawyer who drew it up. The original is nowhere. This page explains what Illinois families can do in that spot, what the court will want to see and what happens if the copy is not accepted.

It is general education, not legal advice, and sits inside our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). A lost will is a case where an Illinois probate attorney's early input pays for itself. You can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-lost-will&topic=probate) at any point. For how Illinois finds and files a will in the first place, start with our page on [finding a will after a death](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois), and for the national picture see [lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will). This page is the Illinois version for a family that holds only a copy.

## The short version

- A copy is evidence, not the will itself.
- Search thoroughly and keep a written log.
- The question that decides most cases is whether the maker destroyed the original on purpose.
- The copy is stronger if it carries witness signatures and you can reach the lawyer and witnesses.
- If the court does not accept the copy, an earlier will or intestacy decides.

## Who has to file the will, and what if you only hold a copy?

Anyone who holds the original must file it with the circuit court clerk right after the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). That duty is why a found original goes to the clerk and not into a family member's pocket. The same section makes it a felony to willfully hide a will for 30 days after learning of the death.

If you only hold a copy, the filing duty does not turn the copy into the will. What it does mean is that you should not sit on the papers while relatives talk. Take the copy to an attorney and ask for the next steps in your county. The clerk's office can tell you how a lost-will petition is opened locally, though it cannot advise you on the merits.

## Where should you look before treating it as lost?

Do the search properly first. Most "lost" wills turn up. Use the free [Illinois lost or missing will guide](/free/illinois-lost-or-missing-will-guide) and the [find the will and assets kit](/free/illinois-find-the-will-and-assets-kit), or work through this list and tick each line.

- [ ] The drafting attorney, or the firm that took over the files.
- [ ] The circuit court clerk in the county where your relative lived, in case the will was deposited for safekeeping.
- [ ] A safe deposit box (Illinois lets a family member look for a will there under 755 ILCS 15/1, covered in [finding a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois)).
- [ ] The home: file cabinet, fireproof box, desk, closet shelves, tax files.
- [ ] The named executor, other family and close friends.
- [ ] The accountant, financial adviser and insurance agent.
- [ ] Email, cloud storage and phone photos, which may show a scan and a date.
- [ ] Any second home, storage unit or care facility room.

Write the date, the place and the person you asked beside each line. If the case reaches a judge, that log shows the effort and protects you from accusations about who touched what.

## What does the court need to see when only a copy exists?

It needs to be satisfied of three things. The details of the standard are for your attorney to confirm, but the shape is consistent.

1. **The will was validly signed.** Illinois requires the testator's signature and attestation by two credible witnesses (see [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements)). The execution can be proved by the two attesting witnesses, either through a signed attestation clause or an affidavit, with each saying they saw the testator sign, attested in the testator's presence and believed the testator was of sound mind (755 ILCS 5/6-4). A copy that includes that clause or a [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) answers much of this by itself.
2. **The original was lost, not revoked.** Illinois lists the ways to revoke a will, and one of them is physically destroying it, or having it destroyed at the maker's direction in the maker's presence (755 ILCS 5/4-7). Destroying a copy does not count. So the court asks what happened to the original.
3. **The copy shows what the will said.** A complete, legible copy is the main proof. Missing pages are a problem, and so is a copy with handwritten notes nobody can explain.

Courts in many states start from a presumption that a will last in the maker's hands and missing at death was destroyed on purpose, and the family has to overcome it. Ask your attorney how an Illinois court applies that idea to your facts, because it controls how much evidence you need.

## What evidence helps?

Good evidence answers "where was the original, and why would it be gone?" in a way someone other than the beneficiary can confirm.

| Evidence | What it shows | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Drafting lawyer's file and signed copy | The will was drafted, signed and witnessed as the copy shows | Strong |
| Testimony from the two witnesses | They saw the signing | Strong |
| Records that the lawyer held the original | The maker never had it to tear up | Strong |
| The maker's statements ("my will is with the lawyer") | The maker still stood by it | Useful |
| Evidence of a move, fire, flood or break-in | A route to accidental loss | Useful |
| Evidence of a falling out or a plan to change the will | Possible intent to revoke | Cuts against you |
| An unsigned or incomplete copy | Little | Weak |

If the lawyer kept the original and the firm closed, the old file may still exist. Ask where closed practices' files go, starting with the lawyer's last known address.

## An example

Example only. Marta, 81, signed a will in 2019 at her lawyer's office. The lawyer gave her a signed original and kept a copy. Marta kept the original in her bedroom dresser. She died in March. Her daughter Elena finds the dresser drawer empty. The lawyer's file holds the signed copy with an attestation clause, and both witnesses are the lawyer's staff.

Elena's cousin Paul, who would inherit under intestacy, says Marta tore it up after an argument. Elena has no evidence of an argument. She does have a neighbor who watched Marta's home being cleared out after a burst pipe six months earlier, and a letter from Marta saying the will "is in my dresser." The attorney collects the staff witnesses' statements, the neighbor's account and the letter. The family has to prove each point. Paul could still contest, and if he does, the case becomes a hearing.

The point of the example is not the result. It is that every piece of evidence came from someone other than the person who benefits, and that the work began early.

## What if the court does not accept the copy?

Then the estate passes as though the lost will had never been signed. An earlier will, if there is one, controls. Otherwise Illinois intestacy law applies (755 ILCS 5/2-1), and the order of heirs is in [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

If every adult heir agrees, the family sometimes divides the estate the way the copy describes. That depends on unanimous cooperation and on the court's role in the estate administration, so get advice before any distribution. A signed agreement does not replace an executor's authority over accounts and deeds. The usual process is in the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process).

## What happens after a will is admitted?

Heirs and legatees get written notice within 14 days after the court admits or denies the will (755 ILCS 5/6-10). An interested person has 42 days from the effective date of the order admitting the will to ask for formal proof (755 ILCS 5/6-21). Those clocks run on a lost-will case like any other, so put the dates in your calendar the day the order is entered. [Contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains the usual grounds.

## What should an executor do in the meantime?

Do the few things that protect the estate, and nothing that cannot be undone.

- Secure the home, keep the mail and do not remove valuables.
- Order several certified copies of the death certificate.
- Avoid paying bills or handing out personal property from the estate. A named executor has no authority until the court acts, though Illinois allows limited steps such as arranging burial and preserving the estate before letters are issued (755 ILCS 5/6-14).
- Call the lawyer who drafted the will and ask in writing what they hold.
- Do not alter, annotate or "repair" the copy.

## If you are the one who made the will

Do not leave a single original in a drawer. Use a lawyer's vault or a safe deposit box, tell your executor in writing where it is and keep a signed copy with the witness clause elsewhere. If your original is already gone and you are alive, a new will fixes this in an afternoon, which is far cheaper than the case your family would face.
