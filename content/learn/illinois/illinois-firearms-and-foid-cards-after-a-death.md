Someone has died, and there is a gun safe in the bedroom closet. The family is grieving, the house needs to be secured, and nobody is sure who is allowed to touch the guns. In Illinois the answer is narrower than most people expect, because the state ties possession to a card issued to a living person.

This page covers the Illinois side: the FOID card rules for the executor and heirs, what to do with the guns in the meantime, and how a lawful transfer or sale works. Federal rules, including restricted items such as suppressors, are covered in our general article on [passing on firearms safely and legally](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning). It belongs to our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). This is general education, not legal advice.

## Do heirs need a FOID card to inherit a gun in Illinois?

Yes, in the ordinary case. The Firearm Owners Identification Card Act says no person may acquire or possess a firearm in Illinois without a FOID card issued in that person's name by the Illinois State Police (430 ILCS 65/2). The list of exemptions in the Act covers groups such as certain law enforcement and military personnel, certain nonresident hunters, and minors under a parent's supervision. Heirs, executors and administrators are not named on the list.

That does not mean the gun cannot be inherited. A will can leave it to a named person, and ownership on paper can pass. But the person who physically holds it needs a card. An heir who has no card should leave the gun where it is, or with a licensed dealer, until the card arrives.

## Does the dead person's FOID card pass to the family?

No. The card is personal to the person it was issued to. When the owner dies, nobody can use it, and the family should not try. A surviving spouse who lives in the house with the guns has the same problem as an adult child who drives over to collect them. Both need their own card.

Applying takes time, and the Illinois State Police sets the process and the fee, so check its site before you plan around a date. An applicant who is prohibited under state or federal law will not be approved. Prohibited categories are technical, and our firearms article lists the federal ones.

## What should the executor do with the guns right away?

Treat them like any dangerous asset in the house. The executor's first job is to keep them safe and keep the estate out of trouble.

1. Do not remove, sell or give away any firearm yet.
2. Lock them where they are, unloaded, with ammunition stored separately if you can. If the house is empty, consider that a burglary is the real risk.
3. Make a list with the make, model, caliber and serial number of each gun, and photograph them in place.
4. Look for a safe combination, a key, and paperwork: receipts, old transfer records, any license for a restricted item.
5. Find the FOID card and put it in the estate file. Do not use it.
6. Check the will for gifts of firearms, and check for a trust that owns any of them.
7. Tell the people who live in or visit the house not to touch them.

Only the executor named in the will, or one appointed by the court, has authority over estate property. Until letters of office issue, the person with the key should act as a caretaker only. Our [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) article explains when letters arrive, and the [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) page lists the rest of the job.

## How do you transfer a firearm to an heir in Illinois?

There are three lawful routes, and which one fits depends on who receives the gun.

| Route | How it works | What the receiver needs |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer by operation of law or court order | The estate passes the gun under the will or a court order | A valid FOID card in the receiver's name |
| Bona fide gift to a listed relative | An heir who already holds the gun later gives it to a spouse, child, sibling or other relative named in the statute | A valid FOID card in the receiver's name |
| Sale through a licensed dealer | The dealer runs a background check and handles the paperwork | The buyer's own FOID card |

The Act says a transfer needs the receiver to show a valid FOID card or concealed carry license (430 ILCS 65/3). For private sales, the seller must contact a licensed dealer or call the Illinois State Police with the buyer's card number and get an approval number, valid for 30 days (65/3(a-10)). The exemptions from that step include transfers under operation of law or a court order and bona fide gifts to a list of relatives (65/3(a-15)). The transferor must keep a record of the transfer for 10 years (65/3(b)).

Notice that those exemptions skip the approval call. On their face they do not skip the card, because the card rule sits in a different section. Have an attorney confirm how they apply to your estate before anyone relies on them.

The Illinois State Police also says family transfers of firearms among immediate family or household members are exempt from a separate certification rule for retail dealers, provided both the sender and the receiver hold a valid card (430 ILCS 68/5-25). The theme repeats: the card is the key.

## What if no one in the family can or wants to keep the guns?

Sell them through a licensed dealer and divide the cash. The dealer takes on the background check, you get a receipt, and the proceeds are estate property. A dealer will usually give a value in writing, which helps if heirs disagree. Auction houses that deal in guns work the same way.

A beneficiary who does not want a gun can also decline the gift with a formal disclaimer. Our article on [disclaiming an inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois) explains how that works. The gun then passes as if that person had died first, or falls back into the estate, depending on the will.

## Who is not allowed to receive a gun?

Anyone barred by state or federal law, whether or not a family member. If an heir has a felony conviction, a protective order, or other disqualifier, the executor cannot hand over the gun. Give that heir the sale proceeds instead. The same goes for a minor beneficiary, who should never be given a firearm directly. The executor can hold it until the child is old enough and has a card. Our article on [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) covers the money side.

## An example

Bill, 79, dies in Peoria and leaves his hunting rifles to his son Dan, who lives in Rockford. Dan has never owned a gun and has no FOID card. Bill's daughter Kara is the executor. She locks the rifles in Bill's gun safe, photographs each serial number and tells Dan to apply for a card. Four months later Dan's card arrives. Kara hands over the rifles under the will, files the list in the estate record, and keeps her copy for the final accounting.

If Dan had not wanted the rifles, Kara would have taken them to a licensed dealer, sold them and split the money among the children.

## A word on collections and restricted items

Antiques, machine guns, suppressors and short-barreled rifles follow other rules, including federal approval for some items. Illinois also restricts certain weapons by statute. Before you move or sell anything unusual, ask an attorney. This is the situation where a small mistake carries a criminal penalty for a well-meaning person.
