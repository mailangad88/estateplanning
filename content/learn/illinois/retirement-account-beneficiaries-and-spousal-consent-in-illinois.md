Dana, 58, lives in Naperville. She is on her second marriage, and her 401(k) is the largest thing she owns apart from the house. She wants her two grown sons from her first marriage to receive it. Her husband, Mark, has his own pension and says he does not need the money. Can Dana simply change the form?

For a 401(k), not by herself. This page covers the rules for married Illinois couples. For the national picture, see [whether your spouse has to approve your beneficiary choices](/learn/beneficiary-designations/does-your-spouse-have-to-approve-your-beneficiary-choices) and [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries). Our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) lists the rest of the Illinois pages. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Which accounts require your spouse's consent?

It depends on the kind of account, not on the state.

| Account | Federal spousal consent rule? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Private-sector 401(k), 403(b) under ERISA | Usually yes | Spouse must consent in writing to another beneficiary |
| Pension or defined benefit plan | Yes, survivor annuity | Spouse waives the survivor benefit |
| IRA, Roth IRA | No federal rule | Custodian form controls |
| Governmental plan (some public plans) | Not ERISA, plan rules apply | See Illinois public pension systems |
| Church plan | Often not ERISA | Plan document controls |
| Life insurance | No federal rule | Policy form controls |

Some 401(k) plans qualify for an exception, and the plan's summary plan description says which rules apply. Ask the plan administrator rather than guess.

## How does a spousal waiver work step by step?

1. Ask the plan administrator for its beneficiary form and its spousal consent form. Plans have their own.
2. Complete your new beneficiary form, naming each person and share.
3. Your spouse reads the consent form, which names the new beneficiary or acknowledges giving up the right.
4. Your spouse signs before a notary public or a plan representative, as the form requires.
5. Return both forms together to the plan.
6. Ask for written confirmation that the plan accepted them.
7. Keep copies, and file a copy with your will.
8. Repeat the process if you change the beneficiary again, since some plans need new consent each time.

Federal rules require the consent to be witnessed this way. A casual signature at the kitchen table does not count.

## What does Illinois add?

Illinois is not a community property state. Unlike Texas or California, a spouse here does not automatically own half of retirement money earned during marriage. Rather than community property, Illinois gives surviving spouses other protections. The right to renounce a will and take a statutory share (one-third if there are descendants, one-half if not) comes from the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/2-8). It generally reaches the probate estate, not accounts that pass by beneficiary form. Our article on [spouse rights at death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) explains the details, and a lawyer can tell you how they apply to your facts.

Two exceptions to keep in mind. A court can divide retirement accounts in a divorce. And a premarital agreement can waive rights, which our article on [Illinois prenups and inheritance rights](/learn/illinois/illinois-prenup-and-inheritance-rights) covers.

## A worked example

Dana's 401(k) is worth $480,000. She names her sons, Kyle and Evan, 50 percent each. Mark agrees to sign the plan's consent form in front of a notary. The plan accepts it. When Dana dies, the sons receive the account, which is still taxed as it comes out. Mark keeps his own pension and the house, which they held as tenants by the entirety. See [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).

Change one fact. Mark refuses to sign. The plan pays him the $480,000. He can roll it over into his own IRA and name his own children. Kyle and Evan receive nothing from the account. Dana's choices would then be to buy life insurance for her sons, or to adjust other assets to even things out.

## What about an IRA?

An IRA custodian will accept your form without your spouse's signature. That makes the IRA a flexible tool, and it is why couples sometimes keep a separate IRA for the part they want to direct. Flexible is not risk-free. A spouse can still raise claims in divorce, and honest planning means telling your spouse. Many couples sign a short written agreement. See the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) to list every form.

## What if you are divorced or separated?

Until a judgment of dissolution is entered, you are married for plan purposes. After divorce, Illinois can revoke a former spouse's designation on some assets (750 ILCS 5/503(b-5)), but ERISA plans follow the plan document, so update the form yourself. Our article on [how divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan) covers the sequence.

## What about public pensions in Illinois?

Teachers, state employees, university staff and many local employees belong to Illinois public pension systems with their own survivor rules. Those are not ERISA plans. See our article on Illinois pension survivor benefits.

## What happens to the account after the owner dies?

The tax rules differ for a spouse and for children. A surviving spouse can generally roll an inherited 401(k) or IRA into their own account and delay withdrawals. A non-spouse beneficiary such as an adult child generally must empty the account within 10 years, with some exceptions. Our article on the [inherited IRA 10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) explains the yearly details, and our article on [spousal rollover versus inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira) compares the spouse's choices.

Illinois does not tax Social Security or most retirement income, and our article on Illinois income tax on an inherited IRA or 401(k) covers what that means for beneficiaries who live here. When a spouse gives up the survivor right for a child, the child may face federal income tax faster than the spouse would have. Compare the tax results before you decide, not only the family result.

## What should a couple do before signing anything?

Talk first, then paper. Decide together what the surviving spouse needs to live on, and what should go to children. Many couples find that the spouse keeps the 401(k) while the children receive life insurance or the house, which avoids a waiver altogether. If a waiver is the right choice, put the reasons in writing, and consider having each spouse speak with a lawyer separately. A waiver signed under pressure or without knowing the account's size can be challenged later. Write down the balance on the day of signing, and keep the consent form with your plan papers.
