When someone dies, the will may leave the spouse far less than the spouse expected. It may leave the home to children from an earlier marriage, or give the spouse only a small gift. Illinois does not leave a spouse without options in that case.

This page explains two spouse rights in Illinois: renouncing the will, and the spouse's award. It also covers deadlines, a worked example, and where each right can fall short. It is general education, not legal advice. For the wider picture, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What can a surviving spouse claim in Illinois?

A surviving spouse has up to three layers of protection.

1. **The will itself.** If the will gives the spouse a fair share, the spouse can simply accept it.
2. **A renunciation.** If the will gives too little, the spouse can give it up and take a fixed share set by law.
3. **The spouse's award.** In most estates the spouse may also claim a support amount for nine months.

If there is no will, the spouse takes under the intestacy rules instead. Read about that in [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). Illinois is a separate-property state. It has no community property, so each spouse owns what is titled in their name. The renunciation right is what keeps one spouse from fully shutting the other out.

A civil union partner has the same legal protections and benefits as a spouse under Illinois law (750 ILCS 75/20). This page uses "spouse" to include civil union partners.

## How does renouncing the will work?

Renouncing means giving up what the will says and taking the share the statute sets. The share is one-third of the entire estate if the decedent left a descendant, or one-half if the decedent left no descendant. It is figured after payment of all just claims (755 ILCS 5/2-8(a)). This is the Illinois version of what other states call the [elective share](/glossary/elective-share).

Here is an example. Rosa dies and her will leaves everything to her two adult children. Her husband Sam is not mentioned. Her probate estate is $900,000 after debts and claims are paid. Sam renounces the will. Because Rosa left descendants, Sam takes one-third, or $300,000. The children's gifts are reduced to cover it, and the court adjusts the other gifts proportionately.

### What are the steps and deadline?

To renounce, the spouse must file a signed written instrument in the court where the will was admitted. It must say that the spouse renounces the will (755 ILCS 5/2-8(b)). Here is the usual sequence.

1. Learn the date the will was admitted to probate. The 7-month clock starts on that date.
2. Compare what the will gives the spouse with the statutory share. Include the spouse's award in the comparison.
3. Have the spouse sign a written renunciation.
4. File it with the court clerk within 7 months after admission.
5. If a case that affects the spouse's share is pending, file a petition for more time within the same 7 months. The court may allow a longer period.

The statute calls the filing "a complete bar to any claim of the surviving spouse under the will." Once a spouse renounces, the spouse cannot go back and take the will's gifts. That is why the choice needs a careful look. If the will leaves the spouse a life use of a home plus income from a trust, the numbers can be close.

### What happens to gifts that depend on the spouse?

If the will gave the spouse a life estate or similar interest and the spouse renounces, the later interest takes effect as if the spouse had died before the testator. The statute says this applies unless the will expressly says that a renunciation should not speed up the later interest (755 ILCS 5/2-8(c)). In plain terms, the children may get their share sooner.

## What is the spouse's award?

The spouse's award is a sum for the spouse's support during the nine months after death. It is available when the decedent was an Illinois resident and the estate is administered in Illinois, whether or not there is a will (755 ILCS 5/15-1).

- The court sets an amount it finds reasonable for nine months of support, suited to the spouse's condition in life and the condition of the estate.
- The court also awards an amount for minor children of the decedent who lived with the spouse at the time of death.
- The award cannot be less than $20,000, plus at least $10,000 for each such child. As of 2026, confirm the current minimums, because the legislature can change them.
- It is the spouse's own property and is exempt from judgments, garnishment and attachment while held by the estate's representative.

The award can be lost in one situation. If the will says its gifts to the spouse are in place of the award and the spouse does not renounce, the spouse gets the gifts and not the award. A spouse who renounces keeps the right to the award.

Illinois also lets an adult child who was financially dependent on the decedent, lived with the spouse and is likely to become a public charge apply for an award of at least $5,000 in some cases. The statute sets a 30-day deadline to give written notice, and missing it bars the award (755 ILCS 5/15-1(a-5)).

## What can a spouse not reach?

A renunciation applies to the estate that passes under the will. Many assets never go through the will. These include life insurance and retirement accounts with a named beneficiary, joint accounts, and property in a living trust. We did not find a statute in our research that tells a spouse how the one-third or one-half is measured against those assets. An attorney should review how a trust or joint account affects the claim.

Other limits apply too:

- A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement may waive some or all of these rights. See [prenuptial agreements and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).
- If the will already gives the spouse about as much as the statutory share, renouncing may gain little and gives up the will's gifts.

## What should you do if you are planning ahead?

If you are the one making a will, assume your spouse has these rights. Leaving a spouse out of a will does not prevent a claim. It may only start a dispute. For a second marriage, the choice of how to provide for the spouse and for children from an earlier marriage is the main planning question. ## What should a surviving spouse do first?

Do not sign anything or file anything in a rush. Get a copy of the will and find out when, and whether, it has been admitted to probate. Mark the 7-month date on a calendar. Make a list of assets and who gets each one. Then ask an attorney to compare the will with the statutory share, the award and any non-probate assets.
