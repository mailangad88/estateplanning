Your mother has moved into memory care, the house in Oak Lawn sits empty, and the monthly bill is more than her income. You are her agent under a power of attorney, and someone has to decide whether you can list the house, sign the contract and close. In Illinois the short answer is usually yes, with conditions that matter, and the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the documents around it.

This page takes the agent's view of a sale: what to check, what the closing side usually asks for, what to do with the money, and when someone else signs the deed. If your family's facts are complicated, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-can-a-power-of-attorney-sell-a-house-in-illinois&topic=parent) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Does the Illinois short form cover real estate?

Yes, if your parent granted it. Real estate is one of the listed categories on the Illinois statutory short form, covering buying, selling, leasing and managing property, according to our page on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property). Your parent initials each power they grant, and a power left blank is not granted. The section-by-section guide to [filling out the Illinois statutory short form](/learn/illinois/filling-out-the-illinois-statutory-short-form-poa-for-property) shows where the initials and the limits go.

Read three parts of the form before you call a realtor:

1. **The real estate line.** Is it initialed, and is it free of a line through it?
2. **The limits space.** Some parents write conditions such as "no sale of my house without a written appraisal". A limit like that binds you.
3. **The start date.** If the power begins only on a future event, such as a doctor's finding of disability, you may need that proof first. The tradeoff is explained in [springing versus immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).

An older custom document may be broader or narrower, so have it read before you sign a listing agreement.

## What will the title company and the buyer ask for?

Expect your power of attorney to be read line by line, because the title company insures the buyer's ownership. The national page on [power of attorney and real estate](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate) describes the usual checks. The closing side usually asks for some or all of these:

- The original power of attorney or a certified copy, with the witness signature and notary certificate that the property form requires (755 ILCS 45/3-3).
- A signed statement from you that your parent is alive and the power has not been revoked or ended.
- Photo ID that matches the agent named on the form.
- Information about your parent's spouse, if your parent is married, because a spouse's rights in the home can affect who must sign.
- Sometimes, a letter from a doctor if capacity at signing is in question.

Title companies often ask for the power of attorney to be recorded with the deed. Practices vary, so ask the title company and your county recorder early, before you accept an offer.

If a company refuses for reasons that seem thin, ask for the reason in writing. For banks, Illinois treats some refusal reasons, such as insisting on its own form or saying a statutory short form is too old, as unreasonable on their own (755 ILCS 45/2-8), as explained in [when an Illinois bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/illinois/bank-refuses-an-illinois-power-of-attorney).

## What must you do with the sale money?

Keep it for your parent. The Act requires an agent to act in good faith for the principal's benefit, with due care, and to keep a record of receipts, disbursements and significant actions (755 ILCS 45/2-7). The agent should also try to keep the parent's existing estate plan intact where possible (755 ILCS 45/2-9). Our page on [agent duties and record keeping](/learn/illinois/illinois-poa-agent-duties-and-record-keeping) explains the ledger habits.

For a house sale, that means:

- The proceeds go into an account in your parent's name, never into your own account, even briefly.
- The closing statement goes in your file, with a short note on why you sold, how you set the price and who else you consulted.
- Care bills, taxes and debts are paid from your parent's account, each with a receipt.
- You are reimbursed only for reasonable, documented expenses, and paid for your time only if the form allows it.

After your parent dies, the executor can ask for these records. The [power of attorney agent handbook](/free/power-of-attorney-agent-handbook) keeps the record steps in one place.

## Can the agent sell or give the house to themselves?

Not as a gift, unless the form clearly says so. Under the standard powers an Illinois agent cannot make gifts, appoint property to others or change a beneficiary without specific authority (755 ILCS 45/3-4). A deed to yourself, a sibling or a grandchild for "one dollar" is a gift. The rules and drafting choices are in [Illinois gifting and beneficiary change powers](/learn/illinois/illinois-poa-gifting-and-beneficiary-change-powers).

A sale to yourself at full price is not a gift, but it is still a conflict of interest, because you are on both sides. Our agent duties page suggests getting an appraisal and considering court approval for a sale to the agent or the agent's family. If siblings later suspect misuse, the steps in [court accounting for an Illinois POA agent](/learn/illinois/court-accounting-for-an-illinois-poa-agent) are where the dispute usually goes.

## How does selling differ from gifting for Medicaid?

A sale at fair value usually does not create a transfer penalty, because your parent receives the money. That money then counts as your parent's asset, so it can delay eligibility until it is spent on care. A gift of the house, or a sale for less than it is worth, is a transfer for less than fair market value. Illinois looks back 60 months at those transfers when someone applies for long-term care Medicaid, and the penalty period generally begins when your parent is in a nursing home and otherwise eligible, according to our page on [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility and planning](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning).

That same page lists some transfers of the home as allowable, including to a spouse, a child under 21, a blind or disabled child, and certain siblings or caregiving children. Those exceptions are technical, and an agent still needs gift authority to use them. Gifting also has a tax cost: a house given during life generally keeps the parent's original cost basis, while a house inherited at death usually gets a step-up in basis. The [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) shows how far back the review reaches.

## What if the house is in a trust or your parent has died?

Then the power of attorney is the wrong document for the deed.

**Land trust.** In an Illinois land trust the trustee, often a bank, holds title and signs the deed at the direction of the beneficiary, as our page on the [Illinois land trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-land-trust-when-the-owner-dies) explains. Your role as agent, if any, is giving that direction for your parent. Whether the trustee accepts an agent's direction depends on the agreement and its own forms, so ask the trust department.

**Living trust.** If the deed runs to your parent's revocable trust, the trustee signs. Many parents serve as their own trustee and name a successor trustee to take over at incapacity or death, as covered in [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts). The successor trustee usually proves authority with a certification of trust (760 ILCS 3/1013). You may hold both jobs, but you sign the deed as trustee.

**Your parent has died.** The power of attorney ended at death, as explained in [does a power of attorney end at death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/does-a-power-of-attorney-end-at-death-in-illinois). If the house passes through probate, the executor or administrator sells after letters issue, and an independent executor can sell without a court order (755 ILCS 5/28-8); see [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois). If your parent recorded a [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument), the beneficiary records a notice of death affidavit (755 ILCS 27/75) and then owns the house to sell.

**No power of attorney at all.** If your parent can no longer understand what a power of attorney does, nobody can sign one for them. The family asks the circuit court for a guardian of the estate, and selling real estate generally requires court authority, as explained in [guardian of the person vs guardian of the estate](/learn/illinois/guardian-of-the-person-vs-guardian-of-the-estate-in-illinois). If your parent can still sign, [how to get power of attorney for a parent in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-get-power-of-attorney-for-a-parent-in-illinois) walks through the steps.

| Situation | Who signs the deed |
|---|---|
| House in parent's name, parent can still sign | The parent, usually |
| House in parent's name, valid POA with real estate power | The agent, for the parent |
| House in an Illinois land trust | The land trust trustee, at the beneficiary's direction |
| House in parent's living trust, parent incapacitated | The successor trustee |
| Parent died, house in probate | The executor or administrator, after letters issue |
| Parent died, recorded TODI | The TODI beneficiary, after the notice of death affidavit |
| No POA and parent cannot sign | A guardian of the estate, with court authority |

## How do you sell a parent's house as agent, step by step?

1. **Read the deed.** Confirm the house is in your parent's own name, not a land trust or living trust.
2. **Read the power of attorney.** Check the real estate initials, the limits space and the start date.
3. **Get a value.** An appraisal or a broker's opinion protects you if anyone later questions the price.
4. **Talk to an attorney about Medicaid** if a nursing home application is likely within five years.
5. **Send the power of attorney to the title company** before you accept an offer, and ask what else it needs.
6. **Sign as agent** at closing, in the form the title company gives you.
7. **Deposit the proceeds** into your parent's account and record the sale in your ledger.
8. **Tell the family** what you did and keep the closing file.

## Example: Linda sells her father's bungalow

This is an invented example. Linda, 58, is agent for her father, Gene, 86, who now lives in a Joliet nursing home. His house has been empty for six months. The deed is in Gene's name alone, and the real estate power is initialed with no limits.

Her brother Tom offers to buy the house for half its value. Linda knows that would be a gift the form does not allow and a transfer Medicaid would review. She gets an appraisal, lists the house and sends the power of attorney to the title company, which asks for her signed statement that Gene is alive and the power is unrevoked. Tom buys at full price. The proceeds go into Gene's account, and Linda files the appraisal and closing statement with her ledger.
