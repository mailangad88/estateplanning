You live in Illinois, and you also own a lake cabin in Michigan, a condo in Florida, or forty acres your grandfather left you in another state. You assume your Illinois estate plan covers all of it. For land, it usually does not.

This page explains why an Illinois resident with out-of-state land often leaves two probate cases instead of one, what the second case involves, and the tools that avoid it. It is general education, not legal advice. For the broader subject, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) and the main article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate). We do not cover other states' rules here, because they differ and change.

## Why does an Illinois resident need a second probate for land elsewhere?

Because real estate is controlled by the state where it sits, not by the state where you live. Personal property, such as bank accounts, cars and brokerage accounts, generally follows your home state. Land does not. Your Illinois executor receives authority from an Illinois court, and that authority stops at the Illinois border.

So when title to the out-of-state parcel is in your name alone, the other state normally wants its own court to appoint someone to deal with it. That is the ancillary case. It is usually smaller than the main case, but it has its own filing fees, its own lawyer, its own notice period and its own pace. The Illinois estate can be finished and still be waiting on a county court in another state.

## What does the second case cost in practice?

The cost drivers are easy to list, even when the figures vary. You pay for a second local attorney, a second set of court filing fees, certified copies of the Illinois papers, possible publication of notice, a bond in some places, and months of carrying costs on a property nobody is using. The property tax bill and insurance on a closed cabin keep arriving the whole time.

Here is an example with invented numbers. Ruth, 71, lives in Naperville and owns a $380,000 condo in Arizona in her name. She dies with a will leaving everything to her son Paul. Paul finishes Illinois probate on the house and accounts. The condo sits for months while an Arizona attorney files the case, and Paul pays HOA dues and utilities for each of those months. Nothing about Ruth's Illinois plan was wrong. It just stopped at the border. Compare that with the process in [Illinois probate](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process), which already takes time on its own.

## How can you avoid ancillary probate on out-of-state property?

Move the property out of your individual name while you are alive, using one of these tools.

| Tool | How it avoids the second case | Main catch |
|---|---|---|
| Revocable living trust | The trust owns the deed, so there is no probate estate in the other state | You must sign and record a new deed, and keep it funded |
| Beneficiary or transfer on death deed | Passes at death to the named person | Only works if that state allows it, and the rules differ |
| Joint tenancy with survivorship | Survivor takes the property | Puts the other person on title now, with creditor and gift exposure |
| LLC that owns the property | You own LLC interests, which are personal property | Costs and upkeep; not right for a family home |
| Selling or gifting | Removes it from your estate | Tax and control consequences |

For most Illinois families, the living trust is the plain answer. Our article on [living trusts and owning property in another state](/learn/trusts/living-trusts-and-moving-or-owning-property-in-another-state) covers the steps, and [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) covers the deed. The Illinois-specific guide to [living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) explains how a trust works for your Illinois assets too.

A common mistake our attorneys see: someone signs a trust, puts the Illinois house in it, and forgets the cabin. The trust is fine and the cabin still needs probate. Make a list of every parcel, mineral interest and timeshare, and check each deed.

## Why a transfer on death instrument does not fix it

The Illinois transfer on death instrument is useful, but it is limited to Illinois real estate. It must be recorded in the county where the property sits before you die (see [the Illinois TODI](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument)). A cabin in another state needs that state's own deed or a trust. Do not assume a form you signed here travels with you.

## Will the other state accept my Illinois will?

Usually it will recognize a will that was validly signed where you lived, but recognizing the will is not the same as skipping the case. The second state's court still opens a file, still decides who serves, and still controls the sale or transfer of the land. Some states offer a shortened ancillary procedure when the home-state case is already finished. Others expect a full filing. Which applies to your parcel is something only a lawyer in that state can say, so treat any promise of "no second case" with caution unless a trust or beneficiary deed is already in place.

Your Illinois will still matters. It names who inherits the cabin and who serves as executor, and a clear will keeps the second case simple. Our guide to [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) shows what makes it valid at home.

## What steps should you take now?

1. List every piece of real estate you own, anywhere, with the county and how title is held.
2. Pull each deed. Look for survivorship wording and for co-owners.
3. Ask whether the second state allows a beneficiary deed, or whether a trust is cleaner for that parcel.
4. Have an attorney confirm that the trust deed meets the other state's recording rules, because requirements vary.
5. Record the deed there, update your insurance to name the trust, and tell the title insurer or lender if there is a mortgage.
6. Keep your will, your Illinois [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property), and the trust together so your executor can find them.

If you inherited the property with siblings, decide on one rule for all of you. See [heirs property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) for what happens when relatives share title with no plan.

## What about debts and taxes on the out-of-state property?

Illinois taxes estates over $4 million. How land in another state is counted toward that threshold is a question to put to an attorney if your total is near it. See [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). Federal estate tax applies only above $15 million per person in 2026. Your heirs may also face property taxes, transfer fees or filings in the other state, so ask a local attorney there before you act on a sale.

## What if you have already died owning the property?

If you are the executor, do not wait. Ask an attorney in the state where the land sits what the ancillary process requires, because the court there decides. Gather certified copies of the Illinois letters and the will. Keep the property insured and the taxes current. Our article on [what happens when someone dies owning property in two states](/learn/what-if/die-without-a-will-owning-property-in-two-states) walks through a case with no will, and [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) covers the planning side.
