One child got help with a down payment. Another got a car. A third got nothing yet. When a parent dies, siblings often ask whether those earlier gifts come out of the share, or whether everyone now starts even. The answer in Illinois is that it depends on the paper trail.

This page explains the idea of an advancement, the math with numbers, the documents that matter and the other rules that touch lifetime gifts. For the default order of heirs, see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). Our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) has the other topics.

## What is an advancement, and how does Illinois treat it?

An advancement is a gift during life that the giver meant as an early part of the child's inheritance. Illinois intestacy law has a section on advancements (755 ILCS 5/2-5). As we read it, a gift is not charged against a child's share unless it is clearly shown to be an advancement, and a writing from the parent or an acknowledgment from the child is the usual proof. Confirm the current text with the clerk or an attorney, because the details control.

The starting point is that a gift is a gift. If a parent writes nothing and says nothing, the child who got $60,000 for a house does not lose a dollar of the later inheritance. Most families do not realize this.

## How does the math work when it applies?

Here is a worked example. Ruth, 78, dies without a will with $240,000 in her name. She has three children: Dan, Ellen and Frank. In 2020 she gave Dan $60,000 for a down payment. She signed a note saying "this is an advancement on Dan's share."

1. Add the advancement to the estate: $240,000 + $60,000 = $300,000.
2. Divide by three children: $100,000 each.
3. Subtract the advancement from Dan's share: $100,000 - $60,000 = $40,000.
4. Dan receives $40,000. Ellen and Frank receive $100,000 each. The estate pays out $240,000.

Without the note, each child takes $80,000, and Dan keeps his $60,000 gift on top. The table shows the difference.

| | Gift only (no writing) | Advancement (signed note) |
|---|---|---|
| Dan | $80,000 plus $60,000 gift = $140,000 | $40,000 plus $60,000 gift = $100,000 |
| Ellen | $80,000 | $100,000 |
| Frank | $80,000 | $100,000 |
| Who decides | Default rule | Parent's written intent |

## What if there is a will?

A will controls. If the will divides the estate equally "after taking into account lifetime gifts," the executor adds back the listed gifts. If the will simply says "equally among my children," a court usually reads that as equal shares of what is in the estate, without adding back anything. A parent who wants an even result should say so in the will, with a list of gifts and amounts. A parent who wants unequal shares for another reason should read about [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) and [whether to leave children equal shares](/learn/life-stages/should-you-leave-your-children-equal-shares).

## What is the difference between a gift, a loan and an advancement?

A gift needs nothing back. A loan must be repaid, and an unpaid loan can be an asset of the estate, collected from the borrower's share or forgiven in the will. An advancement is a gift with a note attached to the inheritance. Because families blur these, a one-page document at the time of the transfer prevents years of argument.

## What steps should a parent follow?

1. Decide whether the transfer is a gift, a loan or an advancement.
2. Write a short signed and dated note stating which it is, the amount and the recipient.
3. Have the child sign or initial the note to acknowledge it.
4. Keep a copy with the will, and list the gifts in the will if you want them counted.
5. Check gift tax rules: the annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, and larger gifts need a gift tax return, though most families owe no tax because of the $15,000,000 lifetime exclusion in 2026.
6. If long-term care is a possibility, check the Medicaid look-back before giving.
7. Review the plan every few years or after each large gift.

## How do lifetime gifts affect other rules?

Three other areas can matter more than the advancement rule.

- **Gift tax.** The annual exclusion in 2026 is $19,000 per person. Larger gifts count against the lifetime exclusion, and a Form 709 is generally required. See the IRS gift tax page at irs.gov.
- **Medicaid.** Gifts made within 60 months before applying for long-term care Medicaid can cause a penalty period in Illinois. Our article on [Illinois Medicaid planning](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) explains it.
- **Deeds.** Adding a child to a deed during life is a gift of an interest in the house and has tax and control effects. See [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed).

## What if siblings disagree about past gifts?

Start by gathering evidence: bank records, checks, texts and any notes. Ask the administrator whether the intestacy statute or the will mentions gifts. If the estate is in probate, an attorney can raise the question with the court. A family meeting before the case can also help. Our page on [can you disinherit a child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-illinois) is useful background on parents' freedom, and the [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) can help you pick a document to record your intent.

## What common mistakes cause sibling fights?

The biggest mistake is saying nothing. A parent tells one child "this is yours already" in conversation, and the other children never hear it. After the death, memories differ and no document exists. A second mistake is writing a will that says "equally" while the parent privately expects the earlier gift to count. A third is giving money to a child as a loan, never writing it down, and leaving the estate to argue about whether it was a loan. A fourth is using joint accounts as a way to give gifts, which can blur who owns what.

The fix is short and cheap: a one-page letter for each major gift, a clear will and a copy shared with the executor. It does not need to be long. It only needs to say what the parent meant.
