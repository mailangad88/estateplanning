Evelyn, 72, died last month. Her husband, the beneficiary on her $100,000 life insurance policy, died two years earlier, and the form never listed anyone else. Her two sons ask whether the money is theirs, the estate's, or something in between.

This page walks through that situation in Illinois. For common beneficiary mistakes in general, see our article on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries). Our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) links the process pages. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Who gets the money when the named beneficiary has died?

The policy decides. First the insurer checks for a contingent beneficiary who is alive. If there is none, it applies the policy's default order. Many individual policies move down a list: surviving spouse, then children, then parents, then siblings, then the insured's estate. Some policies skip to the estate right away. Group life through an employer follows the plan documents, which are also part of the contract.

Your will does not change this unless the policy names the estate. A will only distributes property in the estate. Our guide to [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains why.

## What are the possible outcomes?

| Facts | Likely result | Probate needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Contingent beneficiary alive | Contingent beneficiary is paid | No |
| No contingent, policy has family default order | Next relative in the order is paid | Usually no |
| No contingent, default says estate | Estate representative collects, then distributes | Often yes |
| Beneficiary is a trust or charity that no longer exists | Policy default or estate | Often yes |
| Ex-spouse listed after a divorce | Illinois may treat the ex as having died first | Depends on the policy and the plan |
| Beneficiary and insured died close together | Simultaneous death rules or policy wording apply | Depends |

Do not guess from the table. Ask the insurer in writing which default applies and why.

## What does it mean if the estate gets the money?

The proceeds become part of the probate estate. An executor or administrator named by the court collects them with letters of office. Creditors can then be paid from them, after the claims period described in our article on [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate). What is left goes under the will, or to heirs under Illinois law if there is no will. See [who inherits without a will](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

That is a real cost. The Illinois Insurance Code generally protects proceeds paid to a named beneficiary from the insured's creditors, but not proceeds paid to the estate. That is one reason a living contingent beneficiary matters. Check the current statute or ask a lawyer how it applies to your facts.

If the whole probate estate is small, the affidavit may avoid court. A $100,000 policy plus a few accounts could still fit under the $150,000 personal estate limit. See our [Illinois small estate affidavit overview](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit).

## How do you claim the money step by step?

1. Find the policy or at least the insurer. See below if the paperwork is missing.
2. Ask for the claimant's statement and the insurer's list of required documents.
3. Order certified death certificates. Our article on [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need) helps.
4. Ask the insurer in writing who it considers to be the beneficiary and why.
5. If the estate is the payee, decide whether to open probate or use the small estate affidavit.
6. Return the forms with the death certificate and any court papers the insurer asks for.
7. Deposit the payment in an estate account if you are the representative. See opening an estate bank account.
8. Pay debts first, then distribute.

## How do you find a policy you cannot locate?

Look through mail, bank and credit card statements for premium payments, tax returns, the employer's benefits office and a safe deposit box. Use the National Association of Insurance Commissioners' life insurance policy locator, which sends a request to participating insurers. Search the Illinois State Treasurer's unclaimed property database too, since unclaimed proceeds can be turned over to the state. Our article on finding unclaimed property for an Illinois estate walks through the search.

## What about divorce and remarriage?

Under 750 ILCS 5/503(b-5), a judgment of dissolution of marriage can revoke a former spouse's designation as beneficiary of certain life insurance and retirement benefits, with exceptions. It does not reliably reach employer plans governed by federal law, where plan documents control. Remarriage changes nothing by itself, so a new spouse is not added unless the form is updated. See [what if an ex-spouse is still a beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).

## A worked example

Back to Evelyn. Her policy has a family default order after the beneficiary, listing children before the estate. Her two sons, Aaron and Neil, send the claim forms and death certificate, and the insurer pays $50,000 to each, plus interest. No probate was needed for the policy. Had the policy named the estate as the final default, the $100,000 would have gone into a probate estate, and a $12,000 hospital bill would have been paid from it before the sons got the rest.

The lesson is to read the contract. The default order, not the will, was decisive.

## How do you prevent this?

Name a primary and a contingent beneficiary on every policy. Review after each death, birth, marriage or divorce. Use the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) to list each form. If the heirs are minors, use a trust rather than a direct designation. See when a minor inherits money in Illinois.

## What if a beneficiary and the insured died at about the same time?

Policies and state law set a rule for people who die close together. Illinois has a Simultaneous Death Act, and many policies add their own survival requirement, such as outliving the insured by a set number of days. If the beneficiary did not meet it, the policy treats the beneficiary as having died first, and the contingent beneficiary or default order applies.

This is a question for the insurer in writing. Ask which rule it is applying, and ask for the policy provision. If the dates are very close, the order of the deaths may affect which family line receives the money. Families sometimes receive different answers from different departments of the same company, so ask for a written decision from the claims unit. Our article on [what happens if the owner and beneficiary die at the same time](/learn/beneficiary-designations/what-happens-if-the-owner-and-beneficiary-die-at-the-same-time) explains the general idea. When a large policy is at stake, a lawyer can read the contract with you and compare it to the statute.
