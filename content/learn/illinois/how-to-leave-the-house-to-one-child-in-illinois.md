One of your children lives near the house, or in it. Maybe she moved back to help after a fall. The others live in other states and would rather have cash. You want the house to go to one child, and the rest of the family to be at peace with it.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), walks through the four Illinois tools that can send a house to one child, how each handles fairness, and the specific clauses that keep the plan from turning into a sibling dispute. If you already know which child and want help choosing the document, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-to-leave-the-house-to-one-child-in-illinois&topic=will-or-trust) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

## Is it legal to leave the house to just one child in Illinois?

Yes. Illinois has no forced share for adult children, so a parent can leave a house to one child and give the others less or nothing, as covered in [can you disinherit a child in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-disinherit-a-child-in-illinois). Two groups do have protections. A surviving spouse can renounce a will and take one-third of the estate when there are descendants (755 ILCS 5/2-8), and the TODI Act gives a spouse a parallel right against a house passing by instrument (755 ILCS 27/66). A child born after the will was signed may also have a claim. If you are married, read [Illinois spouse's rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) before you plan around the house.

## Which Illinois tool fits your plan?

Four tools can get the house to one child. They differ most in what they can say beyond "this child gets it."

| | Will gift | TODI naming one child | Living trust | Deed during life |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Child owns anything before your death | No | No | No | Yes |
| Goes through probate | Yes | No, if recorded before death | No, if the house is deeded in | No |
| Can attach a buyout or equalizing payment | Yes, but the executor must enforce it | No | Yes | No |
| Can give a sibling a right to live there | Possible, but awkward in probate | No | Yes | No |
| Can you change your mind alone | Yes | Yes, by recording a new instrument | Yes | No |
| Tax basis for the child | Generally stepped up | Generally stepped up | Generally stepped up | Carryover on the gifted part |
| Medicaid look-back concern | No | No lifetime gift | No lifetime gift | Yes, a 60-month review |

A TODI must be signed before two witnesses and a notary and recorded before death (755 ILCS 27/40), as the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) page explains. The deed downsides are in [should I add my child to my deed](/learn/illinois/should-i-add-my-child-to-my-deed-illinois), and [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house) compares those two head to head.

A quick test: if the gift is clean and unconditional, a TODI often does the job. If you need any rule attached, such as a price, a time limit or a sibling's right to stay, you are describing a trust.

## Does it need to be fair, and what does fair mean?

That is your call. What causes trouble is a plan that is unequal by accident. Decide which of these you mean, then write it down.

- **Equal in dollars.** The child who takes the house receives a share worth about the same as the others.
- **Unequal on purpose.** The child gets the house as thanks for care, or because the others already received help. The documents say so.
- **Equal in the end, with a delay.** One child lives there for a period, then the house is sold or bought out and everyone shares.

### Equalizing with other assets

The simplest balance is to name the other children as beneficiaries on life insurance or an account. A beneficiary form beats a will, so check every form against the plan. The national guide to [leaving the house to one child and money to another](/learn/property-and-assets/how-to-leave-the-house-to-one-child-and-money-to-another) compares those equalizing methods in detail, so this page focuses on the Illinois mechanics.

### A buyout price formula

If the house is worth more than everything else, a trust can require the child who keeps it to pay the siblings the difference. The clause works only if it answers five questions in advance:

1. Who values the house, and as of what date (usually an independent appraiser at the date of death).
2. Who pays for the appraisal.
3. How long the buying child has to pay, and whether a note secured by the house is allowed.
4. Whether credit is given for the mortgage balance, unpaid taxes or repairs the child made.
5. What happens if the child cannot pay by the deadline: usually the trustee sells and divides the proceeds.

Name a trustee who is not the buying child, or add a co-trustee, so the buyer is not also enforcing the price.

### Counting gifts you already made

If one child already received a large gift, such as a down payment, the default Illinois rule is that it is not counted against their inheritance. A lifetime gift is treated as an advancement only when it is clearly shown to be one, usually through a writing (755 ILCS 5/2-5). The page on [advancements and lifetime gifts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/advancements-lifetime-gifts-and-inheritance-in-illinois) shows the math. If you want the house to balance an earlier gift to a different child, say so in the will or trust and list the gift.

## Worked example: a caregiver child in Peoria

This is an invented example. Gloria, 77, owns a house in Peoria worth about $240,000 with a small remaining mortgage. Her daughter Nora moved in three years ago and handles meals, medications and appointments. Her sons Luis and Andre live out of state. Gloria also has about $90,000 in savings and a $100,000 life insurance policy.

A TODI naming Nora would work, but nothing would say who pays the mortgage, and if Nora sold a month later the brothers would feel cheated. Gloria signs a living trust instead and deeds the house into it. The trust says:

- Nora receives the house outright, together with the remaining mortgage.
- Luis and Andre are named as beneficiaries of the life insurance and split the savings.
- If Nora sells within three years, a stated share of the net proceeds goes to her brothers.
- If Nora dies before Gloria, the house is sold and the proceeds are split among the three families.

Gloria also writes a one-page letter explaining that the house reflects Nora's years of care, meets her attorney alone, and shares the letter with her sons while she is alive. The numbers will not be identical at her death, and Gloria has decided that is fine. The inequality is deliberate and documented.

## What changes when the child is also the caregiver?

Three things. First, care can create its own paper trail. A parent who pays a child for care should use a written agreement made before payment starts, as described in [paying a family caregiver in Illinois](/learn/illinois/paying-a-family-caregiver). That keeps wages separate from the house, so nobody later argues the house was payment or that the payments were gifts.

Second, Medicaid. Illinois reviews transfers made in the 60 months before a long-term care Medicaid application. A transfer of the home to a caregiving child is listed as allowable in certain cases, but the conditions are technical, according to [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility and planning](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning). Do not deed the house to the caregiver as a shortcut without advice. A TODI or trust keeps the house in your name instead, though the state may still look to it after your death under [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery).

Third, contest risk rises, because the caregiver often drives the parent to the lawyer. More on that below.

## What about the mortgage and property taxes on the house?

The mortgage does not disappear at death. It stays attached to the house, and a federal law generally stops the lender from calling the loan just because a relative inherits the home as a residence, as the national page on [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) explains. The practical questions are for you to settle in the documents:

- **Who pays off the loan.** Say whether the child takes the house subject to the mortgage or other assets pay it off first. That choice moves real money between siblings.
- **Can the child afford it.** A house the child cannot carry ends up sold. See [the mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).
- **Property tax exemptions.** Illinois homeowner and senior exemptions depend on who owns the home and lives in it, so they may change once the child takes title. The page on [Illinois property tax exemptions after a spouse dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies) shows how those records follow the owner.
- **A deferred tax lien.** If you use the state's senior tax deferral program, the deferred amount is a lien that comes due at death, covered in [what heirs repay under the Illinois senior property tax deferral program](/learn/illinois/illinois-senior-citizens-property-tax-deferral-program-and-what-heirs-repay).

## How do you lower the odds of a will contest?

Most contests over a house rest on capacity or undue influence. Illinois courts raise a presumption of undue influence when the person who benefits was in a fiduciary role, the parent depended on and trusted that person, and that person arranged the will. The page on [undue influence in Illinois will contests](/learn/illinois/undue-influence-will-challenges-in-illinois) explains the test. A contest of a will must be filed within six months after admission to probate (755 ILCS 5/8-1), and a TODI has its own window (755 ILCS 27/90).

Steps that help:

1. **Choose your own lawyer** and meet without the child who benefits.
2. **If that child is your agent under a power of attorney,** consider naming someone else, since that role can be the fiduciary link.
3. **Write down the reason** for the unequal plan, in the document or a separate signed letter.
4. **Keep the change gradual and consistent.** A sudden switch shortly before death invites scrutiny. Telling the family years ahead helps.
5. **Leave the other children something meaningful,** and consider a [no-contest clause](/learn/illinois/no-contest-clauses-in-illinois), which only has force against someone with a gift to lose.
6. **Ask your doctor for a note** near the signing date if your health is in question. The [testamentary capacity in Illinois](/learn/illinois/testamentary-capacity-in-illinois) page explains the standard.
7. **Name backups.** A gift to a child who dies first follows 755 ILCS 5/4-11 unless the will says otherwise, and a TODI needs its own contingent beneficiary.
