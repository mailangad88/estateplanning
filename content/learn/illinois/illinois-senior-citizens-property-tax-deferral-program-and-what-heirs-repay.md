Your father is 79, widowed, and his property tax bill takes a large bite out of his Social Security. A neighbor told him the state will "defer" the tax so he does not have to pay it now. He asked you whether that is a gift, a loan or a trap.

It is a loan, and the house secures it. This page explains how the Illinois Senior Citizens Real Estate Tax Deferral Program works, what the lien does, and what the family owes when the owner dies. It is general education, not legal advice. The figures below are the ones the Cook County Treasurer publishes and the statute, the Senior Citizens Real Estate Tax Deferral Act (320 ILCS 30). Other counties administer their own applications, so confirm the current numbers with the county collector before you rely on them. The separate exemptions that reduce a bill, rather than defer it, are in our page on [Illinois property tax exemptions after a spouse dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies).

## How does the Illinois property tax deferral program work?

The state pays the deferred portion of your tax to the local taxing bodies, and you owe the state later. The Cook County Treasurer describes it as a loan from the State of Illinois, repaid when the property is sold or when the participant dies. The deferral covers your principal residence only.

| Feature | What the Cook County Treasurer lists |
|---|---|
| Age | At least 65 by June 1 of the application year |
| Household income | $75,000 or less |
| Ownership | Owned and lived in the home for at least the last three years |
| Maximum deferred | $7,500 per tax year, both installments combined |
| Interest | 3% simple interest per year |
| Insurance | Fire or casualty coverage at least equal to the deferred taxes |
| Other taxes | No outstanding property tax or special assessments |
| Deadline | Applications open January 1 and close March 1; reapply every year |

If the bill is larger than the cap, you pay the difference. The Treasurer's example: on a $7,700 bill, the taxpayer pays the $200 above $7,500. Late property tax payments accrue 0.75% a month, so a deferral that is not properly approved becomes expensive quickly.

## What does the lien do?

It attaches the debt to the house. Section 3 of the Act makes the deferred taxes and accrued interest a lien on the real estate and improvements until paid, and Section 4 has the collector record a statement with the county recorder. The county advances the filing fee, which is added to the deferred amount.

The practical effect is a closing problem. The Act says no sale or transfer can be legally closed and recorded until the deferred taxes and interest are paid, unless the collector certifies in writing that prompt payment has been arranged. The same rule applies to a contract of sale. So the lien is invisible while Dad lives in the house and very visible the day a title company runs the search.

Our page on [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois) walks through a closing, and this lien is one of the payoffs that a title company will insist on, alongside any mortgage.

## How much interest builds up?

For tax years 2023 and later the Act sets interest at 3% per year, and the Treasurer calls it simple interest, so it does not compound. Deferrals for earlier tax years carry 6% per year under the statute. The interest is payable on death, on transfer of the home, or earlier if you choose.

An example, labelled as one. Walter defers $6,000 a year for four years, so the deferred principal is $24,000. At 3% simple interest, each full year on that balance adds about $720. If Walter dies after year four and the house sits another year before the estate settles, the family should expect to owe roughly $24,000 plus interest accrued over the whole period, not just the last year. Ask the collector for a payoff figure rather than doing the arithmetic yourself, because the exact accrual dates matter.

## What happens to the deferral when the owner dies?

The balance becomes payable, and the heirs get first call on the house. Section 3 of the Act gives heirs-at-law, assignees or legatees first priority to the property by paying the deferred taxes plus interest in full. An heir who wants to keep the home can pay it off or refinance. One who wants to sell pays it from the sale proceeds at closing.

There is one major exception. If the heir or legatee is a surviving spouse who is 55 or older within six months of the owner's death, the deferral can continue for that spouse's lifetime. The spouse must sign a deferral and recovery agreement before the deferred taxes become due. Miss that step and the exception is gone. A widow in her late fifties who hears about this a year after the funeral may find the balance already due.

If no heir or assignee pays, the Act has the deferred taxes and interest recovered from the taxpayer's estate within one year of death. Payment is also due within 90 days after the property stops being qualifying property, for example if the owner moves out permanently. If payment is not made, foreclosure can be started under the Property Tax Code.

## What should heirs do, step by step?

1. Find the recorded lien. Search the county recorder's records under the owner's name and parcel number, or ask the collector's office whether the parcel has a deferral.
2. Request a written payoff from the collector, showing deferred taxes and interest through a specific date.
3. Decide whether the house is being kept or sold. A spouse aged 55 or older should ask about continuing the deferral before the balance becomes due.
4. If the house is in probate, tell the personal representative. The balance is a claim the estate has to deal with, as our page on [how the Illinois probate process works](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) explains.
5. Keep paying current-year property taxes, insurance and any mortgage. A deferral covers a portion of the tax only for years you were approved.
6. Pay from sale proceeds at closing, or arrange a payoff before the one-year recovery window runs.

## How does this interact with inheriting a house?

The deferral is one more liability that travels with the house, like a mortgage. Our guide to [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois) covers title, mortgage and the first month. Layer the lien on that. If the house passes by a recorded transfer on death instrument or in a trust, the lien still stays on the property, because it is attached to the real estate and not to probate. Heirs sometimes assume that skipping probate also skips the debt. It does not.

Where co-heirs disagree about whether to keep the house, the payoff deadline adds pressure. One sibling who wants to keep the home needs the cash or credit to pay the full balance. Our page on [what Illinois heirs can do about a slow executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor) is useful if the delay is on the executor's side.

## Should an older homeowner use the program at all?

It depends on what the house is for. For someone who intends to stay for life and wants to leave the house to children who understand a payoff will be due, the deferral can ease a cash squeeze. For someone who expects to leave the house to a child who has no savings, or who may need to sell for care costs, the deferral reduces what the family keeps. Run the numbers with an estimated payoff in the year you expect the sale.

Two cautions. First, the program uses household income limits and a three-year ownership rule, so a recent title change can disqualify an applicant. Second, if the homeowner uses an Illinois land trust, the Treasurer requires the applicant to be sole beneficiary, and our page on the [Illinois land trust when the owner dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-land-trust-when-the-owner-dies) explains what happens to the trust interest at death. Someone also thinking about Medicaid should look at the [Medicaid nursing home planning page](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) before any title change.
