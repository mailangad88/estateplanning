If your mother or father lived in Naperville, Wheaton, Downers Grove, Elmhurst or anywhere else in DuPage County, the estate is handled at a single courthouse in Wheaton, by the court and clerk that make up the 18th Judicial Circuit. The law is the Illinois Probate Act, the same as in every other county. What changes is who you call, where the papers go and how to follow the case.

This page is part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). It follows a DuPage family from the first filing to the last order, using what the court and the clerk publish. If you would rather go through your own situation with someone, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-probate-works-in-dupage-county&topic=probate) with an Illinois estate planning attorney. Families in Chicago and its suburbs can compare with [how probate works in Cook County](/learn/illinois/how-probate-works-in-cook-county).

## Which court and clerk handle a DuPage County estate?

The 18th Judicial Circuit Court, which sits in the DuPage County courthouse at 505 North County Farm Road in Wheaton, Illinois 60187. The Illinois Supreme Court's court directory lists that address, with the court administration office in Room 2015, and the clerk's own site gives the same street address. The clerk's civil department number is 630-407-8700, and the court's own office line is 630-407-8904.

Which county applies depends on where the person lived at death, not where the executor lives or where the hospital was. Our article on [probate venue when property sits in two Illinois counties](/learn/illinois/probate-venue-property-in-two-illinois-counties) explains that rule (755 ILCS 5/5-1). If your father lived in Naperville but owned a cabin in another county, the estate is still opened in DuPage.

A small caution: Naperville straddles DuPage and Will County. The county that matters is the one for the decedent's home address, so check the county on the deed and tax bill if you are unsure, and read [how Illinois probate works](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) for the general rule.

## Does the estate need a court case in the first place?

Not always, so sort the assets before you drive to Wheaton. Accounts with a named beneficiary, jointly owned property and a funded trust pass outside court. If there is no real estate and the personal property is $150,000 or less, the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) can replace a case. A house in the decedent's sole name normally means probate, though [heirship affidavit vs probate](/learn/illinois/heirship-affidavit-vs-probate) covers the narrow situations where a title company accepts less.

## How is an estate opened and the will filed in DuPage County?

You file the original will with the clerk, then file a petition asking the judge to admit it and appoint the executor. Illinois law requires the will to be filed immediately after death (755 ILCS 5/6-1), and our page on [filing the original will with the circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk) covers that step. The clerk's Probate form 3807, Petition for Probate of Will and for Letters Testamentary, shows what the court wants to see.

According to that form, the petition lists:

- the decedent's name, residence at death and the date and place of death, and a statement that a will was left;
- an estimate of the Illinois personal property, real estate and yearly real estate income;
- the heirs and legatees, with relationship, address and whether each is a minor or disabled person;
- who the will nominates to serve;
- whether independent administration is requested, which needs an attached exhibit naming a representative for any minor or disabled heir.

The petitioner signs before a circuit clerk or a notary. The form also says the petitioner may file on their own or through an attorney, which is different from some other counties, so ask the clerk how the case should be started before you commit to either route. The next table puts the whole path in order.

| Stage | What happens | Where it happens in DuPage | Illinois rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will goes to the clerk | Original will filed with the clerk | The clerk's office at the Wheaton courthouse | Immediately after death (755 ILCS 5/6-1) |
| Petition filed | Heirs, legatees and asset estimate listed | Clerk, by paper or e-filing | Ask for independent administration (755 ILCS 5/28-2) |
| Hearing and order | Judge admits the will and appoints the executor | Courtroom or remote link, as noticed | Letters of office issue to the executor |
| Notice | Heirs and legatees notified in writing | Mail from the executor or attorney | Within 14 days after the order (755 ILCS 5/6-10) |
| Claims period | Newspaper notice and mailed notice to creditors | Local newspaper and mail | Later of six months from first publication or three months from mailing (755 ILCS 5/18-3) |
| Closing | Report filed, then a wait | Clerk | 42 days before discharge in an independent estate (755 ILCS 5/28-11) |

Without a will, the petition asks for letters of administration, and the court usually requires a bond, covered in [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond). Our guide to [the petition for letters of office](/learn/illinois/illinois-petition-for-letters-of-office) goes line by line, and [independent vs supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains why most families ask for the first.

## How do I file and follow the case electronically?

The court's guardianship page says reports can be e-filed through Tyler eFile Illinois, and that filers who cannot e-file may file in person, by mail, private messenger or parcel delivery with the clerk's office. The clerk's website also lists a Civil Electronic Filing page. Because that page does not spell out probate steps, call the clerk to confirm which method applies to a first petition.

For following a case, the same guardianship page says due dates and future court dates can be checked on the DuPage Circuit Clerk's ePay site, or by calling 630-407-8700. The clerk's court forms page also shows an Online Case Lookup link. Treat any online docket as a convenience and confirm anything important with the clerk. If you are an heir trying to learn whether an estate has been opened, see [how to find out if you are named in a will](/learn/illinois/how-to-find-out-if-you-are-named-in-a-will-in-illinois).

Attorneys filing in DuPage use a DuPage County attorney number, and the clerk's forms page links an Attorney Information Sheet for getting or updating one. That is one reason an attorney familiar with the local filing process can save time.

## What does the court say about remote hearings and scams?

The clerk's site has a Remote Court Hearings page with Zoom links for the 18th Circuit. It also carries a warning that scammers contact people through Zoom's chat during virtual proceedings, posing as attorneys, pretrial officers or judges. The page says neither the court nor the clerk will direct anyone to pay through services such as Venmo, Zelle or PayPal.

For an estate, that warning is worth remembering. A real notice comes from the court or your attorney, and fees are paid at the clerk's office, on the clerk's website or by mail. We could not confirm from official pages which probate courtroom or calendar your case will draw, so rely on your hearing notice for the room, the time and whether to appear in person.

## How long will it take, and what slows it down?

A simple estate usually runs a little over six months, and a year or more when there is a house to sell, a dispute or a missing heir. The claims period and the six month will contest window (755 ILCS 5/8-1) run side by side, so they do not stack. See [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) for the calendar.

## Example: the Naperville house and three heirs in other states

This is an invented example. Ravi, 62, lives in Denver. His mother, Anita, died in her Naperville house, which was in DuPage County. Her will names Ravi executor and leaves everything equally to Ravi, his sister Meera in Atlanta and his brother Dev in Phoenix. The house is in Anita's name alone, and she had one bank account with no beneficiary.

Ravi flies in, finds the original will in a file cabinet and takes it, with a death certificate copy, to the clerk in Wheaton. He then prepares the petition from the clerk's form, lists all three children with current addresses and asks for independent administration. He has the paperwork reviewed by an Illinois attorney, because the house sale will need clean letters.

The judge admits the will and appoints Ravi. Within 14 days he mails notice to Meera and Dev, who both sign approvals by mail and never travel. The creditor notice runs in a local paper. Ravi opens an estate account, pays the last bills, and lists the house once letters issue. It sells in month five. The claims period and contest window pass in month seven with nothing filed. He files the closing report, waits the 42 days and is discharged about nine months after the death.
