The nine months after a death go by fast. There is a funeral, a probate case, a house to clear, and appraisals that take time. If the estate is over $4 million, the Illinois estate tax return and the payment are due at the end of that period. Missing it adds interest and penalties on a tax that can already run into six figures.

This page explains the Illinois late filing and late payment charges, how extensions work, and what to do if you are already behind. The penalty percentages here come from the Attorney General's Form 700, but forms change, so check the current revision. For the filing steps, read our guide to [Illinois Form 700](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-700-estate-tax-return). The overall [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) covers the rest of the estate.

## What happens if you miss the Illinois estate tax deadline?

Three separate charges can apply: a late filing penalty, a late payment penalty and interest. The Illinois estate tax is due nine months after death (35 ILCS 405/6). The Form 700 we reviewed lists these amounts:

| Charge | Rate listed on Form 700 | Cap |
|---|---|---|
| Late filing penalty | 5% of the tax for each month or part of a month | 25% of the tax |
| Late payment penalty | One half of 1% of the tax for each month or part of a month | 25% of the tax |
| Interest | 10% a year, from nine months after death | None |

The federal system has its own penalties, covered in [missing the Form 706 deadline](/learn/estate-tax/missed-the-form-706-deadline). They are separate from Illinois charges. An estate can owe both when it is over the federal exclusion too.

## What does a late filing cost in dollars?

Consider Marcus, 69, who died in January 2025 with a $5 million estate. The Illinois tax is $285,714. His daughter Alicia, the executor, did not realize a return was needed, and filed and paid six months after the nine-month date.

| Charge | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Illinois tax | Per the Attorney General's $5 million example | $285,714 |
| Late filing penalty | 5% x 6 months = 30%, capped at 25% | $71,429 |
| Late payment penalty | 0.5% x 6 months = 3% | $8,571 |
| Interest | 10% a year x half a year | $14,286 |
| Total, if all apply in full | | about $380,000 |

The extra cost is about $94,300 on a tax that was already $285,714. We do not know whether the Attorney General would combine or reduce some of these charges, so this is a worst-case estimate. Call the office or ask an attorney before paying.

## How do extensions work?

The Attorney General's rule is simple. An Illinois extension request should be made within nine months of the date of death, and the Attorney General recognizes federal extensions. The extension form says plainly that an extension of time to pay does not waive or abate interest.

In practice, an extension gives you more time to finish the return, and not more time to pay without cost. If the estate expects to owe about $285,000, the executor should pay a reasonable estimate by the nine-month date and finish the exact return later. Any overpayment can be dealt with after the return is filed.

Extensions are for the paperwork, which is where most executors fall behind. Appraisals of a business or real estate often arrive late. A request submitted in month eight is much safer than one submitted in month ten.

## What steps should you follow if you are already late?

1. Confirm that a return was required. Add up the gross estate and adjusted taxable gifts, and see if the total is over $4,000,000.
2. Gather values, even approximate ones. Do not wait for a perfect appraisal.
3. Estimate the tax with the Attorney General's calculator. Our worked numbers are in [Illinois estate tax worked examples](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax-worked-examples).
4. File Form 700 and pay the estimated tax as soon as possible. Interest stops growing on the amount paid.
5. If a reason for delay exists, such as illness or a late appraisal, explain it in a short written note and ask for relief.
6. Keep the proof of filing and payment, and ask for written confirmation of the account.

The sooner the payment goes in, the less interest builds. The late filing penalty also grows with each month, so the first month matters.

## Can the estate afford to pay?

Illinois estates sometimes have the opposite problem: lots of value, very little cash. A family home, a farm or a business is hard to sell in nine months. Our guide to [what to do if the estate cannot pay the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-if-the-estate-cannot-pay-the-estate-tax) explains the federal options. For Illinois, the Attorney General's instructions mention deferral in some situations (35 ILCS 405/6(b)), so ask the office. The point of acting early is that a payment plan or deferral usually has to be requested, not assumed.

Some executors use a line of credit, sell a stock position, or cash an IRA to pay. Each choice has an income tax effect, so check before acting. Remember, too, that the Illinois tax is deductible in computing the federal taxable estate, which helps only estates large enough to owe federal tax. See [Illinois versus federal estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-vs-federal-estate-tax).

## Who is responsible for the penalty?

The executor files the return, and in many cases the estate pays the penalty from its own funds. If the delay was the executor's fault, heirs can sometimes complain that the executor wasted estate assets. That is one reason to document your steps. For general responsibilities, read [who pays the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/who-pays-the-estate-tax-executor-or-heirs) and our guide to [what heirs can do about a slow Illinois executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor).

## How can an executor avoid a late filing in the first place?

Start the clock on day one. Write the nine-month date on the calendar the day the death certificate arrives. Order appraisals for real estate and any business within the first sixty days. Ask the financial institutions for date-of-death balance letters early, since some take weeks.

Then set two internal deadlines. At month six, decide whether the estate might be over $4 million. At month eight, either file or request an extension, and make an estimated payment. These checkpoints sound simple, but they catch most problems. Executors who work without them often learn the return was needed only when an heir or a title company asks.
