You suspect that the person handling a parent's money has taken more than they should. Maybe the savings fell by $38,000 in a year, or the agent refuses to show a statement. You want to know what Illinois courts can require, and what to bring before you ask.

Our article on [revoking an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney) explains the court's general powers under section 2-10 in one place. This page focuses on the accounting itself: how to ask, how to prepare, and how to read what comes back. The [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) links the related documents.

## What is the first step before going to court?

Send a written request. The Act requires an agent to keep a record of receipts, disbursements and significant actions, and to give it to certain people on request (755 ILCS 45/2-7). The people who can ask are listed in that section, and our article on [agent duties and record-keeping](/learn/illinois/illinois-poa-agent-duties-and-record-keeping) summarizes them. If you are not on the list, an agent may say no, and a court petition or a guardian becomes the way in.

Keep the request short and polite. Name the period you want, such as January 1 of last year through today, and ask for statements, receipts and a ledger. Send it in a way that proves delivery, and note the date.

## How does the court route work, step by step?

1. Write to the agent and ask for an accounting for a specific period.
2. Wait a reasonable time, noting any deadline in the statute that applies to your request.
3. Collect what you can see: statements the principal still receives, mail, online alerts and county property records.
4. Speak with an attorney about standing and where to file, usually the circuit court in the principal's county.
5. File a petition under section 2-10 asking the court to review the agent's conduct and compel an accounting. Notice goes to the agent, the principal and anyone the court directs.
6. Expect the court to ask for proof of the principal's capacity situation. The Act requires a finding that the principal lacks the capacity to control or revoke the agency.
7. The court may appoint a guardian ad litem to speak for the principal.
8. The agent files the accounting. You or your attorney compare it with bank statements and receipts.
9. The court can order repayment, costs and, in the Act's terms, relief that protects the principal.

## What should an accounting show?

Think of it as a story with numbers. It begins on the first day of the period and ends on the last. In between, every dollar in and out should have a date, an amount, a payee and a reason.

| Part of the accounting | What it should include | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Opening balance | All accounts and assets on day one | Accounts missing from the list |
| Income and receipts | Pension, Social Security, rent, interest, sales | Deposits that are not listed |
| Payments for the principal | Care, taxes, insurance, repairs, with receipts | Large cash withdrawals with no explanation |
| Payments to the agent | Reimbursements, pay if the form allows it | Round-number transfers to the agent |
| Gifts and loans | Only if the document allows | Gifts when the form grants no gift power |
| Closing balance | Matches statements | Balance that does not reconcile |

Compare it with the original document. The Act says the statutory short form powers do not include gifts or beneficiary changes unless stated (755 ILCS 45/3-4). Our article on [gift powers](/learn/illinois/illinois-poa-gifting-and-beneficiary-change-powers) explains what to look for.

## What can the court order?

Under section 2-10 the court may construe the document, review the agent's conduct and grant appropriate relief, which can include compelling an accounting. An agent who violates the Act can be liable for restoring the property to the value it would have had, and the principal's funds cannot be used to pay the agent's own defense if the agent did not act for the principal's benefit. The longer table is in our revocation article. In some cases the court appoints a guardian. See [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) for that process.

## Are there other routes at the same time?

Yes. Adult protective services can investigate financial exploitation, and the Illinois Department on Aging explains how to report. See [how to report elder financial abuse in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-report-elder-financial-abuse-in-illinois). A bank can freeze suspicious activity on request. Law enforcement may be involved if theft is likely. A civil petition does not depend on those reports, and the facts you gather help in each. Read about [warning signs of power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) for what to document, and see [how to freeze a parent's credit and add account safeguards](/learn/elder-care/how-to-freeze-a-parents-credit-and-put-safeguards-on-their-accounts) for steps you can take now.

## Example: Marcus and his father's accounts

Marcus, 58, learns that his father Leon's savings dropped from $212,000 to $174,000 in 14 months. His sister Tanya is the agent. Marcus writes asking for a ledger, statements and receipts for that period. Tanya replies with a monthly list showing $22,500 for care and $5,500 for repairs, $28,000 in all, but without receipts. Marcus compares the list with statements he gathered from the principal's mail. The other $10,000 left the account in two $5,000 transfers that are not on Tanya's list. He then speaks with an attorney. The attorney may advise a petition because Leon, who has dementia, cannot revoke the power. The petition asks the court to compel a formal accounting and records for the two missing transfers.

## What does it cost, and is it worth it?

Cost depends on the dispute. A request letter costs little. A contested petition with a hearing costs more, and the court may shift costs if the agent misused funds. Many families settle after the agent produces the records. Use the [plan finder](/plan-finder) to see what protections you want in your own plan, such as naming a neutral agent and requiring annual statements.

## What evidence should you gather before you file?

Courts act on documents, not suspicion. Build a folder before you meet an attorney.

- Account statements for the principal for at least the period in question, including closed accounts.
- Copies of checks, wire records and transfer confirmations, especially to the agent or the agent's family.
- Property records, such as deeds and car titles, showing any changes in ownership.
- Medical records or a doctor's letter that speak to the principal's capacity on key dates.
- A timeline with dates of calls, visits and statements by the agent.
- Copies of the power of attorney and any earlier documents the principal signed.

Do not log in to the principal's accounts without permission. If you lack access, an attorney can ask the court or the bank to produce the records.
