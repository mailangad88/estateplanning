Maybe you run a one-person consulting practice, a two-partner HVAC company or a family bakery set up as an S corporation. You know your customers and margins. You may not know what would happen to the business if you died tonight, or if a stroke left you unable to sign a check for three months.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), sorts the plan by entity type. It shows what happens at death for each form of business, which document covers the gap, who acts during incapacity, and how a trust and a buy-sell agreement fit in. It ends with a checklist and a worked example. If you would rather go through your own documents with someone, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-for-small-business-owners-in-illinois&topic=other).

## What happens to my business if I die in Illinois?

It depends on the legal form. A sole proprietorship stops existing, because it was only ever you. An LLC, corporation or partnership keeps going, and what passes is your ownership share.

If you have no will or trust, your ownership passes to your heirs under the Illinois intestacy rules. With a spouse and children, the spouse takes half and the children share half (755 ILCS 5/2-1), as explained on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). A spouse and adult children can become joint owners of a company none of them wants to run.

## Sole proprietor vs LLC vs corporation at death: what changes?

Each form leaves a different gap, and each gap has a document that closes it. The national guide to [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners) covers succession in depth; this is the Illinois short version.

| Entity | What happens at death | Who can act next | Document that covers it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | The business ends. Equipment, accounts, receivables and debts are yours personally and become part of your estate. | Your executor, after the court issues letters | Will or living trust holding the business assets, plus payable-on-death designations on accounts |
| Single-member LLC | The LLC survives, but you are dissociated as a member (805 ILCS 180/35-45). Nobody is automatically in charge. | A successor manager named in the operating agreement, or a successor trustee if your trust holds the interest | Operating agreement naming a successor manager, plus a living trust holding the membership interest |
| Multi-member LLC | The LLC continues. Your heirs generally get distributions, not a vote (805 ILCS 180/30-1). | The surviving members or the named manager | Operating agreement with death and transfer clauses, plus a funded buy-sell agreement |
| S corporation or corporation | The company continues. Your shares pass by will, trust or intestacy, and the board and officers keep their roles. | Remaining directors and officers, if the bylaws allow it | Bylaws or shareholder agreement with a buy-sell, plus a trust drafted for S corporation rules |
| Partnership | What happens depends heavily on the partnership agreement, which may require a buyout or a wind-down. | The surviving partners | Written partnership agreement with death, disability and buyout terms |

The LLC rows are explained on [what happens to my LLC if I die in Illinois](/learn/illinois/llc-owner-dies). Sole proprietors can read [what happens to a sole proprietorship at death](/learn/business-owners/what-happens-to-a-sole-proprietorship-at-death). For corporations, [what happens to your LLC or corporation shares when you die](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) covers shareholder agreements and board control.

In every row, the company documents usually control who may hold or manage the interest, even if your will says otherwise; see [does an operating agreement override your will](/learn/business-owners/does-an-operating-agreement-override-your-will). Loans you signed for personally follow you into the estate, as [personal guaranties and business debts at death](/learn/business-owners/personal-guaranties-and-business-debts-at-death) explains.

## Who runs the business if I am incapacitated?

Someone you named in advance, or a court-appointed guardian. A spouse, office manager or partner has no automatic power over your bank account, your share of the company or contracts you signed personally.

The main tool is the Illinois power of attorney for property. It must be signed with a witness and notarized (755 ILCS 45/3-3), and its powers can reach business operations. It does not let your agent make gifts, change beneficiaries or amend your trust unless the form gives that specific authority (755 ILCS 45/3-4). The [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) page walks through the form.

For a business owner, three additions make it work:

1. **Name the business.** Banks and vendors act more readily on a document that names your company and your interest.
2. **Consider a separate business agent.** A spouse can handle the household while a partner or manager handles payroll.
3. **Match the company documents.** The operating agreement or bylaws can name an acting manager if you cannot serve and say how incapacity is decided.

## Can my business go in a living trust?

Usually, yes. When your trust holds the business interest, your successor trustee can step in at death or incapacity without waiting for a probate court to issue letters. A certification of trust lets the trustee prove authority to a bank without showing the whole document, and Illinois protects a person who relies on one in good faith (760 ILCS 3/1013).

An LLC interest or corporate shares are assigned to the trust. A sole proprietor assigns the business assets themselves. A partnership interest can move only if the partnership agreement allows it.

<!-- visual: diagram name=TrustFundingAssets -->

Check these before you sign an assignment:

- Does the operating agreement, shareholder agreement or partnership agreement allow a transfer to a trust, or does it need consent?
- Does the transfer trigger a buyout under the buy-sell agreement?
- Do your loans or lease treat a change of owner as a default?
- If the company is an S corporation, does the trust qualify to hold the shares after your death?

An ineligible trust can end an S election; [S corporation shares inherited by a trust](/learn/business-owners/s-corporation-shares-inherited-by-a-trust) explains which trusts qualify. The general how-to is on [can I put my business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust), and the Illinois retitling steps are on [funding a living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/funding-a-living-trust-in-illinois).

If the business does go through probate, an [independent representative](/glossary/independent-representative) can manage and sell estate property without a court order for each step (755 ILCS 5/28-8), as [independent vs supervised administration in Illinois](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains. Someone still has to be appointed first, and that wait is long for a business that runs on weekly cash.

## Do I need a buy-sell agreement?

If you have any co-owner, almost certainly. A [buy-sell agreement](/glossary/buy-sell-agreement) says what happens to an owner's share at death, disability, divorce or departure. Without one, a partner's spouse or children can become your co-owners.

A working agreement answers four questions:

1. **Trigger.** Which events require or allow a purchase, including long-term disability.
2. **Price.** A fixed figure updated each year, a formula or an appraisal.
3. **Buyer.** The other owners (cross-purchase) or the company (redemption).
4. **Funding.** Usually life and disability insurance sized to the price.

The [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) guide covers the structure, and [cross-purchase vs entity redemption](/learn/business-owners/cross-purchase-vs-entity-redemption) compares the two designs and their tax treatment. For the disability trigger, see the [disability buyout provision](/learn/business-owners/disability-buyout-provision-when-a-business-partner-becomes-incapacitated) page. A sole owner needs a named successor or a plan to sell instead; see [how to choose who will run your business after you](/learn/business-owners/how-to-choose-who-will-run-your-business-after-you-family-or-outside-manager).

## What should an Illinois business owner's plan include?

The general [estate planning checklist for Illinois](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-checklist-illinois) covers the personal side. This one covers the business.

- [ ] Your entity documents are found and read: operating agreement, bylaws, shareholder or partnership agreement.
- [ ] The company documents name a successor manager, officer or signer.
- [ ] A durable Illinois power of attorney for property is signed, witnessed and notarized, and names the business.
- [ ] A backup signer is on file with each business bank.
- [ ] A will names an executor who can handle a business, or a living trust holds the business interest.
- [ ] The business interest or assets have actually been assigned to the trust, with any required consents.
- [ ] If the company is an S corporation, the trust has been checked for eligibility.
- [ ] With co-owners, a buy-sell agreement sets a trigger, price, buyer and funding source.
- [ ] Life insurance beneficiaries match the buy-sell terms; [key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance) is considered.
- [ ] Logins and a short operating memo are stored where your successor can reach them.
- [ ] Your total estate, business included, has been compared with the $4 million [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) threshold. If the company is most of it, read [when the business is most of your estate](/learn/business-owners/when-the-business-is-most-of-your-estate).

The [Illinois small business succession guide](/free/illinois-small-business-succession-guide) and the [buy-sell agreement checklist](/free/buy-sell-agreement-checklist) help you gather the documents first. ## What does this look like for a real business? A worked example

This is an example with invented names. Marisol, 51, owns a commercial cleaning company in Joliet with Kenji. It began as her sole proprietorship and became a two-member LLC, 60 percent hers and 40 percent his, with no operating agreement. Marisol has a 2012 will leaving everything to her husband, Arturo, and no power of attorney. A van and floor equipment are still titled in her own name.

If nothing changes and Marisol has a stroke, nobody can sign for her 60 percent until Arturo is appointed guardian of her estate. If she dies, her interest passes through probate to Arturo, who gets distributions without a vote. Kenji runs a company with a silent majority owner, and neither man can afford a buyout. The van and equipment go through probate separately.

Here is what Marisol and Kenji do instead:

1. They sign an operating agreement that names Kenji as acting manager if Marisol cannot serve, and allows each member's interest to be held in a living trust.
2. Marisol signs an Illinois power of attorney for property naming Arturo for personal matters and Kenji's sister, the company bookkeeper, for business matters.
3. She creates a living trust, assigns her LLC interest to it and moves the van and equipment into the LLC.
4. They sign a cross-purchase buy-sell agreement with a price they update each January, funded by life and disability policies on each other.
5. Marisol replaces the 2012 will with a pour-over will that sends anything left outside the trust into it.

If Marisol dies now, Kenji buys her share with the insurance, Arturo receives cash instead of a stake, and payroll never freezes.
