Many families are not built in a straight line. A man has a child from one marriage, then a daughter from another, and the siblings may have met only at funerals. When one of them dies without a will, people ask whether the half sibling counts.

In Illinois the answer is simple on paper and more detailed in practice. This page explains the rule, shows the shares with numbers, and covers the proof a half sibling needs. For the full order of heirs, read [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

## Do half siblings inherit in Illinois?

Yes. The Probate Act ends its list of heirs with a plain rule: there is no distinction between kindred of the whole blood and of the half blood (755 ILCS 5/2-1). A half sibling who is in the right position on the list takes exactly what a full sibling would take.

The words "right position" matter. Siblings are not first in line. They inherit only if the decedent leaves no spouse and no descendants. If the decedent has a spouse and no children, the spouse takes everything. If the decedent has children, the children come first. The sibling level is reached with the family situation in the next section. Our article on [dying with no spouse and no children](/learn/illinois/no-spouse-no-children) describes that group.

## How are the shares split with parents and half siblings?

When there is no spouse and no descendants, the estate goes to the parents and the brothers and sisters in equal parts. If only one parent survives, that parent takes a double share. The children of a dead sibling, whether whole or half blood, take their parent's share.

Take this example. Tom, 58, is single and has no children. His probate estate is $400,000. His father died years ago. His mother, Rita, is alive. His full brother Sam and his half sister Joy, who is his father's daughter from a second marriage, are alive.

1. Count shares: Rita takes two, Sam one, Joy one. That is four shares.
2. Divide $400,000 by four: $100,000 per share.
3. Rita receives $200,000. Sam receives $100,000. Joy receives $100,000.

Joy's half-blood status does not reduce her share by a cent.

| Who survives Tom | Shares | Result on $400,000 |
|---|---|---|
| Mother, full brother, half sister | 2 + 1 + 1 | $200,000, $100,000, $100,000 |
| Full brother, half sister (both parents dead) | 1 + 1 | $200,000 each |
| Half sister only, with a niece through a dead brother | 1 + 1 | $200,000 each |
| Spouse alive, no children | Spouse takes all | $400,000 to the spouse |
| Child alive | Child takes all | $400,000 to the child, siblings get nothing |

## What proof does a half sibling need?

A half sibling must show a legal link to the shared parent. For a mother, a birth certificate showing the mother's name is usually enough. For a father, it depends on how the child was born. If the parents were married, the marriage proves the link. If they were not, the statute lets a child born outside marriage inherit from the father only in listed ways, such as a court order, a signed acknowledgment or clear proof, and our page on [children born outside marriage](/learn/illinois/children-born-outside-marriage-inheritance-in-illinois) goes through them.

The estate's administrator and the court need names of all heirs. A half sibling who is not named in the petition can be missed. In an open case, the usual steps are:

1. Tell the estate's administrator or lawyer, in writing, that you are a half sibling.
2. Provide a certified birth certificate and any other proof of your link to the shared parent.
3. Ask whether an affidavit of heirship or a court finding of heirship will be used. See [proving heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois).
4. Watch for the notice that a petition was filed and respond by the date given.
5. Keep copies of everything you send.

## What if the decedent had a will?

The statute works only when there is no will. A will can leave everything to one half sibling, to a friend or to a charity, and can leave a full sibling nothing. Having a will that names relatives by their names, rather than as "my brothers and sisters", also keeps the question of half blood from coming up. A person who would be an heir under intestacy generally has standing to challenge a will, and the filing window is short. See our page on the [six-month will contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline).

Assets with beneficiary forms, joint ownership or a payable-on-death designation pass outside the estate. A half sibling named on an account gets it regardless of this statute, and a half sibling not named gets nothing from it. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) can sort which items the intestacy list reaches.

## What if the half sibling is estranged or unknown?

A half sibling the family has not met is still an heir. The administrator must make reasonable efforts to identify heirs, and the court may require notice by mail or publication. Families sometimes learn of a half sibling only when the court's heir list comes back. If that happens, do not hide the person, since a missed heir can reopen the case later. Our guide to [what happens when heirs cannot be found](/learn/what-if/heir-cannot-be-found-or-no-family-survives) covers the search.

## What are common mistakes families make about half siblings?

Families often assume that half siblings are "less" related and so take a half share. Illinois does not work that way, and a half sibling who is told otherwise may accept far less than the law gives. Another mistake is leaving a half sibling off the list of heirs because the family has never met. The court needs every heir named, and a missing one can later ask to reopen the estate. A third mistake is mixing up the sibling level with the spouse level: if the decedent has a spouse and no children, siblings of any kind take nothing, which surprises people who expected a share.

Ask the administrator for a copy of the heir list and compare it with your own family tree. If a name is missing, send a short written note with the supporting records. Keeping copies of what you send makes it easier to show that you acted promptly.
