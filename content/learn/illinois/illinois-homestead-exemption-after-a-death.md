When a spouse dies and the house is mortgaged, the debts are not far behind, and the surviving partner often asks the same thing: can anyone take the house? Part of the answer in Illinois is the homestead exemption. It is real, and it is small. This page explains what it protects, how it carries on after a death, and what it does not do.

It is general education, not legal advice. The statute is in the Code of Civil Procedure, Article XII, Part 9 (735 ILCS 5/12-901 and following). Our separate article on [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) covers the spouse's award and renouncing a will, so we do not repeat them here. The [Illinois pillar page](/learn/illinois) lists the rest of our Illinois guides.

## What does the Illinois homestead exemption protect?

It protects up to $15,000 of value in your residence from creditors. The statute gives each individual a homestead estate "to the extent in value of $15,000" in a qualifying interest. The interest can be a farm or lot with buildings, a condominium, personal property, or an interest in a cooperative that owns property you use as a residence. You must own it or lawfully possess it, for example under a lease, and occupy it as your residence (735 ILCS 5/12-901).

The protection is against attachment, judgment, levy and judgment sale. It also shields the homestead from the laws of conveyance, descent and legacy, except as the Code or section 20-6 of the Probate Act provides.

Two details change the math.

- It does not apply between joint tenants or tenants in common, but it does apply against their creditors.
- When two or more people co-own exempt homestead property, each person's exemption cannot exceed their share of a $30,000 total, based on ownership percentage.

The amount is set by statute and could change, so confirm the current number before relying on it. As of 2026 it is $15,000.

## Does it survive the owner's death?

Yes, for the people the statute names. The exemption survives the holder's death for the benefit of the surviving spouse, as long as the spouse continues to occupy the home, and for the children until the youngest becomes 18 (735 ILCS 5/12-902). The same section continues the exemption for whoever stays in the home as a residence if a spouse deserts the family.

Consider Rachel, 52, whose husband Ed dies with $38,000 in credit card and medical debt and a house worth $310,000 with a mortgage. Ed's creditors file claims in the estate. Whether the estate owes those debts is a separate question, covered in our article on [credit card debt after death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois). The homestead exemption comes into play only if a creditor tries to reach the house itself. It keeps $15,000 of equity out of reach, and only while Rachel lives there.

Notice what it does not say. It does not say the surviving spouse owns the house. It does not freeze the mortgage. And the statute speaks of a spouse and of children until the youngest turns 18, so do not assume it protects an adult child who inherits the house.

## What is the homestead exemption not good against?

Several debts can reach a homestead anyway (735 ILCS 5/12-903):

- Unpaid taxes or assessments.
- Debts tied to buying or improving the property, which is why a mortgage lender is not stopped by it.
- A lien under the Condominium Property Act (paragraph (g)(1) of section 9).
- Enforcement of certain eviction orders under the Code of Civil Procedure.

The exemption can also be waived. A release, waiver or conveyance is valid only if it is in writing and signed by the individual and any spouse (735 ILCS 5/12-904). Other valid routes include abandoning possession, giving possession under a conveyance, or continuing the exemption to a child without a court order. When a lender takes a mortgage, the release applies only to that mortgage.

If a homestead is sold to pay a debt, the balance after the debt and costs is exempt up to $15,000 and applied to the homestead exemption (735 ILCS 5/12-904).

## How does it work when the house is sold?

The proceeds stay protected for a time. Selling a homestead does not create new claims against the property beyond those that existed while the owner lived there. Sale proceeds are shielded from judgments and other legal process up to $15,000 for one year after receipt, and if the proceeds buy another homestead, that property gets the same protection as the original (735 ILCS 5/12-906).

The practical lesson: if a surviving spouse sells the family home to downsize, do not park the exempt money where it is hard to trace, and do not wait over a year to reinvest if a creditor threat exists.

| Situation | Homestead exemption effect |
|---|---|
| Judgment creditor of the owner tries to levy on the home | Up to $15,000 of value is protected |
| Surviving spouse keeps living in the home | Exemption continues for the spouse |
| Children at home when the owner dies | Exemption continues until the youngest turns 18 |
| Lender holding a purchase mortgage forecloses | Exemption does not block it |
| Unpaid property tax sale | Exemption does not block it |
| Home sold, spouse buys another home within a year | Proceeds up to $15,000 protected, new home gets same protection |

## What can the probate court do with a homestead?

When a real estate sale or mortgage is requested in a probate case, the court has power to set off the homestead exemption and sell the rest. If the property cannot be divided and its value exceeds the exemption, the court may sell the whole property free of the homestead, with or without the owner's consent, and pay the homestead's gross value to the person entitled to it (755 ILCS 5/20-6(d)).

The person entitled to the homestead still receives its value from the sale. Our article on the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) explains where a sale fits in an administration, and [what to do about a house in probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) explains the heirs' side.

## Why does a small exemption still matter?

Fifteen thousand dollars will not save a house worth several hundred thousand. Its value is mostly in the margins. It can keep a judgment creditor from forcing a sale when a home has little equity, and it gives a family breathing room while they work out a plan. For a home with real equity, an estate planning attorney will usually look at other tools, such as how the house is titled, a [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts), or an insurance and savings strategy, rather than lean on the exemption alone.

It also helps to know what the exemption is about. It limits what a creditor can take. It says nothing about who inherits the house. That is decided by the deed, the will, a trust or the intestacy rules in our article on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

## What should a surviving spouse do now?

1. Keep paying the mortgage, taxes and insurance. The exemption protects equity, not payment defaults.
2. Do not sign anything that waives or releases the homestead until you have had it read by an attorney.
3. Find out how the deed reads. If you held title as joint tenants or tenants by the entirety, read our page on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety), because the house may already be yours without probate.
4. If creditors write, do not ignore them, and do not pay old debts out of your own money under pressure. Ask an attorney who is handling the estate.
5. Think about the rest of your plan. Our article on [what to do when your spouse has died](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies) covers updating your documents.
