Helen, 79, was diagnosed with early Alzheimer's in March. In May she met her attorney and signed a new will that gave her house to her granddaughter, who had moved in to help. In September she died. Her son now says, "She could not have understood what she was signing."

The law asks a narrow question about that moment. This page explains the Illinois standard for testamentary capacity, how it differs from other kinds of capacity, how it is proved, and what a planner can do now. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) and the [will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) cover the basics.

## What does Illinois require for capacity to make a will?

The Probate Act says a person who is 18 or older and of sound mind and memory can make a will (755 ILCS 5/4-1). Courts have filled in that phrase. A person has capacity if, when signing, they could do three things.

1. Know the people who are the natural objects of their bounty, such as a spouse and children.
2. Understand in a general way what they own, without needing to list every account.
3. Make a plan for who gets it, and see how the will carries out that plan.

The signer does not need to be sharp in every way. They may forget a name, repeat a story or need help with a pill box. What matters is the three points above at the time of signing. Our glossary defines [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity).

## How is it different from other kinds of capacity?

Capacity depends on the task. The will standard is among the lower ones.

| Task | What it asks | Typical level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Make a will | Know family, know property in general, form a plan | Lower |
| Sign a power of attorney | Understand what an agent can do and whom you are choosing | Moderate |
| Sign a contract or deed | Understand the terms and what you give up | Higher |
| Manage all finances alone | Handle ongoing money matters | Highest |

So a person may no longer manage a checkbook and still make a valid will. The reverse matters too: a will made when the person understood the plan can stand even if they lost capacity the next week. See the page on [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).

## Does dementia or a guardianship change the rule?

Dementia alone does not. A diagnosis shows risk, but the court looks at the day of signing, and a person with dementia may have lucid periods. For more, read [can a will be contested because of dementia](/learn/what-if/can-a-will-be-contested-because-of-dementia).

Guardianship does change the rule. Under 755 ILCS 5/4-1, a will signed after a court has appointed a guardian is presumed invalid. The person defending it must show by clear and convincing evidence that the maker had capacity when signing. Read about guardianship in [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship).

## Who must prove what in an Illinois will contest?

The person challenging a will bears the burden of showing it is not valid, and the proponent usually proves the signing through the witnesses (755 ILCS 5/6-4 and 8-1). Either side can ask for a jury. A contest must be filed within 6 months after the will is admitted. Our article on the [six-month deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) explains how to count it.

Capacity and [undue influence](/learn/illinois/undue-influence-will-challenges-in-illinois) are separate claims. Helen's son could argue both, but the proof is different. One asks about her mind. The other asks whether someone replaced her choices with theirs.

## What evidence shows capacity or the lack of it?

The strongest evidence comes from the signing day and the months around it.

- Statements of the two witnesses, which the attestation clause records.
- The attorney's notes of what the client said about family and property.
- Medical records and cognitive test scores near the date.
- Whether the plan fits earlier wills and long-held statements.
- Testimony from friends and neighbors who spoke with the person that week.
- Whether the maker could explain the plan in their own words.

Evidence of a diagnosis helps the challenger, but only partly. A finding of confusion in the hospital a month earlier says little about a clear Tuesday morning.

## What steps protect a will if capacity may be questioned?

1. Pick a time of day when the person is most alert, and avoid new medicines or hospital days.
2. Meet the attorney alone first so they can ask open questions about family and property.
3. Ask the doctor for a short letter on that day, noting any test given.
4. Choose witnesses who can speak clearly about what they saw, and who gain nothing.
5. Have witnesses sign an attestation clause that states the maker seemed of sound mind (755 ILCS 5/6-4).
6. Keep notes, the letter and the drafts in the file and tell the executor where they are.

## What is a lucid interval, and how do courts treat it?

A lucid interval is a period of clear thinking inside a longer illness. Courts accept that a person with dementia, a brain injury or severe depression may have good and bad hours. A will signed in a clear stretch can be valid even if the person was confused the day before. The challenger must show confusion at the time of signing, not around it. That is why a note from the signing day carries so much weight, and why a rushed signing in a hospital at night draws more suspicion than a mid-morning appointment at the attorney's office.

For Helen, her attorney's notes could be decisive. If they record that she named both children, described the house and a $38,000 savings account, and explained why her granddaughter should have the home, that is direct evidence of the three points above. Without such notes, the family would rely on memory years later.

## What should a family do if they doubt a will?

Check the admission date first. Get the attorney's name, ask for the medical records through the proper channels and talk to an attorney before the third month.
