You have the Illinois statutory short form in front of you and a pen. The form looks simple, but each paragraph does a different job, and a missed initial or a blank line can cause trouble at the bank months later. This page goes through the form in the order you meet it.

Our overview, [the Illinois statutory power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property), explains what the document is and why witnesses matter. This page is for the moment of filling it out. The [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links the rest of the documents you may need.

## What does the form do from top to bottom?

The form appears in the Illinois Power of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45/3-3). It begins with a notice to you, the person signing, then the grant of powers, then a series of numbered paragraphs, and ends with signature blocks. The paragraph numbers and wording can change when the law is amended, so check the current text on ilga.gov before using an older printed copy.

The notice to you says in plain terms that the form lets you name successor agents but not co-agents, that you can cross out or limit powers, and that you can strike the paragraph on compensation. Read that notice. It tells you what the form allows you to change.

## How do you fill it out, step by step?

1. **Name the agent.** Write the full legal name and address of one person. Do not name two people here. Co-agents are not allowed on the short form, as our article on [co-agents and successor agents in Illinois](/learn/illinois/co-agents-and-successor-agents-in-illinois) explains.
2. **Initial the powers.** The list covers categories such as real estate, financial institutions, stocks and bonds, tangible personal property, safe deposit box, insurance and annuities, retirement plans, government benefits, tax matters, claims and litigation, business operations, borrowing, estate transactions and all other property. Initial each one you grant. Cross out or leave blank the rest.
3. **Write limits.** Use the exceptions space for anything you want held back, such as "no sale of my home without a written appraisal".
4. **Add extra powers.** Gift powers, trust funding and beneficiary changes are not part of the standard list. If you want them, the next section covers how to write them.
5. **Set the start.** Choose immediate effect, or describe a date or event.
6. **Set an end date** if you want one. Most people leave this blank.
7. **Name successors.** List them in the order they should step in.
8. **Decide on pay.** Strike the compensation paragraph if the agent should only be reimbursed.
9. **Sign before one witness and a notary,** and have the agent sign the specimen signature line.

## Which powers should you initial?

Initial the powers your agent will actually need in the next ten years, not every line. A person managing a parent's bills needs the financial institution, government benefits, tax matters and insurance powers. A real estate power matters only if there is property to buy, sell or refinance.

Some powers carry more risk. The business operations and borrowing powers let an agent run a company or take out loans in your name. The claims and litigation power lets an agent start or settle lawsuits. If you do not own a business, or do not want debt taken on, leave those blank.

| Part of the form | What you do | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Agent line | Write one full legal name | Writing "my children" |
| Power categories | Initial each power you grant | Initialing all, then not reading them |
| Exceptions | Write limits in plain words | Writing limits that conflict with a power |
| Added powers | Spell out gifts, trust funding | Assuming gifts are included |
| Effective date | Choose immediate or future | Leaving both options marked |
| Successors | List in order | Leaving the line blank |
| Compensation | Strike or keep | Forgetting the paragraph exists |
| Signatures | Sign with witness and notary | Notary only, no witness |

## How do you add gift or trust powers?

Write them in the space for added powers, in specific words. The Act says an agent cannot make gifts, appoint property to others or change a beneficiary under the listed powers (755 ILCS 45/3-4). Our article on [gifting and beneficiary change powers](/learn/illinois/illinois-poa-gifting-and-beneficiary-change-powers) shows sample limits. For example, Marisol, 74, writes: "My agent may give each of my three grandchildren up to $19,000 per year, but not to the agent."

## When should the power start and end?

Choose "effective immediately" if you trust your agent and want to avoid proof-of-incapacity delays. A future-date or event-based start protects privacy but often delays help, because a bank may ask for a doctor's letter. Our comparison of [springing versus immediate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) covers the tradeoffs.

The form lets you add a termination date. If you add one, the power stops on that date even if you are ill, so most people skip it. The power ends at death in either case.

## What about successors and pay?

Name at least one successor. If your agent dies, resigns or cannot serve and no one else is named, no one holds your authority, and your family may need a guardianship. On pay, the form states that the agent is entitled to reimbursement of reasonable expenses, and that the agent is also entitled to reasonable compensation unless you strike that paragraph. Our article on [paying or reimbursing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-agent-be-paid-or-reimbursed) explains the choices.

## How should you sign it?

Sign in front of a witness and a notary at the same sitting. The witness cannot be your agent or a successor, and certain relatives and providers are excluded. Our overview lists who. Have the notary complete the certificate and keep the original in a safe place, as described in [where to keep your power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/where-to-keep-your-power-of-attorney-after-you-sign-it).

Give your bank a copy now. For real estate, a county recorder may need the document recorded, so ask the recorder in your county, such as [Cook](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/cook) or [DuPage](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/dupage), what they require.

## Example: Walter fills out the form

Walter, 81, wants his daughter Lena to pay bills and manage his IRA. He initials financial institutions, retirement plans, government benefits and tax matters. He crosses out business operations and borrowing, strikes the compensation paragraph, and names his son Paul as the successor. He writes "no sale of my house without a written appraisal" in the limits. He signs with a neighbor as witness and a notary. ## What mistakes do banks see most often?

Banks and county recorders reject forms for small, avoidable reasons. Knowing them lets you check your own draft before signing.

- **Blank or unclear initials.** If a power is initialed but a line through it also appears, the reader cannot tell what you meant. Use one clear mark per power.
- **Limits that cancel the power.** Writing "no banking" in the exceptions space while initialing the financial institution power leaves the agent with no clear authority.
- **Agent signs as witness.** The witness must be someone other than the agent or a successor.
- **No notary seal.** The statutory form for property needs both a witness and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3).
- **Old addresses.** A bank that cannot match the agent's name and address to an ID may pause the account.

Read the finished form aloud once. If a sentence confuses you, it will confuse a teller. A short cover letter that says "this is the Illinois statutory short form, signed with one witness and a notary" also helps.
