You paid for a living trust, or you are thinking about one, and someone told you the house has to be "put in" it. That is true, and it is the step most often left undone. In Illinois it comes down to a deed, a recording and a handful of phone calls.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is the Illinois walk-through. For the general version of these steps, see [how to transfer a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust). If you want an Illinois estate planning attorney to prepare the deed and check the title and loan, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-to-put-your-house-in-a-living-trust-in-illinois&topic=trust).

## Why does an Illinois house need a deed to go into a trust?

Because ownership of real estate is shown by the recorded deed, not by your trust agreement. An Illinois living trust under the Trust Code (760 ILCS 3) can list the house on a schedule, but real estate generally needs a signed deed to move into the trust, as our [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) page explains. If the deed still names you, the house is likely a probate asset at death. That is the result most people sign a trust to prevent.

## What are the steps to put your house in a living trust in Illinois?

Follow these in order.

1. **Finish the trust.** It should be signed, dated and notarized, with an exact name, such as "The Linda Okafor Revocable Living Trust dated November 5, 2026." The Illinois Code says a trust is revocable only if the document says so (760 ILCS 3/602), so check that language.
2. **Pull your current deed.** Get the legal description and the parcel number (PIN) from the recorder's website or your closing file. Do not retype the street address as a substitute.
3. **Have the new deed prepared.** The grantor is you and the grantee is you as trustee of the named trust. An Illinois attorney chooses the deed form with the title insurance in mind. Our page on [types of deeds](/learn/property-and-assets/types-of-deeds-quitclaim-warranty-grant-and-trustee-deeds) compares the common forms.
4. **Sign before a notary.** Recording needs an acknowledgment.
5. **Ask the recorder what else is needed.** Ask about cover sheets, transfer declarations, exemption statements and the fee.
6. **Record the deed with the county recorder** where the house sits.
7. **Make the follow-up calls** to the lender, insurance agent, title insurer and assessor, covered below.
8. **File the recorded deed with the trust,** and tell your successor trustee where both are.

## Does the deed to the trust get recorded, and where?

Yes, recorded, and with the county recorder in the county where the land is. A deed that is signed but not recorded can leave the public record showing you as the owner. If you own a second property, each parcel needs its own deed in its own county. A trust helps with a house in another state only if the deed there is recorded too, as discussed in [Illinois residents owning property in another state](/learn/illinois/illinois-resident-owning-property-in-another-state).

## Will I keep my homestead exemption and property tax breaks?

Usually yes, if you still live in the house and the trust is your own revocable trust. Property tax exemptions generally follow the person who lives there and holds the beneficial interest, but some assessors ask you to certify that after a deed is recorded. Call the assessor before you record and ask what the office needs. Our article on [homestead and property taxes with a living trust](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-affect-homestead-exemption-and-property-taxes) has a checklist.

The Illinois word "homestead" also covers a separate creditor protection of up to $15,000 of value in your residence (735 ILCS 5/12-901). A trust does not add to it. The law also lets a spouse and children keep it after a death in some cases (735 ILCS 5/12-902), covered in the [homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death) page.

## What happens to my mortgage?

For most owner-occupied homes of one to four units, the loan stays as it is. The federal Garn-St Germain Act (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)(8)) generally prevents a lender from enforcing a due-on-sale clause when you transfer your residence into a living trust where you remain a beneficiary and occupy the home. The protection has limits, because it does not cover every property type or a trust that benefits someone else. Read your loan papers, send the lender a short notice with a certification of trust, and keep what the lender sends back. The [certification of trust](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-certification-of-trust) article explains that document. If you refinance later, tell the lender the title is in a trust, since some ask for a temporary deed back. If they do, record a new deed putting the house back in the trust after closing, as covered in [selling or refinancing a house in a living trust](/learn/property-and-assets/can-you-sell-or-refinance-a-house-that-is-in-your-living-trust).

## What about homeowners and title insurance?

Both are worth a call. Tell your homeowners agent that the trustee now holds title, so the owner named on the policy matches the owner of record. Whether your owner's title policy continues after the transfer depends on the policy wording and the insurer's practice, so ask the title company or your attorney and keep the answer in writing. Insurers vary here, and the deed form can matter.

## What about married couples, condos and land trusts?

Married owners should talk to their attorney about how title is held. Illinois lets spouses hold their homestead as tenants by the entirety, including through a revocable trust where the married settlors are the primary beneficiaries and the deed says so (765 ILCS 1005/1c). That choice affects survivorship, and our [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) page explains it.

A condo is deeded like a house, after you check the association's rules. A co-op uses an assignment of shares, not a deed, so start with [Chicago condo and co-op](/learn/illinois/chicago-condo-and-co-op). If your home is already in a land trust, the beneficial interest is what you assign, and [land trust vs living trust](/learn/illinois/land-trust-vs-living-trust) sorts out which document does what.

## What happens if you forget to put the house in the trust?

The house stays in your name, and your family will probably need probate for it. The trust does not control it, a successor trustee cannot sell it, and the small estate affidavit cannot transfer real estate (755 ILCS 5/25-1). A pour-over will can send it to the trust, but only after the probate court has acted. Our [probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) page shows what that involves, and [how to tell if your trust is actually funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded) is a quick test.

## Is a transfer on death instrument a simpler choice?

Sometimes. If the house is your only asset that needs planning, a recorded transfer on death instrument passes it without probate and costs less in effort, and it is covered in [the Illinois TODI page](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument). It does not help if you lose capacity, because an agent cannot sign or revoke one for you. A trust covers incapacity and death, which is why people with several assets choose it. If you want a broader map, read [how to avoid probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-avoid-probate-in-illinois).

## Worked example: Linda and her Oak Park house

This is an invented example. Linda, 70, is single and lives in an Oak Park house with a small mortgage. She signs a living trust naming her daughter Grace as successor trustee. She forgets the deed for two years.

A friend asks whether Linda funded it. Linda finds the legal description, has a deed to herself as trustee prepared, signs it before a notary and records it with the county recorder. She sends the lender a notice with a certification of trust, adds the trustee to the homeowners policy, and calls the assessor about her senior exemption. She files the recorded deed in her trust binder. Had she died before the deed, Grace would have faced probate for the house.

## A checklist before you record

- The trust is signed and says it is revocable.
- The legal description and PIN come from the current deed.
- The trust name on the deed matches the trust exactly.
- The lender, insurance agent, title insurer and assessor each answered in writing.
- Every co-owner and spouse on title has been asked to sign if needed.
- The recorded deed is stored with the trust.
