You have just found out that a parent's will was filed with the court, and something about it feels wrong. Maybe the will leaves everything to one child, or it was signed while your father was already confused. Before you worry about proof, there is a more urgent question in Illinois: how long do you have?

The answer is short, and it is strict. This page explains the six-month period in the Probate Act, who gets notice, how a contest is tried and what an executor must do when one is filed. For the larger probate picture, see [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) or our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## The short version

- File within 6 months after the will is admitted to probate (755 ILCS 5/8-1).
- The date of death does not start the clock. The date of admission does.
- Not being told about the case does not buy more time.
- Talk to an attorney before the third month if you are thinking about a contest, because gathering medical records and witnesses takes weeks.

## How long do you have to contest a will in Illinois?

Six months from the date the will is admitted to probate. Section 8-1 says an interested person may file a petition to contest the validity of a will within 6 months after its admission. That applies to wills admitted under section 6-4, to wills admitted under the Electronic Wills, Electronic Estate Planning Documents, and Remote Witnesses Act (sections 20-20 or 20-25), and to foreign wills admitted under Article VII (755 ILCS 5/8-1).

The admission date is on the court's docket, so you can find it. Compare it to the date on a letter if you got one, because they are often different. A family that waits for the lawyer's letter before counting may already be weeks behind.

## What if no one told you the will was filed?

You still have only the 6 months. The statute says that failure to notify an heir or legatee does not extend the deadline (755 ILCS 5/8-1). A related line says that failing to mail or deliver a copy of a contest petition to an heir or legatee does not affect the validity of the judgment.

That is harsh, so this is where people get hurt. Suppose Ruth lives in Arizona and her brother handles their mother's estate in Illinois. He mentions in March that "the will is in court." Ruth assumes she will receive formal papers. Nobody sends them. If the will was admitted in February, her six months ends in August whether or not anyone told her anything.

If you suspect a will has been filed, our guide on [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) explains where to look.

## Who counts as an interested person?

The statute uses the phrase without a long definition, so the practical test is whether you stand to gain or lose from the outcome. A child who would inherit under intestacy but gets nothing under the will is the standard example. So is a beneficiary of an earlier will who was cut out of the later one. A person who stands to receive the same share either way has little reason to sue.

The right to contest also survives the death of the person who held it, and passes to their heir, legatee, representative, grantee or assignee (755 ILCS 5/8-1). If your sister planned to contest and then died, her estate or heirs may continue.

Whether someone is interested is a legal question. The [who-inherits tool](/tools/who-inherits) can show you who would take without the will, which is the first step in working out whether you have a stake.

## Where and how is a contest filed?

In the pending estate proceeding. If no proceeding is pending, the petition goes to the court that admitted the will (755 ILCS 5/8-1). Here is the sequence in order.

1. Get a copy of the will and the order admitting it, and note the admission date.
2. Calendar the six-month date and a safe filing date weeks before it.
3. Gather the facts that support invalidity, such as medical records, witness names and drafts.
4. File the petition in the open estate, or with the court that admitted the will if none is pending.
5. Serve a copy on the representative, the representative's attorney of record, and each heir and legatee named in the admission petition or any amended petition under section 6-11. Service is by mail or delivery to the address in the petition.
6. Expect the representative to defend, and decide whether to demand a jury.

Filing a pleading waives notice to the person who files it, so once you appear you will be in the loop.

## What does the court actually decide?

The issue is whether the document produced is the testator's will. The contestant presents evidence first to show it is not valid, and the proponent then presents evidence supporting it. Either side may demand a jury (755 ILCS 5/8-1). Authenticated transcripts of testimony given at the admission hearing, and certain affidavits, can be used as evidence.

The usual grounds are the ones in our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will), such as lack of capacity, [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) or a signing that did not meet the formal rules. For what Illinois requires of a valid will, see [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements). Being unhappy with an unequal split is not a ground by itself. A person can generally leave property to some children and not others.

## What must the executor do when a contest is filed?

Defend it. The statute directs the representative to defend the contest, and the court can order the representative to defend or to pursue an appeal. If the representative refuses to act or fails to follow an order, or if nobody is acting, the court may appoint a special administrator on its own motion or on the request of an interested person (755 ILCS 5/8-1).

This matters if the executor is also the main winner under the will. They can be both defender and beneficiary, which can strain relations. Our article on [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) covers when that tension becomes a problem.

## Does the 6 months also apply to a revocable trust?

For one specific situation, yes. If a revocable trust or declaration of trust gives a legacy under an admitted will, a contest of that trust has to be brought within the same period, set by section 8-1(a) and section 13-223 of the Code of Civil Procedure (755 ILCS 5/8-1). We read only the Probate Act text, not the Code of Civil Procedure section, so a trust dispute is a clear signal to get legal advice quickly. Our general piece on [contesting a living trust](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested) lays out the trust side.

## What about a no-contest clause?

Some wills say a beneficiary who challenges the will loses their gift. That is a separate matter from the deadline. See [no-contest clauses](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) to weigh the risk. If you stand to receive little under the will anyway, the clause costs you less than if you are a major beneficiary.

## How can you avoid a contest in your own planning?

Most contests come from surprise. A parent who explains an unequal split while healthy, signs with proper witnesses and keeps medical evidence of capacity makes a challenge harder and less likely. A trust can keep matters private, though it does not make a plan contest-proof. Our attorneys often suggest a short letter explaining the reasons, which belongs outside the will.
