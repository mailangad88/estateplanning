Sometimes an inheritance is a burden. The house needs a new roof and has back taxes, a gift would push you into a tax problem, or you simply want your share to go to your own children instead. Illinois has a statute that lets you refuse property, if you do it the right way.

This page walks through the Illinois disclaimer statute, what the document must say, where it goes, what the property does afterward and which common acts quietly close the door. For the general idea, see our article on [how to disclaim an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance). The rest of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers neighboring topics.

## The short version

- A disclaimer is a signed writing that describes what you are refusing (755 ILCS 5/2-7).
- Once valid, it cannot be undone.
- The property moves as though you had died first. You do not pick the next person.
- Taking the property or its benefits first can ruin your right to refuse.

## Who can disclaim under Illinois law?

Any person to whom property or an interest passes, by any means, may disclaim it (755 ILCS 5/2-7). That reaches beyond a will. A gift under a trust, an interest passing by survivorship, and property that comes to you as an heir all fit. The statute also treats a powerholder as holding an interest in the property subject to the power.

The right applies even where a spendthrift provision or similar restriction limits the disclaimant's interest. So a trust beneficiary whose share is protected from creditors can still refuse it.

## Can you disclaim just part of an inheritance?

Yes. The statute permits a partial disclaimer, including a fractional or undivided share, a specific asset or amount, a limited interest or estate, or an interest acquired by survivorship (755 ILCS 5/2-7). A son who inherits half of his mother's estate could refuse one-half of his half, or only a rental property that comes with trouble.

Partial disclaimers are the tool people use when they want to keep the cash and leave the problem asset. They also let a person pass on enough of an inheritance to keep a federal tax result, though the tax side deserves a conversation with an advisor. Federal rules for a "qualified" disclaimer, generally requiring written refusal within nine months and no acceptance of benefits, are separate from the Illinois statute.

## What must the disclaimer say?

Three things. It must describe the property, part or interest being disclaimed, be signed by the disclaimant or the disclaimant's representative, and declare the disclaimer and its extent (755 ILCS 5/2-7). A one-page document can do that. Banks and title companies may want more detail.

## Where do you deliver or file it?

Delivery comes first. You deliver the disclaimer to the person who made the transfer or their representative, or to the trustee or other person holding legal title. If none of those is readily identifiable, you can deliver it to a person in possession of the interest, or to a person entitled to it because of the disclaimer, or file or record it as described below (755 ILCS 5/2-7).

Filing is the safety belt. The statute describes two options:

| Property | Where an executed counterpart may go |
|---|---|
| Interest passing by death | Circuit court clerk in the county where the estate is administered or, if administration has not begun, where it could be started |
| Disclaimed real estate | County recorder, or the registrar of titles for registered land |

Anyone can presume, absent actual knowledge to the contrary, that a properly delivered or filed disclaimer is valid and not barred. That helps a title company or bank rely on it.

## What happens to the property after you disclaim?

Unless the transferring document says otherwise, the property passes as if the disclaimant had died before the relevant date. For a death transfer that date is the date of death. For a revocable transfer it is the date the maker can no longer take full ownership back, and for other lifetime transfers it is the transfer date (755 ILCS 5/2-7). The disclaimer relates back to that date for all purposes.

So the question "who gets it?" is answered by the will, trust or intestacy rules, with you removed. Take Anita, who is named in her uncle's will to receive the house, with a gift over to her daughter Lila if Anita does not survive. If Anita disclaims, the house passes as if she had died first. Lila gets it, because the will says so. If there were no gift over, the house would fall into the residue or follow intestacy, and it might land on a cousin Anita never intended.

Future interests also speed up, unless the instrument says otherwise. A future interest that follows the disclaimed estate moves into possession as if the disclaimant had died earlier. For who steps in when someone is out of the picture, read [lapsed gifts and simultaneous death](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death) and our piece on [Illinois intestacy](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

A disclaimant is not barred from receiving the same property in some other capacity, or from receiving other interests in it.

## What can bar your right to disclaim?

Four things, according to the statute (755 ILCS 5/2-7):

1. A judicial sale of the property before the disclaimer is made.
2. An assignment, conveyance, encumbrance, pledge, sale or other transfer, or a contract for one, by the disclaimant or the disclaimant's representative.
3. A written waiver of the right.
4. Acceptance by the disclaimant or the disclaimant's representative.

Acceptance has to be affirmatively proven. It includes taking possession, accepting delivery or receiving benefits. Lapse of time alone, or the mere creation of the interest, is not acceptance. For joint tenancy with survivorship, acceptance reaches only a fraction equal to one divided by the number of joint tenants, and for a ward, only property actually received for the ward.

Think about what that means in daily life. Wendell inherits a rental house and collects the first month of rent. That could be receiving benefits. A cousin who lists the house for sale, or signs a contract to sell, risks the transfer bar. If you are even considering a disclaimer, stop touching the asset and call a lawyer first.

## Is there a deadline in the Illinois statute?

The text we read does not set a specific deadline. That does not mean you have unlimited time. The bars above can arrive quietly, and federal tax treatment has its own deadline, as noted above. Income, estate and gift tax consequences vary, and some benefits programs treat a refusal differently. Do not assume the Illinois statute is the only rule that matters.

## Can an executor or guardian disclaim for someone else?

A representative of a decedent or a ward may disclaim for them with court leave. For a decedent's estate, the court must find that the disclaimer benefits the estate and interested persons generally, even if it changes who receives the property. For a ward, the court must find it benefits interested persons generally and is not materially detrimental to the ward. No leave is needed if a will or other instrument signed by the decedent or ward specifically authorizes the representative to disclaim without court approval (755 ILCS 5/2-7).

This is why some estate planners include disclaimer authority in a power of attorney. See our article on [Illinois powers of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).

## Is a disclaimer the same as giving the property away?

No. A gift is a transfer by you, and it is generally treated as the property having reached you first. A disclaimer treats you as never having received it. That difference is why the statute is strict about acceptance and why a disclaimer is not a way to direct the property to a favorite relative. If you want to choose the recipient, you are talking about a gift and about [gift tax rules](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion), a separate path.
