Linda is 61 and remarried five years ago. She has two grown sons from her first marriage. Her second husband, Ray, has a daughter. They live in Ray's house in Elmhurst, and Linda has her own savings and a 401(k). Linda assumes her sons will inherit from her, and Ray assumes his daughter will inherit from him. Neither has written anything down.

That is a blended family with no plan, and Illinois law will fill in the blanks. This page explains what those defaults do, what a spouse can claim by renouncing the will, and how to protect the children you love without leaving your spouse stranded. Read the numbers below with your own family in mind. To plan with an attorney, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-blended-family-inheritance&topic=trust). This is general education, not legal advice.

## Do stepchildren inherit in Illinois?

No, not on their own. The intestacy rules in the Probate Act list a spouse, descendants, parents, siblings and more distant blood relatives (755 ILCS 5/2-1). Stepchildren are not on that list unless the stepparent adopted them. A will, a trust or a beneficiary form is the only way to include them. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) has the full order of heirs, and the general article on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) covers ways to include them.

The same rule runs both directions. A stepparent who dies without a will leaves nothing to a stepchild. And a stepchild's own estate does not go to a stepparent.

## What happens in a second marriage if you have no plan?

Say Linda dies first with no will. She leaves a spouse and descendants, so the estate is split one-half to Ray and one-half to her two sons, shared per stirpes (755 ILCS 5/2-1). That sounds fair until you follow the money.

Ray now owns half of what had been Linda's. When Ray dies, what he got from Linda goes where Ray says or, with no will, to his own heirs. Her sons may see none of it. Ray's daughter may receive all of it. This is the pattern behind most blended family disputes: not cruelty, just the default.

| If Linda dies with... | Ray gets | Her sons get | Ray's daughter gets |
|---|---|---|---|
| No will | One-half of the probate estate | One-half, split between them | Nothing |
| A will leaving all to her sons | Nothing under the will, but he can renounce | Everything, reduced by any renunciation | Nothing |
| A trust for Ray for life, then her sons | Income and use for life | The rest after Ray's death | Nothing, unless Linda names her |

Only assets in the probate estate follow those rules. A joint account, a retirement account with a beneficiary and property held in joint tenancy go by their own paperwork. That is why a blended family plan starts with a list of titles and beneficiary forms, not with the will.

## Can my spouse renounce my will in Illinois?

Yes. If you leave your spouse little or nothing, your spouse can renounce the will and take a share set by law. If you leave descendants, the share is one-third of the entire estate after claims are paid. If you leave none, it is one-half (755 ILCS 5/2-8). The renunciation must be filed in the court that admitted the will within 7 months after the will is admitted, unless the court extends the time.

Consider an example. Marcus dies with a will that leaves his $900,000 estate to his two children from his first marriage. His second wife, Dana, is not mentioned. Dana renounces. Because Marcus left descendants, she takes one-third, which is $300,000. His children share the remaining $600,000, less the gifts reduced to cover her share.

Two points matter for planning:

- **A renunciation bars any claim under the will.** If the will gave Dana a life estate in the home plus income from a trust, she has to choose one or the other. Our guide to [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) walks through the steps and the spouse's award, which she keeps either way.
- **The right can be waived in writing.** A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement can change it. See [prenuptial agreements and inheritance rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-prenup-and-inheritance-rights).

So a will that leaves everything to your children is not a safe way to protect them. If you want to leave your spouse less than a third, the way to do it is a signed agreement, not silence.

## How do you protect your children and your spouse at the same time?

The most common answer is a trust that supports your spouse for life and then passes what is left to your children. The trust names a trustee, ideally someone neutral or one of your children, who must follow the terms. Your spouse gets income or use of the home. Your children get the remainder. The spouse cannot redirect it later.

Here is the usual sequence.

1. List every asset and how it is titled: house, bank accounts, retirement plans, life insurance, business.
2. Decide what your spouse needs to live on and what you want to reach your children.
3. Choose a trust structure. Our article on the [A-B trust for a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/a-b-trust-for-a-second-marriage) shows one common form.
4. Name a trustee who can be fair to both sides.
5. Retitle accounts and update beneficiary forms so they match the trust.
6. Ask your spouse to review the plan and, if needed, sign a waiver of the renunciation right.

The renunciation share and the trust should be considered together. If the trust gives your spouse real value, renouncing becomes less appealing. If it gives almost nothing, your spouse may renounce and defeat the plan.

### What about the family home?

The house is where most blended families get stuck. A life estate or a trust-held home lets the surviving spouse stay for life and still passes the home to your children later. The [family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) article compares the options. In Illinois, also look at how the deed is held. A deed that says joint tenancy sends the whole house to the survivor, no matter what the will says. See [Illinois joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).

## What breaks a blended family plan?

- **Joint titles that undo the will.** Retitle the house or accounts, or the trust has nothing to control.
- **Old beneficiary forms.** An ex-spouse or a parent can still be named on a retirement account.
- **A joint trust with no locks.** The survivor can often amend it and disinherit your children.
- **Naming the new spouse as the only trustee.** Your spouse then decides what your children receive, which invites suspicion.
- **No conversation at all.** Silence among the adults is how a grief becomes a lawsuit.

## What if your children might contest the plan?

An heir who would gain if the will failed can challenge it, and the deadline is six months after the will is admitted. A plan that is explained in advance, properly signed and consistent with the paperwork is harder to attack.
