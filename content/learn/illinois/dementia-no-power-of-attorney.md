Your parent was just diagnosed, or the diagnosis is a few years old, and you find out nobody ever signed a power of attorney. The bank will not talk to you. A bill is overdue. You want to know if it is too late and if you need to go to court.

The honest answer is that it depends on your parent, and an Illinois attorney has to assess it in person. This page, part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), explains how to tell which path you are on. To get started, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-dementia-no-power-of-attorney&topic=parent) for you and your parent. For the general national picture, see our page on [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney). This one is about Illinois.

## Is it too late to sign a power of attorney?

Not if your parent can still understand the document. Illinois has no number or test score that ends the ability to sign. The question is whether your parent, on the day of signing, understands that they are giving someone authority over their money, who that person is, and roughly what they own. A bad day is not the same as lost capacity, and a good morning does not prove it either.

There are three common situations:

| What you see | What it may mean | Likely path |
|---|---|---|
| Your parent follows a conversation, names who they trust and explains their accounts | Capacity to sign may still exist | See an Illinois attorney soon and sign the full set |
| Good days and bad days | Capacity may come and go | Attorney assessment, ideally on a good day, with a doctor's note |
| Your parent cannot follow what the papers do | Signing is probably not possible | Guardianship or a narrower tool |

Capacity is a legal and medical judgment. No one in the family, and no bank teller, should decide it. The attorney who meets your parent makes the call and writes down what they saw.

## Which Illinois documents should a parent sign?

Two documents do different jobs, and Illinois keeps them separate.

- **Power of attorney for property.** It lets an agent handle money, bills and the house. The statutory short form needs the parent's signature, one witness who is not the agent, and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3). See [the Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
- **Power of attorney for health care.** It lets an agent speak with doctors and decide about treatment if the parent cannot. See [the Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

Choose a power that starts now. A document that waits for proof of incapacity forces the family to find a doctor's letter at the worst time. The page on [springing versus immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) explains the tradeoff.

Pick the witness with care. The statute excludes the agent, successor agents, the parent's attending physician or mental health provider and certain relatives. If the signing is ever challenged, the witness and the attorney's notes are the evidence.

## What if capacity is gone?

Then nobody can sign for your parent, and broad authority comes from a court. Illinois adult guardianship is under Article XIa of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/11a). A judge must find that your parent is a person with a disability, meaning an adult who, because of mental deterioration or physical incapacity, is not fully able to manage their person or estate, and the finding must rest on clear and convincing evidence (755 ILCS 5/11a-3).

The sequence is predictable:

1. A reputable person files a petition in the circuit court.
2. A doctor examines your parent. The report is filed within 3 months of the petition.
3. Your parent receives the petition and summons at least 14 days before the hearing, and listed relatives get notice too (755 ILCS 5/11a-10).
4. The court appoints a guardian ad litem to investigate. Your parent may attend, hire a lawyer and ask for a jury.
5. At the hearing, the judge decides whether guardianship is needed and how much. A limited guardianship can leave your parent with decisions they can still make (755 ILCS 5/11a-3).
6. A guardian of the person and a guardian of the estate then report to the court. The court may require a bond.

The detailed steps, with deadlines, are in our [Illinois guardianship guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship), and a free [Illinois adult guardianship process guide](/free/illinois-adult-guardianship-process-guide) puts them in order.

### How long does it take, and what does it cost?

Neither can be quoted honestly without the county and the facts. A case with a cooperative family and one hearing is far simpler than one where a sibling objects, and costs follow: court filing fees, a physician's report, attorney fees for the petitioner, and possibly fees for the guardian ad litem. After appointment, guardianship brings annual reports and court approval for some transactions. That ongoing supervision is part of the real cost, and part of why a signed power of attorney is so much cheaper.

Courts in Illinois may appoint a temporary guardian for an emergency such as a scam or a hospital discharge, but that order expires within 60 days or sooner when a regular guardian is appointed (755 ILCS 5/11a-4).

## What can you do while you decide?

Some help does not need a court.

- **Hospital decisions.** Even without a health care agent, the Health Care Surrogate Act sets who decides (755 ILCS 40/25). See [who decides when there is no agent](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).
- **Social Security.** The agency can name a representative payee for benefits. It does not touch other money.
- **Look for old papers.** A power of attorney signed years ago, a trust or an old attorney's file may already exist.
- **Fraud.** Report suspected exploitation of an older adult; our page on [reporting elder financial abuse in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-report-elder-financial-abuse-in-illinois) says where.

And avoid the shortcuts. Do not sign your parent's name. Do not put your name on their house or accounts. A transfer can trigger Medicaid penalties, which look back 60 months in most states, and can expose the asset to your creditors. The page on [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) explains the stakes.

Priya, 54, found her father had mild dementia and no documents. His neurologist wrote a short note on a Tuesday, and she took him to an attorney on Thursday morning. He met the attorney alone, named Priya as agent and her sister as successor, and signed both powers. Six months later he could not have done it. A family whose parent has advanced dementia and cannot follow the questions would be on the court path instead.

## How to prepare for the meeting

Bring the diagnosis and any doctor's notes, a list of accounts and bills, the deed, any old legal papers and the names of siblings. Write down what you have seen: dates, missed bills, repeated questions. Our free [dementia diagnosis legal checklist](/free/dementia-diagnosis-legal-checklist) and the [is-it-too-late-to-sign checklist](/free/is-it-too-late-to-sign-checklist) can help you gather it. If your parent also wants to change a will or trust, those have their own capacity rules; see [signing a will with dementia](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).

If a hospital is about to discharge your parent to a nursing home, read [signing nursing home papers with no power of attorney](/learn/illinois/hospital-no-power-of-attorney).
