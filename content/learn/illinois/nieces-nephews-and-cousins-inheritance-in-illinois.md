If you have no spouse, no children and no surviving parents, your closest family may be the next generation of your own family: nieces and nephews. Or you may be a niece wondering whether an aunt's death means a share for you. And when a family tree has thinned, cousins can end up in line.

This page follows the Illinois statute through those layers with real numbers. For the top of the list, read [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois), and for a single person's case, [no spouse and no children](/learn/illinois/no-spouse-no-children). Our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) lists the rest.

## When do nieces and nephews inherit in Illinois?

They inherit when their own parent, a brother or sister of the decedent, died before the decedent. The statute sends the estate, when no spouse or descendants survive, to the parents and the brothers and sisters in equal parts, and the descendants of a dead sibling take that sibling's part (755 ILCS 5/2-1). That is the same per stirpes idea described for grandchildren, applied one branch over.

If a sibling is alive, the sibling's children do not inherit. The living sibling stands between them and the estate. Half siblings count the same as full siblings; see [half siblings and inheritance](/learn/illinois/half-siblings-inheritance-in-illinois).

Here is an example. Grace, 70, never married and had no children. Her parents died long ago. Her estate is $480,000. She had three siblings: Hank, alive; Irene, who died leaving two children, Jake and Kim; and Lou, who died leaving none.

1. Count the branches that still have someone: Hank and Irene's line. Lou's line is empty.
2. Divide $480,000 into two shares: $240,000.
3. Hank takes $240,000.
4. Irene's share goes to Jake and Kim: $120,000 each.

If Hank were also dead with a daughter, Mona, she would take his $240,000 alone.

## What is the order after nieces and nephews?

The statute keeps moving outward until it finds someone. Here is the order after siblings and their descendants.

| Step | Who takes | How it splits |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Parents, siblings, siblings' descendants | Equal parts; dead siblings' share to their descendants |
| 2 | Grandparents and their descendants (aunts, uncles, cousins) | Half to the mother's side, half to the father's side |
| 3 | Great-grandparents and their descendants | Same two-sided split |
| 4 | Nearest relatives by degree | Equal shares |
| 5 | No kin | County or State |

## How do cousins fit in?

Cousins reach the estate through step 2, as descendants of grandparents. The estate is divided in half. One half goes to the mother's grandparents and their descendants, and the other half to the father's grandparents and their descendants. If one side has no living person, the other side takes everything. Within a side, grandparents take first if alive. If they have died, their descendants take: aunts and uncles first, and their children (the decedent's first cousins) only for any aunt or uncle who has died.

Example: Ruth, 66, dies unmarried with no children, parents, siblings or nieces. Her estate is $360,000. On her mother's side her aunt Pat is alive and has two children. On her father's side her uncle Joe has died and left three children.

1. Split the estate in half: $180,000 per side.
2. Mother's side: Pat is alive, so Pat takes $180,000. Her children get nothing.
3. Father's side: Joe is dead, so his three children share $180,000, or $60,000 each.

Those three cousins get less than half of what Pat gets, because the law follows the branches, not the headcount of cousins.

## What steps should you take if you may be an heir?

1. Write down how you are related: your parent, your aunt or uncle, the grandparents on each side.
2. Find out whether the decedent had a will and whether an estate was opened. Ask the probate clerk in the county of residence, such as [Cook County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/cook) or [Champaign County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/champaign).
3. Gather birth, marriage and death certificates that prove each link in your family tree.
4. Give the administrator your name and contact information in writing.
5. Watch for the notice of the heirship hearing, and respond by the date on it.
6. Ask whether an affidavit or a court finding of heirship will be used. See [proving heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois).

## What if the family tree is large or unknown?

Tracing cousins is slow. The court may appoint a guardian ad litem, or the administrator may hire a genealogist. If no heir is found, the property goes to the county or state; see [what happens when there are no heirs](/learn/illinois/dying-with-no-heirs-in-illinois). Be careful of unsolicited offers from heir hunters who ask for a large share of the inheritance in return for telling you about it.

## How can you plan around this?

If you want nieces, nephews or cousins to inherit, name them in a will. Name them in beneficiary forms for accounts. A will lets you pick exact shares, so that a favorite niece is not stuck splitting with unknown cousins. See [leaving an inheritance to nieces and nephews](/learn/life-stages/leaving-an-inheritance-to-nieces-and-nephews) and [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares). The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) shows which of your assets the default order would reach.

## What mistakes do relatives make?

Many assume that a niece or nephew inherits automatically from an aunt or uncle. The statute says no when a sibling is alive. Others assume that all cousins share equally, which is also wrong. The statute follows branches and sides, so one first cousin can receive far more than another. Some relatives assume they must act at once or lose their share; in fact the court takes months, but you should give your contact details to the administrator early. Others forget that a will changes everything, and that the default order matters only if there is no valid will.

Keep your family records together. Birth and marriage certificates for each person in the line between you and the decedent are what the court wants to see.

A final practical point: heirs who live far away can usually take part by mail and by phone, and the court may accept certified copies of records in place of a trip to the courthouse. Ask the clerk what your county allows.
