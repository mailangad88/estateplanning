Your son is 19, finishing school, and the transition team has said, "Make sure he is on PUNS." You may have heard that the wait for adult services in Illinois can be long, and you are wondering what to do while you wait. The honest answer is two-part: register now, and also plan as though services may arrive late.

This page explains PUNS in plain terms and ties it to the estate documents that protect an adult child. For the general trust rules, read [special needs trust rules in Illinois](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-rules-in-illinois). For the larger plan, read [special needs planning in Illinois for a disabled child](/learn/illinois/special-needs-planning-in-illinois-for-a-disabled-child) and the [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois).

## What is PUNS?

PUNS stands for Prioritization of Urgency of Need for Services. It is a database operated by the Department of Human Services that includes Illinois children and adults with developmental disabilities who have or expect to have needs for services. It is the state's waiting list for developmental disability services funded through Medicaid waivers.

The key rule: a person who is not on PUNS is not in the queue for state-funded developmental disability services. Our article on [housing and Medicaid waivers for adults with disabilities](/learn/special-needs/housing-and-medicaid-waivers-for-adults-with-disabilities) explains the services that waivers can pay for, such as in-home supports and residential settings.

## How does registration work?

A family completes a PUNS form with the person's information, the current supports and the services the person wants or will want. The form is usually completed with a regional coordinating agency, often an Independent Service Coordination agency, and the information goes into the state database. The categories describe how soon services are needed, for example whether the person is seeking services now or planning for a future need. Category names and forms have been revised over the years, so confirm current terms with DHS.

You do not need guardianship, a lawyer or a court order. The person's own preferences are part of the process.

## How are people selected for services?

The Department chooses from the list based on urgency of need and other factors, and the statute directs it to consider the length of time a person has been on the list. Funding limits mean many people wait. Because the length of time can matter, an early registration helps even if the need is not immediate. Update the record if the situation becomes more urgent, such as when an aging parent can no longer provide care.

## What should parents do while waiting?

PUNS is only one piece. A parent's death or illness can create an emergency before funding arrives. A practical plan covers money, decisions and daily care. Use this list:

1. Register your child on PUNS, and keep a copy of the form.
2. Review the record at least once a year and after any big change.
3. Sign a will that leaves nothing outright to the child and sends the share to a third-party special needs trust. See [first-party versus third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust).
4. Check beneficiary forms on IRAs, 401(k)s and life insurance so none names the child directly.
5. Name a successor trustee and a backup caregiver.
6. Write a letter of intent that describes routines, medications and preferences. See the [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) article.
7. Decide whether the child needs guardianship, supported decision-making or only powers of attorney. See [turning 18 with a disability](/learn/illinois/turning-18-with-a-disability).
8. Consider an [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) for everyday expenses.

## How do these pieces compare?

| | What it does | Needs court? | Affects SSI or Medicaid? |
|---|---|---|---|
| PUNS registration | Puts the person in line for state services | No | No |
| Third-party special needs trust | Holds family money without losing benefits | No | Protects them |
| Letter of intent | Tells future caregivers what matters | No | No |
| Supported decision-making agreement | Lets the person choose helpers | No | No |
| Guardianship | A court names a decision-maker | Yes | No direct effect |

## Example: the Okafor family

Chidi, 61, and Ngozi, 59, have a son, Emeka, 21, with autism who qualifies for SSI. Emeka was registered on PUNS at 17 and has been waiting. The couple learns that their will leaves everything to their three children equally, which would put about $250,000 in Emeka's name and end SSI. They sign new wills that send Emeka's share to a third-party trust, name his sister Adaeze as trustee and update their IRA forms. They also ask the PUNS agency to update the record because Chidi's health has changed, and write a letter of intent.

Families around the state can look up their area under [Illinois counties](/estate-planning/illinois), and the [plan finder](/plan-finder) can help build a checklist.

## What changes when a parent can no longer provide care?

This is the moment the list matters most. A parent's illness, move into a care home or death can shift the person's urgency, so tell the coordinating agency right away and ask for the record to be updated. Keep an up-to-date list of the child's doctors, medications, routines and favorite activities, and share the letter of intent with the backup caregiver. If a family has no relative able to help, the trustee can use trust funds to pay for support that benefits do not cover. See [who cares for a person with a disability after parents die](/learn/special-needs/who-cares-for-a-person-with-a-disability-after-parents-die).

## Why does an estate planning attorney care about a state list?

Because the plan has to work with the benefits, not against them. A trust that holds family money can supplement waiver services, pay for items the programs do not cover and keep the person from crossing resource limits. A plan that ignores the list can leave a family with a good trust and no daily support. The documents and the list are two halves of one plan.

## What are common mistakes?

- Waiting for a crisis to register.
- Registering and never updating the record.
- Leaving money to the child directly in a will or on a beneficiary form.
- Assuming guardianship is required.
- Skipping the letter of intent, so the next caregiver has to guess.
- Treating PUNS as a promise of a placement date.

A short annual review, tied to a birthday or the school year, catches most of these.
