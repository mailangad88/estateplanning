If you own a condo in Chicago, your unit sits inside a building with rules, a board and a monthly bill. If you own a co-op, the situation is stranger still, because you may not own real estate at all. When you die, your family inherits the unit and the association that comes with it.

This page covers the Illinois angle: whether a transfer on death instrument works on a condo, why co-op shares are a different kind of asset, and what the association can demand while an estate is open. For the national version, see [what happens to a condo or co-op when the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/what-happens-to-a-condo-or-co-op-when-the-owner-dies). If you want to talk through your own building, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-chicago-condo-and-co-op&topic=trust). ## Can I use a transfer on death deed on a Chicago condo?

Yes, as a general matter. Illinois does not use a "deed" for this. It uses a transfer on death instrument, or TODI, under the Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument Act (755 ILCS 27). A condo unit is real estate, and a TODI is a recorded document that passes Illinois real estate at your death without probate. Our page on the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) covers the full rules. The short version for a condo owner:

1. Pull your current deed so the legal description and the parcel number (PIN) are exact. A condo's legal description includes the unit number and the percentage interest in the common elements, and a sloppy copy is a common reason these fail.
2. Name a beneficiary and a backup. If the only beneficiary dies first, the unit falls back into your estate and probate.
3. Sign in front of two credible witnesses and a notary.
4. Record the instrument with the county recorder before you die. An instrument that is not recorded before death is void.
5. Keep paying the association. The TODI changes nothing about your duties while you are alive.

The beneficiary has no rights while you live, and you can still sell the unit or change your mind by recording a new instrument. A will cannot revoke a TODI.

### What a TODI does not fix

A TODI is not a cure-all, and condo owners tend to expect more from it than it gives. The beneficiary takes the unit subject to the mortgage and to unpaid assessments. Creditors can still reach it to the extent the law allows, and a surviving spouse who is left little can renounce and claim a statutory share. Our page on [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) explains how that works.

It also does nothing if you lose capacity. An agent under a power of attorney cannot sign or revoke a TODI for you. If you worry about a long illness as much as a death, a [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) covers both, and you can compare the options in [TODI deed vs. trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust).

| Tool for a Chicago condo | Avoids probate | Works if you become incapacitated | Main watch-out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will only | No | No | Executor needs court letters before signing a deed |
| Recorded TODI | Yes, if recorded before death | No | Strict signing and recording rules; no help during incapacity |
| Revocable living trust | Yes, once the unit is deeded in | Yes | Check lender and association rules before re-titling |
| Joint tenancy with a child | Yes | Partly | Exposes the unit to the child's creditors and divorce |
| Land trust | Not by itself | No | Holds title only; see below |

## Are co-op shares real estate in Illinois?

Generally no. A co-op owner holds shares in a corporation plus a proprietary lease on one apartment, and those shares are generally treated as personal property. That is the opposite of a condo. It also means a deed-style tool may not be the right fit.

Illinois statutes treat cooperative units as residential property in some settings, so the label is not the whole story. Do not assume that a TODI works on your shares because it works on your neighbor's condo. Ask an Illinois attorney, and ask the co-op's managing agent for the proprietary lease and bylaws. The transfer, in practice, runs through the co-op's own paperwork: the stock certificate, an assignment of the lease, and board consent for a new occupant.

Here is how that plays out. Rosa, 79, owns shares in a Hyde Park co-op. Her daughter Elena lives in Schaumburg and wants to keep the apartment for a year while she decides. Rosa's bylaws say the board must approve any new shareholder, and the board has 60 days to answer. (These details are made up for the example; yours will differ.) Because Rosa's estate holds the shares in the meantime, her executor needs court letters to sign the assignment. Elena should ask the managing agent for the application before she is certain of anything.

## What happens to the unit while the estate is open?

The association does not wait for the court. Dues keep coming, and the Condominium Property Act (765 ILCS 605) gives a condo association a lien for unpaid assessments. That lien is strong enough that it can reach a homestead the debtor would otherwise protect (735 ILCS 5/12-903). Our page on the [Illinois homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death) lists the other debts that can do that.

If the condo is in the owner's name alone, the executor opens probate and gets letters of office before signing a deed. How long that takes is the question families ask most, and our [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) page and [how long probate takes in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) cover it. Months of dues add up while the unit sits.

A worked example, labeled as an example. Walter, 74, owned a Lincoln Park condo with $520 in monthly dues and no will, TODI or trust. His estate stayed open for eight months. That is 8 x $520 = $4,160 in dues, plus a $3,000 special assessment approved before he died, for $7,160 the estate had to cover from his accounts before the unit could be sold. A recorded TODI would have let his son record a notice of death affidavit and take over the bills in weeks, though he would still have owed the same dues going forward.

### The executor's first-week list

- Call the property manager. Ask for the balance, any special assessment voted or pending, and a copy of the declaration and rules.
- Confirm the unit's insurance. The association's master policy does not cover contents or the interior, so the owner's own policy matters. Tell the insurer if the unit will be vacant.
- Find the mortgage lender and keep payments current. See [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).
- If the family will sell, expect a request from the buyer for the building's documents. Illinois requires a condo seller to provide the declaration, bylaws, financial information and similar items on request, so ask the manager early.
- For a co-op, ask the managing agent for the steps to approve an heir or sell the shares.

## Does a land trust change any of this?

It can. Many Chicago condo owners have a unit in a land trust, which holds title in a trustee's name and leaves the owner as beneficiary. That makes the question "who owns this?" more than a formality, because the transfer at death runs through the trust agreement. Our [land trust vs. living trust](/learn/illinois/land-trust-vs-living-trust) page explains the difference, and [what happens when the owner of a land trust dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-land-trust-when-the-owner-dies) walks through the steps. Some associations and lenders have their own rules for units held this way, so ask before you re-title anything.

## Will an Illinois estate tax apply to a condo?

Only if the whole estate is large. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026, and Illinois taxes estates over $4 million, with no portability between spouses. A condo alone rarely gets there, but a condo plus savings, a retirement account and life insurance can. See [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) and the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker). If the heirs sell, they generally take a stepped-up basis, so a quick sale at about the date-of-death value usually creates little taxable gain.

## A short plan for condo and co-op owners

1. Decide which kind of unit you have: deed, land trust, or co-op shares.
2. Request the declaration and bylaws (or proprietary lease) and read the sections on sales, rentals and transfers at death.
3. Pick the tool: TODI for a simple condo going to named people, living trust if incapacity or several assets matter, or the co-op's own transfer process plus a will for shares.
4. Tell the person who will handle it where the documents are, and give them the manager's name.
5. Review the plan when you move, refinance or add a name to title.
