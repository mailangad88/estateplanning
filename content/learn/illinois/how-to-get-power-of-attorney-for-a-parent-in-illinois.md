Your parent is slowing down. A bill went unpaid, or a hospital stay showed that nobody in the family can speak for them. You search for how to "get" power of attorney and find forms, but no office to apply to. In Illinois, a power of attorney is something your parent gives, not something you obtain.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), walks an adult child from the first conversation to the first trip to the bank. If you would like help with your family's situation, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-to-get-power-of-attorney-for-a-parent-in-illinois&topic=parent).

## Can an adult child get power of attorney for a parent?

Not on their own. A power of attorney is a document in which your parent, the principal, names an agent to act for them. Your parent decides whether to sign, whom to name and what powers to give. No court, clerk or state agency hands one out, and being the oldest child or the caregiver does not create one.

What you can do is help: raise the subject, find an attorney, drive them to the appointment and accept the role if chosen. The general approach is in [how to help an aging parent get their estate plan in order](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan). This page focuses on the Illinois mechanics.

## How does the process work, step by step?

Most families move through the same eight steps.

1. **Talk with your parent.** Ask who they would want to handle money and medical decisions if they could not. Let them name the person. The [aging parent conversation kit](/free/aging-parent-conversation-kit) has scripts for the first talk.
2. **Look for old documents.** A power of attorney signed years ago may still be valid.
3. **Decide on agents.** Your parent picks a primary agent and at least one successor for each document. See [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent).
4. **Book an Illinois estate planning attorney.** The attorney meets your parent, assesses understanding and prepares the forms.
5. **Gather information.** Accounts, the deed, insurance, doctors and any diagnosis notes.
6. **Sign with the right witnesses.** Property and health care forms have different signing rules, covered below.
7. **Distribute copies.** The agent, the bank, the doctors and the hospital each need one.
8. **Start using it carefully.** Keep records from the first transaction.

## Does your parent still have capacity to sign?

This is the question that decides everything. Your parent can sign if, on the day of signing, they understand that they are giving someone authority, who that person is, and roughly what they own. A diagnosis of dementia does not settle it either way, as the page on [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney in Illinois](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney) explains in detail.

Capacity is judged by the attorney who meets your parent, often with a doctor's note, not by you or a bank teller. Expect the attorney to speak with your parent privately. That builds a record that the choices were your parent's, which protects you if a sibling later says the document was your idea. If your parent's memory is slipping, the [is it too late to sign checklist](/free/is-it-too-late-to-sign-checklist) helps you prepare for that meeting.

## Which two Illinois forms does your parent sign?

Illinois keeps money and medicine in separate documents, and your parent usually needs both. Your parent can choose different agents for each.

| | Power of attorney for property | Power of attorney for health care |
|---|---|---|
| What it covers | Bank accounts, bills, taxes, real estate, benefits paperwork | Treatment decisions, talking with doctors, medical records |
| Statute | 755 ILCS 45/3-3 | 755 ILCS 45, Article IV |
| Witnesses | One witness who is not the agent | One witness, 18 or older |
| Notary | Required | Not required (755 ILCS 45/4-10) |
| Who cannot witness | Agent, successor agents, attending physician or mental health provider, facility owner or operator, certain relatives | Agent, successor agents, attending physician and listed providers, facility owner or operator, certain relatives (755 ILCS 45/4-5.1) |
| Co-agents | Not on the short form; successors allowed | Successor agents allowed |

The property form asks your parent to initial each power they grant and to choose when it starts. A power that starts right away avoids the hunt for a doctor's letter later; [springing versus immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) explains the tradeoff. The line-by-line walk-through is in [filling out the Illinois statutory short form for property](/learn/illinois/filling-out-the-illinois-statutory-short-form-poa-for-property), and the overview is the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).

The health care form names who speaks with doctors and makes treatment decisions, and records your parent's wishes about life-sustaining treatment. Your parent's attending physician cannot be the agent (755 ILCS 45/4-5). See the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) page. Ask the doctor's office for a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) as well, so you can receive information even before the agent's role begins.

## Who can witness the signing?

A neutral adult with no role in the document or in your parent's care. The most common mistake families make is having the agent, or the agent's sibling, sign as witness. The property statute excludes a parent, sibling or descendant of your parent or of any agent, and the spouse of one of them, according to the [Illinois property power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) page. So if you are the agent, your brother should not witness either.

A neighbor or a law office staff member usually fits both forms. The detailed health care list is on [who can witness an Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/witness-rules-for-an-illinois-health-care-power-of-attorney). In a hospital or care home, bring an outside witness instead of asking a nurse.

## What if your parent refuses?

If your parent understands the choice and says no, the answer is no for now. An adult with capacity may manage their own affairs, even in ways you disagree with. A document signed under pressure from the person who benefits is exactly what a sibling or a court may later question. These approaches work better than insisting.

- **Find the real worry.** Many parents fear losing control. Explain that an Illinois property power can be limited, can name successors and can be revoked while they have capacity. The page on [revoking an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney) shows how.
- **Offer someone else as agent.** A parent who will not name you may name a sibling or a trusted friend. The goal is that someone holds the authority.
- **Start with health care.** Some parents sign the health care form first because it feels less like giving away their money.
- **Bring in a third voice.** A doctor, clergy member or old friend may be heard where a child is not. See [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan).

What you should never do: sign your parent's name, ask a notary to accept a signature your parent did not make, or add yourself to their accounts or deed as a substitute. Joint ownership exposes the asset to your creditors and can cause Medicaid problems, because Illinois looks back 60 months at transfers when someone applies for long-term care Medicaid. If you suspect someone else is pressuring your parent, see [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) and [how to report elder financial abuse in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-report-elder-financial-abuse-in-illinois).

## When is it too late, and what happens then?

It is too late when your parent can no longer understand what the document does or whom they are naming. Nobody can sign for them at that point. The family then asks the circuit court to appoint a guardian under Article XIa of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/11a), and the court must find disability by clear and convincing evidence. For an emergency, a temporary guardian can be appointed, but that order lasts no more than 60 days (755 ILCS 5/11a-4).

Guardianship is slower and court supervised. The [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) page has the full process, and [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) compares the two.

Some decisions do not wait for a court. In a hospital, the Health Care Surrogate Act lets adult children and other relatives decide about treatment in a set order (755 ILCS 40/25). That does not reach bank accounts. See [who decides when there is no health care agent](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).

## Example: Denise helps her father sign

This is an invented example. Denise, 56, lives in Naperville. Her father, Raymond, 84, lives alone in Joliet and recently missed two utility bills. His doctor notes mild memory loss.

Raymond names Denise to help with bills, with her brother Mark as backup. Denise books an attorney and gathers his bank statements, deed and pension letters. The attorney talks with Raymond alone and writes down what he explained about his accounts. Raymond signs a property power with Denise as agent and Mark as successor, effective immediately, with a law office paralegal as witness and a notary present. He signs a health care power naming Denise, with the same paralegal as witness.

Denise does not witness anything, and neither does Mark. The next week she brings the property form to Raymond's bank.

## How do you use the power of attorney at banks and with Social Security?

Start with the bank. Bring the original or a certified copy and your photo ID, and be ready to certify that the power has not been revoked. If the bank insists on its own form or says the document is too old, those reasons are treated as unreasonable on their own for a properly signed statutory short form (755 ILCS 45/2-8). The steps for pushing back are in [what to do when an Illinois bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/illinois/bank-refuses-an-illinois-power-of-attorney).

Social Security is different. The agency does not accept a power of attorney as authority over benefits. To receive and manage your parent's benefits, you apply to become a representative payee, using a request such as Form SSA-11. Form SSA-1696 appoints a representative for a claim or appeal. Once the benefit lands in your parent's account, the Illinois property power covers that account. The details are on [Social Security and an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/social-security-and-an-illinois-power-of-attorney).

From the first transaction, act as the form's notice requires: in your parent's interest, without mixing their money with yours, and with a complete record. The [agent duties and record-keeping](/learn/illinois/illinois-poa-agent-duties-and-record-keeping) page explains the habits, and the [agent handbook](/free/power-of-attorney-agent-handbook) keeps them in one place. Your authority ends when your parent dies.

## Checklist for the adult child

- Your parent has chosen an agent and a successor for each form.
- An attorney has met your parent and recorded their understanding.
- The property form has your parent's signature, a qualified witness and a notary.
- The health care form has your parent's signature and one qualified adult witness.
- The bank, doctors and hospital have copies.
- You have a separate ledger for your parent's money.
