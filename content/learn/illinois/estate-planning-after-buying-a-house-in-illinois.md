You signed a stack of papers, got the keys, and moved the boxes in. The closing folder is somewhere in a drawer. Somewhere in that folder is the deed, and it already answers a question you may not have asked: if one of you dies next year, who owns this house, and who owes the loan?

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is for the weeks after closing: the title you took, the mortgage at a death, a transfer on death instrument or a trust, life insurance, and unmarried co-buyers, with a 90-day checklist and a worked example. If you would rather go through your deed and loan papers with someone, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-after-buying-a-house-in-illinois&topic=will-or-trust).

## How should we title the house, and how do we know what we already chose?

Start with the recorded deed. Find the granting clause, the sentence naming who receives the property, and match it to one of three forms.

- **Tenancy in common.** This is the Illinois default. A deed to "Sam Ortiz and Jamie Lee" with no more wording creates it (765 ILCS 1005/1). Each owner's share passes under that owner's will, or to relatives under the intestacy rules if there is no will.
- **Joint tenancy.** The deed must expressly declare it, in words like "not in tenancy in common but in joint tenancy." The survivor takes the whole house without probate, and a will cannot redirect the share.
- **Tenancy by the entirety.** Only for a married couple's homestead, and only if the deed says so (765 ILCS 1005/1c). The survivor keeps everything, both spouses must sign any sale or mortgage, and a divorce turns it into a tenancy in common unless the court orders otherwise.

The full comparison, with what each form does at a divorce or a sale, is on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety). Many buyers assume survivorship the deed does not give them. If yours does not match what you want, an attorney can prepare a new deed that restates the title, after a check with your lender and title insurer. The [types of deeds](/learn/property-and-assets/types-of-deeds-quitclaim-warranty-grant-and-trustee-deeds) page explains the forms that come up.

Survivorship works for a couple whose plan is "everything to each other." It works badly when one owner has children from an earlier relationship, who then get nothing from the house. See [blended family inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/blended-family-inheritance) before you pick a form.

## What happens to the mortgage if one of us dies?

The loan does not go away, and the answer depends on who signed the note. The general rules are laid out in [what happens to a mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies). For new buyers they come down to three situations.

| Your situation | Who owes the loan after a death | What keeps the house |
|---|---|---|
| Both of you signed the note | The survivor still owes the whole balance | Keeping payments current |
| Only the person who died signed | The estate owes it, and the lien stays on the house | Paying from the estate, insurance or the survivor's own funds |
| A relative inherits and lives there | No personal debt unless they signed | Asking the servicer to recognize them and keep the loan |

Two federal points help. The Garn-St Germain Act generally stops a lender from calling the loan due when an owner-occupied home passes to a spouse or relative at death, or into the borrower's own living trust. And heirs who did not sign the note are not personally liable for it. None of this pays the loan, which is where insurance comes in.

The Illinois homestead exemption is no help against the lender. It protects up to $15,000 of equity in a home you live in from judgment creditors, with co-owners sharing a $30,000 total by ownership percentage (735 ILCS 5/12-901), but it does not block a purchase-money mortgage or a tax sale (735 ILCS 5/12-903). See the [homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death). Property tax exemptions are a separate thing, covered in the checklist.

## Should we get a TODI or a trust?

A [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) under 755 ILCS 27 passes the house at death without probate, if it is signed before two credible witnesses and a notary and recorded before death. A revocable [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) costs more to set up, but its successor trustee can also manage the house if you become ill.

A short way to decide:

1. **Are both of you on title with survivorship, and is the plan "to each other, then to our kids equally"?** A TODI signed by the joint owners, naming the children, often handles the second death. Revoking it then needs all surviving joint owners (755 ILCS 27/70).
2. **Do you have children under 18?** A TODI would give a minor the house outright. A trust can hold it until an age you choose. See [estate planning for new parents in Illinois](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-for-new-parents-in-illinois).
3. **Is incapacity a real worry, or do you own other property?** A trust plus a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) covers it. An agent cannot sign or revoke a TODI for you.
4. **Is there a second marriage or stepchildren?** A trust can let a spouse live in the house and then pass it to your children.

The side-by-side table is on [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house), and the broader question is in [will vs trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/will-vs-trust-in-illinois).

A trust controls the house only after a deed to you as trustee is recorded with the county recorder. The steps, and the calls to the lender, insurer and assessor, are in [how to put your house in a living trust in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-put-your-house-in-a-living-trust-in-illinois). Many buyers close in their own names and record the trust deed a few weeks later.

## Do we need life insurance to cover the mortgage?

If one income pays a share of the mortgage, usually yes, though the amount is a family decision. Ordinary life insurance does not pay the lender. It pays the named beneficiary, who can pay off the loan, keep making payments, or sell the house. Mortgage protection insurance is the exception, because it is tied to the loan and pays the lender.

Size it by what the survivor would need: the loan balance or years of payments, plus lost income and childcare. The [life insurance needs calculator](/tools/life-insurance-needs) walks through it.

Then check the beneficiary form, since a minor cannot simply take the money; see [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors). Couples with a trust often name the trust so the money and the house follow the same rules.

## What do unmarried co-buyers need to do differently?

More, because Illinois gives them nothing by default. An unmarried partner is not on the intestacy list (755 ILCS 5/2-1), Illinois does not recognize common-law marriage, and an unmarried couple cannot hold title as tenants by the entirety. A civil union is treated like marriage for state-law purposes (750 ILCS 75/20), so partners in a civil union should read [civil union partners and Illinois estate law](/learn/illinois/civil-union-partners-and-illinois-estate-law) instead.

Unmarried co-buyers usually face one real choice:

- **Joint tenancy.** The survivor takes the whole house. Simple, but it ignores who paid more.
- **Tenancy in common with a plan.** Each owner keeps a share, often set by contribution, and leaves it by will or by a recorded TODI naming the other owner. A TODI can cover any interest in Illinois real estate that can be transferred at death (755 ILCS 27/10).

Either way, put a written co-ownership agreement next to the deed. It should say who paid what, who pays the mortgage, and what happens if you split up or one of you dies. The general version is in [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples), and the Illinois hospital and funeral rules are on [unmarried partner inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/unmarried-partners).

## What should we do in the first 90 days after closing?

- [ ] **Week 1 to 2:** Find the recorded deed (from the closing file or the county recorder) and read the granting clause.
- [ ] **Week 1 to 2:** Write down who signed the note and the mortgage, and file the loan papers with the deed.
- [ ] **Week 2 to 4:** Ask the county assessor how to get the homeowner exemption on the parcel. The Cook County Assessor describes it as renewing automatically once granted, so the task is getting it granted the first time.
- [ ] **Week 2 to 4:** Confirm the homeowners policy names every owner on the deed.
- [ ] **Week 3 to 6:** Decide on the house plan: survivorship, a TODI, or a trust. Get any new deed or instrument signed and recorded.
- [ ] **Week 3 to 6:** Sign wills. Illinois needs a signed writing and two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3); see [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements).
- [ ] **Week 4 to 8:** Sign a property power of attorney (one witness and a notary, 755 ILCS 45/3-3) and a [health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) (one witness, 755 ILCS 45/4-10).
- [ ] **Week 4 to 8:** Apply for or adjust term life insurance and name beneficiaries that match the plan.
- [ ] **Week 8 to 12:** If you chose a trust, record the deed to the trustee and send the lender a written notice.
- [ ] **Week 8 to 12:** Unmarried co-buyers: sign the co-ownership agreement and keep it with the deed.
- [ ] **Day 90:** Store the deed, plan and policies together, and tell your executor or successor trustee where they are.

For everything beyond the house, use the [Illinois estate planning checklist](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-checklist-illinois). The free [first home buyer estate checklist](/free/first-home-buyer-estate-checklist) is a printable version of the post-closing steps.

## Worked example: Kevin and Alana's Naperville townhouse

This is an invented example. Kevin, 34, and Alana, 32, are not married. They buy a townhouse in Naperville. Kevin puts in most of the down payment from an inheritance, and both sign the mortgage. The deed reads "Kevin Ross and Alana Price," because nobody asked them how they wanted to hold title.

Six weeks later Alana reads the deed. They are tenants in common. If Kevin died now with no will, his half would pass to his relatives under 755 ILCS 5/2-1, most likely his parents. Alana would still owe the whole mortgage as a co-borrower, while sharing ownership with Kevin's parents.

They keep the tenancy in common, because Kevin paid more. Each signs a will and a TODI naming the other as beneficiary of their share, with a sibling as backup. They sign a co-ownership agreement stating the down payment split and who pays what each month. Each buys a term policy roughly equal to the loan balance, naming the other. Both sign property and health care powers of attorney, and they will revisit the deed if they marry.
