A parent, spouse or adult child can no longer manage safely, and nobody holds a power of attorney. A bank will not talk to you, a hospital wants a decision, and someone has told you that you need guardianship. The next question is practical: who can start the case, and what does the court need to see in the first filing?

This page covers the filing step in Illinois. It does not repeat the full case, which our [overview of Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) explains. For the national picture, see [guardianship of an adult parent](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-of-an-adult-parent-who-can-petition-and-what-the-court-needs). Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links the other pages in this series.

## Who is allowed to file for adult guardianship in Illinois?

Under Article XIa of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/11a), a petition may be filed by a reputable person, and the person who may need a guardian can file for themselves. In practice, filers are usually a spouse, an adult child, a sibling, a close friend, a social worker or a care facility. You do not need a blood tie. You need to know the facts well enough to swear to them.

The statute does not rank relatives. A daughter does not outrank a son just because she filed first. The court decides who serves as guardian based on the person's interestss, and it may choose someone other than the filer. The text of the petition statute is on [ilga.gov](https://www.ilga.gov), and we suggest confirming the current wording there.

One age rule is firm: the person must be 18 or older. A court cannot appoint an adult guardian for a 17-year-old. Families of teens with disabilities often prepare early and file soon after the birthday. See [turning 18 with a disability in Illinois](/learn/illinois/turning-18-with-a-disability).

## What does the petition need to say?

The petition is a sworn court document. It states the person's name, age and address, the nature of the disability, and why the person cannot manage their own care or money. It says what kind of guardian is requested, of the person, of the estate or both, and whether the request is for limited or full powers. It names the proposed guardian and lists the person's closest relatives.

Courts expect specific facts rather than conclusions. "Mom forgets things" is weak. "Since March, my mother has twice left the stove on, missed three mortgage payments, and does not know the year" is useful. Include dates, names of professionals and what you have already tried, such as a power of attorney she refused to sign.

## What is the physician's report, and who writes it?

A licensed physician must examine the person and prepare a written report, and the court expects it to be recent, generally within three months of filing. Check the current statute or local rule for the exact window. The report covers diagnosis, the person's mental and physical condition, their ability to handle daily tasks and the decisions they can and cannot make.

Ask the doctor to address function, not only labels. A report that says the person can still choose where to live but cannot manage bills supports a limited order. Our article on [limited and full guardianship](/learn/illinois/limited-vs-plenary-guardianship-in-illinois) explains why that distinction drives the outcome.

## Who must be named and told?

The petition must list the person's spouse, adult children, parents and siblings, with addresses if known. Each listed relative gets notice of the hearing, and the person themselves gets the petition and a summons. The notice window is generally at least 14 days before the hearing (755 ILCS 5/11a-10). The rights that come with notice are covered in [rights of the person in an Illinois guardianship case](/learn/illinois/rights-of-the-person-in-an-illinois-guardianship-case).

Leaving out a relative is a common error. It can delay the hearing or let the order be attacked later, so ask family early who should be on the list.

## How do you file, step by step?

1. Talk with the person if you can, and ask whether they will sign a power of attorney, since that avoids the case.
2. Ask a licensed physician to examine the person and write the report.
3. Get the circuit clerk's forms for adult guardianship in the person's county.
4. Complete the petition, list all required relatives, and sign it under oath.
5. File the petition and report with the clerk and pay the filing fee, or ask about a fee waiver.
6. Arrange for the person to be served with the petition and summons, and for the relatives to be sent notice.
7. Attend the hearing. The court appoints a guardian ad litem and sets the date.

## How is filing different from being appointed?

| | Filing the petition | Being appointed guardian |
|---|---|---|
| Who can do it | A reputable person, or the person themselves | Someone the court finds suitable |
| What it takes | Sworn petition and physician's report | A finding of disability and a interest finding |
| Is it automatic? | No, it starts a case | No, the court may pick another person |
| Duties after | None yet | Reports, possible bond, court supervision |

## Example: Denise and her father Walter

Denise, 52, finds that her father Walter, 84, paid $6,200 to a caller who said his computer was infected. His doctor writes that moderate dementia prevents him from managing money. Denise files in his county with the doctor's report and lists herself and her brother Marcus as relatives. She asks for guardianship of the estate only, since Walter still chooses what he eats and wears. Marcus receives notice and does not object.

If your parent could still sign, read [what to do about dementia with no power of attorney](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney) first. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) can help you sort the options.

## What should you gather before you go to the clerk?

Bring more than the doctor's note. A clerk cannot give legal advice, but a prepared filer saves weeks. Collect these items first:

- The person's full legal name, date of birth, address and current location.
- Names, ages and addresses for the spouse, adult children, parents and siblings.
- A list of the person's income and assets in round numbers, since the petition for an estate guardian describes them.
- Copies of any power of attorney, health care directive or trust, because the judge will ask why they cannot be used. See [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).
- The name of the person you propose as guardian, with a backup if you can.

If the person is in a nursing home or hospital, ask the social worker for a statement about discharge plans. It shows the court why the case cannot wait. For emergencies, read about [temporary guardianship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/temporary-guardianship-in-illinois).

## What if the family disagrees about filing?

Disagreement is common, and it is better to learn about it before the hearing. A sibling who disagrees can object to the petition, to the choice of guardian, or both. Telling relatives in advance, sharing the physician's report and asking who is willing to serve usually lowers the temperature. If the family cannot agree, the court may choose a neutral person or a professional guardian. Our article on [professional and public guardians](/learn/guardianship/professional-and-public-guardians-when-no-family-can-serve) explains that choice.
