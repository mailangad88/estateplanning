Many Illinois families sign a transfer on death instrument for one reason: to keep the house away from probate, and with it, away from creditors and Medicaid. Only half of that is true. A TODI changes how the house is transferred. It does not change who may be owed money.

This page looks at the creditor and Medicaid questions. The mechanics of the instrument are in our overview of the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument). The broader set of Illinois articles starts with our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois). This is general education, not legal advice.

## Do creditors lose their claim when a house passes by TODI?

No. The Act says a TODI beneficiary takes subject to claims against the owner's estate, to the same extent as the beneficiary of a revocable trust under Illinois Trust Code section 505 (755 ILCS 27/85). In plain terms, if the owner's probate estate cannot pay valid debts, funeral costs and certain statutory claims, a creditor may look to property that passed by TODI.

The limit is the value received. A beneficiary who inherits a $200,000 house is not liable for $500,000 of debt, but the house can be pulled in for the shortfall.

## How does the process work when debts exist?

Here is the usual path.

1. The owner dies and the beneficiary records the notice of death affidavit. See [the notice of death affidavit article](/learn/illinois/notice-of-death-affidavit-for-an-illinois-todi).
2. A probate estate may or may not be opened. A creditor can ask for one.
3. The executor publishes notice and the creditor claims period starts. See [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate).
4. Valid claims are paid by priority (see [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate)) from the probate assets first.
5. If those assets fall short, the claimant may look to the TODI property under the Act.
6. The beneficiary can negotiate, pay from the sale proceeds, or contest the claim.

Because step 5 is conditional, many families never see a claim against the house. The risk is highest when the owner's other assets were few and the debts were large.

## What about Illinois Medicaid estate recovery?

HFS describes estate recovery as reaching real and personal property that is subject to probate. It says it does not collect from assets that pass directly to a beneficiary outside probate, such as life insurance with a named beneficiary or a payable-on-death account. Our article on [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) explains HFS's own rules, including the $25,000 protection for deaths on or after July 1, 2022, and the groups for whom recovery waits.

A TODI house is a different kind of nonprobate transfer, and the Act expressly makes it answerable for claims. We could not confirm how HFS handles a TODI house in a specific case. The statute governing recovery may define the estate more broadly than the plain-language page does. So treat this as an open question, and ask HFS or an attorney before relying on a TODI to protect a house from recovery.

## How do the main routes compare?

| Route for the house | Probate needed? | Creditor claims | HFS recovery question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will only | Yes | Paid through the estate | Within the probate estate |
| TODI | No | Beneficiary answers if estate falls short | Confirm with HFS or counsel |
| Joint tenancy with spouse | No | Survivor owns outright, with caveats | Spouse protection while living |
| Living trust | No | Trust answers to claims under the Trust Code | Confirm with HFS or counsel |
| Sold before death | No | Money is gone from the house | Look-back rules for gifts |

Our article on [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) and the creditor protection article on trusts explain why trusts also do not defeat claims by themselves.

## A worked example with numbers

Ellen, 83, lived in a nursing home on Medicaid for two years. She left a house worth $180,000 by TODI to her son Marcus. Her probate assets were $6,000 in a checking account. Her debts: $11,000 for funeral and a final medical bill, and a possible Medicaid claim.

The $6,000 pays part of the funeral first. A shortfall of $5,000 remains. Marcus, who inherited the house, is asked to cover it from the house, which he can do from the sale. Whether HFS also pursues the house is a separate question that Marcus's attorney raises with the agency, based on the statute and the facts of Ellen's care. Marcus keeps records, because anything he spends first is harder to unwind.

## Can planning reduce the risk?

Yes, but only with advice that fits the facts. For Medicaid planning, the 60-month look-back for gifts applies in most states, and a TODI is generally not a lifetime gift because the beneficiary has no rights until death. That is not the same as being protected from recovery. See [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning). A change made too close to a nursing home admission can backfire.

## What should a beneficiary do first?

Do not sell or move the money right away. Order the death certificate, record the notice, ask whether an estate has been opened, and read any letters from creditors or HFS. Our [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) helps list what the estate has. Then ask a lawyer whether to wait for the claims period before distributing sale proceeds.

## What questions should you ask HFS or a lawyer?

Because the answer to the Medicaid question depends on the facts, go in with a list. Bring the owner's dates of care, the recorded TODI and a list of the owner's other assets and debts.

1. Did the owner receive long-term care benefits that are subject to estate recovery?
2. Does HFS treat property passing by TODI as part of the estate for recovery, in this case?
3. Does a protected family member, such as a surviving spouse or a child under 21, delay or block recovery? See Illinois Medicaid estate recovery.
4. Is the estate worth more than the first $25,000 that is protected for deaths on or after July 1, 2022?
5. Would an undue hardship waiver apply if the house is the heirs' only home?
6. What is the deadline for responding to any notice, and who should respond?

Get the answers in writing. A beneficiary who sells the house first and learns about a claim later has fewer options than one who asked early. If a notice has already arrived, read it right away and note any response date it states.
