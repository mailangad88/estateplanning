Six months before Tomas turns 18, his mother Elena sits in a Naperville meeting with his school team. The case manager says something Elena had not expected: from the birthday on, the decisions are Tomas's. He has an intellectual disability, reads at a second-grade level, and can tell you exactly which bus he takes. Does Elena need a judge before September?

Probably not, and not on that date. This page covers the Illinois decisions at 18 for a child with a disability: how guardianship works, the lighter options, what happens to SSI, and where an ABLE account fits. ## What changes when a child with a disability turns 18 in Illinois?

The law stops treating your child as a minor, whatever their abilities. In Illinois a parent has no legal power over an adult child just because of the relationship or the diagnosis. Authority has to come from a document your child signs or from a court order.

| Area | Day before 18 | Day after 18, no papers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medical consent | Parent signs | Your child signs, or no one does |
| Doctor talking to you | Allowed | Needs a signed release |
| School rights | Parent holds them | Move to the student, with notice |
| SSI | Parents' income and assets counted | Only your child's own count |
| Bank account | Parent controls | Parent has no access unless named |

Our general guide for 18-year-olds, [documents an 18-year-old needs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/documents-an-18-year-old-needs-in-illinois), covers the standard forms. This page is for families where capacity is the real question.

## Does my child need guardianship at 18 in Illinois?

Only if no lighter tool will work. Illinois guardianship of an adult sits in Article XIa of the Probate Act. The court must find that your child is a person with a disability, meaning someone 18 or older who, because of mental deterioration or physical incapacity, is not fully able to manage their person or estate, and it must find that by clear and convincing evidence (755 ILCS 5/11a-3). The same section says guardianship should be used only as necessary and only to the extent the person's actual limits require.

That wording matters more than parents expect. A diagnosis is not the test. The question is what your child can and cannot do:

- Tomas can choose his doctor and say what hurts, but cannot judge a lease or a loan.
- His friend Priya can handle her debit card with weekly check-ins, but cannot follow a surgeon's consent form.

Those are different orders, one limited to money, the other to medical consent, or neither if the family finds a lighter fit. Our guide to [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) walks through the process and the guardian's duties.

## What are the options between doing nothing and full guardianship?

There are four, and they stack.

1. **Releases and consents.** A HIPAA release and a FERPA education release let doctors and the school keep talking to you. Your child signs them.
2. **Powers of attorney.** A health care power of attorney needs one adult witness and no notary, and a property power needs a witness and a notary under the Illinois Powers of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45). They work only if your child understands who the agent is and what the agent can do. A person with a mild or moderate disability often does.
3. **Supported decision-making.** The Illinois agreement under 755 ILCS 9 lets an adult with an intellectual or developmental disability name helpers who explain choices while the adult still decides. It needs two adult witnesses and no notary. See [the Illinois supported decision-making agreement](/learn/illinois/illinois-supported-decision-making-agreement) for who can serve and how it ends.
4. **Representative payee.** For SSI money, Social Security can name you to manage the benefit without a court. That does not give you power over medical choices.

Nothing stops a family from using several of these together. The main thing to avoid is signing one and assuming it covers everything. A supported decision-making agreement, for example, does not let you sign a form for your child when a bank or hospital insists on a signature. For a wider comparison, see [guardianship alternatives for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children).

## How does an Illinois guardianship case for a young adult work?

A petition goes to the circuit court in your county, and a judge decides after a hearing. The steps, from the statute as it applies to adults:

1. You file a petition. A guardian can only be appointed for a person 18 or older.
2. A physician's report is filed within 3 months of the petition.
3. Your child receives the petition and summons at least 14 days before the hearing, and listed relatives get notice too (755 ILCS 5/11a-10).
4. Your child may attend, have a lawyer, and ask for a jury. The court appoints a guardian ad litem to look at the case.
5. At the hearing the judge decides whether guardianship is needed and how much, and names the guardian.

Parents usually need to plan for two things they do not expect. The hearing is public and talks about your child's abilities in front of them. And a guardian answers to the court, which means reports and approvals, not the free hand parents sometimes imagine. Your child also keeps the right to ask the court to change or end the order later.

One practical question is timing. Because only an adult can be placed under guardianship, you can gather the medical opinion and talk with an attorney well before the birthday, then file when the petition is allowed. Ask the circuit clerk in your county how far ahead they will accept a filing. If your child would be fine with a lighter tool, skipping the case entirely is a perfectly good outcome.

## What happens to SSI at 18?

For many families it improves. Social Security counts part of a parent's income and assets for a child under 18, called deeming, and that stops at 18 (ssa.gov). A family that earned too much to qualify at 17 can apply again for the adult. Social Security also judges disability at 18 under the adult definition rather than the child standard (ssa.gov), so a child who was on SSI is looked at again.

Prepare the same way each time:

- Medical records, the IEP and school evaluations, and a list of providers.
- Your child's own bank statements, kept under the $2,000 resource limit for a single adult.
- A decision about representative payee, if you will manage the money.
- A question for the local office: is a separate Medicaid application needed in Illinois, or does approval carry over? Do not assume.

The practical point for guardianship is that SSI does not require it. Social Security will name a representative payee without a court order. See [what counts as income and resources for SSI](/learn/special-needs/what-counts-as-income-and-resources-for-ssi) for what is counted, and the [free SSI rules cheat sheet](/free/ssi-rules-cheat-sheet) for the rules on one page.

## Where does an ABLE account fit at 18?

It is the spending account that sits beside SSI. An ABLE account holds savings that SSI generally does not count up to $100,000, if the disability began before age 46. Contributions from anyone are capped each year at the gift tax exclusion, $19,000 in 2026. The money can pay disability-related costs, including housing.

A young adult can own an ABLE account, and a parent or other authorized person can manage it if your child cannot. That makes it a good first account at 18 for earnings, birthday gifts and small family contributions. Larger sums, such as an inheritance, belong in a trust. [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) explains the limits and the Medicaid claim that can follow at death.

## What about PUNS and adult services in Illinois?

Register on PUNS well before school services end. Illinois uses the Prioritization of Urgency of Need for Services list to record which people with developmental disabilities need adult services, and waiver funding is limited, so a registration made late has less behind it. Your school transition team or the Illinois Department of Human Services, Division of Developmental Disabilities, can tell you how to enroll and how often to update. Update it when your family's situation changes, not only on the annual cycle. Housing and waiver options are covered in [housing and Medicaid waivers for adults with disabilities](/learn/special-needs/housing-and-medicaid-waivers-for-adults-with-disabilities).

## What should you do before the birthday?

Work backward from the date with this list.

- **12 months out:** Ask your child's doctor and school team how they would describe decision-making. Start gathering records.
- **6 months out:** Meet an Illinois estate planning attorney. Decide among releases, powers of attorney, supported decision-making and guardianship.
- **3 months out:** Sign the documents, or have the physician's report ready if you are going to court. Open an ABLE account.
- **At 18:** Apply for SSI as an adult and confirm any payee arrangement.
- **Within 3 months after:** Rewrite your own will and beneficiary forms so no inheritance passes straight to your child.

The last item gets missed. A family that sorts out guardianship and still leaves the whole estate "to my children equally" has just put the SSI at risk later. The fix is a third-party special needs trust, described in [special needs planning in Illinois](/learn/illinois/special-needs-planning-in-illinois-for-a-disabled-child).
