You teach fourth grade in Joliet, or you are a nurse at a county hospital, a village police officer, a firefighter, a university lab manager or a clerk at a state agency. Most of what your family would receive if you died is not in a bank account. It sits in a pension, a 403(b) or 457(b), and a group life policy that came with the job. Each one has its own beneficiary form, and each form was probably signed on your first day and never looked at again.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), puts the whole plan for a public employee in one place: which forms control which money, what happens if you die before retiring, when a trust belongs on a form, and which documents still have to be signed. It ends with a checklist and a worked example. If you would rather go through your own forms with someone, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-estate-planning-for-teachers-and-public-employees-in-illinois&topic=will).

## Why is estate planning different for a public employee?

Because the forms carry more weight than the will. A private-sector worker with a house and savings leaves much of their estate under the will. A teacher or public employee often leaves the largest pieces under pension rules and plan forms that the will never touches.

| What you have | What decides who gets it | Where to check |
|---|---|---|
| Pension (TRS, IMRF, SURS, SERS, or a local police or fire fund) | The system's rules plus the beneficiary form on file | Your system's member portal or forms page |
| 403(b) or 457(b) account | The beneficiary form with the plan provider | Provider website or your HR office |
| Group life insurance through work | The beneficiary form with the insurer | HR or the insurer's portal |
| Supplemental life or accidental death coverage | Its own separate form | HR |
| House, bank accounts, car | Title, any payable on death or TODI designation, then your will | Your own records |

Teachers usually belong to TRS, state university staff to SURS and state agency staff to SERS. Local government workers are often in IMRF. A nurse at a public hospital or a police officer or firefighter may be in IMRF or in a separate county, city or local fund, depending on the employer. Your pay stub or annual statement names the fund. The fund names and phone numbers are listed on our page about [Illinois pension survivor benefits](/learn/illinois/illinois-pension-survivor-benefits-imrf-trs-surs-sers), which explains what each system pays after a death.

## Does my will control my TRS or IMRF pension?

No. A pension system pays under its own rules and the form on file, whatever your will says. If your will leaves everything to your spouse and your TRS or IMRF form still names your mother, the system pays your mother. The same rule applies to your 403(b), your 457(b) and your group life policy. The general order of which document wins is explained in [which controls: a will, trust, joint account or beneficiary form](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form).

That is not a flaw. Money that passes by form skips probate and reaches your family faster. It just means the forms have to say what your will says, or the two will pull in different directions. A parent who leaves "equal shares" in the will but names one child on the pension has, in practice, left unequal shares. Our page on [leaving unequal shares with beneficiary forms](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-unequal-shares-with-beneficiary-forms) shows how to balance that on purpose.

## Who gets my pension if I die before retiring?

It depends on your system, your service and who survives you. Most Illinois systems can pay two kinds of benefit after an active member dies: a refund or lump sum, and, for certain survivors, a monthly benefit. In some systems those go to different people. TRS, for example, says the refund of contributions and the survivor benefits can each go to different beneficiaries.

If no form is on file, the default varies. IMRF says its default beneficiary is the member's estate. SURS sends the death benefit to the estate when no designation is on file. TRS sends the refund to a surviving spouse or civil union partner, or the estate if there is none. SERS publishes its own rules by tier, so ask it directly. A benefit that falls into the estate needs a probate case to collect and is exposed to creditors. Our page on [what happens if there is no beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/what-happens-if-there-is-no-beneficiary-on-a-bank-or-brokerage-account) describes the same problem for ordinary accounts.

Do not rely on a coworker's memory of how survivor benefits work. Call or write to your own system and ask three questions: what is paid if I die while working, who qualifies, and what my current form says.

## What about my 403(b), 457(b) and group life insurance?

Each needs its own form, and each is easy to forget.

- **403(b) and 457(b).** These pass to the person named with the plan provider. Public employer plans are generally outside ERISA, the federal law that gives a spouse automatic rights in most private 401(k) plans, so the spousal consent rules may differ. Ask the plan. A non-spouse beneficiary will generally have to empty an inherited account within 10 years, with exceptions for certain eligible beneficiaries. Our page on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) covers those choices.
- **Group life insurance.** This is often your family's fastest cash after a death. Name a primary and a [contingent beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries), and ask HR whether the coverage ends when you leave or retire and whether you can convert it. If you named "my estate," the proceeds go through probate. Our page on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) explains the options.
- **Old accounts.** A 403(b) from a district you left ten years ago still pays whoever that old form names.

## Should my pension beneficiary be a trust?

Sometimes, and only after someone reads your system's rules. A trust makes sense when your beneficiaries are young children, a person with a disability, or someone who should not receive a lump sum outright. Naming a child directly usually means the probate court appoints a guardian of the child's estate and the child receives what is left at 18, as explained in [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois).

<!-- visual: whatif id=minor-inherits-without-trust -->

Pension systems put conditions on trusts. IMRF says it will need a full copy of the trust document. TRS allows a trust to receive monthly benefits for a minor child or a disabled adult dependent only if the trust has specific language. There is also a trade-off for married members: IMRF says a spouse must be the only primary beneficiary to qualify for its surviving spouse pension when an active member dies, so naming a trust or adding children can cost the spouse that pension.

A common middle path is to name your spouse on the pension, then send the 403(b), 457(b) or group life to a trust for the children, or name the trust as contingent beneficiary. Whether that fits depends on your system and your family. Our pages on [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) and [beneficiary form wording for trusts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/beneficiary-form-wording-for-trusts-charities-and-overseas-beneficiaries) cover how to fill in the form.

## What documents does a teacher or public employee need?

The same core documents as anyone in Illinois, plus a careful pass through every work benefit. Use this checklist.

- [ ] **A will** signed and witnessed under Illinois rules. See [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements).
- [ ] **A guardian nomination** for children under 18, with a backup. No beneficiary form can do this. See [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children).
- [ ] **A trust for children**, either in the will or as a living trust, if they could inherit a 403(b), 457(b), life insurance or a house.
- [ ] **A power of attorney for property.** Under the Illinois statutory short form, an agent cannot change a beneficiary unless you add that power in specific terms (755 ILCS 45/3-4), as explained in [Illinois POA gifting and beneficiary change powers](/learn/illinois/illinois-poa-gifting-and-beneficiary-change-powers). Even then, IMRF says it will not accept a beneficiary change signed by an agent, so update your forms while you can sign them yourself.
- [ ] **A health care power of attorney.** See [the Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).
- [ ] **A pension beneficiary form** checked against your current family, with written confirmation from your system.
- [ ] **403(b) and 457(b) forms**, primary and contingent, for every current and old account.
- [ ] **Group and supplemental life forms**, plus a note on whether coverage continues after you leave.
- [ ] **A one-page benefits list** with each plan, member number and where the paperwork is kept.

## A worked example: Tanya's forms

Example only, with invented names. Tanya, 44, teaches high school English in Rockford and is a TRS member. She is remarried to Luis, a village police officer in his village's police pension fund. Tanya has a son, Eli, 15, from her first marriage, and she and Luis have a daughter, Rosa, 7. Her will from 2015 leaves everything to Luis.

When Tanya pulls her forms, she finds four problems:

1. **TRS form.** Signed in her twenties, it still names her first husband. She asks TRS how a new form would affect each benefit, because TRS warns that when a member names a new spouse after a divorce, children from the earlier marriage are not eligible for monthly survivor benefits. She wants to know what that means for Eli before she chooses.
2. **403(b).** It names Eli directly. If she died now, a court would likely supervise that money until he turns 18, then hand him the rest. She names a trust for both children instead, after her attorney confirms the plan accepts it.
3. **Group life through the district.** The form says "my estate." She names Luis as primary and the children's trust as contingent.
4. **No guardian for Rosa and no powers of attorney.** Eli's father is living, so Tanya reads [whether the surviving parent gets custody](/learn/guardianship/does-the-surviving-parent-get-custody-if-you-die). For Rosa, Tanya and Luis name a guardian and a backup in new wills, and each signs both Illinois powers of attorney.

Luis does the same review with his police pension fund. Neither will is long. The forms took more time than the documents.
