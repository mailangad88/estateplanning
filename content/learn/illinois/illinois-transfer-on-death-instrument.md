Many Illinois homeowners want their house to pass to a child without probate or a trust. Adding a child to the deed now can bring gift tax, loss of control, and exposure to the child's creditors. Illinois offers a different option, the transfer on death instrument.

This article explains how it works under the Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument Act (755 ILCS 27), what steps make it valid, and where it falls short. It is general education, not legal advice. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the full picture.

## What is an Illinois transfer on death instrument?

A transfer on death instrument, often called a TODI, is like a deed that takes effect only at your death. The Act defines real property as an interest in real estate in Illinois that can be transferred at the owner's death. It applies to all real property, with changes that took effect in 2022 under the Act's scope section (755 ILCS 27/10). It is similar to what other states call a [transfer on death deed](/glossary/transfer-on-death-deed).

While you are alive, you keep full control. The beneficiary gets no ownership interest and has no right to notice or to sell. The Act says the instrument is effective without notice to, delivery to, or acceptance by the beneficiary, and no payment is needed (755 ILCS 27/50).

## How do you make a valid TODI?

An invalid instrument can fail without warning. The Act requires the instrument to contain the elements of a properly recordable deed, to say that the transfer happens at the owner's death, and to be executed, witnessed, and acknowledged in substantial compliance with the Act (755 ILCS 27/40). Follow these steps.

1. Confirm that you own the property in your own name and have testamentary capacity (755 ILCS 27/35).
2. Get the correct legal description and parcel number from your current deed.
3. Name the beneficiary and a contingent beneficiary, and say how shares divide.
4. Sign in front of two or more credible witnesses and a notary.
5. Have the witnesses sign a statement that you signed freely and were of sound mind.
6. Record the instrument with the county recorder where the land sits before you die.

Recording is the step people miss. An instrument not recorded before death is void and ineffective (755 ILCS 27/40). An agent under a power of attorney cannot create or revoke a TODI for you, so you must do it yourself while you have capacity.

## What happens when there are several beneficiaries?

Under the Act, multiple beneficiaries receive equal and undivided shares with no right of survivorship, unless the instrument says otherwise. If the only beneficiary dies first, the property goes to the owner's estate. If a named beneficiary who is a descendant dies first, that person's descendants may take (755 ILCS 27/65). Naming a contingent beneficiary avoids a surprise. Meet Joel, 68, who names his daughter Amy and says nothing else. If Amy dies before him, the house could end up in his probate estate, which he did not want.

## Can you change or cancel a TODI?

Yes, but only in the way the Act allows. A TODI can be revoked even if it says it cannot be. You may revoke it only by recording a later TODI or a recorded instrument of revocation, signed and witnessed with the same formalities, before your death (755 ILCS 27/25 and 27/55). A will cannot revoke it. If you sell the house, the buyer gets the property, and the TODI no longer matters because the property is gone.

If the property is held in joint tenancy, one or more joint owners can sign. Revocation then requires all surviving joint owners, and the instrument does not end the joint tenancy (755 ILCS 27/70). Our article on [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) explains why that matters.

## Does a TODI protect the house from creditors or a spouse?

No. The Act treats beneficiaries as subject to claims from creditors, funeral costs, and statutory claims to the same extent as beneficiaries of a revocable trust under Illinois Trust Code section 505 (755 ILCS 27/85). The Act also lets a surviving spouse renounce within 7 months after the owner's death and claim a share, one-third if the owner had descendants and one-half if not (755 ILCS 27/66). Confirm this with a lawyer, because the details affect who owns the house. See our [Illinois spouse's rights](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) article.

Challenges also have limits. A contest must start by the earlier of two years after the owner's death or six months after letters of office are issued (755 ILCS 27/90). A bona fide buyer or lender who deals before the filing of certain notices is protected.

## What does the beneficiary do after the owner dies?

The beneficiary can record a notice of death affidavit. It lists the beneficiary, the legal description, the address and parcel number, the recording details of the instrument, and the date and place of death (755 ILCS 27/75). Recording it helps clear title when the beneficiary sells or refinances. It is not a condition of the transfer. A death certificate is usually needed, and many people also check whether a title company has added requirements.

## When is a TODI a good fit, and when is a trust better?

A TODI is simple and cheap, and it keeps control with you. It suits a single piece of real estate going to named people. A trust may suit you better if you have several properties, want to plan for incapacity, or want to leave property to children in stages. Our [living trusts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) article compares those options. Some assets are not real estate at all. For those, see our article on [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) and our [Illinois probate step-by-step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) guide.
