An executor in Illinois has a lot to track: the court case, the bank accounts, the house, the final income tax return. If the estate might be over $4 million, there is one more form with its own office and deadline. Form 700 is the Illinois estate tax return, and it works differently from the IRS return in several ways.

This page walks through who files Form 700, what to gather, where it goes and how it differs from the federal Form 706. For the tax itself, read our explanation of the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). For the whole job of settling an estate, start at the [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois).

## Who has to file Illinois Form 700?

An estate must file when its gross value plus adjusted taxable gifts is over $4,000,000. The Illinois Attorney General says the return is required "whether or not a federal return is required." The person responsible for the federal return files the Illinois return as well (35 ILCS 405/6).

| Situation | Federal Form 706 | Illinois Form 700 |
|---|---|---|
| Illinois resident, estate $3.5 million | Not required | Not required |
| Illinois resident, estate $4.6 million | Not required | Required |
| Illinois resident, estate $16 million | Required | Required |
| Nonresident with $5 million, $1 million in Illinois | Not required | Required if the gross estate is over $4 million, tax apportioned |
| Married couple, $3 million estate, filing only for portability | Optional | Not required |

The last row is a common surprise. A federal portability election does nothing in Illinois, because Illinois does not allow a surviving spouse to add a late spouse's unused exclusion. See [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples).

## What is the deadline?

Form 700 and the tax are due nine months after the date of death. The Attorney General says an extension request must also be made within those nine months, and it recognizes federal extensions. An extension of time to file does not stop interest on tax that is not paid by the due date. Our guide to [Illinois late filing, interest and penalties](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax-late-filing-interest-and-penalties) covers what happens if the date is missed.

The nine-month date is easy to miscount. Death on March 12 means a due date of December 12 of the same year. If the ninth month has no same-numbered day, use the last day of that month.

## What do you gather before you start?

You need a date-of-death value for every asset, because both the filing test and the tax depend on those numbers. Gather these:

- The death certificate and the will, plus any trust agreement and amendments.
- Real estate appraisals dated near the date of death, and county tax bills.
- Statements for every bank, brokerage and retirement account as of the date of death.
- Life insurance statements, and proof of who owned each policy.
- Records of lifetime gifts above the annual exclusion, and any Form 709 filed.
- Debts, funeral bills and administration costs you expect to deduct.
- Business valuations, if the person owned a company. See [how assets are valued for estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/how-assets-are-valued-for-estate-tax).

## How do you file Form 700 step by step?

1. Add up the gross estate at date-of-death values, including non-probate assets and revocable trust property.
2. Add adjusted taxable gifts. If the total is $4,000,000 or less, no return is generally required.
3. If it is over, prepare Form 700 from the Attorney General's current form packet. Estates with property outside Illinois also need the schedule for apportioning the tax by location.
4. Attach a copy of the federal Form 706 if one was prepared, or the equivalent figures if not. Add any Illinois-only election, such as a QTIP election. See [credit shelter trusts and the Illinois QTIP election](/learn/illinois/illinois-qtip-election-and-credit-shelter-trusts).
5. Send the return to the correct Attorney General office. Cook, DuPage, Lake and McHenry County estates go to Chicago, and the rest go to Springfield.
6. Pay the tax by check to the Illinois State Treasurer, as the form directs, by the nine-month date.
7. Keep a copy of everything, including proof of mailing, and ask the Attorney General's office for written confirmation that the account is closed.

## What is different from the federal return?

The Illinois return is not a copy of Form 706 on a different letterhead. Four differences matter.

First, the office is different: the Attorney General, not the IRS. Second, the threshold is $4 million, not $15 million. Third, Illinois does not honor portability, so a federal election made on Form 706 has no effect on the Illinois tax. Fourth, Illinois allows its own QTIP election, which can be made without making the federal one (35 ILCS 405/2). Our comparison of [Illinois and federal estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-vs-federal-estate-tax) goes through all the differences.

Some executors also ask whether they can prepare the return themselves. Simple estates sometimes can. We explain the trade-offs in [filing Form 706 yourself or hiring an attorney](/learn/estate-tax/can-i-file-form-706-myself-or-do-i-need-an-attorney), and the same reasoning applies to Form 700.

## What if the estate is just under the line?

Take Ruth, 80, whose estate is made up of a $1.8 million house, $1.6 million in accounts, a $500,000 IRA and a $90,000 life insurance policy she owns. The total is $3.99 million. No return is required. But that is within $10,000 of the line, and values can change when an appraiser gets involved. If the house comes in at $1.85 million, Ruth's estate is $4.04 million and a return is due.

Executors in that position should get written appraisals early, document how each number was reached, and decide before the due date whether to file. A protective filing costs preparation fees. A missed one can cost penalties on a tax that turns out to be owed.

## Where do you get help?

Each county's probate court can answer questions about the probate case, but the tax itself is handled by the Attorney General's estate tax staff.
