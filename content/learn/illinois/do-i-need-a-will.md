"I don't own enough to need a will" is the most common reason adults in Illinois give for not having one. It is also the sentence that most often falls apart once a person writes down what they actually own. This page, part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), helps you count it, shows what Illinois does with whatever you leave, and says plainly when a will is optional.

If you want the full list of who inherits, read [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). If you want to talk it through, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-do-i-need-a-will&topic=will-or-trust) and an Illinois estate planning attorney will go over your situation with you.

## The short version

- Illinois has no minimum estate size for a will. Anyone 18 or older of sound mind can make one.
- A will controls only what is in your name alone. A 401(k), an IRA, life insurance and a payable on death account go to the person named on the form.
- With no will, state law divides what is left and a court appoints someone to manage it.
- A will does things no other document does. It nominates a guardian for minor children and names the person who runs your estate.
- If you rent, have no children and every account names a beneficiary, you can reasonably skip a will, but not the powers of attorney.

## How much do you really own?

Count before you decide. Most people stop at "I don't have a house" and miss the rest. Try this list and add a rough number to each line.

| What to count | Why it gets missed | Where it goes at death |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Checking and savings accounts | Seen as pocket money | Your estate, unless joint or payable on death |
| 401(k) or 403(b) through work | Feels like a retirement number, not an asset | The beneficiary on the plan form |
| Group life insurance from your employer | Many people never saw the form | The named beneficiary |
| Car, in your name | Treated as a household item | Your estate, unless titled otherwise |
| Final paycheck and unused pay | Not yet received | Your estate |
| Tax refund or security deposit | Small, but owed to you | Your estate |
| Phone, photos, online accounts | Valued emotionally, not in dollars | Depends on the account terms |

Take Ruben, 34, who rents an apartment in Cicero and says he owns nothing. In this example his checking account holds $2,800, his car is worth $9,000, his employer's group life insurance pays $50,000 and his 401(k) holds $41,000. That is about $103,000, and Ruben never wrote it down. Only the checking account and the car are in his name alone. The $91,000 in the plan and the insurance goes where his beneficiary forms say. That is the point: his assets are not small, but a will would control only about $11,800 of them.

## What happens if I die without a will in Illinois?

Illinois treats you as intestate and applies a fixed formula to your probate property (755 ILCS 5/2-1). A spouse and children share it half and half. With no spouse, children take everything. With neither, the law reaches parents and siblings, then more distant kin. Unmarried partners, close friends and stepchildren receive nothing from that formula.

The formula is the lesser of the two problems. The bigger one is who is in charge. Without a will, nobody is named, so a relative has to ask the court for letters of administration. Illinois gives the surviving spouse first preference, then others by a set order, and the court usually requires a bond (755 ILCS 5/9-3). The family pays for that bond and the court time out of the estate. The [letters of administration article](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) walks through it.

None of this is a punishment. It is a default written for the average family, and it works fine for some. It fits badly if you are in a blended family, have an unmarried partner or want one child to handle things.

## Who should not skip a will, even with little money?

A short list, because it is shorter than most people expect.

1. **Parents of minor children.** Only a will lets a parent nominate a guardian in writing for the court to consider. See [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship). Without a nomination, relatives may disagree and a judge decides.
2. **Unmarried partners.** Illinois intestacy gives a live-in partner nothing. A civil union partner is treated differently, as the [civil union guide](/learn/illinois/civil-union-partners-and-illinois-estate-law) explains.
3. **People with a stepchild or a close friend they want to include.** Neither is on the statutory list.
4. **Anyone who wants a particular person to be in charge.** A will names your executor and a backup. Without one, the order in the statute decides.
5. **Blended families.** If your spouse is not the parent of your children, the half-and-half split still sends half of what is in your name to the children, even if they are minors. See [when a minor inherits](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois).

## When can you reasonably skip one?

You can, if all of these are true. You rent or own nothing in your name alone. You have no minor children and no one you need to name. Every account that allows a beneficiary has a current one, with a backup. And you have signed a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) and a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).

Even then, review it every few years. A new baby, a marriage or a first home changes the answer. Our [plan readiness tool](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) takes about two minutes and flags the gaps for your situation.

## What if my estate is small enough to skip probate?

Illinois lets heirs collect up to $150,000 of personal property with a sworn affidavit instead of opening an estate, as of 2026 (755 ILCS 5/25-1). That helps a family with a bank account and a car. It does not help with a house in your name alone, and it does not choose who inherits. The statute or your will decides that. Read the [small estate affidavit guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) for the limits.

So a small estate shrinks the paperwork after you die. It does not remove the reason to say who gets what.

## Quick quiz: does this sound like you?

Answer yes or no to each.

1. Do I have a child under 18, or a partner I am not married to?
2. Do I own a car, a bank account or a final paycheck in my name alone?
3. Is there someone I want to handle things, or someone I want to leave out?
4. Would a family disagreement over my things surprise nobody?

One yes is enough to justify a will. Two or more means you should not wait. All no means the beneficiary forms and powers of attorney may be enough for now.

## What to do this week

1. Write down every account and policy, with who is named on each.
2. Fix any beneficiary form that is blank, out of date or names an ex.
3. Sign a healthcare power of attorney and a property power of attorney.
4. If you answered yes to the quiz, decide who you would name as executor and, for parents, guardian.
5. Get a will drafted and signed with two witnesses, as the [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) explain.
