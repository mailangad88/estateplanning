Married couples with a house, retirement accounts and life insurance often reach $4 million without feeling rich. At that size, Illinois estate tax can apply to the survivor, even when the first death produced no tax at all. The tools to prevent it are the credit shelter trust and the QTIP election, and the Illinois version of the second has a feature the federal version does not.

This page explains how the two trusts work together for an Illinois couple, with a worked $8 million example. For the broader tax rules, see [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). For the general comparison with portability, read [bypass trust vs portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability). The overall [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) links the rest.

## Why do Illinois couples need a credit shelter trust?

Illinois does not allow a surviving spouse to add the first spouse's unused exclusion. The Attorney General states that portability does not apply to the Illinois estate tax. So when the first spouse leaves everything outright to the survivor, that $4 million exclusion disappears, and the survivor's estate is tested against only one $4 million.

A credit shelter trust fixes this. At the first death, assets up to the Illinois exclusion go into a trust for the survivor and the family. They are not left outright, so they are not part of the survivor's estate later. The first spouse's $4 million is used. The survivor can often receive income, and sometimes principal for health, education, maintenance and support.

## What is a QTIP trust and how does Illinois treat it?

A QTIP trust (qualified terminal interest property trust) gives the survivor all the income for life, with the first spouse choosing who gets the principal afterward. If the executor elects it, the trust qualifies for the marital deduction, so no tax is due at the first death. The price is that the trust is counted in the survivor's estate later. It defers the tax and does not remove it. See [marital deduction explained](/learn/estate-tax/marital-deduction-explained).

Illinois adds a useful option. Under 35 ILCS 405/2, the executor may make a QTIP election for Illinois that is independent of the federal election. It is made on Form 700 with the Illinois return. That means a trust can qualify as marital property for the state tax and still sit outside the federal marital deduction, or the reverse.

## Why does the federal $15 million cause trouble?

Many older documents say "fund the shelter trust with the largest amount that avoids federal estate tax." In 2026 that amount is $15 million. For an Illinois couple, that formula can pour far more than $4 million into the shelter trust, and Illinois taxes that first-death excess. Our article on [formula clauses](/learn/estate-tax/what-is-a-formula-clause-in-a-trust) explains the drafting.

Here is a worked example with invented numbers. Walter and Nora, both in their seventies, own $8 million: $6 million in Walter's name and $2 million in Nora's. Walter dies first.

| Plan | At Walter's death | At Nora's death | Illinois tax, both deaths |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everything outright to Nora | $0 | Nora's estate is $8 million | about $680,600 |
| Federal-formula shelter trust funded with $6 million | Illinois tax on $6 million, about $456,100 | Nora's estate $2 million, no tax | about $456,100 |
| $4 million shelter trust plus $2 million QTIP trust, Illinois election made | $0 | Nora's $2 million plus the $2 million QTIP, $4 million total | $0 |

The third line is the point. Walter's $4 million exclusion is used by the shelter trust, and the $2 million excess is deferred under the QTIP election, not taxed. At Nora's death her own $2 million and the $2 million QTIP trust add up to exactly $4 million, which is right at the threshold. In real life, growth would push the estate over the line, so the numbers need regular review.

The dollar figures come from our own arithmetic. The Attorney General's calculator gives the official answer.

## How is the plan carried out after the first death?

Steps for the surviving spouse and the executor:

1. Value every asset at the first spouse's date of death, including retirement accounts and life insurance.
2. Compare the total with the $4 million Illinois exclusion and the $15 million federal figure.
3. Divide the assets as the trust directs: the shelter share to the credit shelter trust, the rest to a QTIP or marital share, or outright to the survivor.
4. Retitle accounts and real estate into the trust names and obtain a new tax ID for the trusts.
5. Decide which elections to make on the federal return, if one is filed, and on Illinois Form 700. See [Illinois Form 700](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-700-estate-tax-return).
6. Keep the trusts' records and file fiduciary income tax returns for them. The Illinois side is covered in [Form IL-1041](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-il-1041-estate-and-trust-income-tax-return).

Steps 1 to 3 must be done well because the elections cannot be freely changed later. Ask an attorney before the Form 700 is filed.

## What are the trade-offs?

Shelter trusts cost money to set up and administer. The survivor may have limits on using principal, and the trust may need annual tax returns. A shelter trust also does not get a second [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) when the survivor dies, which can mean capital gains tax for heirs on appreciated property.

Another option is a disclaimer plan. The survivor decides within nine months whether to disclaim part of the inheritance into the shelter trust, depending on the size of the estate then. This keeps flexibility but depends on the survivor acting in time, and on the document being drafted for it. See [disclaiming an inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois).

For couples with a joint living trust, check that the document actually splits at the first death. Our article on [joint living trusts for married couples](/learn/trusts/joint-living-trust-for-married-couples) covers common gaps.

## What does this look like for a couple under $4 million?

Many couples ask whether any of this applies to them. Consider Dan and Priya, both 58, with a $2.6 million estate. Today neither is near the Illinois line, and a simple plan that leaves everything to the survivor is reasonable. But a $2.6 million estate can double in 20 years of saving and market growth. The question is less about today's number and more about whether the documents can adjust.

A flexible plan has three features. First, a clause that divides assets at the first death if the survivor's estate would otherwise be over $4 million. Second, a disclaimer option so the survivor can send part of the inheritance into the shelter trust. Third, a schedule of review, such as every three to five years or after any big change. Our [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) can help you remember. Couples under $4 million who do not plan for growth sometimes find out too late that the first exclusion is gone.
