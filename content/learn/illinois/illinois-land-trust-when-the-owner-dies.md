A parent dies, and in the filing cabinet there is no deed to the house. There is a thick agreement from a bank, with a trust number on the first page. That is an Illinois land trust, and it changes the first week of the estate, because the house is not owned the way most houses are owned.

This page is for the family and the executor after the owner has died. It does not compare land trusts with living trusts, which our article on [Illinois land trust vs living trust](/learn/trusts/illinois-land-trust-vs-living-trust) covers. Here we look at what to do, in order. For the wider Illinois picture, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Who owns the house when the owner of an Illinois land trust dies?

The trustee still holds legal title, and nothing is recorded at death. The owner held the beneficial interest, which is the right to direct the trustee, to live in or rent the property, and to receive sale proceeds. Illinois treats that interest as personal property, not as land.

That one fact explains most of what follows. Your family does not inherit a deed. It inherits a piece of paper-based property that sits behind the trustee, and the path it takes depends on what the agreement says.

## What should you read first?

Read the land trust agreement, then any assignments, then the will. In that order. The agreement usually has a paragraph on what happens when the beneficiary dies. It may name a successor beneficiary, say the interest passes under the beneficiary's will, or say nothing useful.

Look for these items and write them down.

1. The name of the trustee and the trust number.
2. The date of the agreement and every amendment.
3. Whether a successor beneficiary is named, and whether that person is alive.
4. Any signed assignment of the beneficial interest, including one to a [living trust](/glossary/living-trust).
5. Who holds the power of direction, and whether it is one person or several.
6. Any collateral assignment to a lender, which is common when the property has a loan.

If you cannot find the agreement, do not guess. Ask the trustee for a copy. A bank trust department can confirm who the beneficiary of record is once it has proof of death and proof of who is asking.

## How do you find the trustee and the trust number?

Start with the recorded deed, which reads something like "First Bank, not personally but as Trustee under Trust Agreement dated June 4, 2026, known as Trust No. 5521." If you have no copy, the county recorder's office can pull it by address or parcel number. Property tax bills, insurance declarations and mortgage statements often carry the trust number too.

Banks merge. A trust that began at one bank in 1994 may now sit with a bank whose name your family has never heard. Work backward from the last recorded document and call the institution named on it. A title company can often trace the successor faster than a family can, and the cost is small compared with weeks of calling.

## Does the family need probate for a land trust?

Often yes, but only if the agreement leaves the interest to the estate. The table shows the usual outcomes.

| What the paperwork shows | Who controls the interest | Probate needed for the interest? |
|---|---|---|
| Interest assigned to a living trust | The successor trustee of the living trust | No |
| Successor beneficiary named and alive | That person, after giving proof of death | Usually no |
| Agreement says the interest passes by will | The executor, then the people named in the will | Yes |
| Agreement silent, no assignment | The estate, under the will or Illinois intestacy | Yes |
| Spouses held it as tenants by the entirety | The surviving spouse | Usually no |

The last row comes from how Illinois lets married owners hold a homestead. The statute allows tenancy by the entirety in a beneficial interest in a land trust if the document says so (765 ILCS 1005/1c). Our page on [Illinois joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains the wording that matters.

When the interest does go through the estate, the executor needs authority first. In Illinois that means a court order opening the estate and letters of office. Our [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) article walks through the case, and the [small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) page explains why a house will rarely fit that shortcut.

## What does the trustee need before it acts?

Expect a short list, and expect it to be strict. The trustee is a bank or title company, and it does not want to take instructions from the wrong person.

- A certified copy of the death certificate.
- Proof of who now holds the power of direction: a living trust certification, an assignment, or letters of office.
- A photo ID for the person giving instructions.
- Its own forms, such as an assignment of beneficial interest or a direction letter.

Ask what it requires before you drive downtown. Some trust departments will accept a certification of trust. Others want the full document. The firm that drafted the original assignment is a good first call if the family knows who it was.

## Can you sell or refinance a house held in a land trust?

Yes, once the right person can direct the trustee. The trustee signs the deed to the buyer at the direction of the beneficiary. That is the main reason Chicago-area owners like land trusts: sales can close without the owner appearing on a record.

After a death the sticking point is who the "beneficiary" is today. If the interest is part of the estate, a title company will want to see letters of office before it insures the sale. Our article on [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois) covers the timing and the buyer's side, and [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois) covers keeping it.

## What about the mortgage, insurance and property taxes?

Keep all three current while the paperwork catches up. A lender may hold a collateral assignment of the beneficial interest, so call it early. Insurance should name the trustee and the land trust, and the carrier should be told about the death, especially if the house is now vacant. Property tax bills continue to arrive in the trust's name, and a missed installment can bring penalties and, over time, a tax sale. Our page on [Illinois homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death) covers exemptions that may change.

## A worked example

Teresa, 71, put her Berwyn two-flat in a land trust in 2003. She has a will leaving everything equally to her sons, Marcus and Paul. The land trust agreement says the interest passes under her will. Teresa never assigned it to anyone.

When she dies, the bank still holds title to the building. Marcus, the named executor, finds the agreement in a folder and calls the trust department. The bank tells him it will not take instructions until it sees letters of office. Marcus opens an estate, receives letters, and signs an assignment of the beneficial interest to himself and Paul. They can then direct the bank to sell or keep the building.

Had Teresa signed a one-page assignment to her living trust, her successor trustee could have walked into the bank with a death certificate and been done in days. The difference is a single page.

## Common mistakes

- Looking for a deed in the estate papers and giving up when there is none.
- Assuming the will decides everything. The agreement can override it.
- Telling a tenant to pay rent to the family before anyone has authority.
- Letting insurance lapse because the policy names the trust, not the person.
- Forgetting that a bank trustee charges an annual fee that keeps running.
