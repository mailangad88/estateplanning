Many people ask for an Illinois living will because they want to be sure no machine keeps them going against their wishes. The Illinois Living Will Act (755 ILCS 35) lets you do that, but only in one situation: a terminal condition, when you cannot speak for yourself. This page explains what the document covers, when it switches on, and why it often sits second behind a health care agent.

It is general education, not legal advice. For the document that names a person to decide for you, see our guide to the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney), and for the full state picture start at the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What does an Illinois living will cover?

It covers death-delaying procedures. The Act defines these as procedures that would only postpone death, such as assisted ventilation or artificially provided nutrition and hydration. Its statutory form says that if you have an incurable and irreversible condition that your attending physician judges terminal, procedures that would only prolong the dying process are to be withheld or withdrawn (755 ILCS 35/3). You are then allowed to die naturally, with medication, sustenance and comfort care still given.

The key words are "terminal condition." The Act defines it as an incurable and irreversible condition where death is imminent and death-delaying procedures would only prolong dying (755 ILCS 35/2). A car accident that leaves you in a coma with an uncertain outlook may not meet that test. A stroke where doctors cannot say whether you will recover may not either. That gap is why the living will is a narrow tool.

## When does a living will actually apply?

A declaration becomes operative only when four things are true at once (755 ILCS 35/9.5):

1. It was validly signed and witnessed.
2. You have not revoked it.
3. You cannot give directions about life-sustaining or death-delaying procedures.
4. You are a "qualified patient," meaning your attending physician, who has personally examined you, has diagnosed and verified the terminal condition in writing (755 ILCS 35/2).

Once those conditions are met, the physician records the determination and the declaration's terms in your medical record (755 ILCS 35/4). The Act does not require a separate certification form.

Take Gloria, 79, with advanced pancreatic cancer. Her oncologist writes in the chart that her condition is terminal. A month later she is unconscious on a ventilator after a complication. Her declaration, signed years ago, now speaks for her. Compare her neighbor Dale, 52, who suffers a brain injury with no terminal diagnosis. The living will does nothing for him, and his family would have to rely on an agent or the [Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).

## How does a living will work with a health care power of attorney?

The agent comes first. Section 3.5 of the Act points to section 4-11 of the Illinois Power of Attorney Act, and the result is that a declaration is not operative while an agent authorized under a health care power of attorney is available to make life-sustaining or death-delaying decisions. Nothing in the Living Will Act reduces that agent's authority.

So the two documents do different jobs:

| | Living will | Health care power of attorney |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | You, in advance, in writing | The agent you name |
| Trigger | Terminal condition diagnosed in writing | Your inability to decide |
| Reach | Death-delaying procedures only | Medical decisions generally |
| Role when both exist | Backup, and guidance for the agent | Takes the lead while available |

In practice, many Illinois families sign both. The living will tells your agent what you said you wanted, and it is the fallback if the agent cannot be found. The Health Care Surrogate Act also keeps its own role. The Living Will Act does not impair a surrogate's authority for a patient who lacks decisional capacity and has a qualifying condition (755 ILCS 35/9).

A common mistake we see is a living will that says one thing while the agent's form leaves life support to "my agent's judgment." Read both together, and make sure your agent has seen the living will.

## How do you sign an Illinois living will?

The declaration must be signed by you, or by someone else at your direction, and witnessed by two individuals who are at least 18. You must be an adult of sound mind or an emancipated minor (755 ILCS 35/3). The statute does not name excluded witnesses in the section itself, but the optional statutory form includes a witness statement. Each witness confirms that they are not entitled to any part of your estate under intestacy or, to their knowledge, under a will or other instrument taking effect at death, and that they are not directly financially responsible for your medical care.

Follow these steps.

1. Read the statutory form, or have an attorney draft one that matches your values.
2. Choose two adult witnesses who are not relatives who would inherit, and not the people paying your bills.
3. Sign in front of both witnesses and have them sign.
4. Give copies to your doctor, your health care agent and your hospital.
5. Keep the original somewhere your family can find it.

A notary is not part of the Act's signing rules. Our article on [whether a living will needs a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary) explains why people still ask.

## Does the pregnancy rule apply in Illinois?

Yes. The Act says a declaration has no effect for a pregnant patient while the fetus could develop to the point of live birth (755 ILCS 35/3). This surprises many women who sign a living will in their thirties. If it matters to you, discuss it with your attorney, because it is a limit written into the statute, not something wording can remove.

## Can you cancel or change it?

You can revoke it at any time under section 5, even if you are ill. The Act lists these ways: destroy the document, sign and date a written revocation, express your revocation orally to an adult who then signs and dates a confirming writing, or delete an electronic declaration with the intent to revoke. The revocation takes effect when it is communicated to your attending physician, who records the time, date and place.

If you simply change your mind about a treatment while you can still talk, that matters too. A qualified patient's current wishes override the declaration (755 ILCS 35/7). So the document never overrides you in the room.

## Who is protected for following it, and what cannot be required?

Providers may presume that a declaration is valid unless they know otherwise, and a provider who acts in good faith under reasonable medical standards faces no civil or criminal liability or unprofessional-conduct finding (755 ILCS 35/7). The Act also says no one may be required to sign a declaration as a condition of insurance or care (755 ILCS 35/9), and following it is not suicide and does not affect a life insurance policy.

If your doctor is unwilling to comply, the physician must say so, and the Act sets a transfer process (755 ILCS 35/3). Pair the living will with a [POLST or DNR order](/learn/illinois/illinois-polst-and-dnr-orders) if you are seriously ill, because paramedics act on orders, not on a living will in a drawer.

## What should you do now?

Start with the conversation, not the form. Ask yourself what you would accept and what you would refuse if the doctors said you were dying.
