If you are the person named in a guardianship petition, the papers can feel like a verdict before a hearing. They are not. A judge has to decide, and Illinois gives you several rights to speak for yourself during that process. If you are a relative, knowing these rights helps you see why the case moves at the pace it does.

This page lists those rights in the order they usually come up. It supports our overview of [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship), and it fits with the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What notice does the person get?

The person must receive a copy of the petition and a summons at least 14 days before the hearing (755 ILCS 5/11a-10). The papers include a plain statement of the person's rights. Close relatives named in the petition also receive notice by mail. The point of the delay is to give time to read, to find a lawyer and to ask questions.

If the papers were handed to someone else or left with a staff member, the person may not have been properly served. Tell the court or a lawyer immediately rather than waiting for the hearing date.

## Does the person have the right to attend?

Yes. The person has the right to be at the hearing, and the court may not simply decide without them. A judge may excuse attendance only on narrow grounds, such as a medical condition that makes it harmful or impossible, and the court generally wants a physician's statement for that. A judge can also move part of the hearing to where the person lives. Courts treat this as a real decision, not a formality.

## Can the person have a lawyer?

Yes. The person can hire their own lawyer, and the court can appoint one when the person has none or when the circumstances call for it. A lawyer for the person argues for what the person wants. That is different from the guardian ad litem, who reports on what the person needs.

If a relative says the person "doesn't need a lawyer," that is not the test. Ask the court. The costs of both roles are covered in [what adult guardianship costs in Illinois](/learn/illinois/cost-of-guardianship-in-illinois).

## What does a guardian ad litem do?

The court appoints a guardian ad litem to investigate and to report in writing or at the hearing (755 ILCS 5/11a-10). The guardian ad litem meets the person, reads the physician's report, may speak to family and caregivers and tells the judge what they recommend. They are the court's eyes. They do not take sides, and they are not the person's lawyer.

Be open with the guardian ad litem. A calm, truthful visit helps the judge more than a rehearsed one.

## Can the person ask for a jury?

Yes, the person may demand a jury, and the court must honor a timely demand. This is rarer than a bench hearing, and it is more expensive and slower. People use it when they strongly dispute the claim of disability. The demand has to be made within the time limit, so a lawyer should file it early. The statute gives the details, and the current text is on [ilga.gov](https://www.ilga.gov).

## What must the petitioner prove?

The petitioner must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the person is a person with a disability and needs a guardian (755 ILCS 5/11a-3). That is a higher standard than the usual civil test. Evidence includes the physician's report, the guardian ad litem's findings, testimony from caregivers and the person's own answers to the judge's questions.

A bad financial choice, an eccentric lifestyle or a family disagreement does not meet that standard. Our national article on [contesting a guardianship](/learn/guardianship/can-a-guardianship-be-contested-or-ended) compares Illinois with New Jersey.

## How do these rights play out, step by step?

1. The person is served with the petition, a summons and a written statement of rights.
2. The person decides whether to hire a lawyer, or the court appoints one.
3. The guardian ad litem meets the person and reviews the file.
4. The person or lawyer decides whether to demand a jury, and files the demand on time.
5. The person attends the hearing, hears the evidence and may testify or present witnesses.
6. The judge rules and, if guardianship is ordered, explains the person's right to ask for changes later.

## How does it compare when a person agrees or objects?

| | Person agrees | Person objects |
|---|---|---|
| Hearing length | Often short | Can take hours or days |
| Lawyer | Court may still appoint | Person usually hires one |
| Jury | Rarely requested | Possible |
| Guardian ad litem | Appointed in both | Appointed in both |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |

## Example: Helen's objection

Helen, 79, is served with a petition filed by her nephew Carl, who says she cannot manage her $140,000 in savings. Helen's doctor says she has mild memory loss but handles her bills. Helen hires a lawyer, the guardian ad litem visits twice, and the judge asks Carl for more proof. Helen later signs a power of attorney naming her daughter, and Carl withdraws.

If someone you love might need a different tool, see [the Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) and the [alternatives to guardianship](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children).

## What can relatives do to respect these rights?

Relatives sometimes mean well and still make the person feel cornered. A few habits help the process and the family:

- Explain the petition in plain words before the papers arrive, if you can do so calmly.
- Do not pressure the person to sign a consent or waive the hearing without a lawyer's advice.
- Give the guardian ad litem honest, complete information, including facts that do not help your side.
- Let the person speak first at the hearing if the judge allows it.
- Keep the person's daily life steady, since the judge may ask how they are doing.

If the person is able, a power of attorney can end the case. See the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) and [revoking an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney), which shows that the person keeps control while they have capacity.

## What if the person does not understand the papers?

Some people cannot read or follow the summons because of illness. In that case the guardian ad litem and the person's lawyer become more important, and the judge may hold the hearing where the person is. The goal is a fair look at the person's real abilities, not a quick result. If you think the papers were not delivered properly, tell the court right away.
