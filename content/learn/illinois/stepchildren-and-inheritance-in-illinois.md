You raised your wife's two children for twenty years. Or you are a stepchild and your stepfather has just died with no will. Families in this position usually feel that the law will recognize the bond. In Illinois, without a will, it does not, and the outcome depends on who in the family is still alive.

This page gives the Illinois rule, walks through three realistic cases with numbers, and lists the fixes. For the general national picture, see [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance). For other cases in Illinois, start at the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Do stepchildren inherit without a will in Illinois?

No. The intestacy statute (755 ILCS 5/2-1) names a surviving spouse, descendants, parents, siblings and then more distant blood relatives. A stepchild is none of these. The only way a stepchild becomes a descendant for inheritance is legal adoption (755 ILCS 5/2-4). Years of living together, financial support and family photos do not count.

A stepchild is also not left out by accident in a way a court can repair. The judge applies the statute as written. If the stepparent wanted the stepchild included, the stepparent had to sign something while alive.

## How do three common cases turn out?

The result turns on who survives. Here are three families.

**Case 1. The parent survives.** Hal, 70, marries Jo, 66, who has two adult children, Pam and Lee. Hal has no children. Hal dies without a will with a probate estate of $300,000. Jo takes all of it, because Hal leaves no descendants. Pam and Lee get nothing from Hal, but Jo now owns the money and can leave it to them.

**Case 2. The parent dies first.** Same family, but Jo died four years ago. Hal never made a will. At his death, his parents and siblings inherit. If his sister Gail is his only survivor, she takes $300,000. Pam and Lee, who grew up with Hal in the house, receive nothing.

**Case 3. The stepparent has children too.** Rose, 62, has two children and a husband, Ken, with a daughter from before. Rose dies without a will with $400,000. Ken takes $200,000 and Rose's two children share $200,000. Ken's daughter is not Rose's heir.

| Situation at death | Who takes | What the stepchild gets |
|---|---|---|
| Stepparent leaves a spouse (the stepchild's parent) and no children | The spouse takes all | Nothing now; may inherit later from the parent |
| Stepparent leaves spouse and own children | Spouse one-half, children one-half | Nothing from the stepparent |
| Stepparent's spouse already dead, no own children | Parents and siblings, then cousins | Nothing, unless adopted or named |
| Stepchild was adopted | Treated as the adopting parent's child | A full child's share |

## What is the real path for a stepchild?

The surviving parent. In Case 1 the money stays in the family only if Jo then keeps a will that names Pam and Lee, or if they would inherit from her by default, which they would as her children. Jo's death can then trigger a second round, in which Hal's relatives get nothing at all. This is why a blended family often works if the parent survives and fails if the parent dies first.

## What are the steps to include a stepchild?

1. Decide what you want to leave and to whom, including what you want for your own children.
2. Write the stepchild's full name in a will or trust. Avoid "my children" or "my family".
3. Update beneficiary forms for life insurance and retirement accounts to name the stepchild.
4. Consider a recorded transfer on death instrument or a joint title for a home, after checking the effects on your spouse. See [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).
5. Ask whether adopting a stepchild, even an adult, fits your goals. See [adopting an adult stepchild](/learn/blended-families/adopting-an-adult-stepchild-to-make-them-an-heir).
6. Tell the family where the documents are.

## What if the stepchild is a child living at home?

A minor stepchild has the same lack of rights, but the practical risk is bigger: a house and support disappear. The spouse's award and child's award in an Illinois estate are tied to the decedent's own children, so a stepchild may not qualify. If you are raising a stepchild, add a gift in your will, name a guardian only if you have legal authority, and consider a trust so a minor does not receive money outright. Our page on [a minor inheriting money](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) explains why.

## Can a stepchild challenge a will or a gift?

Only an interested person can. If a will excluded the stepchild and an earlier will included them, the stepchild may have standing. If the stepchild was never in any document, they usually do not. The contest window is six months after the will is admitted (755 ILCS 5/8-1). Our page on the [will contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) has more.

## What should you do if your spouse has children from before?

Do both of you a favor and decide together. A shared plan can give your spouse the use of the home for life, leave a share to each set of children and name a neutral trustee. A trust can protect the surviving spouse and the stepchildren from each other. Our page on [blended family inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/blended-family-inheritance) compares the usual designs, and the [plan finder](/plan-finder) will suggest where to begin.

## What do families get wrong about stepchildren?

The most common mistake is assuming that "family" in the everyday sense is "family" for the law. Stepchildren who cared for a stepparent for years are often shocked to learn they have no standing. A second mistake is relying on a handshake promise, such as "the house will go to you kids." An oral promise is not a will, and an estate cannot honor it unless a document says so. A third mistake is a will that leaves everything to the spouse with an unwritten expectation that the spouse will pass it on. The spouse is then free to change plans, remarry or spend the money.

A calm family conversation helps, and so does a written plan. If you are a stepchild, ask your parent to confirm the plan in writing. If you are a stepparent, a short will naming each stepchild takes little time and removes the guesswork.
