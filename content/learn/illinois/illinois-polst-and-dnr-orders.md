Walter is 84 and lives in a care home in Springfield. He has heart failure and has told his daughter, more than once, that he does not want to be shocked, pounded on or put on a breathing machine. His living will says so. But at 3 a.m. the paramedics who arrive do not read living wills. They look for an order.

That order, in Illinois, is the POLST, and the Illinois Department of Public Health (IDPH) publishes the uniform form. This page explains what the form does, what the Health Care Surrogate Act says about it, and the practical traps. For the general, multi-state explanation, see [DNR and POLST orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst). This is general education, not legal advice, and part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## The short version

- A POLST is a **medical order** signed by a practitioner. A living will is **your statement**. They do different jobs.
- It is meant for people who are seriously ill or frail, or who know they do not want CPR.
- It is voluntary. Nobody can make you sign one to be admitted or treated.
- Your health care agent or a surrogate can sign it for you if you cannot, within the rules of the Act.

## What is the Illinois POLST form?

IDPH describes the Uniform POLST as a medical order that communicates a patient's advance decisions about CPR and life-sustaining treatment. It covers items such as mechanical ventilation and artificially administered nutrition and hydration. It can also say yes to treatment: IDPH notes that a patient can mark "YES CPR," which helps avoid wrong assumptions about patients with disabilities.

The statute defines POLST as "practitioner orders for life-sustaining treatments." It also recognizes a "POLST portable medical orders form," which covers MOST, MOLST, POST and POLST forms formally authorized by another U.S. state or territory (755 ILCS 40/65).

### Who should have one?

IDPH says people with serious, progressive illness, including frailty, who are near the end of life should consider a POLST, as should people who do not want CPR based on their own preferences. Walter fits. A healthy 40-year-old usually does not need one, and is better served by a [health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) and a living will. A POLST is a snapshot of treatment decisions for a person with a medical situation to match.

## How is a POLST different from an advance directive?

| | POLST | Health care power of attorney | Living will |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Medical order | Names an agent | Written statement of wishes |
| Who completes it | Patient and practitioner together | The patient, with witnesses | The patient |
| Who it is for | Seriously ill or frail patients | Every adult | Every adult |
| Who reads it | EMS, hospitals, nursing homes | Doctors, hospital staff | Doctors, agent |
| Works in a cardiac emergency | Yes, if present and visible | Not on its own | Rarely, since it is not an order |

IDPH puts it plainly: the POLST allows more specific directions than advance directives alone, and it travels with the patient between settings, while advance directives mainly guide decisions when the person cannot make them.

## Who can sign for the patient?

<!-- visual: whatif id=end-of-life-wishes-unknown -->

Section 65 says consent may come from the individual, a person acting at the individual's direction, a guardian, a health care agent or a surrogate. An adult, an emancipated minor, or a legal decision maker for an adult or minor may complete one, according to IDPH. A witness is no longer required.

An adult or emancipated individual of sound mind may also execute a document directing that resuscitation not be implemented, and may revoke it at any time. A qualified practitioner may execute such a document as well. Organ donation care may still continue (755 ILCS 40/65(a)).

### What about a surrogate under the Health Care Surrogate Act?

Section 20(h) allows a surrogate to execute a POLST form to forgo life-sustaining treatment, consistent with the rest of Section 20. That Section requires, among other things, a patient who lacks decisional capacity and a qualifying condition: a terminal condition, permanent unconsciousness, or an incurable or irreversible condition, certified in writing by the attending physician and at least one other qualified practitioner. The surrogate should follow the patient's likely wishes, and use best interests if those are unknown. If the surrogate is not a court-appointed guardian and the patient objects to the surrogate or the decision, the Act does not apply. Our article on [who makes medical decisions under the Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides) explains the priority list.

## Will hospitals and EMS honor it?

Section 65(c) says that, except as otherwise provided by law, EMS, providers and facilities must comply with a qualifying form that is apparent and immediately available. Qualifying forms include Illinois Uniform POLST forms, National POLST forms, other states' POLST portable medical orders, and out-of-hospital DNR orders sanctioned by a U.S. state. The form must be executed by an adult.

Providers may presume a completed form is valid unless they know otherwise, and good-faith compliance protects them from civil or criminal liability, except for willful and wanton misconduct. It is also not unprofessional conduct (755 ILCS 40/65(d)). Nothing in the section limits a physician's ability to write a DNR order (65(e)).

IDPH adds that the form applies when the patient cannot speak for themselves or has no pulse. A patient who can speak can accept or decline care differently from what the form says. That matters. The form is not a gag order on a patient who changes their mind in the room.

## How do you complete and use one?

1. **Talk to your practitioner.** IDPH says completion should follow a detailed discussion with a qualified health care practitioner.
2. **Complete the form together.** The patient or authorized representative prints and signs the consent section. IDPH notes electronic signatures may be acceptable under certain conditions, so ask.
3. **Put it where people will see it.** IDPH suggests keeping it where you live, such as on the refrigerator, and giving copies to your practitioner, your health care agent or surrogate, and any facility you move to.
4. **Review it.** The statute says to review annually and when your condition changes.
5. **Change it deliberately.** To revoke, IDPH describes writing "VOID" in large letters across the first page, dating it and re-signing, or destroying the form and all copies. Then tell your practitioners, caregivers and legal representative.

For where to store your other papers, see [where to keep your advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/where-to-keep-your-advance-directive).

## What if you move or travel?

A form issued by another state that is formally authorized there should be honored under Section 65(c), and IDPH says older Illinois forms and several out-of-state forms (MOST, MOLST, POST) are also honored. Even so, a form that looks unfamiliar slows a rushed crew. If you split the year between states, ask your practitioner whether to complete a form for each state.
