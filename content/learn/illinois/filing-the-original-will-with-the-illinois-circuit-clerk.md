You have found the will, or you are the person who was given it. Someone in the family says there is no hurry, or that the bank account is small enough to skip the court. The Illinois statute does not agree. This page explains the filing duty, what the clerk does with the paper, and the two 30-day clocks that people often mix up.

If you are still looking for the will, start with [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois). To see where filing fits in the full opening of a case, read [how to open a probate estate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-open-a-probate-estate-in-illinois). The wider guide is at [Illinois estate planning](/learn/illinois).

## Who has to file the will, and when?

Anyone who has the original will in their possession must file it with the circuit clerk of the proper county immediately after the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). That includes a spouse, an adult child, a friend, a lawyer who drafted it and a bank holding it in a box. The duty is personal. It does not wait for the executor to be chosen.

The clerk guidance in some counties speaks of 30 days after the date of death. We read that as shorthand for the section 6-3 clocks below, not a safe harbor. A person who waits a month to decide what to do is already late under the statute's wording.

## What are the 30-day clocks?

Two different 30-day periods appear in the Probate Act, and they bind different people.

| Clock | Who it binds | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Executor's 30 days | The person named as executor | Within 30 days after learning of the nomination, start a case to admit the will or decline in writing (755 ILCS 5/6-3) |
| Court's 30 days | The judge | If 30 days pass after the death and no petition has been filed, the court may proceed to admit the will without a petition (755 ILCS 5/6-3) |

The first clock matters to anyone named in the will. Our article on [refusing to be executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-refuse-to-be-executor-in-illinois) covers declining. The second is a backstop, so the law does not depend on a family getting around to it.

## What are the steps to file the original will?

1. Make sure you have the original, with the signatures, not a scan or photocopy.
2. Make a full-color copy for your records before you hand it over.
3. Find the correct county. It is generally the county where the person lived at death. Our article on [property in two counties](/learn/illinois/probate-venue-property-in-two-illinois-counties) explains the exceptions.
4. Deliver or mail the will to the circuit clerk, or follow the clerk's instructions for the will to be received. Ask for a receipt or file stamp.
5. Keep the receipt with the death certificate.
6. Tell the named executor, in writing, that the will is on file and the 30 days are running.
7. If you are the executor, plan the petition. See [the petition for probate and letters](/learn/illinois/illinois-petition-for-letters-of-office).

## What does the clerk do with the will?

The clerk takes custody and keeps it in the court file. Filing is not the same as admitting the will. Admission happens later, at a hearing, when a judge reviews the petition. Until then the will is simply safe. After it is filed, the document is generally available for the public to view in the court file. Our article on [seeing the will after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-see-the-will-after-someone-dies) explains who can get a copy.

Some clerks charge no fee to receive a will. Cook County's published guidance, for example, lists no filing fee for depositing a will with the probate clerk. Check your county on its clerk's page. See [Cook County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/cook) for the probate office address.

## What if there are two wills or only a copy?

If you find more than one will, file them all. The court decides which one controls, usually the latest valid one. Do not decide on your own. If only a copy exists, the court must be satisfied that the original was lost and not revoked, and it may require extra proof. Our page on [what happens if the family cannot find the will or the safe deposit box](/learn/what-if/family-cannot-find-your-will-or-open-the-safe-deposit-box) covers the lost-will case.

## What are the risks of waiting?

Marta, 54, finds her father's will in a desk drawer on March 2. His bank account holds $38,000. She and her brother decide to wait until after the holidays. By the time they come back, an uncle has asked about a copy and the family is arguing about whether a later will exists. A will that sits unfiled invites suspicion, and the person who held it is the first one questioned.

Delay also holds up the executor's authority. Banks will not release accounts, the estate cannot collect a final paycheck without help, and the six-month window to contest a will does not start until the will is admitted (755 ILCS 5/8-1). A fast filing helps everyone, including people who might later disagree.

## Does filing the will mean the estate needs probate?

Not always. If you are not sure whether the estate also needs probate, try the [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker). It does not replace filing the will.

## What should you not do with the will?

Do not write on it, staple new pages to it or remove the staple to copy it. Keep the pages in order and flat, and carry the original in a folder rather than loose in a bag. Do not hand the only original to a relative "for safekeeping." If a lawyer drafted the will and holds it, ask the lawyer to file it with the clerk and to give you the file stamp. Do not read the will to the family as a substitute for filing it. Reading it aloud is a kindness, but only the court can admit it, and only the clerk's file proves it was deposited on time.

If the will is in another county or state, ask the holder to mail it by a tracked method to the clerk of the county where the person lived. Keep the tracking number with the death certificate. A will that is lost in the mail is a lost-will problem, so use a trackable method or deliver it in person.
