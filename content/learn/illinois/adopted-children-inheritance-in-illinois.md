Families formed by adoption often ask two questions after a death. Does the adopted child inherit the same as any other child? And does that child still inherit from the birth family? Illinois has a written answer to both, with a few exceptions that matter.

This page covers the rule in the Probate Act, the main exceptions, how wills use words like "children" and what to do if you are the adopted person or the parent. For the general order of heirs, see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

## Does an adopted child inherit like a birth child in Illinois?

Yes, from the adopting parents. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-4, an adopted child is a descendant of the adopting parent for inheritance. The rule goes beyond the parent: the child also inherits through the parent's family, so a grandparent's estate can pass to the adopted child's branch, and adopted children can inherit from their adoptive siblings.

Take Mia, adopted at age 4 by Dan and Carla. Dan dies without a will with a $360,000 probate estate. Carla survives, and Dan and Carla also have a birth son, Noah. Carla takes one-half, $180,000, and Mia and Noah split the other half, $90,000 each. Nothing in the statute gives Noah more because he was born into the family.

## What happens to inheritance from the birth family?

Usually it ends. The statute says that, for inheritance from or through a birth parent, an adopted child generally stops being that parent's child. If Mia's birth father, Kurt, dies without a will years later, Mia is not his heir. Kurt's estate goes to his other relatives.

The Probate Act lists exceptions. As we read 755 ILCS 5/2-4, an adopted child can still be a child of both birth parents when:

- the child is adopted by a stepparent, in which case the adopting parent's spouse is treated as an adopting parent, so the child stays connected to the birth parent who is married to the stepparent
- the child is adopted by a close relative, defined in the statute by descent from the child's great-grandparent
- a birth parent died before the adoption

These exceptions are narrow and fact specific. The 1998 effective date for the current text also matters for older deaths. A family with an older adoption should have an attorney read the order and the statute together.

| Adoption type | Inherits from adoptive parent | Inherits from birth parent |
|---|---|---|
| Unrelated adoption, birth parents' rights ended | Yes, fully | Generally no |
| Stepparent adoption | Yes | Keeps the birth parent married to the stepparent |
| Adoption by a relative (see 2-4) | Yes | Yes, from both birth parents under the exception |
| Birth parent died before adoption | Yes | May inherit from that deceased parent |
| Foster care only, no adoption | No | Yes, from birth parents |

## What are the steps if you are not sure the adoption counts?

Start with the paperwork, because the final order controls.

1. Find the adoption decree or order, issued by an Illinois court or another state's court.
2. Check the date. The current rules apply to deaths on or after January 1, 1998.
3. Find out which birth parent, if any, died before the adoption.
4. List who is alive: adoptive parents, birth parents, siblings.
5. Ask the administrator of the estate or an attorney to name the heirs and confirm.
6. If the estate is in probate, an affidavit or finding of heirship can record the answer. See [proving heirship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois).

## How do wills treat adopted children?

A will can say anything. When a will leaves property to "my children" or "my descendants", the usual reading includes adopted children, but a will can limit this by saying "natural-born" or "biological" children. Older wills and trusts sometimes do. If you are reading one that appears to exclude adopted people, do not assume it works that way until an attorney has looked at the wording and at the family's state law.

When you make a plan, name each child by name. Our page on [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) lists what a will needs, and the [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) helps you pick a document.

## What about foster children and informal care?

A foster child who is not adopted has no intestacy rights against a foster parent. A child raised by an aunt, a grandparent or a family friend under an informal arrangement has none either. These caregivers can leave property by will or trust, and they can name the child on beneficiary forms. Grandparents who are raising grandchildren should look at [estate planning for grandparents raising grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-grandparents-raising-grandchildren).

## Can an adult be adopted to create inheritance rights?

Illinois permits adult adoption through the courts, and it has been used to include stepchildren. It is permanent and can change the person's rights in the birth family. Because the 1997 statute changes covered adult adoptees in some detail, an attorney should check the current text first. For the tradeoffs, read [adopting an adult stepchild](/learn/blended-families/adopting-an-adult-stepchild-to-make-them-an-heir) and our page on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/illinois/stepchildren-and-inheritance-in-illinois).

## What questions should an adopted person or a parent ask?

Adopted people sometimes ask whether they should also look for rights in a birth family. Usually the answer is no for inheritance, because the adoption ended the legal link, but the exceptions above matter. A birth parent who dies without a will does not owe the adopted child a share if the adoption was a standard one. Parents who adopted a child should ask whether their own documents, especially older ones, use the words "natural" or "biological," and update them. Grandparents who want to include an adopted grandchild should name the grandchild.

Records matter too. Keep the adoption decree with the will, since the administrator may need it to show who the heirs are. If the decree is sealed or hard to find, ask the court that entered it how to get a certified copy.
