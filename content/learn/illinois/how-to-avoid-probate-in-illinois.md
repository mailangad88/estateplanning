Maybe you have heard that Illinois probate takes months and that "a trust fixes it." Maybe a parent put a child on a bank account years ago and now you wonder whether that was a good idea. Either way, the real question is simpler: for each thing you own, what keeps it out of court, and what can go wrong with that tool?

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), works through your assets one at a time. It does not repeat how the court process runs, which is in [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process). If you would rather have an Illinois estate planning attorney sort your list with you, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-to-avoid-probate-in-illinois&topic=trust).

## Which assets actually go through probate in Illinois?

Probate covers assets titled in your name alone with no beneficiary, no survivor and no trust. A house deeded to you, a bank account with no payable-on-death designation, a brokerage account with no beneficiary and a car are typical examples. Anything that already passes by contract, by survivorship or through a trust is outside the probate estate. Our [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) article draws the line in more detail, and the [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) lets you list your own items.

<!-- visual: tool slug=probate-asset-sorter -->

The goal is not to avoid every dollar. It is to make sure the assets that would be slow, public or expensive in court have a direct route, and to let a will catch the rest.

## What tool fits which asset?

Match the asset to the tool in this table, then read the traps below it.

| Asset | Tool that keeps it out of court | Main trap |
|---|---|---|
| 401(k), IRA, annuity, life insurance | Beneficiary designation on the account | Named person died, is a minor, or the form was never updated |
| Bank and credit union accounts | Payable-on-death designation | Beneficiary gone, or the account used as a joint account by mistake |
| Illinois house or land | Recorded transfer on death instrument, or a deed into a living trust | Signed but not recorded; trust signed but deed never done |
| House owned with another person | Joint tenancy with survivorship or tenancy by the entirety | Co-owner's debts, divorce, and loss of control |
| Brokerage accounts, business interests, valuables | Beneficiary designation where offered, or assign to a living trust | Left in your name alone |
| Everything else, if small | Small estate affidavit | Personal property only, with a dollar cap, and the signer is responsible for debts |

## How do beneficiary forms keep retirement accounts and insurance out of probate?

The form is a contract with the company, and the company pays the named person directly. The will does not override it, which is why the form needs the same attention as the will. If it names your ex-spouse, the result after a divorce can depend on the form and the law that applies, so update it rather than hope. If it names no one, the account usually defaults to your estate.

<!-- visual: whatif id=minor-inherits-without-trust -->

The most common trap is a minor. A child cannot manage a large payout, and in Illinois a court may have to appoint a guardian of the child's estate. Our page on [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) explains that process, and the [UTMA accounts and gifts to minors](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) article covers the custodian option. Another trap is naming "my estate" or leaving a form blank, which sends the money back into probate. Always name a [contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary), and see [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) for wording. Most non-spouse beneficiaries of a retirement account must empty it within 10 years, so the form also shapes the beneficiary's tax picture. The [beneficiary designation audit](/free/beneficiary-designation-audit) is a checklist for going through every form you have.

## How do POD accounts work for bank accounts?

A payable-on-death designation tells the bank to pay the balance to your named beneficiary when the last account holder dies, if you signed a POD agreement with the bank (205 ILCS 625/4). The beneficiary has no rights while you are alive. If every named beneficiary has died, the account falls into your probate estate. A joint account is a different tool: a surviving holder may be paid (765 ILCS 1005/2), but a joint holder can also withdraw today. Our article on [payable-on-death and joint bank accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) covers both, and the [POD and TOD accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) article covers brokerage versions.

## How does a transfer on death instrument work for a house?

A transfer on death instrument (TODI) is a recorded document that passes Illinois real estate to a named beneficiary at your death, with no probate and no rights for the beneficiary while you are alive. It needs two credible witnesses and a notary, and it must be recorded with the county recorder before you die or it is void (755 ILCS 27/40). It does not shield the house from your creditors or from a surviving spouse's claim (755 ILCS 27/85 and 27/66). Our [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) page walks through the steps. If you own a Chicago condo, read [the condo and co-op page](/learn/illinois/chicago-condo-and-co-op) first.

## What are the risks of joint tenancy?

Joint tenancy avoids probate because the surviving owner takes the whole property, but you give the co-owner a present ownership interest. In Illinois a deed naming two owners is a tenancy in common unless it expressly says joint tenancy (765 ILCS 1005/1), and a tenancy in common share goes through probate. Adding a child to the deed also exposes the house to the child's lawsuits and divorce, needs the child's signature to sell, and can leave out your other children. Read [Illinois joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) and [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) before using it as a shortcut.

## When does a living trust make sense?

A living trust makes sense when you own several kinds of property, want a plan for incapacity, or want to hold a child's inheritance until an age you choose. The trust avoids probate only for assets that are actually in it (760 ILCS 3). That means a recorded deed for the house, retitled accounts and updated beneficiary forms where the trust should be named. A signed trust with an unfunded house leaves the house in probate. Our [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) page covers the legal rules, [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) lists the steps, and [land trust vs living trust](/learn/illinois/land-trust-vs-living-trust) clears up the other kind of Illinois trust. A land trust holds title but is not your whole plan.

## What if the estate is small and has no house?

Then the fallback is the small estate affidavit. It lets a person collect personal property without opening probate when the personal estate is $150,000 or less, not counting registered vehicles, and no letters of office are outstanding (755 ILCS 5/25-1). It cannot transfer real estate, and the person who signs must pay valid debts first. This is a cleanup tool, not a plan, so see [the Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) and the [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker). Probate is still the route when nothing else applies, and a [will](/learn/illinois/do-i-need-a-will) is still needed to direct what the other tools miss.

## Does avoiding probate avoid the Illinois estate tax?

No. Illinois tests your whole estate, including your house, retirement accounts, life insurance you own and non-probate accounts, against $4 million, and the tax is not avoided by using beneficiary forms or a trust. Our page on [whether your estate will owe Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/will-my-estate-owe-illinois-estate-tax) has a worked couple example. A revocable trust does not shelter assets from it either.

## Worked example: Priya and a house, an IRA and a checking account

This is an invented example. Priya, 66, is a widow in Naperville. She owns a house in her own name, an IRA, a checking account and a car. Her son Dev is 41 and her granddaughter Asha is 9.

- **IRA:** Priya names Dev as beneficiary and Asha as contingent, but Asha is a minor, so she asks about a custodian or trust before relying on that.
- **Checking:** Priya adds a POD designation naming Dev, and confirms the bank has it on file.
- **House:** She signs and records a TODI naming Dev, with Asha as contingent beneficiary.
- **Car:** It is left to pass under her will or the small estate route.
- **Estate tax:** Her total is under $4 million, so she notes it and moves on.

If Priya had only added Dev to the deed, Dev's creditors could reach the house, and the IRA form would still have needed a fix.

## A checklist before you rely on any of this

1. List every asset and how it is titled today.
2. Check each beneficiary form for a living, adult primary and a contingent.
3. Record any TODI or trust deed, and keep the recorded copy.
4. Add up the whole estate, probate and non-probate, against the $4 million test.
5. Keep a will for everything else, and review all of it after a marriage, divorce, birth or death.
