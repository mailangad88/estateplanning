Maybe your parent signed a trust years ago, and now the bank, the county or the hospital says the trust does not own anything. Or maybe you suspect your own trust has gaps. In Illinois the result depends on each asset, one at a time.

This page is the Illinois route map. Our general article on [what happens if you never funded your living trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) gives the big picture, and the [Illinois funding guide](/learn/illinois/funding-a-living-trust-in-illinois) explains how to prevent the problem. Here we focus on the Illinois tools that decide what happens after the fact. The pillar for the state is our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What route does each unfunded asset take in Illinois?

Each asset goes by its title. The trust only controls assets titled to the trustee, and the Illinois Trust Code treats a trust as created by a transfer of property to a trustee or a declaration that the owner holds specific property as trustee (760 ILCS 3/401). Without one of those, the asset is an ordinary probate asset.

| Asset in the owner's name alone | Likely route | Court needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Checking or savings, under the limit | Small estate affidavit (755 ILCS 5/25-1) | No |
| Brokerage account | Affidavit if the total fits, otherwise probate | Maybe |
| House or land | Probate, or a recorded transfer on death instrument | Usually yes |
| Registered car | Secretary of State process, apart from the cap | No |
| Account with a named beneficiary | Passes to the beneficiary | No |
| Retirement account | Beneficiary form controls | No |
| Joint account or joint tenancy | Survivor takes it | No |

Assets with a living beneficiary, a joint owner or a survivor skip both the trust and probate. Our article on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the sort.

## When can the family avoid court?

The family may avoid court only when what is left is small and has no real estate. The small estate affidavit works when no letters of office are outstanding and the personal estate is not over $150,000, not counting motor vehicles registered with the Secretary of State (755 ILCS 5/25-1). Read the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) article before relying on it. The person who signs takes personal responsibility for valid debts.

Two cautions apply to a trust plan. First, the will may leave everything to the trust, and a bank may ask whether an affidavit can pay a trustee. Holders decide this, so ask in writing. Second, the dollar limit is about the total of the estate, so a $90,000 account plus a $70,000 brokerage account is over it even though each is under it.

## What happens when probate is needed?

The pour-over will is filed with the circuit court clerk, and the executor opens an estate. Anyone holding a will must file it with the clerk within 30 days after death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). The court admits the will, issues letters of office and starts the creditor claims period. Then the executor collects the assets, pays valid claims and delivers what remains to the trustee. The trustee then follows the trust. Our guide to [Illinois probate](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) shows the stages, and the page on [how long probate takes](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) lists the usual waits.

The family keeps the privacy of the trust terms but not of the probate file. Creditors also keep their rights. The trust cannot shorten the claims period for assets that passed through the estate.

## What if the owner becomes incapacitated first?

The successor trustee has no power over assets outside the trust. For those, the family needs an agent under a [power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property). Whether that agent can retitle assets into the trust depends on the powers written in the form, so read it. If there is no usable power of attorney, the family may need a court-supervised guardianship of the estate under the Probate Act. Compare the cost and public record of that route with a signed deed. Our article on [dementia without a power of attorney](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney) covers it.

## What can you do to fix it now?

If the owner is alive and has capacity, fix it with simple steps:

1. List every asset and how it is titled.
2. Sign and record a deed for the house and any other real estate.
3. Retitle bank and brokerage accounts, or add a beneficiary designation.
4. Sign a general assignment for personal items.
5. Check that the pour-over will matches the trust.
6. Keep proof of each change with the trust document.

If the owner has died, you cannot add assets to the trust yourself. Do not move assets informally. A cleaner method is to work with the executor so each item goes through the proper route.

## Example: Walt's trust with a $250,000 gap

Walt, 74, signed a trust in 2018 and deeded his Peoria home to it. His $68,000 savings account and $182,000 brokerage account stayed in his own name. When Walt died, the house passed under the trust. The two accounts totaled $250,000, which is over the $150,000 affidavit limit, so his daughter Carla opened probate in [Peoria County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/peoria) to collect them and pour them into the trust. If Walt had retitled the brokerage account in 2018, only the $68,000 would be left, and Carla could have used an affidavit.

## Is an unfunded trust still worth keeping?

Yes. The trust document still holds your instructions for the people who will inherit, and the pour-over will sends the leftover estate to it. Trust terms for a child with a disability, a young beneficiary or a second marriage still apply once assets arrive. What you lose is speed and privacy, and you pay probate costs. Our page on [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) lists the usual items. Treat an unfunded trust as a half-built plan, not a failed one, and finish it with deeds and retitled accounts.

If you are the family member reading this after a death, make a list of every account and deed you can find, and share it with the executor and the trustee. Good records shorten the process and keep siblings on the same page.
