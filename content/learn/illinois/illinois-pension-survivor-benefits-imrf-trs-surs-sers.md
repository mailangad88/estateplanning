Your father taught in Illinois public schools for thirty years, then died in March. His will leaves everything to your sister. The envelope from his pension fund names you. Who is right?

You are, if the pension form says so. Illinois public pensions are paid under the retirement system's rules and the beneficiary form on file, not under the will. This page covers the four big systems, IMRF, TRS, SURS and SERS, what each one pays after a death, and the mistakes that cost families money. For the general version of this topic, see [how to claim life insurance, a pension and VA survivor benefits](/learn/after-a-death/claiming-life-insurance-pension-and-va-survivor-benefits). The rest of the state's rules are in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Which Illinois systems cover public employees?

Four statewide systems cover most public workers. Each has its own board, its own forms and its own rules, which is why a single answer never fits all of them.

| System | Who it covers | Where to start |
|---|---|---|
| IMRF (Illinois Municipal Retirement Fund) | Local government and other participating employers' staff | 1-800-275-4673 |
| TRS (Teachers' Retirement System) | Public school teachers and other covered education employees | (877) 927-5877 |
| SURS (State Universities Retirement System) | State university employees | 800-275-7877 |
| SERS (State Employees' Retirement System) | State agency employees | 217-785-7444 |

Some Chicago and local public employees belong to separate funds that this page does not cover. If you are not sure which fund paid the pension, check the last pay stub or the 1099-R tax form, or the deceased's mail.

## Who gets the benefit if there is a beneficiary form?

The person named on the fund's own beneficiary form. Not the will, not the family's agreement, not the person who paid for the funeral. IMRF says you can name any person, trust, charity or organization. SURS says the same and allows the estate to be named.

This is the same logic that applies to life insurance and retirement accounts. Our article on [which document controls](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form) walks through the order. For the idea of assets that skip probate, see [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

Here is a worked example. Example only: Denise is a retired county clerical worker with IMRF. Her will leaves "everything" to her daughter Kayla. Her IMRF beneficiary form, signed years ago, names her son Marcus. When Denise dies, the death benefit goes to Marcus. Kayla can read the will to the pension fund all day. The fund follows the form. IMRF says so itself: if you have an old form on file, it must follow the directions on that form, even if it names someone you no longer want.

## What happens if no form is on file?

The default depends on the system, and in several cases the money ends up in the estate. That means it joins the probate case, is open to creditor claims and is paid out under the will.

- **IMRF:** the default beneficiary is the member's estate.
- **SURS:** if there is no designation on file, the death benefit goes to the estate. If the primary beneficiaries have died, it goes to the contingent beneficiaries.
- **TRS:** with no form, the refund of contributions goes to the surviving spouse or civil union partner, or the estate if there is none. Survivor benefits go to an eligible dependent beneficiary, or the estate if there is none.
- **SERS:** the fund publishes death benefit pages for each tier, and we have not summarized them here. Call 217-785-7444 and ask what happens when no form is on file.

When a benefit lands in the estate, the executor needs letters of office to claim it. Our guide to [the Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) covers when a smaller estate can skip court, and [the Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) covers the rest.

## What does each system pay a surviving family?

Two different things can be paid: a refund or lump sum, and an ongoing survivor pension. Read the benefit letter carefully, because they go to different people in some systems.

### IMRF

IMRF pays a lump sum death benefit to the beneficiaries. IMRF has announced that this lump sum rises from $3,000 to $8,000 beginning January 1, 2027, for members who first retire on or after that date and certain vested members who die. Those who first retired on or before December 31, 2026 receive $3,000. A surviving spouse may also be entitled to a surviving spouse pension, paid for life even if the spouse remarries. For a spouse to qualify for that pension when an active member dies, IMRF says the member must name the spouse as the only primary beneficiary on the form. A spouse who is named alongside a child can lose out.

### TRS

TRS pays a refund of the member's remaining contributions and survivor benefits, and each can go to different beneficiaries. A spouse or civil union partner married at least one year, certain unmarried children and a dependent parent can be dependent beneficiaries. A dependent can choose monthly benefits or a lump sum, while a nondependent gets a lump sum. Monthly benefits require 1.5 years of TRS service credit and at least 60 days of creditable service in the 18 months before death. Children are eligible only if they are the children of the surviving parent receiving monthly benefits, unless named on the form. TRS also warns that when a member names a new spouse after a divorce, children from the earlier marriage are not eligible for monthly survivor benefits. That one catches blended families off guard.

### SURS

SURS pays differently by plan. For the Traditional plan, Tier I, its member guide lists several kinds of qualifying survivors. One is a spouse or civil union partner married at least a year who is 50 or older or caring for eligible children. Others are unmarried children under 18 or up to 22 as full-time students, disabled children and dependent parents age 55 or older. After retirement the benefit is either a survivor benefit or a death benefit, not both. SURS says it takes a current beneficiary form and that a former spouse is disqualified from survivor benefits after a divorce.

### SERS

SERS organizes its death benefits by Tier 1 and Tier 2 and by occupational versus nonoccupational death. Ask SERS which category applies, because the amounts and eligibility differ.

## What should the family do first?

Move in this order. It is mostly phone calls.

1. Find out which fund or funds paid or would have paid the person. Check mail, direct deposits and the last tax form.
2. Order certified death certificates. Our page on [Illinois death certificates](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph) explains the county clerk and state routes.
3. Call the fund and report the death. IMRF asks for the member's name, the last four digits of the Social Security number, the date of death and who you are. It mails a letter to the person handling the member's affairs, and after it gets the death certificate it sends a benefit option letter to the beneficiary or beneficiaries.
4. Do not ask the fund to tell you who the beneficiary is. IMRF does not disclose beneficiary names over the phone, so expect that the letter to the person handling the affairs will contain the information.
5. A surviving spouse should have the marriage certificate and birth certificate ready for IMRF.
6. Hold the pension deposit that arrives after the death. Payments for months after the date of death usually have to be returned, and the fund can pull it from the account.
7. If the benefit lands in the estate, tell the executor so it is listed in the inventory.

## Can the beneficiary form be changed, and by whom?

Only by the member while alive, in most cases. IMRF takes changes through its online Member Access system, and it says it will not accept a change by phone or email. It also says it cannot accept the signature of anyone other than the member, which rules out agents under a power of attorney, conservators, trustees and guardians. That matters for families managing a parent with dementia: if the form is wrong and the parent can no longer sign, the form usually stays as it is. Our article on [whether an agent can change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary) takes up the general rule.

Naming a trust is allowed, but it needs care. IMRF says it will need the trust to provide a full copy of the trust document, and TRS allows a trust to receive monthly benefits for a minor child or disabled adult dependent only if it has specific language. Name a trust only after an attorney has read the plan's rules.

## Mistakes our attorneys see

- **Updating the will after a divorce but not the pension form.** The fund follows the form. TRS and IMRF both list marriage, divorce, births and deaths as reasons to review it.
- **Leaving the estate as beneficiary by default.** The benefit then goes through probate and past creditors.
- **Naming a minor child directly.** The fund usually cannot hand money to a child, and a court-supervised arrangement can follow. See [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois).
- **Forgetting QILDROs.** A court order dividing a pension in a divorce, called a Qualified Illinois Domestic Relations Order, can affect who is paid. SURS says its benefits may not be paid to anyone other than the member unless a QILDRO names an alternate payee.
- **Treating the pension as part of the will's math.** A parent who gives "equal shares" in the will but names one child on the pension form has unequal shares in practice.
