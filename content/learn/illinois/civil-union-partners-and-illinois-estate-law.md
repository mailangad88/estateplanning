You and your partner entered a civil union in Illinois, and now you want to know what happens if one of you dies. Do you inherit automatically? Can a will leave you out? Does the law treat you like a married couple?

This page answers those questions under Illinois law and points out where a civil union stops matching marriage. It is general education, not legal advice. For the wider topic, start with our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## How does Illinois treat a civil union partner for inheritance?

Like a spouse. The Illinois Civil Union Act says a party to a civil union is entitled to the same legal obligations, responsibilities, protections and benefits as a spouse (750 ILCS 75/20). Our Illinois spouse pages use "spouse" to include a civil union partner for that reason.

In practice, that means the rights described in [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) should apply to a surviving partner: renouncing a will to take a statutory share, and claiming the spouse's award. We cover those two rights below, but the full rules, amounts and deadlines are on that page.

Illinois does not recognize common-law marriage, so living together for years gives no rights. A couple who never entered a civil union or married is covered in [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples).

## What happens if my civil union partner dies without a will?

Your partner is generally treated as a surviving spouse under the intestacy list in 755 ILCS 5/2-1. Using the spouse rules in [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois), a surviving spouse takes everything if there are no descendants, and shares half with the descendants if there are.

Here is an example. Tomas and Greg are in a civil union. Tomas dies with no will, no children, and a house, a car and savings in his name alone worth $450,000. If the partner is treated as a spouse, Greg takes the whole probate estate and Tomas's parents and sister take nothing. If Tomas had a daughter from an earlier relationship, the estate would be split half and half, and Greg would share the house with her.

Two cautions. First, the intestacy list covers only the probate estate. Retirement accounts, life insurance, a joint account and a trust pass by their own paperwork. Second, the family house often needs a conversation, not just a rule. Half to a stepchild can mean a sale.

## Can a will cut out a civil union partner?

Not entirely. A will can leave a partner little or nothing, but a surviving spouse can renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if the decedent left descendants, or one-half if not (755 ILCS 5/2-8). The renunciation is filed within 7 months after the will is admitted to probate. The spouse's award is a separate claim, at least $20,000 as of 2026, so confirm the amount.

Because 750 ILCS 75/20 puts civil union partners on the same footing, the same rights should reach a surviving partner. An attorney should confirm how a court would apply them to your facts, particularly if the civil union is recent or the will was signed before it. A prenuptial-style agreement can waive these rights, as covered in [prenuptial agreements and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).

## Where does a civil union differ from marriage?

Mostly outside Illinois. The three differences that matter most are shown below.

| Area | Illinois estate law | Outside Illinois law |
|---|---|---|
| Inheriting without a will | Partner generally treated as a spouse | Another state may not recognize the union |
| Renouncing a will | Same as a spouse (755 ILCS 5/2-8) | Uncertain elsewhere |
| Federal programs and tax rules | Illinois cannot change them | Often written around marriage; check each one |

Federal tax law is the largest gap. Rules like the unlimited marital deduction for gifts and estate transfers are written around marriage, so a civil union partner may not get that treatment on a large transfer. That matters most when an estate is large. Illinois taxes estates over $4 million (see [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax)), and a surviving spouse cannot add the other spouse's unused amount to their own. Federal estate tax applies above $15 million per person in 2026. Retirement accounts and Social Security also follow federal definitions, so ask each plan administrator how it treats a civil union partner.

Some couples decide to marry for this reason alone. Others keep the civil union. Either is a legitimate choice, and the plan should be built on whichever you pick. ## What should civil union partners sign?

Treat the civil union as a strong starting point, then add the paper that tells people what you want. This checklist is what our attorneys usually put in place.

1. **Wills.** Each partner names the other, names backups, and chooses an executor. See [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) for the signing rules.
2. **Power of attorney for property.** Names the person who can pay bills and manage accounts if you cannot. Read about the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
3. **Health care power of attorney.** Names your agent for medical decisions. Our guide to the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) explains how to complete it.
4. **Beneficiary forms.** Check the 401(k), IRA, life insurance and bank accounts. A form beats a will.
5. **Deed review.** If you own a home together, check whether you hold it as joint tenants, tenants in common, or tenants by the entirety. See [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).
6. **Funeral and burial wishes.** Put them in writing. See [the Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act).
7. **A copy of the civil union certificate** kept with your documents, in case a bank, hospital or agency asks for proof.

## Which assets does the partner's status not control?

A surprising amount. Status helps only with property that passes through the probate estate. Everything else follows its own form, so the civil union does not decide it.

- **Retirement accounts and life insurance** go to the named beneficiary. If the form still names an old partner or a parent, that person is paid, whatever the intestacy list says.
- **Joint bank accounts** with survivorship go to the survivor. A joint account with no survivorship wording may not. See [payable on death and joint bank accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts).
- **Real estate in a living trust** follows the trust document. Tomas and Greg could hold their home in a joint trust that names the survivor, then their chosen heirs, avoiding probate entirely.
- **A car** needs the title steps in [transferring a car title after death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/transfer-car-title-after-death-in-illinois).

Run through your accounts once a year. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you find forms that no longer match your plan.

## What about children from another relationship?

A civil union does not by itself give a partner any rights to a partner's child, and a child's rights to inherit from a non-parent depend on adoption or a legal parent relationship. If either of you has children, the will and any guardian nominations matter more, not less. Our article on [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) can help, and a trust can hold money for a child while your partner manages it.

## What if the relationship ends?

A civil union ends through a court order, so a breakup without a court order leaves your partner with spousal rights. Update beneficiary forms, powers of attorney and your will once the process starts. [How divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan) explains what the law changes automatically and what it does not.
