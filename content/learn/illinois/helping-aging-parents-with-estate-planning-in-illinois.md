Your parents are in their late seventies or eighties. They have some papers somewhere, maybe a will from when you were in school, and you are not sure anything still works. You want to help without taking over.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), is written for the adult child. It covers how to audit what your parents already have, what you can and cannot do around the attorney meeting, and why your parent has to be the one deciding. It ends with an example of two siblings in Naperville. When your parents are ready to meet someone, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-helping-aging-parents-with-estate-planning-in-illinois&topic=parent) on their behalf.

## Who is the client when you help a parent plan?

Your parent is. The attorney represents your mother or father, takes instructions only from them and keeps what they say confidential, even if you found the attorney, made the call and paid the bill. That is what keeps the plan valid.

Two Illinois rules sit behind it. A will is valid only if the person signing had sound mind and memory at that moment (755 ILCS 5/4-1), which the page on [testamentary capacity in Illinois](/learn/illinois/testamentary-capacity-in-illinois) explains. And Illinois courts presume [undue influence](/learn/illinois/undue-influence-will-challenges-in-illinois) when a trusted helper who benefits also prepared or arranged the will. A child who picks the lawyer, attends the meeting and inherits more is the textbook case.

So the useful question is not "what should Mom sign" but "how do I help Mom decide for herself." The general limits on acting for another adult are on [can you make an estate plan for someone else](/learn/basics/can-you-make-an-estate-plan-for-someone-else).

## How do you start the conversation without taking over?

Open with the papers, not the plan. "If you were in the hospital, I would not know where anything is. Can you show me?" is a request for help, not a demand, and most parents can say yes to it. Looking through existing documents together often surfaces the problem without you naming it: a will that names a brother who has died, or a power of attorney from 2005 that nobody can find.

Keep decisions out of that first talk. The wording for harder moments is on [how to talk to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan), and the [aging parent conversation kit](/free/aging-parent-conversation-kit) has scripts.

## What should you look for in your parents' papers?

Look for anything that controls property or decisions, and check each item for three things: is it signed properly, does it name people who are alive and willing, and does it match what your parents now want. Use this as a checklist while you sit with them.

| Item to find | What to check | Common problem |
|---|---|---|
| Original will | Signed, two witness signatures, where the original is kept | Only a copy exists; executor has died or moved away |
| Living trust | Whether the house and accounts were retitled into it | Trust signed but never funded |
| Power of attorney for property | Agent and successor still able to serve; notary present | Names only a spouse, who is now also elderly |
| Power of attorney for health care | Agent named; one witness signature | One parent has one, the other never signed |
| Living will or POLST | Present and current | Wishes never written down |
| HIPAA release at each doctor | On file with current providers | Doctors will not talk to the children |
| Deed to the house | How it is titled; any recorded TODI | Joint tenancy with a child added years ago |
| Bank and brokerage titles | Joint owners, payable on death names | A child added "for convenience" who would now take the whole account |
| Retirement and pension forms | Primary and contingent beneficiaries | Contingent left blank, or an ex-spouse still named |
| Life insurance and annuities | Current beneficiaries | Policy names the estate |

Write down what you find, but leave originals where your parents keep them. A missing original will can be treated as revoked in Illinois, as [when the original will cannot be found](/learn/illinois/lost-original-will-in-illinois) explains, so do not take it home. The [estate plan document locator](/free/estate-plan-document-locator) is a place to record locations, and the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps sort the forms.

## Which documents should your parents end up with?

Each parent needs their own set, because each document speaks for one person: a will or a will with a living trust, a power of attorney for property, a power of attorney for health care, a HIPAA release, and beneficiary forms that fit the plan. The [Illinois estate planning checklist](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-checklist-illinois) lists every item with its signing rule.

Two things are worth flagging for retired parents. Some forms can only be changed by the parent personally; IMRF, for example, will not accept a beneficiary change signed by an agent, as the page on [estate planning for retirees in Illinois](/learn/illinois/estate-planning-for-retirees-in-illinois) notes. And a transfer on death instrument for the house cannot be created by an agent either. If the audit shows a form or a deed that needs changing, your parent has to do it while they can.

## Can you make the appointment and pay for it?

Yes to both, with limits. You can research attorneys, call, book, send the audit list ahead and drive your parent there. You can pay. What changes is what happens next.

- **The attorney will talk to your parent directly**, often by phone before the meeting, to confirm your parent wants it.
- **Your parent sets the agenda.** If you send notes, send facts (accounts, the deed, the old will), not instructions about who should get what.
- **Paying does not make you the client.** You may not be told what your parent decided unless they agree.
- **Reminders are help; scripts are not.** Reminding your parent of the appointment is help. Telling them what to say is not.

The [what to bring to an estate planning consultation](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) page lists the papers to pack.

## Can you be in the room when they sign?

Usually not for the decisions, and not as a witness. Expect the attorney to ask you to wait outside while they talk with your parent alone, then perhaps invite you back afterward. That private time is the record that the choices were your parent's.

Witnessing is a separate problem. An Illinois will needs two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3). The property power of attorney needs a witness and a notary (755 ILCS 45/3-3), and the witness cannot be a descendant of the parent or a relative of an agent, so neither you nor your siblings qualify. The health care form needs one adult witness (755 ILCS 45/4-10). The signing steps for the power of attorney are on [how to get power of attorney for a parent in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-get-power-of-attorney-for-a-parent-in-illinois). For a will, staff at the attorney's office usually serve.

## What safeguards protect the plan, and you, from a later challenge?

The safeguards that defeat a [capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) or undue influence claim are mostly things the helping child does or does not do. Follow these steps in order.

1. **Let your parent choose the attorney,** or offer two or three names and let them pick.
2. **Stay out of the meeting** where wishes are discussed, and out of the signing room.
3. **Schedule for when your parent is most alert,** away from a hospital stay or a new medication.
4. **Ask whether a doctor's note would help** if there is any diagnosis or memory concern. The [is it too late to sign checklist](/free/is-it-too-late-to-sign-checklist) helps prepare.
5. **Do not keep the plan secret from siblings.** Telling a brother or sister that Dad is seeing an attorney costs little and removes the look of isolation.
6. **Keep your own role small if you benefit.** If the plan favors you, it matters even more that someone else drove, booked or sat in the waiting room.
7. **Write down what you see** if you suspect someone else is steering your parent. The [undue influence warning signs checklist](/free/undue-influence-warning-signs-checklist) has a log, and [how to report elder financial abuse in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-report-elder-financial-abuse-in-illinois) says where to turn.

The pattern courts examine is described in [what if one child took a parent to the lawyer to change the will](/learn/what-if/what-if-one-child-took-a-parent-to-the-lawyer-to-change-the-will).

## What if a parent refuses or cannot sign?

If your parent understands the choice and says no, the answer stands for now. The approaches that tend to change a no, such as starting with the health care document, are on [what to do if your parent refuses to make a will or sign a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/what-to-do-if-your-parent-refuses-to-make-a-will-or-sign-a-power-of-attorney).

If your parent may no longer understand, that is an assessment for the attorney, not the family. The page on [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney in Illinois](/learn/illinois/dementia-no-power-of-attorney) explains how it is judged and what guardianship involves. If one parent can sign and the other cannot, the healthy parent's documents still matter, often more.

## Example: Karen and Luis help their parents in Naperville

This is an invented example. Frank, 82, and Joan, 79, have lived in the same Naperville house for forty years. Their daughter Karen, 54, lives ten minutes away. Their son Luis, 50, lives in Rockford. After Frank's fall in the spring, Karen asks to see where their papers are.

**The audit.** At the kitchen table they find 1998 wills naming Frank's brother, who has since died, as executor. Frank has a 2009 power of attorney for property naming Joan only. Joan has no health care power of attorney. The house is in joint tenancy between Frank and Joan. Frank's IRA names Joan, with no contingent beneficiary. Karen's name is on a checking account, added years ago "so she can pay bills."

**Splitting the work.** Karen writes the list. Luis, who lives farther away and stands to gain nothing extra, calls two attorneys and lets his parents pick one. Karen sends the list of documents to the office, with no notes on who should inherit.

**The meeting.** The attorney speaks with Frank and Joan without the children. Joan has mild memory complaints, so her doctor writes a short note that week. Karen waits in the lobby; Luis drives back to Rockford.

**What the parents decide.** They name each other as agents, with Luis as successor for property and Karen as successor for health care. Their new wills name Karen as executor and split everything equally. The attorney raises the joint checking account, since after both parents die it would pass to Karen alone, and Frank and Joan decide to change how it is titled. Frank names his children as contingent IRA beneficiaries. Office staff witness the documents; neither child signs anything.

Afterward, Frank and Joan tell both children who holds which role and keep the rest to themselves.
