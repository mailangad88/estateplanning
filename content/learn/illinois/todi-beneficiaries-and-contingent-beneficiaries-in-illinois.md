Most TODI mistakes are not about the signing. They are about the names. A parent writes "to my daughter Nora," Nora dies in a car accident, and the house now has no one to receive it. Or a parent writes "to my three children" and later learns two of them cannot agree about the roof.

Our overview of the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) explains the signing and recording rules. This page covers only the beneficiary clause, which is where most of the real decisions are made. See also the [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois). This is general education, not legal advice.

## How do shares work when there is more than one beneficiary?

Under the Act, multiple beneficiaries take equal and undivided shares, with no right of survivorship, unless the instrument says otherwise (755 ILCS 27/65). "Undivided" means each person owns a percentage of the whole house, not a room or a floor.

That default fits many families, but it has consequences. Co-owners need each other to sell or refinance. If one co-owner cannot be found, the others may need a court action to divide or sell. Our article on [heirs property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) explains how those fights start.

## What does a contingent beneficiary do?

A contingent, or backup, beneficiary takes the share if the first person cannot. Without one, a share that fails may go to the owner's estate. The Act also has a rule for a beneficiary who was the owner's descendant and dies first: that person's own descendants may take that share (755 ILCS 27/65). Because that rule has conditions, write the result you want in the instrument instead of relying on the default.

| Situation | Without a backup clause | With a clear backup clause |
|---|---|---|
| Only beneficiary dies first, was owner's child | Descendants may take under the Act | Named backup takes as written |
| Only beneficiary dies first, was a friend | Property can pass to the owner's estate | Named backup takes |
| One of three children dies first | Descendants of that child may take | Fractions and substitutes follow the text |
| All beneficiaries die first | Property passes to the estate and probate | Backup or a trust takes |

Our article on [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) covers the general idea for forms. Remember that a TODI is also not the only document that can leave a house. If a TODI fails, a will might still control. See [do you need a will](/learn/illinois/do-i-need-a-will).

## How do you word the beneficiary clause step by step?

1. List each beneficiary by full legal name and relationship.
2. State each person's share as a fraction that adds to one whole.
3. Name a contingent beneficiary for each, or for the group.
4. Say whether a deceased beneficiary's descendants take that share.
5. Decide whether a minor, an adult with benefits, or a person with creditor problems needs a trust.
6. Have an attorney read the legal description and signatures, because the instrument must be recorded to work. See [recording an Illinois TODI](/learn/illinois/recording-an-illinois-todi).

## What if the beneficiary is a minor or has special needs?

A minor cannot manage real estate. If a child inherits directly, someone may need court authority to sell or lease the house, as our article on [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) explains. A better path is to name a trust for the child as the beneficiary. For an adult who receives benefits such as Supplemental Security Income, a direct gift can affect eligibility, which is why a [special needs plan](/learn/illinois/special-needs-planning-in-illinois-for-a-disabled-child) uses a trust.

## A worked example with numbers

Walter, 74, owns a home worth about $300,000. He signs a TODI naming his daughter Karen and his son Paul, each for one half. He does not name a backup. Karen dies when Walter is 78, leaving two children. Under the default rule for a descendant who dies first, Karen's two children may take her half, about $75,000 each in value. Walter would have preferred that, but he never wrote it, and a title company may still want a lawyer to confirm.

Now suppose Karen had no children. Her half could fail and pass to Walter's estate, where it follows his will or the intestacy rules. Paul would own half the house under the TODI and be in a court case for the other half. A single line naming Paul as backup would have kept the house out of probate.

## When is a TODI the wrong tool for a complex family?

If you want a house to stay in the family for years, to pay a surviving spouse for life, or to protect an adult child from a creditor, a TODI is too blunt. See our comparison of [a TODI and a living trust](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house). Blended families also face spouse rights, which a TODI does not remove. Our article on spouse rights at death in Illinois explains.

## How do you check the plan stays current?

Reread the beneficiary clause after a death, a divorce, a birth or a falling out. A TODI cannot be fixed by a will. Changing it takes a new recorded instrument or a recorded revocation. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you list your other forms at the same time.

## What are common wording problems?

Short phrases look harmless and cause the most trouble. Here are the usual ones and a cleaner way to say each.

| Phrase on the instrument | Problem | Clearer wording |
|---|---|---|
| "To my children" | Which children, and what about a child who dies first? | List each child by name and state what happens to that child's share |
| "To my wife" | Divorce or a second marriage can make this unclear | Use the spouse's full name, and review after any change |
| "To my son Mark and his wife" | Names a person who may not be a family member later | Name each person you mean |
| "To my family" | Too vague for a recorder or title company | Name individuals or a trust |
| "To Mark, or to his heirs" | Does not say who the heirs are or how they share | Say whether descendants take, and in what shares |

Take time with the clause even if you use a standard form. A recorder will accept almost any wording that is properly signed, so the check has to come from you or your lawyer. Read the clause aloud and ask who gets the house in each of these cases: the beneficiary dies first, two beneficiaries die together, or the beneficiary cannot be found.
