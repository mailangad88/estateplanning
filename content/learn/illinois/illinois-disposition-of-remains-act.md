Rosa dies on a Tuesday. She was 71, divorced, and lived with her partner of nine years, Carl. Her two grown children live in Naperville and Tucson. Within hours the funeral home asks one question: who is authorizing the arrangements, and do we cremate or bury?

In Illinois, a statute answers that, and Carl is not on it. The Disposition of Remains Act (755 ILCS 65) tells hospitals, funeral homes and crematories whose instructions control. This article gives the order, the paperwork that changes it, and the disputes that tend to follow. It is part of our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) and is general education, not legal advice.

## The short version

- Your own written instructions come first, or the agent you name in them.
- If you left nothing, the law goes down a list: spouse, adult children, parents, nearest kin.
- Same-rank relatives decide by majority. Courts decide the rest.
- Naming a person on a notarized form is the only way to put a partner or friend in charge.

## Who has the right to control burial or cremation?

Section 5 of the Act gives the right, in this order, to the following people. The order applies when the decedent left no written directions and no agent under the Crematory Regulation Act or Section 40 of the Act (755 ILCS 65/5):

| Rank | Who | How a group decides |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A person named in a written instrument that meets Sections 10 and 15 | One person |
| 2 | The executor or legal representative, following written instructions in a will | One person |
| 3 | The surviving spouse | One person |
| 4 | Surviving adult children | The sole child, or a majority |
| 5 | Surviving competent parents | If one parent is absent and cannot be found after reasonable efforts, the other acts |
| 6 | Surviving adult next of kin in the nearest degree | Majority |
| 7 | A recognized religious, civic, community or fraternal organization willing to take legal and financial responsibility | The organization |
| 8 | For indigents or deaths the State must handle, a public official such as a public administrator, medical examiner or coroner | The official |
| 9 | For donated bodies, or deaths in a nursing home or private institution responsible for arrangements, a representative of that institution | The representative |
| 10 | Any other person or organization willing to take legal and financial responsibility | The person |

An adult means age 18 or older. A person lower on the list has the rights, duties and liabilities of Section 5 only if nobody ranked above them exists (Section 20). Service members get a special rule: if a member of the armed forces, the Illinois National Guard or the reserves completed a DD Form 93 (or its successor), the person named on that form controls.

Back to Rosa. She wrote nothing. Her two children are the adult children, so a majority of them controls, and with two children that means they agree. Carl has no vote and no authority to sign the cremation order, even though he is the person who knows what she wanted.

## How do you put someone else in charge?

<!-- visual: whatif id=unmarried-partner-no-will -->

Write it down. Section 40 lets you give written directions for disposition, or name an agent to make the decisions. Directions can appear in several places.

- a will.
- a prepaid funeral or burial contract.
- a health care power of attorney under Article IV of the Illinois Power of Attorney Act that includes disposition powers.
- a cremation authorization form under the Crematory Regulation Act.
- a notarized instrument that meets Sections 10 and 15.

The directions can be changed or revoked only by a later signed writing. Those with the right to control must carry them out so far as the estate or the controlling person can afford it (755 ILCS 65/40).

### What does the agent form require?

Section 10 supplies a model form: your name and a statement that you are of sound mind, your agent's name and any limits on the agent's authority, alternates who act in order if the first agent dies, becomes disabled, resigns or refuses, and signature blocks. The form also lets you choose cremation options, either barring survivors from canceling a cremation or allowing only named survivors to cancel. It states that the appointment takes effect at death and revokes earlier appointments, and that funeral homes and crematories may rely on a copy.

Section 15 makes the paper legally sufficient if it substantially follows that form, is completed, and is signed by both you and the agent, with your signature notarized. The agent may sign at any time, but has authority only after signing. That two-signature rule is where homemade versions fail. A form you sign alone, with the agent never accepting, does not do what you think.

## Do funeral instructions in a will work?

Yes, and they work quickly. Directions in a will must be carried out immediately, without probate. If the will is never probated or is held invalid, the directions stay valid to the extent they were acted on in good faith (755 ILCS 65/40(b)). Still, wills are often found late. Many families read the will after the funeral. If your wishes matter, give the agent form to the person named and keep a copy where the family can find it, and see [planning your own funeral in advance](/learn/basics/plan-your-own-funeral-in-advance).

Also note Section 30: no one may authorize cremation if you left written instructions that you did not want to be cremated.

## What happens when family members disagree?

Disputes between people listed in Section 5 about who controls the remains go to a court, and the Act directs resolution within 30 days after the dispute is filed. A cemetery or funeral establishment is not liable for refusing to accept or dispose of remains until it receives a court order or other suitable confirmation that the dispute is settled (755 ILCS 65/50). Disinterment disputes are heard by a circuit court as promptly as is reasonably possible, and a court that finds bad faith may order that party to pay costs, including attorney's fees.

A dispute in the first week is miserable for everyone. The better move is to avoid it. Name an agent, name an alternate, and tell your family.

## What to do if you are the one planning the funeral

1. Ask the funeral home to confirm who they believe controls under Section 5.
2. Look for a will, a prepaid contract, a cremation authorization or a health care power of attorney with disposition language.
3. If you are one of several adult children, talk to your siblings before calling anyone. A majority controls.
4. Sign the identity statements carefully. Under Section 35, anyone who signs an order claiming to know the decedent's identity is liable for resulting damages.
5. Keep receipts. The people with the right also carry the responsibility for reasonable costs. See [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral).
