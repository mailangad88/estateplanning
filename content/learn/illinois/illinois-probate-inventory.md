Once an Illinois executor has letters, the next question is usually, "What exactly do I list?" The answer is a document with a plain name and some precise rules. This page covers what goes on the inventory, what stays off, how to value each item and who is entitled to see it.

For general accounting habits, read [probate inventory and estate accounting](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting). For the whole case, see [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) and the [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois).

## What is an Illinois probate inventory?

It is a written list of the probate property that the executor or administrator takes into control, with a value for each item as of the date of death. The Probate Act calls for a verified inventory of the real and personal estate (755 ILCS 5/14-1). Verified means the representative swears it is complete as far as they know.

The inventory is the starting line for everything that follows. It sets the number from which fees, taxes and distribution are measured, and it is the first section of the closing report.

## Who sees the inventory, and who files it?

The rule depends on the type of administration.

| | Supervised | Independent |
|---|---|---|
| Filed with the court | Yes, generally within 60 days after letters | No, kept by the executor |
| Who gets a copy | Court file is available to interested persons | Interested persons who ask; surety if there is a bond |
| Later assets | Supplemental inventory filed with the court | Update the list and share on request |
| Used at closing | Reviewed by the court in the account | Described in the verified report |

The statute also speaks to copies for the surety (755 ILCS 5/28-6). Our page on [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains the difference in oversight. If you are not sure which applies, the order granting letters says.

## What goes on the inventory and what stays off?

Only probate assets go on. That means property in the decedent's sole name with no beneficiary and no co-owner.

| Asset | On the probate inventory? | How to value |
|---|---|---|
| House in the decedent's sole name | Yes | Appraisal or broker opinion at date of death |
| Bank account in sole name | Yes | Date-of-death balance from the bank |
| Account with payable-on-death beneficiary | No | Not probate; keep a separate list |
| Joint account with right of survivorship | No | Passes to the survivor |
| Brokerage account in sole name | Yes | Closing prices on the date of death |
| Car titled in sole name | Yes | Book value from a standard guide |
| IRA or 401(k) with a named beneficiary | No | Passes by beneficiary form |
| Furniture, jewelry and tools | Yes | Fair market value, grouped if low value |
| Final paycheck or refund | Yes | Amount owed |
| Business interest | Yes | Written valuation or accountant's opinion |

## What are the steps to prepare the inventory?

1. Collect the letters, death certificates and the last year of statements for every account.
2. Search for assets: mail, tax returns, safe deposit box, unclaimed property. See [finding unclaimed property for an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/finding-unclaimed-property-for-an-illinois-estate).
3. List each asset with its title, account number ending and location.
4. Value each asset at the date of death and note the source.
5. Order an appraisal for real estate and for items that are unusual, such as art or a business.
6. Total the real estate and the personal property separately.
7. Sign the verification. File it if the estate is supervised. Keep a dated copy if it is independent.
8. Send a copy to anyone entitled to it, and to the surety if there is a bond.
9. Update the list when new assets appear.

## How do you value a house, a car or a collection?

Use fair market value on the date of death. For a house, most executors order a written appraisal, which also helps with income tax basis and step-up, because the appraisal fixes the value at death. A broker's written opinion is a cheaper start. For a car, use a standard guide and note the mileage. For tools, furniture and clothing, a lump estimate is normal unless a single item is worth real money. Our article on [how an executor values the estate](/learn/after-a-death/how-an-executor-values-the-estate) shows the methods.

## What does a worked example look like?

Dennis, 70, dies in Peoria County with a house ($214,000 by appraisal), a checking account ($18,420), a brokerage account ($61,300), a 2017 SUV ($14,100) and household goods ($3,000). His IRA ($150,000) names his daughter. His joint savings account ($22,000) passes to his wife. The probate inventory totals $310,820. The IRA and joint savings are listed on a separate sheet as non-probate. The executor, his daughter Joy, keeps the inventory, mails it to her brother on request and uses the total in the closing report. Peoria County's clerk gives more local detail on the [Peoria County page](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/peoria).

## What mistakes do executors make with the inventory?

The most common is mixing probate and non-probate assets on one list. That makes the estate look larger than it is and can confuse heirs about who gets what. The second is guessing at values without writing down the source, which leaves the executor with no answer when an heir asks how a number was reached. The third is waiting for a perfect appraisal before starting. Begin with what you have, mark the value as an estimate, and refine it. The fourth is forgetting the small items that add up, such as a refund, a security deposit or a final paycheck.

Also remember the tax link. A date-of-death value for a house or stock sets the basis the heirs use later, which can reduce capital gains when they sell. Our page on the [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) explains the idea. A solid inventory today saves arguments and tax questions tomorrow.

## When should you update the inventory?

Update it whenever something new turns up: a refund check, a forgotten account, a deed found in a drawer. Date each addition and note how you learned of it. Review the whole list once before you prepare the closing report, and compare it with the bank statements for the estate account. If a number on the inventory does not match what the estate actually collected, write a short explanation, such as a sale price that differed from the appraisal. An executor who explains differences as they happen has a far easier closing than one who tries to reconstruct them a year later.
