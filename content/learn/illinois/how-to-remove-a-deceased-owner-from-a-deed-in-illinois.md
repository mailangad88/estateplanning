After a death, the house often still has two names on the deed. You may want to take the dead person off so you can sell, refinance, or just stop worrying about it. The answer is not one form for everyone. It turns on how title was held when the owner died.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), walks through each case: joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety, tenancy in common, a home in one person's name, and a recorded transfer on death instrument. It also covers which office records the paperwork, what a title company is likely to ask for, and the property tax step people forget. If you want an attorney to read your deed with you, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-to-remove-a-deceased-owner-from-a-deed-in-illinois&topic=probate).

## Can you remove a deceased owner from a deed in Illinois?

You do not remove the name by rewriting history. You record documents that show the owner died and who now holds title. Which documents depend on how the owners held the property. Start by pulling the recorded deed, which you can often get from the county recorder's office, and read the granting clause.

| How title was held | What happens at death | What you usually record |
|---|---|---|
| Joint tenancy (deed says "not in tenancy in common but in joint tenancy") | Survivor owns the whole, no probate | Death certificate and an affidavit or similar proof |
| Tenancy by the entirety (spouses, homestead) | Survivor keeps the whole | Same kind of proof of death |
| Tenancy in common (the default if the deed names two owners with no survivorship wording) | Dead owner's share passes under their will or by heirs | Court-based paperwork after probate |
| Sole owner, no recorded transfer on death instrument | House goes through probate | Court order or deed from the representative |
| Sole owner with a recorded transfer on death instrument | Beneficiary takes without probate | Notice of death affidavit, with the death certificate |

## What if you owned as joint tenants or by the entirety?

This is the simplest case. In Illinois a deed to two or more people is a tenancy in common unless it expressly declares a joint tenancy. Married couples can also hold a homestead by the entirety (765 ILCS 1005/1 and 1005/1c; see [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety)). When one owner dies, the survivor keeps the whole estate. No probate case is needed for that parcel.

The survivor then records proof of the death in the county where the land sits. In practice that means a certified death certificate and an affidavit or similar paper that names the property and the owners. Recorders set format rules, and a title company may have its own, so ask both before you sign. Our page on [Illinois death certificates](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph) explains who can order certified copies. Order several, because each office may keep one.

Here is an invented example. Ruth and Daniel held their home by the entirety. Daniel died in March. Ruth ordered four certified death certificates, had an affidavit prepared, and recorded it with the county. When she refinanced a year later, the title company found the record in order and closed without a delay.

One caution. If the deed names two people who are not spouses and tries to create an entireties estate, Illinois treats it as a joint tenancy, per our page on that subject. If it was a tenancy in common, move to the next section.

## What if the owners held as tenants in common?

Then the dead owner's share does not pass to the survivor. Many families assume it does, because "we owned it together" sounds like survivorship. The share goes to whoever the will names, or to the heirs under the intestacy rules in our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). That means the surviving owner may now share the house with children or other relatives.

Usually this requires a probate case so that someone has authority to deal with the share. See our [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) page. An affidavit alone does not give anyone that authority, as we explain in [heirship affidavit vs probate](/learn/illinois/heirship-affidavit-vs-probate).

## What if the house was in the dead person's name alone?

Then it is part of the probate estate, unless a recorded transfer on death instrument or a trust applies. Heirs cannot simply sign a deed. A court appoints an executor or administrator, who receives letters of office and can sign for the estate. Our page on [a parent who died with the house in their name](/learn/illinois/parent-died-house-in-their-name) lists the first steps, and [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois) covers what the heirs can decide once the court case is open.

If the house is being sold, [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois) explains the order of events. In Cook County, that page describes taking the signed order and the letters to the office that records deeds so the record changes.

## What if there is a transfer on death instrument?

If the owner recorded a transfer on death instrument (TODI) before death, the house passes to the named beneficiary without probate. An instrument not recorded before death is void (755 ILCS 27/40, per our page on the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument)). After the death, the beneficiary can record a notice of death affidavit that names the beneficiary, describes the property and the death, and refers to the original instrument (755 ILCS 27/75). That filing helps clear title when you sell or refinance but is not a condition of the transfer. Check whether the TODI was challenged or revoked, and check the property for creditor or lender issues before you rely on it.

## Which office records it, and what will a title company ask for?

Documents affecting land are recorded in the county where the property sits. In most Illinois counties that is the recorder of deeds. In Cook County, recording is handled by the Clerk's recordings division, and our page on [selling a house in probate](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois) describes the signed order and letters being taken there. Recording rules, fees and forms change, so check the Clerk's website or call the office before you file.

Title companies set their own standards. A buyer's or lender's company will often ask for:

- A certified death certificate.
- The recorded deed, or a legal description and parcel number.
- For a joint tenancy, the recorded affidavit or proof of death.
- For a sole owner, letters of office and a court order if one is needed.
- For a TODI, the recorded instrument and the notice of death affidavit.
- Proof that taxes and any mortgage are current.

Ask your title company what it will accept, in writing, before you list the house.

## What about property taxes after the name changes?

Update the tax side separately. Exemptions are tied to the owner of record who lives in the home. Our page on [Illinois property tax exemptions after a spouse dies](/learn/illinois/illinois-property-tax-exemptions-after-a-spouse-dies) explains that the homeowner and senior exemptions often keep renewing if the survivor still lives there, but the senior freeze must be filed every year. If the deed still shows only the late spouse, the exemption record may point to the wrong name. Fix title first, then ask the county assessor what they need.

## What should you do first? A short checklist

1. Pull the recorded deed and read how title was held.
2. Order certified death certificates.
3. Look for a recorded TODI or a trust that holds the house.
4. Ask the title company, or the lender, what they will want to see.
5. Record the proof of death or open the estate, depending on the answer above.
6. Update the assessor's records and check the next tax bill.
