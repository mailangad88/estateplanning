Sooner or later, many families ask the same hard question. Dad signed a power of attorney years ago, naming his younger son. Now the older son thinks something is wrong with the money. Or a mother has had a falling out with the sister she named, and wants someone else. Either way, the question is how to end the authority, and what to do if the person holding it will not behave.

This page covers revocation of a property power of attorney under the Illinois Power of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45) and the court remedies when an agent misuses it. This page sits in our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois), and the [Illinois estate planning overview](/estate-planning/illinois) has the state-wide picture. For the basics of the form itself, see our article on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property). For the health care version, see the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney), which has its own revocation rules. This is general education, not legal advice.

## How do you revoke an Illinois power of attorney?

You communicate the revocation, and you must still have legal capacity when you do. The Act says a principal with legal capacity may amend or revoke an agency at any time, and the change must be communicated to the agent or to another person connected with the agency's subject matter (755 ILCS 45/2-5). The statute does not require a particular form, but a signed paper is far safer than a phone call.

A practical sequence:

1. **Write the revocation.** Name the principal, the agent, the date of the original document, and say that it is revoked in full. Sign and date it. Having it notarized is not demanded by the section we read, yet many banks like it.
2. **Deliver it to the agent.** Use a method you can prove, such as certified mail with a return receipt, or hand delivery with a witness.
3. **Deliver it to every institution.** Banks, brokerages, the insurance company, the title company, a care facility, a doctor's office. Anyone who has seen the old document should receive the new paper.
4. **Take back the original and copies** where you can. A banker holding a copy of the old form may keep honoring it.
5. **Record it if real estate is involved.** If the old power was recorded in a county for a property transaction, ask the county recorder or a title company how to record the revocation too.
6. **Sign a new power of attorney** if you still want someone to help. Say in it that it revokes all prior powers of attorney.

That sixth step matters. The statute is explicit that signing a new power of attorney does not cancel an earlier one unless the new document expressly revokes the earlier one or revokes all other powers of attorney (755 ILCS 45/2-5).

## When does revocation take effect against the agent?

Against the agent, it takes effect when the agent actually knows about it. Under the Act, amendments or terminations of the agency do not affect the agent until the agent actually knows of them (755 ILCS 45/2-7). This is why delivering proof matters more than writing a good letter. An agent who never received it can keep acting honestly, and a bank that never heard of it may keep dealing with the agent.

Three other things end or reshape the agency without a revocation:

- **Death of the principal.** The power does not survive death. Authority then passes to the executor or administrator.
- **Divorce from a spouse who is the agent.** The spouse is treated as having died at the time of the judgment of dissolution or legal separation (755 ILCS 45/2-6).
- **A successor agent.** If the document names successors, the next one steps in when the earlier agent resigns, dies, becomes incapacitated, is not qualified or declines (755 ILCS 45/2-10.3).

## What if the principal can no longer revoke?

If the principal has dementia or another condition that took away the capacity to revoke, the principal cannot just fire the agent. This is where the court process comes in. Under the Act, an interested person may petition after notice to the agent, the principal and anyone else the court directs. The court must first find that the principal lacks the capacity to control the agency or the capacity to revoke it (755 ILCS 45/2-10).

Who counts as an interested person is broad. It includes the principal, the agent, a guardian or other fiduciary, and a spouse, parent or descendant. A presumptive heir or a trust beneficiary counts too. So do certain agencies, such as an adult protective services provider or the long-term care ombudsman. A caregiver, or anyone else with enough interest in the principal's welfare, can also petition.

What the court can do:

| Court power | What it means |
|---|---|
| Construe the document and review the agent's conduct | The judge reads the power of attorney and looks at what the agent did, and can award compensatory damages |
| Order a guardian to exercise the principal's powers, including revoking the agency | Used when the agent is not acting for the principal's benefit, or causes or threatens substantial, unauthorized harm |
| Issue other orders without appointing a guardian | Orders aimed at protecting the principal's best interests |
| Interpret unclear terms and instruct the agent | The court cannot amend the agency |
| Deny the agent fees from the principal's funds | If the agent did not act for the principal's benefit, the agent cannot use the principal's money for their own legal defense |

The court can also order the agent to pay reasonable costs and attorney's fees to a prevailing agency or governmental petitioner if substantial harm is found. Absent a court order directing a guardian to exercise the principal's powers, a guardian generally has no power over property or matters the agency covers. A guardianship petition can ask for suspension or revocation of the power of attorney, and an agent who seeks guardianship must specify the powers needed beyond those in the document (755 ILCS 45/2-10). If money has been taken, our article on [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) explains the signs and reporting. Our article on [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) explains the larger process, and [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) compares them.

## What can you demand from the agent before going to court?

Often, an accounting. The agent must keep records of receipts, disbursements and significant actions and, on request, give a copy to the principal, a guardian, another fiduciary, a court, or after death the principal's personal representative or successors in interest. Representatives of adult protective services providers, the long-term care ombudsman, the Office of Inspector General at the Department of Human Services, and the State Guardian or a county public guardian have the same right in the situations the statute lists. If the agent fails to respond within 21 days to certain of those requests, the agency can ask a court to compel production, and the court may award costs and fees against the agent (755 ILCS 45/2-7).

Agents are not otherwise required to disclose unless the document says so. A family member who is not on the statutory list, such as a sibling, usually needs to go through the court route or a guardian first.

An agent who violates the Act is liable to the principal or successors to restore the property to the value it would have had absent the violation and to repay attorney's fees paid on the agent's behalf (755 ILCS 45/2-7).

## What are the most common problems?

- **Gifts to the agent.** Statutory powers do not include making gifts unless the document says so. Our article on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) lists warning signs.
- **Estate plan changes.** The agent must consider the principal's estate plan and try to keep it intact, and can be liable to a plan beneficiary only for bad faith (755 ILCS 45/2-9).
- **Accounts that cannot be reached.** Banks vary on how they treat old documents.

Take Frank, whose mother has moderate dementia. His brother Paul, her agent, has moved $40,000 into his own account for "care costs" with no receipts. Frank cannot revoke for his mother. He can ask Paul for an accounting, and if Paul stalls, he or an adult protective services provider can petition the court under the section above. Frank should also talk to the bank, and document every transfer he can see.
