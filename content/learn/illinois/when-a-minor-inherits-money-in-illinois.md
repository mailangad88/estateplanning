Your brother died and left a share of his estate to his nine-year-old daughter. Or a grandparent named a grandchild on a life insurance form. The check cannot be written to a child, and the family asks whose job it is to take it. In Illinois the answer is usually a court-appointed guardian of the estate, unless the will or a form set up something else in advance.

This page explains what happens to a minor's inheritance, how the guardian of the estate works, how it differs from a UTMA custodian, and what changes at 18. Planning ahead is covered on our page about [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors). For the wider Illinois picture, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Who controls money a child inherits in Illinois?

If nobody was named in advance, nobody does until the court picks a person. A minor in Illinois is a person under 18 for most purposes. A child cannot give a bank or an executor a valid receipt, which is why the executor will not simply hand over the share. And being a parent does not automatically make you the manager of the child's property. The parent has custody and a duty to support the child, which is not the same as legal control of the child's money.

Three paths decide who holds it:

| Path | Who holds the money | When the child gets it |
|---|---|---|
| Court guardian of the estate | The guardian, under court supervision | At 18 |
| UTMA custodian named in the will or form | The custodian | At 21 (see our UTMA page) |
| Trust named in the will or by the donor | The trustee, under the trust's terms | At whatever age the trust sets |

The first path exists when no one planned. The second and third are chosen by the person who left the money. That is why our attorneys spend so much time on what a will says about children.

## How does a guardian of the estate work?

Illinois separates a guardian of the person, who handles the child's care, from a guardian of the estate, who handles money. Article XI of the Probate Act governs both, as our page on [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) explains. The court appoints a guardian of the estate on a petition, and a parent with custody is the usual choice if willing and able. If the court thinks the child's interests need independent review, it may appoint a guardian ad litem to report to it.

Once appointed, the guardian must manage the estate carefully and for the child's benefit. The court may require a bond, which is an insurance policy against misuse, and the guardian reports to the court. The guardian keeps the child's money separate from the guardian's own, invests it prudently, and spends it only as the court allows. Court supervision is the feature and the cost: it protects the child, and it takes time, filing fees and paperwork that an uncomplicated trust or custodianship avoids. Fees and bond premiums vary, so ask the clerk of your county before you plan around a number.

Every county has its own habits. In Cook County, for example, the Probate Division of the Circuit Court handles minors' estates, and other counties handle them in the county's probate court. Ask the clerk what forms are used and whether a restricted account is required for the funds.

## What is a restricted account, and when is it used?

For a smaller inheritance, a court may order the money deposited in an account that cannot be touched without a further order. The bank holds it and releases it only on that order, usually for a stated need such as education or medical costs, or when the child turns 18. It is simpler than a full guardianship, and local practice decides whether it is available and for how much. Do not open one on your own: the order has to come from the court, and a bank that opens it without one may freeze it later.

## What happens when the child turns 18?

The guardianship of the estate ends. The guardian files a final accounting with the court, and whatever is left goes to the young adult, in full, with no strings. A court-supervised estate cannot hold the money until 21 or 25 just because the family would prefer it.

That is the main problem with the default route. Take this example, with invented numbers. Hannah, 17, inherits $140,000 from a grandfather's life insurance policy because he named her directly on the beneficiary form. Her mother is appointed guardian of the estate. A year later, Hannah turns 18, and the court closes the guardianship. She now has all the money, a car dealership offer and a boyfriend with ideas. Nobody did anything wrong. The system worked as designed. Her grandfather could have named a trustee, or at least a UTMA custodian to keep it to 21, for a few hundred dollars of legal drafting.

A UTMA custodian under the Illinois Act hands over at 21 for gifts and for transfers under a will or trust, as our page on [Illinois UTMA accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) sets out. A trust can go further, with staggered ages and a trustee who may pay for school or medical costs without paying out cash. Our page on a [testamentary trust for minor children](/learn/guardianship/testamentary-trust-for-minor-children) explains how a will can create one.

Timing is the other thing parents underestimate. A guardianship petition needs notice, a hearing date and sometimes a bond before the executor or insurer will release a dollar, so a family that waits until the check is ready may wait weeks more. Start the petition as soon as you know the child will receive money, and ask the executor whether the payment can be held in the meantime.

## What if the money comes from a beneficiary form, not a will?

This catches many families. A life insurance policy, an IRA or a bank account with payable-on-death wording that names a minor directly does not pass through the will. The insurer or custodian will not pay a child. It will ask for a guardian of the estate or a custodian, and it may hold the money until the family produces one. Our page on [what happens when a minor inherits without a trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust) shows how that plays out.

The fix is cheap. Name a trust or a UTMA custodian as the beneficiary, or at least confirm that the form allows "as custodian for" wording. A beneficiary form is not changed by a will, so check each one. If you are the person who must now sort out someone else's mess, the page on [when an heir cannot receive their share](/learn/after-a-death/when-an-heir-cannot-receive-their-share-died-minor-incapacitated-or-in-debt) covers related situations.

## How do you avoid a court guardianship of a child's inheritance?

1. Name a trustee or a UTMA custodian in your will for any gift to a minor.
2. Update every beneficiary form so that no child is named directly.
3. For a large inheritance, use a trust with a later age than 18 or 21.
4. For a child with a disability, use a trust built for benefits, not a UTMA account.
5. Tell the person you chose, and give them a copy of the plan.
