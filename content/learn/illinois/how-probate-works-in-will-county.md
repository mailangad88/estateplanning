If a parent or spouse lived in Joliet, Bolingbrook, Plainfield, New Lenox or anywhere else in Will County, their estate is handled by the 12th Judicial Circuit, and most of the paperwork starts in one building in Joliet. The state law is the same Probate Act used in every Illinois county. What changes is where you file, how you file and what the clerk expects.

This page is part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). It follows a Will County estate from the moment you hold the will to the point where an executor can act on their own, and it flags what the clerk's own pages say. If you want to talk through your family's situation, you can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-how-probate-works-in-will-county&topic=probate) with an Illinois estate planning attorney.

A quick note on the name. "Will County" and "a will" sound alike, and people search for both at once. Here, "Will County" is always the place. A Will County case may involve a will, or it may involve an estate with no will at all.

## Where do Will County probate filings go in Joliet?

They go to the Office of the Circuit Clerk at the Will County Courthouse, 100 W. Jefferson Street, Joliet, IL 60432. The clerk lists the phone as (815) 727-8592 and the hours as 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., Monday through Friday, except court holidays. The clerk's court locations page calls this main courthouse "full service."

The county has other buildings, but they do different jobs. According to the clerk's locations page:

| Location | What the clerk says it does |
|---|---|
| Will County Courthouse, 100 W. Jefferson, Joliet | Full service; other case types go here |
| River Valley Justice Center, Joliet | Limited service; its window accepts filings only in guardianship cases |
| Bolingbrook, Plainfield and Frankfort branch courts | Court only, no services |
| Nicholson Archive Center, Joliet | Records services only, by appointment |

The page does not say which case types are heard at each branch, so do not assume a family in Plainfield will have a hearing in Plainfield. Your hearing notice names the place. The clerk also lists courthouse rules worth knowing: no weapons or firearms, no outside food or drink, and video or audio recording needs preauthorization from the Chief Judge's Office.

Which county is right depends on where the person lived at death, not where the executor lives (755 ILCS 5/5-1). Our page on [probate venue when property is in two counties](/learn/illinois/probate-venue-property-in-two-illinois-counties) explains what to do if the family also owned land elsewhere.

## How do I file the will in Will County?

You deliver the original will to the Circuit Clerk, in person or by mail. The clerk's probate page says there is no fee to file a will, and that you need the original will and the date of death. It advises keeping copies before mailing the original.

Illinois law says whoever holds a will must file it with the clerk immediately after the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1), as covered in [filing the original will with the Illinois circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk). That duty applies even if the family does not plan to open a full estate.

One point surprises people who have heard that everything is e-filed. The clerk's e-filing page says original wills, even as part of a probate case, must be filed conventionally. Order certified death certificates early, because the date of death is required; [who can order Illinois death certificates](/learn/illinois/illinois-death-certificates-who-can-order-county-clerk-or-idph) explains the options.

## How does an estate open once the will is on file?

An attorney or the representative files a petition, a judge holds a hearing, and the clerk issues letters of office. Filing the will alone does not open anything; it just puts the document in the court's custody.

The clerk's probate page covers administration of a decedent's estate, of a missing person's estate, and wrongful death or collection of judgment matters. For a decedent's estate it says new estate cases must be filed electronically, and the filing needs the original petition and the fee. You do not have to call for a court date: put a return date on the cover letter or file a Notice of Motion with a date certain.

The clerk's e-filing page adds the rules of the road. Civil filings go through eFileIL using a certified provider, the clerk does not accept emailed documents, and the clerk says it cannot assist with individual filings. Courthouse kiosks exist for e-filing help in cases where e-filing is required, and rejection emails state the reason, often a PDF defect. Attorneys do not qualify for an exemption from e-filing.

The clerk publishes a Decedent Estate Packet of forms and a Small Estate Affidavit form for certain cases that do not need court-supervised probate. Whether your estate qualifies is a separate question; [the Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) and [opening a probate estate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-open-a-probate-estate-in-illinois) walk through it.

The state-law sequence after letters issue is the same everywhere:

1. Mail notice to heirs and legatees within 14 days after the order admitting the will (755 ILCS 5/6-10).
2. Publish and mail the creditor notice; the claim date is the later of six months from first publication or three months from mailing (755 ILCS 5/18-3).
3. Allow for the six-month window to contest the will (755 ILCS 5/8-1).
4. Collect assets, pay valid claims, then distribute.
5. File a closing report and wait 42 days in an independent estate (755 ILCS 5/28-11).

[The Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) explains each step in more detail.

## Can I attend a Will County probate hearing by Zoom?

Possibly, but confirm for your own case. The clerk's site links a "Virtual Court" page for remote hearings and the Illinois Courts remote proceedings page for participants. The Virtual Court page itself was not readable when this page was prepared, so check it, your notice or the courtroom before assuming either way.

What the Illinois Courts page does say is general: the Illinois Supreme Court has emphasized that remote appearances should not be viewed as merely temporary pandemic measures, and the page links Zoom how-to videos and Remote Proceeding Guidelines. It does not state Will County's own arrangements, so treat remote access as something your attorney or the courtroom confirms.

## How do I look up a Will County case online?

Use the 12th Judicial Circuit's iPublic case lookup, linked from the clerk's site. It shows case information, but the site says images of filed documents are not available in iPublic. To get copies, you can register with i2File, the state-approved vendor, and pay a fee for the copies you receive, or use the public access terminals at the main courthouse or view the physical file there during business hours. The clerk's page on ordering copies of case documents covers the request process. During one visit the lookup was showing a notice that it was unavailable, and it lists scheduled downtime overnight, so try again later if it does not load.

If you are not sure an estate was ever opened, [how to find out if you are named in a will](/learn/illinois/how-to-find-out-if-you-are-named-in-a-will-in-illinois) explains where to start.

## When can the executor act without going back to court?

After the judge grants independent administration. Illinois courts must grant it unless the will forbids it or another protection rule applies, and an interested person can object (755 ILCS 5/28-2), as explained in our page on [independent vs supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois). Once granted, the executor can collect assets, pay claims and make distributions without asking the judge to approve each one.

Selling real estate is the common example. An independent executor can sell without a court order (755 ILCS 5/28-8), as covered in [selling a house in probate in Illinois](/learn/illinois/selling-a-house-in-probate-in-illinois). Independence does not mean no accountability. Heirs and legatees can still ask the court to step in, and an interested person can petition to end independent administration (755 ILCS 5/28-4). The court is involved at the start, when it grants the powers, and at the end, with the closing report.

## Do I need a lawyer, and where is free help?

The clerk's probate page does not address attorneys or courtroom assignments, so the practical answer comes from the process itself. New estate cases must be e-filed, and the petition, notices and closing report have strict rules, so many executors hire an attorney for at least part of the work. ## Example: Marcus and his mother's house in Plainfield

This is an invented example. Marcus, 52, lives in Denver. His mother, Lorraine, died in her Plainfield home. Her will names Marcus executor and leaves everything equally to him and his sister Dana. Lorraine owned the house alone and had one checking account with no beneficiary.

Marcus assumes the case belongs at the Plainfield branch court. A call to the clerk in Joliet and a look at the locations page set him straight: the branch is court only, and filings go to the main courthouse. He mails the original will with the date of death, after keeping a copy.

An attorney e-files the petition and asks for independent administration. The judge admits the will and Marcus receives letters of office. Dana gets written notice within 14 days, and the creditor notice is published. Marcus checks the iPublic lookup from Denver and sees the case entries, but he asks the attorney for copies of the documents, since images are not online.

Because the court granted independent administration, Marcus lists the house and signs the sale without asking the judge for approval. He pays the final bills from the estate account. After the claims date and the contest period pass quietly, the attorney files the closing report, the 42 days run, and the court discharges him.
