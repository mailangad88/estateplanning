A common worry goes like this: a daughter dies, then her father dies a year later without a will. Do her children get anything, or does everything go to the father's other children? Illinois has a clear answer, and it helps to see the arithmetic before a family sits down at the kitchen table.

This page covers the intestacy rule, the separate rules for wills and beneficiary forms, and what happens when a grandchild is a minor. For the full order of heirs, see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) links the rest.

## When does a grandchild inherit under Illinois intestacy law?

A grandchild inherits only if the grandchild's own parent died before the grandparent. The Probate Act sends the estate to a decedent's descendants "per stirpes" (755 ILCS 5/2-1), which means that a dead child's share goes down to that child's children. A grandchild whose parent is still living is not an heir at that moment, because the parent stands between them.

Think of it as a family tree. Each child of the decedent starts a branch. If the child is alive, the branch ends there. If the child is dead, the branch splits among that child's own children, and so on down. The [per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes) idea is easiest to see with numbers.

## How does the per stirpes split work with numbers?

Walter, 82, dies without a will, a widower. His probate estate after debts is $600,000. He had three children. Anita is alive. Ben died two years ago and left two children, Cal and Dee. Carla died five years ago and left one child, Evan.

1. Count the branches: three, one for each child of Walter.
2. Divide the estate by the branches: $200,000 each.
3. Anita, alive, takes her $200,000.
4. Ben's branch splits between Cal and Dee: $100,000 each.
5. Carla's branch goes to Evan: $200,000.

Evan receives twice as much as each of his cousins, because he is the only child on his branch. That surprises families, and it is the rule, not a mistake. A method that divides everything equally among all grandchildren is called [per capita](/glossary/per-capita), and it applies only if a will asks for it.

| Situation | Who takes the branch | Example result on $600,000 |
|---|---|---|
| Child alive | The child | $200,000 to Anita |
| Child dead, two children | The grandchildren equally | $100,000 each to Cal and Dee |
| Child dead, one child | That grandchild alone | $200,000 to Evan |
| Child dead, no children | Nobody on that branch, the others share it | Anita and the rest divide it |

If Walter had left a spouse, she would take one-half first and the descendants would share the other half by the same method (755 ILCS 5/2-1). The [who inherits tool](/tools/who-inherits) lets you try your own family.

## What happens if there is a will?

A will controls, and the family tree matters only as far as the will says. Many wills leave everything "to my children in equal shares." If one child has died, the answer depends on the wording and on an Illinois anti-lapse rule. Under 755 ILCS 5/4-11, a gift to a child or other descendant of the testator who dies first generally passes to that person's descendants, unless the will shows a different intent.

That rule does not help a gift to a friend, a niece or a sibling, and it does not apply when the will says "to my surviving children." Our article on [lapsed gifts and children born after a will](/learn/illinois/children-born-after-an-illinois-will-and-lapsed-gifts) walks through the wording. Wills that say nothing about a dead child's family are a common source of disputes, as is a grandparent's wish to treat a branch differently. For options, read about [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).

## What about life insurance and retirement accounts?

Beneficiary forms are their own contract. If Walter named only Ben on a $100,000 life insurance policy and Ben died first, the policy does not automatically go to Cal and Dee. The insurer follows the form: it may pay a contingent beneficiary, or the estate, which then follows the will or the intestacy rule. Naming a contingent beneficiary, or writing "to my descendants per stirpes" where the company allows it, closes the gap. See [what happens when a child beneficiary dies first](/learn/beneficiary-designations/what-happens-if-a-child-beneficiary-dies-before-you-and-leaves-grandchildren) and [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

## Who handles a minor grandchild's share?

If Cal is 9, he cannot hold $100,000. Without a trust, the court may appoint a guardian of his estate, usually a parent or relative, who must post a bond, keep records and use court-approved spending. The funds are usually released at age 18. Our guide to [a minor inheriting money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) explains the options, and [custodial accounts under UTMA](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) offer a simpler route for gifts you make yourself.

## What if a parent gives up the share?

A parent can sometimes disclaim an inheritance, which generally treats that person as having died first. The share can then pass to the parent's own children. That can help a parent who does not want the money, but a disclaimer is final and must be done correctly. See [disclaiming an inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois).

## How can a grandparent make sure grandchildren are included?

Do not rely on the default. A grandparent can name grandchildren in a will, fund a trust for them or use beneficiary forms with contingent names. A grandparent can also write that a dead child's share goes to that child's descendants. Our page on [leaving an inheritance to grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/leaving-an-inheritance-to-grandchildren) lists the tools, and the [plan finder](/plan-finder) can point you to the right documents.

## What mistakes do families make with grandchildren's shares?

Three come up again and again. The first is assuming grandchildren are equal. As the example shows, a branch with one child pays that child the whole branch share, while a branch with four children pays each of them a quarter. The second is leaving a child's family out of a will because the child "has already been taken care of." If that child dies first, the anti-lapse rule may send the gift to the child's children anyway, which may not be what the grandparent wanted. The third is forgetting the other side of the family: a grandchild whose parent was disinherited, or who lives out of state, still counts as an heir on the family tree and must receive notice of the estate case.

If the family is unsure who the grandchildren are or where they live, say so to the administrator early. The court needs a full list of heirs before it can close the estate. Our article on [proving heirship](/learn/illinois/proving-heirship-in-illinois) explains how the list is documented.
