Dennis, 54, lives in Naperville and has just learned he is the beneficiary on his late mother's traditional IRA. The balance is $240,000. His first question is not about the 10-year rule or the form. It is simpler: will Illinois take a bite out of this?

For most heirs the Illinois answer is better than the federal one. This page explains how Illinois treats retirement distributions to heirs, why the federal rules still drive the plan, and what to confirm before you withdraw. The general federal picture is in our page on [tax on an inherited IRA, 401(k), annuity or savings bond](/learn/after-a-death/tax-on-inherited-ira-401k-annuity-and-savings-bonds), and this article does not repeat it. It is general education, not tax or legal advice, and it belongs inside the wider [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Does Illinois tax inherited IRA and 401(k) withdrawals?

Generally little, and it comes from how Illinois calculates income. The Illinois return starts with federal adjusted gross income and then applies additions and subtractions. One of the subtractions, in 35 ILCS 5/203(a)(2)(F), removes amounts that are included in income under several federal sections: 402(a), 402(c), 403(a), 403(b), 406(a), 407(a) and 408 of the Internal Revenue Code. Those sections cover distributions from employer plans and IRAs. The same subtraction also covers distributions under retirement or disability plans for employees of governmental agencies.

The text of that subtraction, as published by Justia, does not say the person receiving the money must be the original owner, and it does not mention beneficiaries. That is why the usual reading is that retirement-plan distributions are not taxed by Illinois whether the owner or an heir takes them out. Treat this as the statute's plain reading, and have a tax preparer confirm it against the Illinois Department of Revenue's instructions when you file.

So Dennis, in the example, would owe federal income tax on what he withdraws, and Illinois would start from that figure and subtract it. His planning concern is the federal bracket, not the state one.

## What does Illinois not tax at all?

Illinois has no inheritance tax. The state's tax at death is an estate tax paid by the estate, not by the person receiving the inheritance. Our page on the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) explains the $4 million threshold and says Illinois does not allow a spouse to carry over an unused exclusion. For a broader comparison, see [estate tax versus inheritance tax](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-vs-inheritance-tax).

The estate tax can still touch a retirement account indirectly. The value of an IRA or 401(k) is generally part of the gross estate when the Illinois threshold is tested. A person who dies with a $1.5 million house, $1.2 million in a 401(k) and a $1.6 million brokerage account has crossed $4 million without owning anything exotic. The estate may owe tax before Dennis ever sees a dollar, and the executor deals with that. Heirs do not pay a second Illinois tax when they receive the account.

## What federal rules still apply to Illinois heirs?

All of them. The state subtraction does nothing about the federal side. Four points do most of the work.

1. **The 10-year rule.** Most non-spouse heirs must empty the inherited account by the end of the tenth year after the owner's death. A surviving spouse, a minor child of the owner until majority, a disabled or chronically ill heir, and someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner have other options. Our page on the [inherited IRA 10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) covers yearly withdrawals.
2. **Ordinary income.** Traditional account withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income in the year you take them. There is no step-up in basis for a retirement account.
3. **Roth accounts.** Qualified Roth withdrawals are generally tax-free, but the 10-year deadline applies to most heirs.
4. **Spouses.** A spouse can usually roll the account into their own, which our page on [spousal rollover versus inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira) compares.

Some years Dennis may earn more than others. Taking $24,000 a year for ten years is not the same as taking $240,000 in year ten, and the tax difference can be large. That is the most useful planning conversation for an Illinois heir.

## How does it work when the estate or a trust is the beneficiary?

The tax depends on who is named, and naming the estate is the common trap. When an estate is the beneficiary, the money is paid to the estate, it generally moves on a shorter schedule than a person would get, and income earned and distributed may be reported on the estate's return. Illinois uses Form IL-1041 for that return, covered in our page on the [Illinois estate and trust income tax return](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-il-1041-estate-and-trust-income-tax-return). The decedent's own final return is a separate matter, covered in the [final Illinois income tax return](/learn/illinois/final-illinois-income-tax-return-for-someone-who-died) article.

A trust named as beneficiary adds its own tax identity and rules. Our page on [a trust as beneficiary and its own tax ID](/learn/beneficiary-designations/does-a-trust-named-as-beneficiary-need-its-own-tax-id-and-cost-more) explains the cost.

## Does it matter where the heir lives?

Yes, and this is the detail Illinois families miss. The Illinois subtraction helps people who file Illinois returns. If Dennis's sister Carla lives in a state that taxes retirement distributions, her state decides what she owes, whatever Illinois does. Some states exempt retirement income, others tax it, and some tax only part. Check the rules of your own state before you decide when to withdraw.

## What should an Illinois heir do before the first withdrawal?

1. Get a copy of the beneficiary form and the death certificate, and ask the custodian for the account value on the date of death.
2. Do not take a lump sum until you know the federal tax. A payout can look like a gift and then show up as a bracket jump.
3. Retitle the account properly as an inherited IRA or ask the 401(k) plan for its beneficiary options. Do not roll an inherited IRA into your own, unless you are the spouse.
4. Find out whether the owner had begun required distributions, since that decides whether you need a withdrawal each year.
5. Ask your preparer to confirm the Illinois treatment on your return and to estimate the federal tax for different payout schedules.
6. If the estate was large, ask the executor whether an Illinois estate tax return was filed and whether any estate tax can be deducted.

## What mistake do our attorneys see most often?

Heirs cash out. A grieving person gets a letter, assumes the account is like a bank account, and withdraws everything to pay off the mortgage. The federal tax on a large lump sum can be a surprise, and it cannot be undone. A short conversation before the first withdrawal is cheaper than the bracket. Another is missing the year-one distribution when the owner had already started required minimum distributions. The IRS penalty for a missed distribution is avoidable, and a custodian will not always warn you.
