The will is admitted and the executor has letters. What happens next is one of the easiest steps to get wrong and one of the most expensive to skip. Every heir and legatee is owed a written notice, and the clock for an objection starts running because of it.

This page explains who gets notice, what it must say, how to mail or publish it, and what rights it triggers. For the order of the whole case, see [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process). The broader guide is at [Illinois estate planning](/learn/illinois).

## Who gets the notice?

Two groups get it: heirs and legatees. An heir is someone who would inherit if there were no will. A legatee is someone the will names. A child who was disinherited is an heir, so the child gets notice. A neighbor left $1,000 in the will is a legatee, so the neighbor gets notice. The executor does not get to decide whose objections might be welcome.

The notice goes to people whose addresses are known or can be found with reasonable effort. Anyone who attended the hearing, or signed a written waiver of notice, does not need a separate mailing. A minor or an adult under a disability is notified through a parent, guardian or, in some cases, a [guardian ad litem](/learn/illinois/guardian-ad-litem-in-illinois-probate).

## What is the deadline?

The deadline is 14 days after the order admitting the will (755 ILCS 5/6-10). For estates with an administrator and no will, the parallel notice duty is in section 9-5. The executor files proof of mailing with the clerk.

| Event | Deadline | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Mail notice of admission to heirs and legatees | 14 days after the order | 755 ILCS 5/6-10 |
| Ask for formal proof of the will | 42 days after admission | 755 ILCS 5/6-21 |
| File a will contest | Six months after admission | 755 ILCS 5/8-1 |
| Heir petitions to end independent administration | Any time | 755 ILCS 5/28-4 |

## What are the steps to send the notice?

1. Pull the heir and legatee list from the petition and add any new addresses you learned.
2. Get certified copies of the order admitting the will and the petition, or the copies the court tells you to send.
3. Prepare the notice using the form your clerk or local rules call for. It states the rights described below.
4. Attach the explanation of the right to ask the court to end independent administration, with the petition form (755 ILCS 5/28-2).
5. Mail the packet to each person on the list within 14 days, by a method that produces a record.
6. File an affidavit or proof of mailing with the clerk.
7. For any person with no known address, ask the court about publication and run it.
8. Keep copies of everything in the estate file.

## What rights does the notice describe?

The notice tells the reader that the will has been admitted, that the person may ask for formal proof of the will within 42 days, and that a will contest must be filed within six months. Our page on the [six-month will contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) explains the grounds, and the general rules are in [how to object to probate](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate). The packet also includes the form heirs can use to ask for supervised administration, which our guide to [independent versus supervised administration](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains.

The executor is not giving legal advice by mailing this. The point is that every person with a stake knows the clock has started.

## What if you cannot find an heir?

Search first. Check the decedent's address book, ask other relatives, review old mail, and search public records. If you still cannot find a person, ask the court for guidance on publication. The notice is published once a week for three successive weeks in a local newspaper, and proof is filed with the clerk. Our page on [heirs who cannot be found](/learn/what-if/heir-cannot-be-found-or-no-family-survives) covers the harder cases. The newspaper charge is part of [probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs).

## Can heirs waive notice?

Yes. A signed waiver filed with the clerk takes the place of mailing for that person. Families often use waivers when everyone is cooperating. The cost of asking is low. A waiver does not remove the right to contest in the future if the person later learns something new, and it should not be pressured.

## What if the notice is late or missed?

A missed notice does not automatically undo the case, but it creates a record problem. A person who was not told may argue that deadlines did not begin for them, or may raise it at closing. The executor risks having to redo steps and may face questions from the court. If you realize the 14 days have passed, send the notice now, document the reason, and tell the clerk. Our page on [what heirs can do about a slow executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor) shows the other side.

## Example: Grace and her father's estate

Grace, 59, is executor for her father Walter. The court admits his will on April 3. Walter left the house to Grace and $20,000 to his granddaughter Tess, and his son Paul got nothing. Grace mails the packet to Paul, Tess and herself on April 10, files proof on April 12, and puts a reminder on her calendar for May 15, 42 days from the order. Paul reads the notice, talks to a lawyer and decides not to ask for formal proof. The six-month window for a contest runs out on October 3 with no filing.

## What should the executor keep as proof?

Keep a mailing log, not just the envelopes. For each person, write the name, address used, date mailed, method and the page count. Keep the certified mail receipt or the tracking record. If the notice comes back as undeliverable, keep the returned envelope and write down what you did next. Proof of mailing is filed with the clerk, and the executor will rely on the same record months later when the closing report says that notice was given. A neat log also answers any heir who later says nothing arrived.

If an heir is a minor, mail the notice to the parent or guardian, name the child in the letter, and note the relationship. If the heir has a disability and a guardian, send it to the guardian. Where the estate is large or the family is divided, a lawyer can review the packet before it goes out.
