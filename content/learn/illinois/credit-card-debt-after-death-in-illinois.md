A parent dies, and a week later a card statement arrives for $11,400. Then another. Then a phone call from a collector who says the family "should take care of this." Most people in that spot assume the debt is theirs now. Under Illinois law, it usually is not.

This page explains what the Probate Act says about creditors, how the notice and deadline work, the order in which debts get paid, and the cases where a living person really does owe. For the bigger picture, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Who pays a dead person's credit card?

The estate pays first. A credit card balance is an unsecured debt, which means no property was pledged to back it. After death, the card company's only route is a claim against the estate, which is the property the person owned in their own name. If the estate has enough, the claim is paid. If it does not, the claim goes unpaid.

The family's own savings are not on the hook just because of the relationship. A spouse, a child or a sibling is not liable for a debt they did not sign for. The exceptions are the ones that show up in a living person's life too:

- A joint account holder or co-signer owes the full balance, because they signed for it.
- An authorized user does not usually owe, but a person who used a card without being added can be treated differently.
- Property that passes outside the estate, such as a [payable-on-death account](/glossary/payable-on-death), is not owned by the estate. Whether a creditor can reach it depends on the facts. Our article on [creditors and beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits) explains the general picture.

## How does the creditor notice work?

Section 18-3 of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/18-3) requires two kinds of notice once an estate is open.

1. **Publication.** The executor publishes a notice once a week for three successive weeks in a newspaper in the county where the estate is administered, then files proof of publication with the clerk.
2. **Mailing or delivery.** The executor sends the notice to every known or reasonably ascertainable creditor.

The notice states that the person died, who the executor and the attorney are, and a date by which claims must be filed. It warns that claims not filed by then are barred. The deadline cannot be shorter than six months after the first publication or three months after mailing or delivery, whichever is later.

That is why a card issuer found in the mail pile gets a letter and the plumber who never sent a bill does not. "Reasonably ascertainable" means the executor has to look through statements and mail, not just wait. Our page on [how long Illinois probate takes](/learn/illinois/how-long-does-probate-take-in-illinois) shows where the claims period fits in the calendar.

## In what order are debts paid?

Section 18-10 (755 ILCS 5/18-10) sorts claims into classes. Higher classes are paid in full before the next class gets anything.

| Class | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Funeral, burial and administration costs, including certain custodial claims. |
| 2 | The surviving spouse's or child's award. |
| 3 | Debts owed to the United States. |
| 4 | Medical, hospital and nursing home bills for the final year of life, plus unpaid wages up to $800 per employee for the last four months. |
| 5 | Money or property the person held in trust that cannot be identified or traced. |
| 6 | Debts owed to the State and its local governments and school districts. |
| 7 | Everything else, which includes credit cards. |

If the estate is short, class 7 shares whatever remains. A card company is no better off than any other general creditor. Our [spouse rights article](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) explains the award that ranks second, because it surprises many families that the award beats the credit card companies.

## What happens if the estate cannot pay everything?

Then the executor pays by class and stops. The executor should not pay a lower class while a higher one is unpaid, and should not pay anyone out of order because a collector is loud. In an insolvent estate, that care protects the executor too.

A creditor that was never paid has two years from the death at most. Under Section 18-12, claims that could have been barred are barred two years after death, with or without letters of office. After that, creditors can still go after undistributed estate assets for claims that are not barred. They can also go after people who received distributions, but only up to the amount that would keep that person's share from dropping below what they would have received if the claim had been paid. A representative who acts in good faith to identify and notify creditors is generally not personally liable, except to a known claimant whose valid claim was left unpaid.

An insurance exception also exists: the bar does not stop a lawsuit meant to reach liability insurance that covers the estate, such as an auto policy after a crash.

## Is there any claim that jumps the line?

One unusual claim is worth knowing about because it can come from inside the family. Section 18-1.1 (755 ILCS 5/18-1.1) lets a spouse, parent, sibling or child who lived with and personally cared for a person with a disability for at least three years make a claim against that person's estate. The court looks at income the caregiver gave up and the other effects of caregiving, and can reduce the award if the arrangement also gave the caregiver free housing or other benefits. It is separate from a reasonable claim for nursing and other care.

Most families never meet this claim. But if one sibling spent years caring for a parent, it changes who is a creditor and who is a beneficiary, and an attorney should look at it before the estate pays anyone else.

## Example: Ruth and the $11,400 card

Ruth's father, Walter, died with a checking account of $6,300, a car worth $4,000, and two credit cards totaling $11,400. His funeral cost $9,000. The estate was about $10,300. The funeral bill, class 1, took nearly all of it. After court costs, the card companies got nothing. Ruth had never signed on either card, so she owed nothing. She did the right thing at the start: she mailed copies of the death certificate, told the issuers an executor would handle the account, and did not pay a dollar from her own money.

## What should the executor do first?

Gather every statement from the mail and the person's phone and computer, then list the creditors, with the balance, the account number and the date of the last statement for each. Notify each card issuer in writing, with a copy of the death certificate. Cancel the cards so no charges post. Do not pay general creditors until the claim period has run and the higher classes are covered.

Then check for other debts that behave differently: a mortgage or car loan is secured by the property, and the lender can take the collateral if no one keeps paying. Taxes also have their own track, and our page on [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) lays out what to file. For the general rules every state shares, see [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

A small estate may not go through probate at all, in which case the affidavit signer has duties of their own. Read our guide to the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) before paying anything.
