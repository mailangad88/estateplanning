Many couples set up a living trust together, name each other as trustee and beneficiary, and put the house in it. Years later they divorce. Now the trust still names the person you are no longer married to, and the house may still be titled to "the trustees."

This page focuses on the trust. Our broader article on [how divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan) covers your will, powers of attorney and beneficiary forms. For how revocable trusts work in general, see [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts). The state hub is the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What does Illinois law do to a revocable trust after divorce?

The Illinois Trust Code revokes trust provisions that favor a former spouse after a judgment ending the marriage, unless the trust or the judgment says otherwise (760 ILCS 3/605). The statute covers a judgment of dissolution, an annulment and a declaration of invalidity. In plain terms, the former spouse is treated as if they had died. If the settlor later remarries the same person, the revoked provisions can revive.

That is a default rule, and it has three limits. It works only on provisions of the trust. It begins at the judgment, not at filing. And the document can opt out, for example when a spouse is meant to keep raising the children's trust.

## What does it leave untouched?

It leaves ownership alone. The statute changes who benefits under the trust. It does not decide who owns a house deeded to the trustees, who keeps the brokerage account or how to split a joint trust. Those answers come from the divorce judgment, which divides marital property under the Illinois Marriage and Dissolution of Marriage Act (750 ILCS 5), and from the deeds and titles that carry it out.

Nor does the statute reach your beneficiary forms, your will or your power of attorney, which follow their own rules. See [beneficiary forms after divorce](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan) for those.

## How do different trust setups fare?

The result depends on who made the trust and who it names.

| Trust setup | What the statute does | What still needs work |
|---|---|---|
| Your trust, ex named as beneficiary | Treats ex as having died | Rewrite who gets the share |
| Your trust, ex named successor trustee | Treats ex as having died | Name a new trustee and backup |
| Joint trust, both spouses as settlors | Does not divide it | Split, revoke or restate; retitle assets |
| House deeded to trustees of a joint trust | No change to title | Deed under the judgment |
| Trust for children, ex as co-trustee | Ex's role ends by default | Confirm the trust, or the judgment, supports that |
| Ex's trust names you | Your benefit is revoked | Plan without it |

If your trust is a joint trust, read our page on [joint living trusts for married couples](/learn/trusts/joint-living-trust-for-married-couples). Its termination clause is the first place to look.

## What if someone dies before the divorce is final?

The statute does not apply yet. A pending case leaves the trust unchanged, so the spouse is still a beneficiary. Our page on [what happens if a spouse dies during a divorce](/learn/what-if/death-during-divorce) explains the gap, and the article on [planning during a separation](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-during-separation-or-a-pending-divorce) shows what you can safely do. Some divorce cases include an order that limits moving or giving away assets. Ask your divorce attorney before you amend or revoke anything.

## What should you do after the judgment?

Update the trust and the title, in that order. A practical sequence follows.

1. Get a certified copy of the judgment and any marital settlement agreement.
2. Read the trust for divorce, termination and trustee-succession clauses.
3. Decide whether to amend, restate or revoke the trust. A joint trust usually needs a new trust for each person.
4. Sign deeds for the house and other real estate under the judgment, then record them.
5. Retitle bank and brokerage accounts, as in our [Illinois funding guide](/learn/illinois/funding-a-living-trust-in-illinois).
6. Name a new successor trustee and a backup.
7. Check your [pour-over will](/learn/illinois/pour-over-wills-in-illinois) so it points to the right trust.
8. Update beneficiary forms, powers of attorney and health care directives.

## Why not rely on the statute alone?

Because it is a default with gaps. It does not name your new trustee, it does not tell the bank the ex has no authority, and a bank may not know about the statute. A former spouse listed as trustee on a bank account or a deed can still appear on records until someone updates them. A clear amendment and a fresh certification of trust solve that. See [what a certification of trust is](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-certification-of-trust).

There is also a family cost. Children can be left with an unclear plan if a trust names an ex as their trustee and a statute is the only fix.

## Example: Renee and the joint trust

Renee, 55, and her husband signed a joint trust in 2015 and deeded their $380,000 Oak Park home to the trustees. Their 2025 divorce judgment awarded Renee the house and gave him a $150,000 account. The judgment says nothing about the trust. The statute removes him as successor trustee and beneficiary, but the deed still shows both names. Renee records a new deed to herself as trustee of a new trust, retitles the accounts she kept and signs a new pour-over will. Without those steps, a title company would still ask for his signature on a sale.

## What about children and the family home?

Children are the part of the trust most worth protecting. If the trust leaves the estate to your children, those provisions stay in place, and the former spouse's share is read as if they had died. If the trust names the ex as the person who manages the children's shares, the statute removes that role by default. You then choose a new trustee yourself, rather than leaving the choice to the trust's backup rules or to a court. As for the home, if it is in the trust and the judgment gives it to one spouse, the other spouse's signature may still be needed on a sale until a new deed is recorded. Tell your lender, and ask the title company what it wants. See our page on [moving a house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).
