A parent or spouse has died, and the lawyer or the will says an estate has to be opened. Most people have never walked into a probate clerk's office. This page covers only the opening: what you gather, where you file, what the hearing is like and what you hold at the end.

For the whole life of an estate, see [Illinois probate, step by step](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process). For the larger picture of Illinois law, start at our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Does this estate need probate at all?

Check this first, because it can save weeks. Probate is needed for assets in the dead person's name alone that have no beneficiary and no co-owner. Bank accounts with a payable-on-death designation, jointly held property and trust assets do not need it. If the person left only personal property and the total is within the limit in 755 ILCS 5/25-1, a family member may use the [small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) and never open a case. A house with a recorded [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) may also pass without court. Real estate in the decedent's sole name, or personal property above the affidavit limit, usually means probate.

## What are the steps to open an Illinois estate?

The order is the same in every county, though local forms and calendars differ.

1. Order certified death certificates. Banks and the court each want one.
2. Locate the original will and deliver it to the circuit clerk of the proper county (755 ILCS 5/6-1). Our article on filing the original will explains the duty.
3. Decide who will serve. A named executor has 30 days from learning of the nomination to act or decline (755 ILCS 5/6-3).
4. Gather the facts for the petition: heirs, legatees, addresses, estimated property values.
5. File the petition for probate and for letters of office, with a request for independent administration. See [what goes in the petition](/learn/illinois/illinois-petition-for-letters-of-office).
6. Attend the hearing. The judge admits the will and appoints the executor.
7. Take the oath and arrange any bond the court requires.
8. Receive certified letters of office and order several copies.
9. Mail notice to heirs and legatees within 14 days after the order (755 ILCS 5/6-10). See [who gets that notice](/learn/illinois/notice-to-heirs-and-legatees-in-illinois-probate).
10. Open the estate bank account and publish the creditor notice.

## Which county do you file in?

You file in the county where the person lived when they died, not where the executor lives or where the house is. If the person had no Illinois home but owned Illinois property, the rules point to the county of the property. Our article on [property in more than one county](/learn/illinois/probate-venue-property-in-two-illinois-counties) goes through the cases.

For large counties, the clerk's probate office has its own rules. See [Cook County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/cook) for verified office details.

## What do you bring on the first day?

| Item | Why the clerk or judge wants it |
|---|---|
| Original will | The court admits the original, not a copy |
| Certified death certificate | Proves the death and the date |
| Heir and legatee list with addresses | Needed for the petition and the 14-day notice |
| Estimate of real and personal property | The petition states a value; it is an estimate, not an appraisal |
| Executor's photo ID | Identity for the oath |
| Filing fee | Set by each county clerk; see [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) |
| Bond information, if the court asks | The will may excuse a bond, but the court can still require one |

## What happens at the hearing?

The hearing is short and mostly procedural. The judge looks at the will, the petition and proof of the death. If the will was signed with the usual two witnesses, the court can generally admit it on their signed statements rather than live testimony. If nobody objects, the judge enters an order admitting the will and naming the executor.

Rosa, 67, is named executor in her husband Luis's will. She files the will on a Monday, and her lawyer files the petition on Thursday with a $240,000 estimate of the house and accounts. The hearing is set three weeks later. She takes the oath that day, the clerk issues six certified letters, and she mails notice to Luis's two children the next week, well inside the 14-day limit.

## What are letters of office and what do they cost?

Letters of office are the certified court paper that proves the executor's authority. Banks, brokers and title companies ask for one dated within a recent window, often 60 to 90 days, so order extra copies and refresh them if the estate drags on. Each certified copy has a small clerk fee. Filing fees vary by county; the clerk's published schedule is the reliable source.

## What should you do right after letters arrive?

Get an estate tax ID number from the IRS and open an [estate bank account](/learn/illinois/opening-an-estate-bank-account-in-illinois). Start the creditor notice. Prepare the [inventory](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-inventory).

## What if there is no will?

The steps are close to the same, with three differences. The petition asks for letters of administration instead of admission of a will. The court names an administrator by priority, usually the spouse and then the children. And a bond is almost always required, because no will can excuse it. Heirs still receive notice, and the same independent administration choice applies. Our article on [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) covers priority and cost.

## Which mistakes cost the most time in the first month?

Three come up again and again. First, filing in the wrong county, which forces a transfer. Second, a petition that omits an heir, which means a second round of notices. Third, waiting to order death certificates, so the bank visit has to be repeated. A short call to the clerk before you file, with the county and the form version confirmed, prevents all three. Write down the case number the day you get it, because every bank and agency will ask for it.

## Do you need a lawyer to open the estate?

The law allows an executor to file the papers, and some families do. The risk is in the details: the heir list, the notice deadlines and the choice between independent and supervised administration. Many executors hire an attorney for the opening and handle the later bookkeeping themselves, which keeps cost down while protecting the early steps that are hardest to repair. Our page on [handling an Illinois estate without a lawyer](/learn/illinois/handling-an-illinois-probate-estate-without-a-lawyer) lists what a do-it-yourself executor takes on.
