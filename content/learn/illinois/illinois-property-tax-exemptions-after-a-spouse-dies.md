Your husband's name was on the property tax bill for thirty years. Now you are the one opening the envelope, and you do not know whether the senior discount he set up still applies to you. That is the usual start of this question, and the answer is mostly reassuring with one exception that costs people money.

This page covers the property tax exemptions that reduce an Illinois tax bill, what happens to them after a spouse dies, and the short list of things a surviving spouse should check. It is general education, not legal advice. The details below come from the Cook County Assessor's published exemption pages, and other counties run their own offices and forms. The creditor protection that goes by a similar name is a different subject, covered in our page on the [Illinois homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death). For the rest of the state's rules, start at the [Illinois pillar page](/learn/illinois).

## Which Illinois property tax exemptions might apply to a surviving spouse?

Three do most of the work for a home that the survivor still lives in. The homeowner exemption is for owners who own and occupy the property as a principal residence. The senior exemption is for owners who are 65 or older and live in the home as their primary residence. The senior freeze, formally the low-income senior assessment freeze, adds an income test and holds the home's equalized assessed value in place. The Cook County Assessor describes the first two as renewing automatically each year and the third as something that must be filed annually.

That difference matters more after a death than at any other time. A survivor who assumes everything renews by itself can miss the one that does not.

| Exemption | Who qualifies | Renews itself? | After a spouse dies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homeowner | Owner who lives in the home as principal residence | Yes (Cook County) | Survivor generally continues if still owner and resident |
| Senior | Owner age 65 or older who lives there | Yes (Cook County) | Survivor who is 65 or older generally continues |
| Senior freeze | Senior exemption plus household income limit | No, file every year | Recheck income and re-file |

The exact dollar value of each exemption is set by statute and by your county's practice, and it changes with reassessment. Read the amounts on your own bill rather than relying on a number from an article.

## Does the exemption die with the spouse?

Not as a rule. An exemption attaches to a parcel and to the qualifying owner or owners, not to one person's account. If you co-owned the home and you still live in it, the county generally has no reason to remove the homeowner exemption.

Two situations deserve a closer look.

**The younger spouse.** Marta is 63 and her husband Leon was 70. Leon's senior exemption was why the bill dropped. When Leon dies, Marta may no longer meet the age rule on her own. She still has the homeowner exemption as a resident owner. She would apply for the senior exemption when she turns 65. A survivor in Marta's position should put the date in a calendar now, because the first year without the exemption arrives quietly.

**Title that never changed.** If the deed lists only the late spouse, or the house is still in a trust or an estate, the person who lives there may not be the owner of record. Whether the exemption can continue then depends on who holds title and who occupies the home. Our articles on [joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) and on [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois) explain how title passes. Sort that out first, because the tax records follow the deed.

## What changes with the senior freeze after a death?

Everything about the income test can change. The freeze requires that you meet the senior exemption rules and that household income stay within a limit. For Cook County the assessor currently lists $65,000 or less for calendar year 2024, and says the freeze must be filed every year. Treat that figure as a snapshot from the assessor's page. It is the number for one tax year, and the limit for the year you file is the one to confirm.

After a death, income usually moves in two directions. A pension may shrink to a survivor benefit, and Social Security changes when one spouse's check stops. Our page on [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) explains that. On the other hand, a life insurance payout or a retirement account that a survivor inherits may or may not count as household income, depending on how the form defines income. Do not guess. Ask the assessor how the application treats those items, and get it in writing from the application instructions where you can.

If you qualified for the freeze before and your income is now lower, you may still qualify. If your income is now higher because of something you inherited, read the definition before you assume you are out.

## What should a surviving spouse do, step by step?

1. Get the current tax bill and find the exemption lines. The bill shows which exemptions are being applied.
2. Look up the parcel on the county assessor's website. In Cook County, the Property Details page shows an Exemption History and a Status. Print it.
3. Compare the owner name on the record with the deed. If the late spouse is still the only owner of record, ask the assessor and the recorder's office what they need to update it.
4. Make a list of what dropped off or never applied. Compare it to the table above.
5. File what needs filing. Homeowner and senior renew by themselves in Cook County. The freeze does not.
6. Keep a copy of every application and confirmation, and note the tax year each one covers.
7. Put the first filing date and your 65th birthday, if relevant, on the calendar.

## Can you recover an exemption you missed?

Often yes, for a limited number of years. In Cook County the normal filing period for a given year closes, and late claims are processed as a Certificate of Error. The assessor states that homeowners can redeem missing savings for tax years 2021 through 2025 through that process, and the same page says to file at cookcountyassessor.com/certificates-error. Other counties handle corrections through their own boards or offices, and their lookback periods may differ.

An example, labelled as such. Joan's husband Ray died in early 2023. Joan never saw the senior freeze lapse because it had always been filed for them, and she did not know it had to be refiled each year. Two years later she checks the exemption history and sees the freeze missing for two bills. With a Certificate of Error she can ask the county to correct those years. She cannot be sure of the result before the assessor reviews it, but she has lost nothing by asking, and the amount at stake is a refund or credit that is hers to claim.

The common mistake our attorneys see is waiting for the bill to say something. It will not. A missing exemption looks the same as a correct bill unless you read the line items.

## How is this different from the homestead exemption?

They share a word and nothing else. The property tax exemptions reduce what you owe each year. The creditor homestead exemption protects up to $15,000 of equity from judgment creditors, and it continues for a surviving spouse and for children until the youngest turns 18. One saves you money on a bill. The other keeps a creditor from taking your equity. A survivor generally wants both, and neither replaces the other.

## What if the house is going to someone else?

If the home passes to a child or another heir, the exemptions do not carry over as a gift. The new owner has to qualify on their own facts: ownership, occupancy and, for the senior exemptions, age and income. An adult child who inherits and rents the house out would not get the homeowner exemption. If you are planning that transfer, the tax effect is worth raising with your attorney before the deed is signed.
