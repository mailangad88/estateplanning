Many Illinois families hear that a living trust "avoids probate." That is partly true, but only if the trust is set up and funded the right way. A trust that sits in a drawer with no assets does little.

This article explains how a living trust works under the Illinois Trust Code, what you must include, how to fund it, and where its limits are. It is general education, not legal advice. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) puts trusts in context with wills and other tools.

## What is a living trust under Illinois law?

A living trust (also called a revocable trust) is an arrangement in which you transfer assets to a trustee who manages them for beneficiaries. You are the settlor. Most people also serve as the first trustee and name a successor trustee to take over at death or incapacity. The Illinois Trust Code (760 ILCS 3) sets the rules.

Illinois lists several ways to create a trust. These include transferring property to a trustee, or declaring that you hold identifiable property as trustee (760 ILCS 3/401). A will can also create one at death, but that is a testamentary trust and does not avoid probate.

## What does a valid Illinois trust need?

The Trust Code lists the elements of a valid trust (760 ILCS 3/402).

1. The settlor has legal capacity.
2. The settlor shows an intent to create the trust.
3. There is a definite beneficiary, or the trust is charitable or has a valid purpose.
4. The trustee has duties to perform.
5. The same person is not the sole trustee and the sole beneficiary.

Oral trusts can exist, but they can be proved only by clear and convincing evidence (760 ILCS 3/407). A written trust is far safer. Real estate also generally requires a signed deed to move into the trust, which is covered below.

## Why must the trust say it is revocable?

The Illinois Trust Code says a settlor may revoke a trust only if the trust instrument expressly provides that it is revocable or that the settlor has an unrestricted power of amendment (760 ILCS 3/602). So a trust that is silent may be treated as not changeable. This is a difference from some other states, so avoid a generic form that skips the language.

While the trust is revocable and you have capacity, the trustee may follow your directions even if they differ from the trust terms (760 ILCS 3/603). If you divorce, the Code revokes provisions that favor your former spouse unless the trust or the divorce judgment says otherwise (760 ILCS 3/605). Read our [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) article for more.

## How do you fund a living trust in Illinois?

Funding means moving ownership of assets into the trust. Without it, those assets may still go through probate. Typical steps follow.

1. Sign a deed that transfers your home to the trustee, and record it with the county recorder.
2. Retitle bank and brokerage accounts into the trust's name, or ask the bank what it requires.
3. Update beneficiary designations on life insurance and retirement accounts where the trust should be named.
4. Assign tangible items and business interests in writing.
5. Sign a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) to catch anything left out.

Our [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) guide gives more detail. Imagine Dolores, 72, who signs a trust but never deeds her house to it. At her death, the house may still need probate, which is the outcome she wanted to avoid. Our article on the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) explains how some small estates avoid full probate, but a house may not fit it.

For a home in the trust, an attorney will check the deed form, the title insurance, and any mortgage terms before you sign.

## How does a bank or title company know the trust exists?

Trustees often use a certification of trust instead of handing over the whole document. Under the Trust Code, it states that the trust exists and the date it was signed, identifies the settlor and the current trustee, lists the trustee's powers, and explains how title is held. A person who relies on it without actual knowledge that it is wrong is protected (760 ILCS 3/1013). This keeps the terms of your trust private.

## What does a trustee have to do after death?

When a trust becomes irrevocable, the trustee takes on duties to the beneficiaries. For trusts that became irrevocable after the Code took effect, the trustee must notify qualified beneficiaries within 90 days of the trust's existence and of their rights to request accountings, and must send at least an annual accounting (760 ILCS 3/813.1). Different timing rules can apply to older trusts (760 ILCS 3/813.2). Ask a lawyer which rule applies to your situation.

Contests are limited too. The Code says a challenge to a revocable trust must start by the earlier of two years after the settlor's death or six months after the trustee sends a copy of the trust and a notice (760 ILCS 3/604). Our [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) article covers who should serve.

## What does a living trust not do?

A living trust is not a tax shield. Its assets are still part of your estate for estate tax purposes, and Illinois has its own estate tax, which is explained in our [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) article. It also does not protect assets from your own creditors while you are alive. Whether you need one at all depends on your property and family, as our [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) article explains. For a single Illinois house, the [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) may be a simpler option.
