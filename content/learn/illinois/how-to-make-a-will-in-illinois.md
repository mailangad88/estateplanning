You know you should have a will, and maybe you have opened a form online and stared at the first blank. The hard part is rarely the wording. It is knowing what to settle first and what to do once the ink is dry.

This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), walks through the whole job in order: the decisions, the draft, the signing and where the original goes. The legal tests a finished will has to pass live on the page about [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements), so this one focuses on the process. If you would rather work through the decisions with someone, you can [book a consultation about your will](/book?from=learn-how-to-make-a-will-in-illinois&topic=will) and bring your notes.

## What are the steps to make a will in Illinois?

There are ten, in four stages. Settle the decisions, turn them into a draft, sign it correctly, then store it and tie up the loose ends.

1. **List what you own** and how each item will pass.
2. **Decide who gets what,** starting with everything left over.
3. **Choose an executor** and at least one backup.
4. **Choose a guardian** for children under 18, and decide who manages their money.
5. **Check whether a will alone fits,** or whether you also need a trust or a deed.
6. **Pick how the will gets drafted:** an attorney, or a form you complete yourself.
7. **Read the draft against your decisions** before signing day.
8. **Sign with two witnesses** who receive nothing under the will.
9. **Store the original** and tell your executor where it is.
10. **Line up your beneficiary forms** and set a date to look at the will again.

## What do you need to decide before you write anything?

Five things, and they take longer than the drafting.

### Step 1: List what you own and how each item passes

Write down your home, accounts, life insurance, vehicles and anything of sentimental value. Next to each, note how it is titled and whether it names a beneficiary. A will only reaches property in your name alone with no other instruction attached. A 401(k), a policy or a payable-on-death account goes to the person on the company's form, and a jointly owned house passes to the surviving owner. The full ranking is in [which controls: will, trust, joint account or beneficiary form](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form).

<!-- visual: diagram name=BeneficiaryBeatsWill -->

### Step 2: Decide who gets what, starting with the leftovers

Start with the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate), the clause that covers everything you do not give away by name. For most families that clause carries the house, the savings and whatever you acquire later. Decide who takes it, in what shares, and who takes a share if that person dies before you. That backup is a [contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary), and leaving it out is one of the most common gaps in a home-made will. Then add any specific gifts.

If you are married, know that a spouse left out of a will can renounce it and take a statutory share (755 ILCS 5/2-8). That rule shapes the plan in a second marriage, and [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) explains it.

### Step 3: Choose an executor and a backup

Your executor files the will, gathers assets, pays bills and hands out what is left. In Illinois the person must be at least 18, live in the United States and meet the other tests in 755 ILCS 5/6-13; they do not need to live in Illinois. The page on [who can be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-can-be-an-executor-in-illinois) covers the bond question for someone out of state, and [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) covers temperament and time. Ask both before you name them. The free [executor candidate scorecard](/free/choosing-an-executor-scorecard) helps you compare people side by side.

### Step 4: Choose a guardian and decide who holds the money

If you have children under 18, your will is where you nominate a guardian, along with a backup (755 ILCS 5/11-5). The judge still makes the appointment, but your written choice is the starting point. [Naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children) covers how to choose and what to do if you and your spouse disagree.

Decide separately who manages a child's inheritance. A share left outright to a minor usually ends up under court supervision and is handed over at 18. A trust inside the will avoids that, as [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) explains.

### Step 5: Check whether a will alone fits

A will passes property through probate. If keeping the house out of court matters to you, Illinois also offers a recorded [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) and the [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts), and either one changes what the will needs to say. Illinois also has its own estate tax for estates over $4 million, explained in the [Illinois estate tax guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). The [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) gives a first read on which fits your household.

## How do you turn those decisions into a draft?

### Step 6: Pick how the will gets drafted

An Illinois estate planning attorney can draft from your decision sheet, or you can complete a form yourself. Illinois law allows either, and [do I need a lawyer for a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/do-i-need-a-lawyer-for-a-will-in-illinois) sorts which households can safely use a form. If you choose a form, use one written for Illinois, because signing rules differ from state to state. If you choose an attorney, bring your decision sheet and the documents listed in [what to bring to a lawyer to make a will](/learn/wills/what-to-bring-to-a-lawyer-to-make-a-will).

### Step 7: Read the draft against your decisions

Check the draft against your decision sheet line by line. Look for full legal names spelled correctly, a backup for every role, a clause for leftover property, and a sentence revoking any earlier will. Make every change in the draft itself, before signing. Once the will is signed, handwritten edits on the page cause trouble, which is why [handwritten wills in Illinois](/learn/illinois/handwritten-wills-in-illinois) warns against them.

## What happens on signing day?

### Step 8: Sign with two witnesses who receive nothing

Signing day is short. You, two adult witnesses and the final printed will are together. You sign while both witnesses watch, and each witness then signs while you are present (755 ILCS 5/4-3). Choose witnesses who receive nothing under the will and are not married to anyone who does, because a gift to a witness can be void (755 ILCS 5/4-6). Have the witnesses also sign an [attestation clause](/glossary/attestation-clause) stating what they saw, so the court can admit the will later without tracking them down (755 ILCS 5/6-4). A notary is not part of the Illinois rule.

The step-by-step order, the witness comparison and the self-proving details are all on the [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) page. If you cannot get to an office, [signing a will or trust remotely in Illinois](/learn/illinois/sign-will-or-trust-remotely-in-illinois) explains the video option. The free [will signing day checklist](/free/will-signing-day-checklist) lists what to bring.

## Where should you keep the signed will?

### Step 9: Store the original and tell your executor

Keep one signed original somewhere safe from fire and theft that your executor can reach after your death: a fireproof safe at home, or the vault of the attorney who drafted it. A bank safe deposit box can slow your executor down. The options are compared in [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will), and the free [where to keep your will guide](/free/where-to-keep-your-will-guide) has a one-page version.

Do not unstaple or write on the original. After a death, whoever holds the will must file it with the circuit court clerk (755 ILCS 5/6-1), as [filing the original will with the Illinois circuit clerk](/learn/illinois/filing-the-original-will-with-the-illinois-circuit-clerk) describes. An original that cannot be found causes its own problems, covered in [when the original will cannot be found](/learn/illinois/lost-original-will-in-illinois). The free [what to tell your executor](/free/what-to-tell-your-executor-guide) guide covers telling them where it is.

## What should you do after the will is signed?

### Step 10: Line up beneficiary forms and set a review date

Go back to your Step 1 list. For every account with a beneficiary form, confirm the named person matches your plan. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) walks through each one.

Then pick the events that should send you back to the will: a marriage, a divorce, a new child, a move, or a named person dying. When that day comes, [how to change a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/how-to-change-a-will-in-illinois) shows whether a codicil or a new will fits.

## Example: one couple's will from first list to file drawer

This is an invented example. Andre, 44, and Keisha, 42, live in Joliet with two children, ages 9 and 12. They own their house together and each has a 401(k) and a life insurance policy through work.

**The decisions.** Their list shows the house, retirement accounts and policies all pass to the survivor, so the wills mostly matter if both die. They agree that everything left over goes to the children equally, held in a trust until age 25. Keisha's sister Renée is their choice of guardian, with Andre's cousin Marcus as backup. Marcus is good with money, so they name him trustee and executor, with Renée as backup executor. Both agree to serve.

**The draft.** They give the decision sheet to an attorney. Reading the draft against it, Keisha notices that the contingent beneficiaries on her life insurance are blank. That would have sent the policy to her estate if Andre died first, so she fixes it with the insurer.

**Signing day.** Each signs a separate will. Two office staff members who receive nothing act as witnesses and sign the attestation clause.

**Storage.** The originals stay in the attorney's vault. Renée and Marcus get a one-page note saying where the wills are and listing the accounts. The couple sets a reminder to look again when their oldest turns 18.
