A widow adds her son to her checking account so he can pay her bills. She dies. The bank pays the whole balance to the son. Her daughter says the money was supposed to be split. Who is right?

Illinois law answers that question with a presumption, and a pile of proof on both sides. This page focuses on the dispute, not on the account types themselves. For the basic rules on POD, trust and joint accounts see [payable-on-death and joint accounts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts). The earlier steps of what to do if a child spends the money are in our article on [adding a child to your bank account](/learn/what-if/child-on-your-bank-account-spends-the-money-or-dies-first). All Illinois pages start from our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois). This is general education, not legal advice.

## What is a convenience account?

It is an account where one person is on the paperwork to help the real owner, not to own the money. The helper writes checks, pays bills and deposits funds on the owner's behalf. Banks often do not distinguish this from an ordinary joint account unless the owner signs a special form. The Illinois Joint Tenancy Act lets a bank pay a joint deposit to any joint owner (765 ILCS 1005/2), so the bank is protected when it pays the survivor. The question for the family is who owns the money as between the survivor and the estate.

## How does Illinois treat a joint account at death?

Illinois courts have generally treated putting an account in joint names with survivorship as raising a presumption that the owner intended a gift to the survivor. The other heirs can try to rebut this with clear and convincing evidence that the owner did not intend a gift. Because case law shapes the standard, ask a lawyer how it applies to your facts.

When the survivor is also the owner's agent under a power of attorney, or otherwise in a position of trust, courts look harder. The presumption can shift, and the survivor may have to show the gift was fair. Our article on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) explains why.

## What evidence do families use?

| Evidence | Helps the survivor if... | Helps the other heirs if... |
|---|---|---|
| Who made the deposits | Both put money in | Only the parent did |
| Who used the money | Both used it for themselves | Only the parent benefited |
| Signature card wording | States survivorship | Labels it convenience or agency |
| Owner's statements | "This is yours when I die" | "This is only so you can pay my bills" |
| Parent's will and other plans | Matches the survivor getting the account | Treats children equally elsewhere |
| Taxes and interest reporting | Reported by both | Reported only by the parent |
| Timing | Added after long planning | Added after a stroke or hospital stay |

## How do you handle a dispute step by step?

1. Get the signature card and account agreement from the bank.
2. Request statements going back as far as the bank allows, ideally the whole joint period.
3. Mark deposits by source and withdrawals by who made them and why.
4. Collect written messages, emails or notes from the owner about the account.
5. Tell the executor, or ask for a representative to be appointed, so the estate can make a claim.
6. Try a family meeting or mediation before a lawsuit.
7. If needed, the estate's representative may file a petition in the probate court or a civil claim. Time limits apply, so ask a lawyer early.

The survivor should avoid spending the money while the dispute is open. Spending can turn a money question into a conduct question.

## A worked example

Doris, 84, adds her son Carl, 55, to her $90,000 savings account in 2022 after a fall. Her daughter Lena lives out of state. Doris dies in 2025. The bank pays Carl the full balance of $86,400.

Lena asks questions. The statements show every deposit was Doris's Social Security and pension. Carl paid her bills from the account, and he took out $14,000 in 2024 to pay his own car loan. Doris wrote a note in 2022 that said "Carl will help with my money." Doris's will leaves everything equally to Carl and Lena. A court would weigh the note, the signature card and Carl's use of the money. If Lena proves the account was for convenience, the balance, less valid payments Carl made for Doris, could go back to the estate and be split $43,200 each. If Carl shows Doris meant a gift, he keeps it.

## How do you avoid this fight in the first place?

- Use a power of attorney, not joint ownership, to give a helper access. See [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property).
- If you want a child to inherit the account, use a POD designation instead and tell all your children.
- If you want the helper not to inherit, sign a short letter and give the bank an agency or convenience form if it has one.
- If you do add a child as joint owner, write down what you intend and give each child a copy.
- Review accounts once every few years with the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit).

## What should a surviving joint owner do?

Keep the account records, do not make large gifts to yourself, and pay estate bills like the funeral from the account if the family agrees. Offer the other heirs a simple accounting. If you are a fiduciary, consider asking a lawyer before distributing. Our article on [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) explains why debts matter even when an account passes by survivorship.

## When does a lawsuit make sense?

Court is a last step, not a first one. Consider it when the amount is large, the records show a clear pattern, and talking has failed. The estate's representative usually brings the claim, because the money belongs to the estate if the account was only for convenience. A beneficiary who thinks the representative is ignoring the claim can raise it with the court.

Weigh the costs before filing. A dispute over an $86,400 account can use up a large part of that money in fees, and it can break a family. Mediation is often cheaper and faster, and a neutral person can help siblings see the other side. If the survivor offers to share part of the balance, a written settlement that releases all claims can end the matter. Whichever path you choose, keep the bank records, ask for them early, and avoid accusing anyone of theft before you have read every statement.
