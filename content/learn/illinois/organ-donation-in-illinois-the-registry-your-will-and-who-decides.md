Priya, 34, ticked the donor box at a driver services facility years ago. Her mother says that means nothing happens unless the family signs, and her father thinks it should be in the will. They are both worried, and they are both wrong in different ways.

This page covers organ donation in Illinois: where the donor registry fits, why a will is the wrong tool, how an advance directive supports the choice, and who may decide when no one registered. The general version of this subject is in our page on [organ donation and your advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/organ-donation-and-your-advance-directive), and this article stays with Illinois law. It is general education, not legal advice. The wider set of Illinois guides starts at the [Illinois pillar page](/learn/illinois).

## How do you register as an organ donor in Illinois?

You register with the Illinois Secretary of State, whose driver services program runs the state registry. People usually sign up when they get or renew a license or state ID, and a registered choice is the record a donation team checks. If you are unsure whether you are on the registry, ask the Secretary of State's office to confirm your status. We would not rely on a card in a wallet alone.

Illinois law has a name for what a registered adult is doing: making an anatomical gift. The statute is the Illinois Anatomical Gift Act, found at 755 ILCS 50. It covers organs, eyes and tissue, and it says who can give, who can decide for someone else and who can change a gift.

Who can register? The Act allows an adult or emancipated donor to make a gift. It also allows a 16- or 17-year-old who is registered in the First Person Consent registry. For a younger child, a parent decides, and for a person under guardianship, the guardian does (755 ILCS 50/5-5(a)). Check with the Secretary of State for the details of the minor registry.

## Does your will control organ donation?

No, and this is the misconception we hear most. A will takes effect at death, and nobody reads it until someone finds it, and it may be days. Organs have a very short window. By the time an executor opens the will, the decision has been made by someone else or it has been missed.

That does not mean you cannot mention it. A sentence in the will stating your wishes does no harm, and it can comfort your family. But it should repeat what you have already done, not stand in for it. If you want the full picture of what a will does and does not control, see [which document controls](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form).

| Document | Reaches the hospital in time? | What to put in it |
|---|---|---|
| Donor registry | Yes, checked at the time | Your yes or no, and any limits |
| Advance directive | Usually, through your agent | The same choice, plus acceptance of short-term support |
| Will | Rarely | A line repeating your wishes, nothing more |
| Conversation with family | Yes, if they are there | Your choice, said out loud |

## How does an advance directive fit?

It handles the part the registry cannot: what happens to you before death is declared. A living will that refuses a ventilator can accidentally rule out donation if it does not carve out an exception. A good directive says you accept temporary support so that donation stays possible, and it names the person who may speak about it. In Illinois that person is your [health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) agent.

Illinois also has a form for emergency treatment orders. If you have a POLST or a DNR order, our page on [Illinois POLST and DNR orders](/learn/illinois/illinois-polst-and-dnr-orders) explains those forms, and your agent should know every one of your documents exists.

## Who decides if you did not register?

The Act sets an order. If you made no gift and nobody knows you objected, a gift may be made by a reasonably available person in this priority (755 ILCS 50/5-5(b)):

1. Your health care agent under a power of attorney for health care.
2. The guardian of your person.
3. Your spouse or civil union partner.
4. An adult child.
5. A parent.
6. An adult sibling.
7. An adult grandchild.
8. A grandparent.
9. A close friend.
10. The guardian of your estate.
11. Any other person authorized or legally obligated to dispose of your body.

Two rules keep the system fair. Someone in a lower class may not make the gift if someone in a higher class is reasonably available. And if several people share a class, such as three adult children, one may act unless they know another member objects. When there is an objection, a majority of the reasonably available members of that class must agree.

The top of that list is why naming the right agent matters. Many Illinois families assume the spouse decides. If you have a signed health care power of attorney naming your daughter, she, not your spouse, is first in line. Our page on the [Illinois Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides) explains who makes treatment decisions when no agent exists, and the two lists are not identical.

Consider Walt, 71, who never registered. He has a daughter, Ellen, who is his named agent, and a wife, Joan, who lives with him. After a stroke, the hospital asks about donation. Because Ellen is his agent, she is first in line if she is reasonably available. If she cannot be reached, Joan decides next. The result may differ from what Walt would have wanted, which is why a registry entry and a conversation matter.

## Can anyone cancel your gift?

Not easily. A donor can amend or revoke by a signed record or a later document of gift. Others are generally barred from changing or revoking a gift the donor made, unless the donor expressly indicated otherwise (755 ILCS 50/5-7). When the gift was made by a relative under the priority list, a person in a higher class can amend or revoke it, but the revocation works only if the transplant team learns of it before an incision is made.

## What about donating your whole body?

That is a different program. Donating your body to a medical school or research program requires an agreement with that program, usually signed in advance, and often a back-up plan if the program cannot accept you. Organ registration does not cover it. Ask the program what happens to the remains and when they are returned. Illinois separately decides who controls disposition of remains, covered in our page on the [Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act).

## What should you do this month?

1. Confirm your registry status with the Secretary of State.
2. Add one sentence to your advance directive repeating the choice and accepting temporary medical support.
3. Check who your health care agent is. They are first on the list above.
4. Tell your spouse, your children and your agent in plain words.
5. Put a line in your will or funeral instructions, as a back-up and not the main step.
