You have a rough idea of who should get what, and you have seen will forms online for little or nothing. The question is whether Illinois makes you hire a lawyer to turn that idea into a will, and if not, whether you should anyway. This page, part of the [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois), gives the honest answer: the law does not require one, and many people do fine without one. It then walks through the specific Illinois situations where a do-it-yourself will tends to go wrong.

If one of those situations sounds like yours, you can [book a consultation about your will](/book?from=learn-do-i-need-a-lawyer-for-a-will-in-illinois&topic=will) and bring whatever draft you have.

## Does Illinois law require a lawyer to make a will?

No. Nothing in the Illinois Probate Act says a will has to be drafted, signed or witnessed by an attorney. The law cares about who signs and how. You must be at least 18 and of sound mind and memory (755 ILCS 5/4-1). The will must be in writing, signed by you or by someone at your direction in your presence, and attested in your presence by at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/4-3). A notary is not part of the rule.

The full signing steps are on the page about [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements). If you already have a will someone wrote by hand or printed from a website, the [six-point Illinois will validity check](/learn/illinois/is-my-will-valid) tests it against those rules, and [handwritten wills in Illinois](/learn/illinois/handwritten-wills-in-illinois) explains why handwriting never replaces the witnesses. For how DIY planning works in general, see [making an estate plan without a lawyer](/learn/basics/making-an-estate-plan-without-a-lawyer), and for the legality of downloaded forms, [are free templates for a will legal](/learn/wills/are-free-templates-for-a-will-legal).

So legality is settled. The real question is whether your situation is one a form can handle.

## Where do do-it-yourself wills go wrong in Illinois?

Mostly in seven places. None of them makes a form illegal. Each is a spot where the form asks the wrong question, or no question at all, and an Illinois rule fills the gap in a way the family did not expect.

### 1. A witness who inherits

Kitchen-table signings tend to use whoever is in the house, which is often a spouse, an adult child or a child's husband. Under 755 ILCS 5/4-6, a gift to a witness, or to a witness's spouse, is void unless the will has enough other witnesses. The witness may still receive up to what they would have inherited without the will. The fix costs nothing: two neighbors or coworkers who get nothing. A form cannot see who is standing at the table.

### 2. The house and the TODI choice

A will moves a house only through probate. Illinois also offers a transfer on death instrument under 755 ILCS 27, which passes real estate at death without probate. It needs two credible witnesses, a notary, and recording in the county before the owner dies, or it is void. A later will cannot revoke a recorded TODI. So a person who records a TODI naming one child and later writes a will splitting "my house" among three children has two documents that disagree, and the recorded one wins. The page on [the Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) covers the form, and [TODI or living trust for an Illinois house](/learn/illinois/todi-vs-living-trust-for-an-illinois-house) compares the options.

### 3. Blended families and the spouse's renunciation right

A form that says "everything to my children" does not shut out a surviving spouse. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-8, a spouse can renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if there are descendants, or one-half if there are none, by filing within 7 months after the will is admitted. Stepchildren, for their part, are not heirs of a stepparent unless adopted. A second-marriage will that ignores both rules invites a fight between the spouse and the children. See [renouncing a will as a surviving spouse in Illinois](/learn/illinois/renouncing-a-will-as-a-surviving-spouse-in-illinois) for how the choice plays out.

### 4. Minor beneficiaries

Most forms leave property "to my children in equal shares." If a child is under 18 when you die, a parent cannot simply take that child's share. The court usually appoints a guardian of the child's estate, the money is held under court supervision, and the child receives what is left all at once at 18. A will can avoid that by holding the share in a trust or naming a custodian. The details are in [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois), and choosing who raises the children is covered in [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/illinois/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children).

### 5. The $4 million Illinois estate tax

Online forms are usually written with the federal estate tax in mind, and the federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026. Illinois has its own estate tax for estates over $4 million, counting certain lifetime gifts, and it does not allow portability between spouses. A couple with a house, retirement accounts and life insurance can cross $4 million without feeling wealthy. A simple "all to my spouse" will can waste the first spouse's $4 million. The [Illinois estate tax guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) explains the threshold, and the [estate tax estimator](/tools/estate-tax-estimator) gives a rough figure.

### 6. Property in another state

Illinois probate does not reach a cabin in Wisconsin or land in Florida. Real estate is governed by the state where it sits, so a will usually means a second court case there, called ancillary probate. A living trust or, where that state allows one, a beneficiary deed can avoid it. A form will not raise the issue. See [an Illinois resident owning property in another state](/learn/illinois/illinois-resident-owning-property-in-another-state).

### 7. A child or relative with special needs

Leaving money outright to a person who receives SSI or Medicaid can cost them those benefits. The usual answer is a third-party supplemental needs trust written into the will or a living trust. A generic form does not include one and often has no place to put one. [Special needs planning in Illinois](/learn/illinois/special-needs-planning-in-illinois-for-a-disabled-child) walks through the layers.

## When is a do-it-yourself will fine, and when is an attorney worth it?

Here is a quick sort. If every row that applies to you sits in the left column, a careful form and a correct signing may be enough.

| Your situation | Fine to do yourself | Worth an attorney |
|---|---|---|
| Marital status | Single, or a first marriage with only joint children | Second marriage, stepchildren, or a partner you are not married to |
| Home | You rent, or the house already passes the way you want | You own a home and have not chosen between a will, a TODI and a trust |
| Children | Adult children, no special circumstances | Children under 18, or an heir who receives SSI or Medicaid |
| Size of estate | Well under $4 million, counting life insurance and retirement accounts | Near or over $4 million for one person or for the couple combined |
| Location of property | Everything in Illinois | Real estate in another state |
| Witnesses | Two adults available who receive nothing | Only family members available to witness |
| Family dynamics | Everyone gets along and expects the plan | Unequal shares, a child left out, or a likely dispute |
| Your health | Healthy and clear-headed | A recent diagnosis, memory concerns or a guardianship in place |

The last row matters because of capacity. If a court has appointed a guardian for you, a will signed afterward is presumed invalid unless clear and convincing evidence shows you had capacity (755 ILCS 5/4-1). A signing with an attorney and independent witnesses creates a record of how you were that day.

## What does an example look like?

This is an invented example. Renee, 61, lives in Naperville with her second husband, Glen. She has two adult sons from her first marriage, and Glen has a daughter. Renee owns the house in her name. She also has a 401(k) naming Glen, and a lake lot in Michigan she inherited from her father.

Renee prints a free form, writes "everything to my sons," and has Glen and his daughter witness it at the dining table.

Look at what follows if she dies first. Glen is a witness, but he gets nothing under the will, so the witness rule does not hurt him. He can still renounce the will and take one-third of the estate under 755 ILCS 5/2-8. The 401(k) goes to Glen by beneficiary form whatever the will says. The Michigan lot needs a separate court case in Michigan. The form did nothing wrong on its face, yet her sons may end up with far less than she meant, after two probate cases. A one-hour conversation would have raised the TODI or trust for the house, a trust for the lake lot, and a written agreement with Glen about the renunciation right.

## What does an Illinois estate planning attorney actually do for a will?

The work is mostly questions, coordination and the signing. In a typical process, the attorney:

1. **Gathers facts.** Who is in your family, including past marriages, stepchildren and anyone on public benefits. What you own and how each item is titled.
2. **Checks what the will cannot control.** Deeds, joint accounts, recorded TODIs, life insurance and retirement beneficiary forms. Anything that passes outside the will is matched to the plan or flagged.
3. **Recommends the tools.** A will alone, a will plus a TODI, or a [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) with a pour-over will, and whether any share should be held in trust for a minor or a person with a disability.
4. **Drafts the will.** Including backup executors and beneficiaries, guardian nominations, and what happens if a beneficiary dies first.
5. **Runs the signing.** Two disinterested witnesses, an attestation clause so the will can be proved without their testimony (755 ILCS 5/6-4), and any notarized companion documents.
6. **Tells you where the original goes** and when to come back, such as after a marriage, a divorce, a move or a new grandchild.
