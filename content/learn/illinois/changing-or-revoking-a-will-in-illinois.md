Plans change. You remarry, a child is born, a beneficiary falls out of your life, or you simply decide the house should go to someone else. The question is how to change an Illinois will so the change holds up when it matters, and which common shortcuts do not work.

This page covers the Probate Act's rules on revocation, what divorce does to an existing will, how codicils fit, and when it is easier to start over. The full set of Illinois pages is in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois). For the signing formalities that apply to any new document, see [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements).

## The short version

- Illinois recognizes four ways to revoke a will: physical destruction, a later will that says so, a later will that conflicts, or a signed and attested revocation document.
- Marriage, children and moving do not revoke a will on their own. Divorce does something narrower.
- A codicil edits a will, but it has to be signed with the same care as the will.
- For anything beyond a small fix, a new will is usually safer than a stack of amendments.

## How can you revoke a will in Illinois?

Section 4-7(a) of the Probate Act lists the methods, and the list is the point. If what you did is not on it, the will stands. In plain terms:

1. **Destroy it.** You burn, cancel, tear or obliterate the will yourself, or someone does it at your direction and in your presence.
2. **Sign a later will that expressly revokes the earlier one.** Most lawyers put a sentence in every new will saying all earlier wills and codicils are revoked.
3. **Sign a later will that conflicts with the earlier one.** The earlier will is revoked to the extent of the conflict. This is how a half-revocation happens by accident, and it is why a second will written without looking at the first can cause trouble.
4. **Sign a separate document declaring the revocation.** It must be signed and attested, which means executed with the same formalities as a will.

The practical lesson sits in method one. Destroying a copy does nothing to the signed original. Maria keeps her original will in a safe deposit box, then tears up the copy on her kitchen table after an argument with her brother. Her will is untouched. If she wanted it gone, she would have to retrieve and destroy the original, or sign something that revokes it.

The reverse problem is just as common. A parent destroys the only original on purpose, and the family later finds a photocopy. A copy is not the will, and the court will want proof of exactly what happened. Our page on a [lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will) explains why that is so hard to untangle.

## What does divorce do to a will?

Section 4-7(b) first states the general rule: a will is not revoked by changes in your circumstances, condition or marital status. Then it carves out divorce. When a marriage ends by divorce or by a declaration of invalidity, the judgment cancels gifts, powers of appointment and fiduciary nominations in favor of the former spouse that appear in a will made before the judgment. Those provisions are treated as if the former spouse had died before you.

That sounds like complete protection, and it is not. Consider Denise, divorced two years ago. Her will, signed during the marriage, leaves everything to her husband and then to her sister if he does not survive. After the divorce, he is treated as having died first, so her sister takes. Good. But the same will might also name her ex-husband's brother as trustee for a child, or leave something directly to the ex-husband's family. Those gifts to other people are not cancelled by 4-7(b). A divorce does not rewrite the rest of the document.

The rule also stops at the will. Life insurance, retirement accounts and payable-on-death accounts follow their own beneficiary forms, and a divorce does not automatically fix those. If your ex is still listed, see [what happens when an ex-spouse is still the beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary). The sensible move after any divorce is a fresh will and a beneficiary audit, not reliance on the statute.

## What about marriage, a new baby or a move?

None of these revokes an Illinois will, because 4-7(b) says a change in circumstances or marital status does not. A will signed when you were single is still your will after you marry. That can leave a new spouse with less than you would want, though a surviving spouse has separate statutory rights. We cover those in [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death).

A child born after you sign has a protection of its own, which is the subject of our article on [children born after an Illinois will](/learn/illinois/children-born-after-an-illinois-will-and-lapsed-gifts). For the general version of these life events, see [marriage, divorce or a new baby and your existing will](/learn/wills/marriage-divorce-or-a-new-baby-and-your-existing-will).

## How does a codicil work?

A codicil is a short, separate document that amends a will without replacing it. In Illinois it has to be treated as a will for signing purposes, meaning you sign it and the required witnesses sign it, just as they did for the original. A codicil that is signed but not witnessed is not a codicil. It is a note.

Codicils suit one narrow job: changing a named executor, swapping one beneficiary, adjusting a dollar amount. They age badly after that. Three codicils over fifteen years means the family reads four documents, some with references like "paragraph 6 of my will dated March 3." Courts can handle that, but it invites disputes about which paragraph survived. Our page on [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) walks through the choice in more detail.

| Situation | Better tool |
|---|---|
| Swap the executor or one named beneficiary | Codicil |
| Several changes, or a codicil already exists | New will |
| You want the old will gone completely | New will with a revocation sentence, or a signed and attested revocation |
| Divorce or remarriage | New will |
| Major change in assets or children | New will, and review the beneficiary forms |

## Can a revoked will be brought back?

Section 4-7(c) says a will that was fully revoked is revived only by re-executing it or by a signed and attested instrument declaring its revival. Simply destroying the later will does not resurrect the earlier one. If a partial revocation is itself revoked, the part that was revoked is restored.

Revival is almost never the best answer. Edward revokes his 2018 will with a 2024 will, then decides he prefers the 2018 version. The reliable path is a new will that says in clear words what he wants. It avoids any argument about whether the old document was properly revived and whether the family can find the original.

## What to do if you want to change your will

1. Find the signed original and read it. Note any codicils.
2. Decide what you want the final result to be, in one sentence per beneficiary.
3. Have an attorney draft a new will or a codicil, and sign it with the formalities in [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements).
4. Revoke the old document in writing inside the new one, then either destroy the old original in a way you can describe later or leave it with your attorney marked as revoked.
5. Update beneficiary designations and any trust to match.
6. Tell your executor where the new original is kept.
