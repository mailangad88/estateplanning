Signing a TODI feels like the finish line. It is not. In Illinois, the document does nothing unless it is recorded in the public land records before the owner dies. An instrument that stays in a safe or a file folder is void under the Act.

This page is about the recording step. For what the instrument must say, see our overview of the [Illinois transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument). Our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois) maps the related pages. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Why does recording matter so much?

The Act requires that a TODI be recorded in the county where the land is located before the owner's death (755 ILCS 27/40). Recording works like recording a deed (see [types of deeds](/learn/property-and-assets/types-of-deeds-quitclaim-warranty-grant-and-trustee-deeds)) and gives public notice, which lets buyers, lenders and title companies find the beneficiary's interest. It also fixes the document's date in the record. A TODI is effective at death even though the beneficiary was never told, but only if it was recorded in time.

Consider Beatrice, 80, who signs a TODI in her lawyer's office in March and puts the original in a drawer. She dies in June. Nobody records it. Her daughter finds it, but the Act treats it as void. The house goes through probate under Beatrice's will instead.

## Where do you record it?

You record with the county office that keeps land records for the county where the parcel is. In most Illinois counties that is the county recorder. In Cook County, land records are handled through the Cook County Clerk's recordings division. Our county pages, such as [Cook County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/cook), [DuPage County](/estate-planning/illinois/counties/dupage) and Lake County, cover probate court information, but recording is a separate office, so confirm it with the recorder.

Many counties accept recording by mail, in person, or through an electronic recording vendor used by title companies and attorneys. Each county posts its own rules.

## How do you record a TODI step by step?

1. Find the exact legal description and parcel number on your current deed or tax bill.
2. Prepare the instrument, with the transfer at death language, beneficiaries and a signature page for witnesses and notary.
3. Sign in front of two credible witnesses and a notary, as the Act requires (755 ILCS 27/45).
4. Check the county recorder's website for page size, margin, preparer and return-address rules.
5. Pay the recording fee, which the county sets and may update.
6. Ask for a stamped copy or a recording receipt with a document number.
7. Look up the document in the county index a few weeks later and save a copy.
8. Tell the beneficiary where the paper is stored. The Act does not require telling them, but families benefit from knowing.

## What do recorders reject?

Recorders look at form, not at your intent. These are common causes of a rejected filing.

| Problem | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Missing or wrong legal description | Copied from a tax bill or a street address | Copy it from the deed in your name |
| Missing parcel number | The county requires it | Add it from the tax bill |
| Notary block incomplete | Seal or date left off | Redo with the notary |
| Unclear who the preparer is | County requires a name and address | Add preparer information |
| Document is the wrong type | A deed was filed when a TODI was meant, or the reverse | Prepare the correct instrument |
| Fee not paid or wrong | Fee schedule changed | Call the office |

A recorder may accept a flawed document and later a title company finds the problem. Acceptance by the recorder is not proof that the TODI is valid, so have a lawyer check it.

## What if the property is in more than one county?

Record a separate TODI in each county, using that county's parcel and legal description. Land in another state needs that state's rules, because a recorded Illinois instrument does not control property elsewhere. Our article on [an Illinois resident who owns property in another state](/learn/illinois/illinois-resident-owning-property-in-another-state) explains the options.

## When do you need to record something new?

Record again if you sell the property, since the buyer's deed replaces the TODI. Record a new instrument or a recorded revocation if you want to change the beneficiary (755 ILCS 27/55). Record a corrected instrument if you find an error in the legal description. A will cannot undo the TODI, so do not rely on a will to fix a recording mistake.

If you refinance, tell the lender about the TODI. A mortgage still attaches to the property, and the beneficiary takes subject to it. Our article on [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) explains.

## How do you confirm the TODI is working?

Order a copy of the recorded document, and ask a title company or lawyer to run a search of the property. Then store the recorded copy with your will and your other papers. After the owner dies, the beneficiary follows the steps in our article on the [notice of death affidavit for an Illinois TODI](/learn/illinois/notice-of-death-affidavit-for-an-illinois-todi). Pair the TODI with a review of your beneficiary forms using the [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit).

## What should you do with the paperwork afterward?

Treat the recorded copy like a deed. Keep it in a fireproof place with your will, your power of attorney and your list of accounts. Tell the person you trust most where it is, even if you do not tell the beneficiary what it says.

Add three items to a short file for your family.

- The recorded document number and the date it was recorded, written on a one-page cover sheet.
- The name and phone number of the lawyer or title company that prepared it.
- The county's online record search address, so someone can confirm the filing years later.

Later, if the property changes, update the file. A new loan, a sale of part of the land, or a divorce can each make the recorded instrument out of date. Our article on [how divorce affects your Illinois estate plan](/learn/illinois/how-divorce-affects-your-illinois-estate-plan) explains why a recorded TODI does not fix itself when a marriage ends. Put a reminder on your calendar to review the instrument every few years, just as you would a beneficiary form.
