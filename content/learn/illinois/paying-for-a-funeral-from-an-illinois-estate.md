Someone has to sign the funeral home contract the day after a death, long before a court appoints anyone. In Illinois that person is usually a spouse or child who has no idea whether the estate will ever repay them. It generally does, and the funeral bill sits at the front of the line, but how you document it decides how smooth the repayment is.

This page covers the Illinois side: who is responsible, where a funeral bill ranks among the estate's debts, and the steps for being repaid. For the national overview of prices, the FTC's rules and veterans' benefits, see [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral). It belongs with our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Who pays for a funeral in Illinois?

Whoever signs for it, to begin with. The funeral home looks to the person who arranges the service, and the Disposition of Remains Act says who has the right to do that. The people with the right also carry the responsibility for reasonable costs, as our page on the [Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act) explains. The order runs from a person named in a written instrument, to the executor following the will, then the spouse, adult children, parents and nearer kin.

A named executor has one early power. Before the court issues letters of office, the executor can carry out burial arrangements and pay necessary funeral charges (755 ILCS 5/6-14). It is a narrow power, and it is one reason the person who signs and the person who ends up as executor are often the same.

If relatives disagree about who decides, see [who decides funeral arrangements when family disagrees](/learn/after-a-death/who-decides-funeral-arrangements-when-family-disagrees) before anyone signs.

## Where does a funeral bill rank among the estate's debts?

At the top. Section 18-10 of the Probate Act sorts claims into classes, and class 1 holds funeral and burial expenses, expenses of administration and certain custodial claims. Two details are worth knowing:

- Funeral and burial expenses count as such even when paid by someone other than the estate. The statute says expenses paid by any person, including a surviving spouse, are funeral and burial expenses (755 ILCS 5/18-10).
- The class covers a burial space, crypt or niche, a marker and upkeep of the space, in reasonable amounts.

Here is where class 1 sits in the whole order, using the table on our [credit card debt page](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois):

| Class | What it covers |
|---|---|
| 1 | Funeral and burial expenses, administration costs, certain custodial claims |
| 2 | The surviving spouse's or child's award |
| 3 | Debts owed to the United States |
| 4 | Final-year medical bills, plus capped recent wages |
| 5 | Untraceable trust money |
| 6 | Debts owed to the State and local governments |
| 7 | Everything else, including credit cards |

Funeral costs share class 1 with the lawyer's fee and court costs. The statute does not cap the amount, but the reasonableness standard applies to the burial space and marker, and an executor can push back on an expense that is out of proportion to the estate.

## How do you get repaid if you paid the funeral?

Treat it like any expense you plan to recover. Here are the steps.

1. Keep the signed contract, the itemized price list and proof of payment. Pay in a way that leaves a record.
2. Tell the other family members what you spent, in writing, before the estate is opened.
3. Once an executor has letters, hand over the paperwork and ask in writing how and when you will be repaid.
4. If you want the record to be airtight, file a written claim. A claim can go to the executor, the court or both, must be in writing and must say enough to identify what it is for (755 ILCS 5/18-1 and 18-2). Our page on [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) explains the deadlines.
5. Expect to wait. The executor will not have estate money until letters issue and assets are collected, and repayment may come from the first cash in the estate account.

An executor can reimburse a family member from the estate, and the payment is an allowed claim, so the executor should note it in the accounting. When the executor is also the person who paid, the paper trail matters more, since the other heirs will read it.

## What if the estate is not enough?

Then order matters, and so does restraint. Consider Elena, whose mother Rosa left a checking account of $9,200 in her sole name, a car worth $5,000 and a credit card balance of $7,400. Elena paid $8,600 for the funeral with her own card, and the estate's attorney and court costs came to $2,400. These figures are invented for illustration.

The estate assets total about $14,200. Class 1 claims total $11,000, so they are paid in full. What remains, about $3,200, goes to the next classes in order, so a final-year medical bill would be paid before the credit card company in class 7. Elena gets her funeral money back because her claim ranks ahead of the card, and the card issuer is the one left short. She cannot be billed for her mother's card in her own name unless she co-signed. That point is explained in [credit card debt after death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois).

If class 1 alone exceeds the estate, the executor should get advice before paying anyone. Our attorneys see this go wrong when a well-meaning executor reimburses the family first, then discovers the lawyer's fees are also class 1.

## What if there is no probate estate?

Then repayment depends on what exists. Most Illinois families have one of three situations:

- **A small estate affidavit.** When the personal estate is within the limit, the person who signs the affidavit pays valid debts in the statutory order before giving anything to heirs. See the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit). Funeral bills belong at the front.
- **Accounts with a beneficiary.** Life insurance and payable-on-death accounts go straight to the beneficiary. The beneficiary is not required to pay the funeral from them, but many families agree to. See [Illinois payable-on-death and joint accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts).
- **A wrongful death case.** The family usually pays first, and the lawsuit may take years. Read about [wrongful death and survival claims in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-wrongful-death-and-survival-claims-for-an-estate) before counting on that money.

## Mistakes our attorneys see

- Signing the contract before checking whether the person left a prepaid plan or written funeral instructions.
- Paying cash with no receipt, then being unable to prove it to the other heirs.
- Mixing the funeral bill with travel, flowers and a large reception, then arguing about what is reasonable.
- Reimbursing the family from the estate before the claims deadline, then learning the estate cannot cover higher-class debts.

The single most useful habit is to write down the plan before the family splits up. Planning your own funeral takes the guesswork away. See [planning your own funeral in advance](/learn/basics/plan-your-own-funeral-in-advance).
