You are writing your Illinois will, and the executor line is blank. Your oldest daughter lives in Texas. Your brother is down the street but is 79. A friend offered, and she has a felony from twenty years ago. Who is even allowed?

The law is more permissive than most people expect, and the practical limits are tighter. This page covers who qualifies under the Illinois Probate Act, how an executor who lives elsewhere is treated, and how to name backups so the court never has to pick. If you are on the receiving end, having just been named, start with [can you refuse to be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/can-you-refuse-to-be-executor-in-illinois), which owns the 30-day decision. The wider picture is in our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## What are the legal requirements to be an executor in Illinois?

Section 6-13 of the Probate Act (755 ILCS 5/6-13) sets them. In plain terms, the person must clear five tests.

| Test | What it means for your choice |
|---|---|
| Age 18 or older | Your 17-year-old cannot serve, even if you name them. |
| Resident of the United States | Living in Illinois is not required. |
| Not of unsound mind | Includes a person adjudged to have a disability under the Act. |
| Not currently in state or federal prison | Checked when the person asks to serve. |
| No felony conviction | Subject to the exception below. |

That is the entire list. Nothing in 6-13 asks for a degree, a clean credit report or a family tie. Your choice can be a friend, a neighbor or a cousin.

## Can someone with a felony conviction be an executor?

Rarely, and only if you plan for it. A felony conviction disqualifies. Section 6-13 makes one exception, and all of these must be true. The will names them. The will states you knew of the conviction before you signed. The law does not bar them from receiving a share of the estate. And they have no conviction for financial exploitation of an older or disabled person, financial identity theft or a similar crime in any state or federal court. They must also meet the other tests.

The second condition is where DIY wills fail. A clause that says only "I name Greg as my executor" does not do it. The will has to acknowledge the conviction. If you want Greg, say so in the will and have an attorney check the wording. Otherwise, name someone else and let Greg be a gifts-only friend.

## Can an executor live outside Illinois?

Yes. A resident of Oregon can be the executor of a Chicago estate. The Probate Act asks for United States residence only. The catch is the bond. For a non-resident executor, the court may require a bond in the amount and with the surety it sets, even if your will says no bond is needed. See [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) for how bond amounts are set.

If you want your out-of-state daughter to serve, you have three choices. Accept the possible bond cost, which comes out of the estate. Name her as executor and a local friend as co-executor. Or name the local person first and your daughter as backup. A distant executor can serve well when the estate is mostly cash and accounts. A house that needs a key, a roof inspection and a dumpster is different. Distance causes more problems than the statute does.

## Should the executor also be a beneficiary?

Often yes, and it is usually fine. A spouse who inherits everything is the natural executor in most families. Trouble comes when a child is both executor and heir in a family that does not trust each other, because every choice the child makes looks like a choice for the child. Our article on [an executor who is also a beneficiary](/learn/probate/executor-who-is-also-a-beneficiary) walks through the conflicts and the safeguards.

One safeguard is to name a professional or a neutral person as co-executor. Another is to keep the will simple, so there is little to dispute. Our story about an [overwhelmed executor](/learn/what-if/executor-overwhelmed-or-unable-to-serve) shows how quickly the job can outgrow one person.

## How many backups should you name?

Name two. One backup covers the executor who declines, dies or gets sick. A second covers the unlucky case where both do. After that you are naming people who may be unable to serve by the time it matters.

Write the backups as an ordered list in the will, with a sentence that each one serves only if the one before cannot. Do not write "my children" as one group without a person in front, because the court cannot tell which child is up first. If your first choice is a spouse, remember the spouse is likely to be older, and the backup is the person who will really do the work.

Example only: Walter, 74, names his wife Joan as executor, his daughter Lena as first alternate, and his son Paul as second alternate. Joan dies before him. When Walter dies, Lena has 30 days to start or decline. She declines because of a new job. Paul takes over without a hearing about who should serve. If Walter had named only Joan, the court would have had to appoint an administrator with the will annexed, and the family would have waited on a petition, notice and bond. The [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) shows where those steps fall.

## Should you waive the bond in your will?

You can excuse the bond, but the court does not have to honor it. Section 12-4 of the Probate Act lets a will excuse a bond or set a maximum amount, though the court can still require security if it suspects fraud or incompetence or believes the estate may not cover its claims, and it can require a bond from a non-resident executor. For a trusted spouse or child who lives in Illinois, a waiver saves the estate a premium. For an out-of-state executor, do not count on it.

## What happens if no valid executor is left?

The court appoints someone. When the will's executor and backups cannot or will not serve, the usual route is letters of administration with the will annexed, and the Probate Act sets out who has the right to ask for the job. That person must follow your will, but you did not choose them. Our article on [what happens if there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) walks through the process. The national guide on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and the page on [when your named executor cannot serve](/learn/wills/when-your-named-executor-cannot-serve) cover the same ground from the planner's side.

## A short checklist before you sign

- Is the first choice 18 or older, a U.S. resident, and free of a felony conviction or a disability finding?
- Does the person live close enough to handle a house and a bank?
- Have you asked them, and have they said yes?
- Are two backups named in order?
- Does the will address the bond?
