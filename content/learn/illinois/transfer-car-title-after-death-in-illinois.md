You have the keys, the car is in the driveway, and the title still says your mother's name. In Illinois the title belongs to whoever the Secretary of State will accept as the new owner, and that depends on how the car was titled and whether a court has appointed anyone to act for the estate.

This page walks through the usual routes, the documents that go with the application, and the mistakes that slow families down. It is general information, and the Secretary of State's current forms and checklist control the details, so confirm them before you go. For the wider picture of what to do after a death, start with our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## Who is allowed to take title to a car after someone dies?

Three situations cover most families. Which one you are in decides everything else.

| How the car was titled | Who usually takes it | Court needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Two owners with survivorship | The surviving owner | No |
| One owner, small estate, heirs agree | The heir who signs a small estate affidavit | No |
| One owner, estate in probate | The executor or administrator, then the person the will or law names | Yes, already open |

Co-owners on an Illinois certificate of title generally have survivorship unless they chose otherwise, which our page on [joint tenancy in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains. A surviving co-owner brings the death certificate and the title and applies to put the car in the survivor's name alone. The wording on the face of the title matters, so read it before you assume.

A car in one name passes under the will, or to the heirs under [Illinois intestacy rules](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) if there is no will. Someone must prove that. That proof comes from letters of office issued by the probate court, or from a sworn affidavit under the small estate statute.

## Can I use the small estate affidavit for a car?

Often yes. Under the current version of 755 ILCS 5/25-1, the affidavit covers personal property up to $150,000, and registered motor vehicles are not counted toward that cap. Our guide to the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) lays out who may sign it and the conditions: no letters of office outstanding, no petition pending, and the signer answers to creditors and heirs for what they do.

Here is an example. Denise, 52, lives in Peoria. Her father Tom died with a 2019 pickup titled only to him, a checking account of about $30,000 and no house. There is no will problem and her brother agrees she should take the truck. Because the estate is well inside the cap and nobody has opened probate, Denise can sign the affidavit and take it to the Secretary of State with the title. Her brother gets half the account, not half the truck, and they settle that between them in writing. The affidavit does not make that fair. It only makes it possible.

The affidavit has a price: the person who signs is on the hook if a valid creditor or another heir shows up later. If there is any doubt about debts or about who the heirs are, a short probate case protects you better.

## What do I bring to the Secretary of State?

Expect to assemble a packet before you stand in line. The usual contents, in the order most people gather them:

1. A certified copy of the death certificate. See our page on [how many death certificates to order](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need), because the Secretary of State will want its own.
2. The Illinois certificate of title, signed where the form asks. If the title is lost, the heir generally applies for a duplicate first.
3. The Application for Vehicle Transaction(s), which the Secretary of State publishes as form VSD 190. Check ilsos.gov for the current version.
4. Proof of authority: letters of office with a recent certification date, a signed small estate affidavit, or the survivorship title itself.
5. The odometer reading, since a title transfer records mileage.
6. Photo identification for the person applying, and payment of the title fee. The fee schedule changes, so read the current one on ilsos.gov rather than a number from a forum post.

Letters of office are the item families most often get wrong. If the court has appointed an executor or administrator, certified letters tell the Secretary of State who may sign. Our page on [letters of administration and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) explains how those are issued and why the certification date matters.

## What if there is a loan on the car?

The lender holds the lien until it is paid, and a lien does not disappear at death. The heir has three practical choices: keep making payments and refinance or assume the loan in the heir's own name later, pay off the balance and get a lien release, or sell the car and pay the lender from the proceeds. Which one is cheaper depends on the loan rate and the car's value.

A loan is also not a reason to stop. The lender wants to be paid, not to repossess a car in a probate dispute. Call it, report the death, and ask what documents it needs. The broader rules on debts that survive a death are in our page on [mortgages, car loans and student loans after death](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-car-loan-and-student-loans-after-death). Heirs are generally not personally responsible for the dead person's loan, but they cannot keep the collateral without dealing with it.

## What if the title is lost or the car is titled in another state?

A lost title is common, because the paper often sat in a desk drawer that nobody can find. The person with authority applies for a duplicate before the transfer, and the lender holds the title if there is a loan, so ask the lender first. If the car carries another state's title, that state's rules govern the title itself, and an Illinois resident heir usually deals with that motor vehicle agency before registering the car here. Bring the same proof of authority either way.

Time matters less than people fear, and more than they hope. Nobody is penalized in the first weeks for grieving. But a car left in limbo for a year can lose its insurance, collect parking tickets, and lose value while the family argues.

## What are the common mistakes?

Our attorneys see the same few stumbles.

- **Waiting.** Months pass, the registration lapses, and the insurer asks who owns the car. Start with the paperwork even if you do not plan to keep the car.
- **Signing the title in the dead person's name.** Never sign for someone who has died. Sign only in the capacity that gives you authority, such as heir under the affidavit or executor.
- **Gifting the car informally.** A verbal promise to a grandchild does not change a title, and other heirs may later object. Put it in the paperwork.
- **Ignoring other heirs.** One sibling taking the car for "the family" without writing it down is a classic setup for a fight over the rest of the estate. Our page on [what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) lists what else is open at the same time.
- **Treating a trust as automatic.** If the dead person put the car in a living trust, the successor trustee transfers it, not the heirs. Confirm what the title actually says.
