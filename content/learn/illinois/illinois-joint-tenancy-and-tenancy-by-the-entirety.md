Most people who own a house "together" assume the survivor simply keeps it. In Illinois, that is not what the law presumes. A deed that names two owners with no further wording makes them tenants in common, and each share goes wherever that owner's will, or the intestacy statute, sends it. This page covers what a deed has to say to create survivorship, how married couples can hold their home as tenants by the entirety, and where each form can go wrong.

It is general education, not legal advice. For the state-wide picture, start with our [Illinois estate planning guide](/estate-planning/illinois) and the [Illinois pillar page](/learn/illinois).

## What is the default when a deed names two owners?

The default is a tenancy in common. The Illinois Joint Tenancy Act says that an interest in land granted or conveyed to two or more people is a tenancy in common unless the instrument expressly declares a joint tenancy. The statutory phrase is "not in tenancy in common but in joint tenancy" (765 ILCS 1005/1). The Act sets this default aside for estates held by executors and trustees.

A tenant in common owns an undivided share, may leave it by will, and can sell it without the other owner's signature. When that owner dies, the share is part of the [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate). If there is no will, it passes to the heirs under the rules in our article on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois).

Here is how that plays out. Maria and her brother Tomas inherit their mother's bungalow in Oak Park and the deed to them reads "Maria Alvarez and Tomas Alvarez." Tomas dies, unmarried, with two children. Maria does not become sole owner. His half goes through his estate to his children, and Maria now co-owns a house with nieces and nephews.

## How do you create a joint tenancy with survivorship?

You say so in the deed, in express words. When the instrument declares a joint tenancy, the survivor takes the whole property at the first owner's death without probate, and a will cannot redirect the share.

A short checklist for a deed that is meant to carry survivorship:

1. Name every owner exactly as they appear on their ID.
2. Include the words "not in tenancy in common but in joint tenancy," or language that plainly says the same thing.
3. Have all grantors sign and acknowledge the deed, and record it with the county recorder or registrar where the land is located.
4. Keep a copy, and tell your estate planning attorney, because the deed overrides what your will says about that parcel.

The comparison below puts the three common forms side by side.

| Feature | Tenancy in common | Joint tenancy | Tenancy by the entirety |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who can hold it | Anyone | Anyone | Married couples, homestead only |
| Survivorship | No | Yes, if the deed says so | Yes |
| Share passes by will | Yes | No | No |
| Needs express wording | No, it is the default | Yes | Yes |
| Effect of divorce | None | None | Becomes a tenancy in common |
| Both must sign a sale | No, each can sell a share | No, each can sell a share | Yes |

## What is tenancy by the entirety in Illinois?

It is a way for a husband and wife to hold their homestead as one unit. The deed must expressly say the spouses hold as tenants by the entirety. Illinois allows this for a homestead held by the couple directly, for a beneficial interest in a land trust, and for a homestead held by the trustee of a revocable trust where the married settlors are the primary beneficiaries and the deed says so (765 ILCS 1005/1c).

Three features make it different from a plain joint tenancy.

- **Both signatures are required.** A deed, contract for deed, mortgage or lease of entireties homestead property is effective only if both tenants sign it. Neither spouse can quietly borrow against the house.
- **The estate lasts only while the couple is married.** If the marriage is dissolved or declared invalid, the estate converts to a tenancy in common unless the court orders otherwise. The spouses can also agree otherwise in writing.
- **The survivor takes everything.** When one spouse dies, the other keeps the whole estate.

Two small points often surprise people. If the spouses later move and set up another homestead together, the old entireties estate does not follow them; the statute turns it into a joint tenancy.

Do not read more into this than the statute says. This page does not cover how entireties ownership interacts with a creditor of only one spouse; that is a question for an attorney who can look at the debt and the deed together.

## Does survivorship work for bank accounts, stocks and cars?

Yes, but through a different section of the same Act. Section 2 of the Joint Tenancy Act abolishes survivorship for joint tenancies in personal property in general, then preserves it in listed situations (765 ILCS 1005/2). The ones that matter to families:

- **Bank accounts.** A deposit in two or more names may be paid to any of them, even after another has died, and a signed agreement with the bank protects the bank when it does. Our article on [Illinois payable-on-death and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) goes deeper.
- **Securities.** Stocks or bonds registered to two or more people as joint tenants with survivorship can be transferred to the survivor without the issuer checking any will.
- **Credit union and savings shares.** Shares issued in joint names can be paid to any of the named persons.
- **Vehicles.** Co-owners on a Secretary of State certificate of title have survivorship unless they elect otherwise.

## What are the risks of holding property jointly?

The main risk is that the deed controls, not your plan. Suppose Helen, 74, adds her son Marcus to the deed of her home "to avoid probate." If the deed creates a joint tenancy, Marcus now has a present ownership interest. His lenders, his divorce and his own mistakes can reach it. If Helen later wants to sell, she needs his signature. And if Helen has two children and wants them to inherit equally, Marcus takes the whole house at her death while her daughter takes nothing from it.

Joint ownership also affects the income tax basis a child receives and can matter for Medicaid planning, so we discuss it case by case. Our general article on [the risks of joint tenancy](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) walks through these, and [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) shows where jointly held property sits in the bigger picture.

For many Illinois families, a [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) or a [living trust](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) gives the same probate-free result without handing a child ownership today.

## What should you do if you already own a house with someone?

Pull the recorded deed. You can often get a copy from the county recorder's office, and the wording will tell you which form you hold. Then:

1. Read the granting clause for the survivorship wording or the words "tenants by the entirety."
2. Compare it to what you want to happen at death. Many people find their deed says something different from what they assumed.
3. If it does not match, talk to an attorney about a corrective deed, a trust or a TODI.
4. After a divorce, check the deed again. An entireties estate has already converted, and the old plan may not fit.
