A sudden illness can leave you unable to speak for yourself. Doctors will turn to someone to make decisions, and your wishes about life support and treatment may be unknown. Illinois gives you three main tools to prevent that: the health care power of attorney, the living will, and the POLST.

Each one does a different job and has its own signing rules. This guide explains all three so you can see how they fit together. It is general education, not legal advice. Our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) covers the rest of your plan.

## What does an Illinois health care power of attorney do?

It names an agent to make health care decisions for you when you cannot make them yourself. The Illinois Power of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45, Article IV) governs the statutory short form. Your agent can consent to or refuse treatment, including life-sustaining treatment, and can get your medical records.

The form is separate from the [property power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property). The property form handles money. The health care form handles medical choices. You usually need both. For a national overview, see our article on the [health care power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## How do you sign it? Witness and notary rules

Sign the form in front of one witness, who also signs. The statute says no notary is needed for the statutory form (755 ILCS 45/4-10). Follow these steps.

1. Pick an agent and at least one successor agent who are 18 or older.
2. Read the form's choices on life-sustaining treatment and fill them in.
3. Add any limits or instructions in the space provided.
4. Sign in front of one adult witness.
5. Have the witness sign.
6. Give copies to your agent, your doctors, and your hospital.

### Who cannot be the witness?

The witness must be 18 or older. The statute excludes your attending physician and certain other providers, such as an advanced practice registered nurse, physician assistant, dentist, podiatric physician, optometrist, or psychologist. It also excludes the owner or operator of a health care facility where you live, and relatives of those people. Your agent and successor agent cannot witness, and neither can certain relatives of you or your agent (755 ILCS 45/4-5.1).

A common mistake is asking the agent or an adult child to sign as witness. Choose a neutral adult instead.

### Who cannot be the agent?

The attending physician and other health care providers who treat you cannot serve as your agent (755 ILCS 45/4-5). A licensed professional who is not caring for you can serve, such as a nurse who is also a close friend.

## What is the difference between the three documents?

| Document | What it does | Signing rule |
|---|---|---|
| Health care power of attorney | Names someone to decide for you | One adult witness; no notary |
| Living will | States your wishes about death-delaying treatment in a terminal condition | Two adult witnesses |
| POLST | A medical order for current treatment preferences | Patient or representative and a practitioner sign |

The Illinois Living Will Act defines a terminal condition as an incurable and irreversible condition where death is imminent and death-delaying procedures only prolong dying. A living will speaks only in that setting. A health care power of attorney reaches more situations, such as a stroke where recovery is uncertain.

## What is a POLST, and who needs one?

The Illinois Department of Public Health describes the POLST as a practitioner's order that reflects your wishes about treatments such as CPR. Because it is a medical order, a qualified practitioner must sign it along with you or your legal representative. Providers must honor a validly completed form. No one can require a POLST as a condition of treatment.

A POLST usually suits people with a serious illness or frailty, and people near the end of life. Healthy adults often rely on the power of attorney and living will instead. Our article on [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) goes deeper.

## Can I change or cancel my health care power of attorney?

Yes, while you have capacity. You may destroy the document, sign a written revocation, or state your revocation aloud in front of a witness age 18 or older (755 ILCS 45/4-6). The form also offers an option to delay a revocation for 30 days, which some people use to guard against a rash change. Tell your doctors and your agent, and collect old copies.

Imagine Carlos, 66, who names his sister Elena as agent. After a falling out he names his daughter instead. He signs a new form, writes a revocation of the old one, and sends both to his clinic.

## What happens if you have no health care power of attorney?

Doctors may turn to a family member under state surrogate rules, but the order of who decides may not match what you would choose. If family members disagree, or no one is available, someone may need to ask a court to appoint a guardian. Read our overview of [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) to see how that works. Putting your choices in writing now spares your family that step.

Talk with your agent about your values before you sign. Our [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) article explains a related form that lets providers share your records.
