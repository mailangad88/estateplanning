Most parents have lain awake with one question: if something happened to both of us, who would raise our kids? The honest answer in Illinois is that it depends on a piece of paper you may not have signed yet. If you have named a guardian in a valid will, the court starts with your choice. If you have not, the decision goes to strangers in a courtroom.

This page explains how Illinois handles that choice, what happens without a will, how to decide when you and your spouse see it differently, and what to do with the money. You can [book a consultation](/book?from=learn-naming-a-guardian-for-your-children&topic=kids) once you have a few names in mind. It is general education, not legal advice. For the wider plan, see our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois).

## How do you name a guardian for your children in Illinois?

You name a guardian in writing. A parent can designate a guardian for a minor child in a will or another signed paper, with at least two credible witnesses (755 ILCS 5/11-5). Most parents put it in the will, because that is the document a court looks for first after a death.

A guardian has to meet basic qualifications. Under the Probate Act the person must be at least 18, live in the United States and be of sound mind, among other things (755 ILCS 5/11-3). A child who is 14 or older may also nominate a guardian for the court to consider (755 ILCS 5/11-5). The judge still decides what is best for the child.

A good clause names a first choice and at least one backup. It says who is guardian of the child's person, which means day-to-day care. It can also say who handles the child's property if you want that to be someone else. Our [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) page shows how to sign so the will holds up, and [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) covers the court side.

## What happens to my kids if I die without naming a guardian?

If both parents die and no guardian is named, nobody has legal authority over the children. A relative or another interested adult has to file a petition with the circuit court, and the judge decides what serves the children's best interest.

Think about how that plays out. Nadia and Tom live in Naperville with two children, ages 4 and 7. Both die in an accident with no will. Nadia's sister in Denver and Tom's brother in Aurora each want the children. Both are loving. Both are willing. The court has no instruction from the parents, so a judge hears two petitions and picks one. The children wait, and the family fights while grieving.

A named guardian does not rule out a dispute. But it changes the starting point from "who wants the children" to "who did the parents choose." That is a far calmer place to begin.

If only one parent dies, the surviving parent generally continues to have authority over the children. That is why a guardian clause matters most for the case where both parents are gone, or where the surviving parent cannot act. Our general article on [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) walks through that.

## How do you choose the right guardian?

Start with what you want your children's life to look like, not with a list of relatives. Parents who skip that step end up choosing the person who would be most offended to be left out.

Here is a ten-minute way to narrow it down.

1. Write down the three things that matter most to you. Common ones are shared values, closeness to the child's school and friends, and energy for raising kids.
2. List up to three candidates. Do not limit yourself to family.
3. Score each person from 1 to 5 on those three things, plus age and health, and plus how they handle money.
4. Compare scores, then ask where the choice feels wrong even when the score looks right.
5. Ask the top two whether they would say yes, and what they would need to say yes.

### Should the guardian be a grandparent?

Maybe, but think about the next 15 years rather than the next one. A grandparent in their late sixties may be ideal now and unable to keep up when the children are in high school. Many parents name a grandparent as a first choice for the early years and a younger sibling or friend as backup.

## What if my spouse and I disagree about who should be the guardian?

You are not the only couple. This is the most common reason the guardian clause stays blank for years.

Disagreement is usually about one of two things: values (his brother is stricter than we are) or logistics (her sister is great but lives eight hours away). Naming the real reason helps. If you can each state what you are worried about, you can often find a third person who answers both.

Some ways couples get unstuck:

- **Score separately, then compare.** Each person fills in the same grid alone, so one voice does not drown the other.
- **Name a shared backup.** You may never agree on the first choice, but both of you may accept a second person.
- **Set a review date.** Agree to pick the strongest available option now and look again after a year or after the next child.
- **Separate the roles.** One person can be guardian of the child's person, and a different person can manage the money. See [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee).

A choice that is 80 percent right and signed beats a perfect choice that never gets written. The point of a will is that it works on the worst day, not that it settles every family debate.

## Can the guardian also handle the money?

They can, but it is often better if they do not. A guardian of the person raises the child. The inheritance, life insurance and other assets need a manager, and a minor cannot hold large sums outright. In Illinois a minor's money is usually handled either through a court-supervised guardian of the estate, a custodial account under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, or a trust. See [when a minor inherits money in Illinois](/learn/illinois/when-a-minor-inherits-money-in-illinois) and [UTMA accounts and gifts to minors](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors).

Many parents use a trust created in the will for the children. The trustee pays for the child's needs and the guardian asks for funds. That gives a check on spending and keeps a court out of the money. The [testamentary trust for minor children](/learn/guardianship/testamentary-trust-for-minor-children) article describes how it works.

Also think about whether your guardian could afford to take in your children. Life insurance is often the tool parents use here, and the [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) gives a starting figure.

## What about the time before you die?

A will's guardian clause says nothing until your death. If you face a surgery, a deployment or a serious diagnosis, Illinois has separate papers for the gap. Read [standby and short-term guardians for children in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-standby-and-short-term-guardians-for-children) so you know which one fits.

## What goes wrong most often?

- **Naming someone without asking.** A person who finds out at the funeral may say no.
- **Naming only one person.** If that person divorces, moves or dies, the clause has nothing to fall back on.
- **Leaving the will at "someday."** A will that is not signed and witnessed does not count.
- **Forgetting new children.** Add the new child and check whether the clause covers them.
- **Never telling the guardian what you want.** A short [letter of intent for your children's guardian](/learn/guardianship/letter-of-intent-for-your-childrens-guardian) shares routines, schools and values. You can start from the [letter template](/free/letter-to-your-childrens-guardian-template).
