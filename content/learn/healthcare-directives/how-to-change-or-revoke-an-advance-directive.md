Your agent was your brother, and then you two stopped speaking. Or you signed a living will years ago and since then watched a parent die the way you hope you never will. Your views shifted. Does the paper have to shift with you, and how do you change it without leaving a mess behind?

You can change it, and it is easier than most people expect. The harder part is making sure the new version is the one a nurse finds. This article is part of our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## The short version

- Only you can change or revoke your directive, and only while you have capacity.
- Sign a new, dated document that expressly replaces the old one.
- Tell your agent, your doctors and your hospital, and take back the old copies.
- Revisit it after marriage, divorce, a move, a diagnosis or the death of your agent.
- If you have lost capacity, the existing directive stays in force as written.

## Can you change or revoke an advance directive?

Yes. A directive is your voice, and you remain free to change what you say for as long as you can form and express a decision. No court, doctor or family member has to approve the change. The glossary definition of an [advance directive](/glossary/advance-directive) makes the same point: it is a document you control.

There is one limit. The right belongs to the person with capacity. If dementia or injury has taken that away, the document is frozen as signed, and neither your agent nor your relatives may redraft it. That is a strong reason to look at your documents earlier rather than later, particularly when a diagnosis is on the horizon. Our article on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) covers how capacity is assessed.

## How do you revoke an advance directive?

State laws list several accepted ways, and most states allow more than one. These are the common ones.

1. **Sign a new directive.** The new document replaces the old to the extent they conflict. Say so plainly: "I revoke all prior advance directives."
2. **Sign a written revocation.** A short, dated statement that you cancel a specific document. It should name the document and the agent.
3. **Destroy the document.** Tearing, burning or deleting with the intent to cancel works in many states. It only affects the copy you destroyed.
4. **Tell your provider.** In most states an oral statement to your doctor or other provider that you revoke counts, if it is recorded or witnessed.

Choose the first or second. They leave a paper trail, and they are hard to misread. The oral route is real but fragile. Imagine Hal, who tells a nurse during a hospital stay, "I don't want my ex-wife making my decisions." If the nurse writes it down, it may be honored. If not, the old directive naming his ex-wife still sits in the chart.

## When should you update your directive?

Review it on a schedule and after events. A five-year check is a fair habit, and these events should trigger one right away.

| Event | What to check |
|---|---|
| Marriage | Whether your spouse should now be agent, and whether your old agent knows |
| Divorce or separation | The ex-spouse's role; many states cancel it automatically, others do not |
| Agent dies, moves, becomes ill or falls out with you | Name a new agent and backup |
| New diagnosis | Whether your instructions match the actual treatments you may face |
| Change in beliefs or experience | End-of-life treatment preferences, feeding tubes, organ donation |
| Move to another state | Formal requirements, witness rules and forms; see [using a directive in another state](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states) |
| New doctor or hospital | New copies in the new record |

Divorce deserves a warning. Many states revoke a former spouse's appointment as agent when a divorce is final, yet some only do so for the period of a filing or not at all. Do not rely on the statute. Sign a new document naming someone else as soon as the case is filed.

## How do you make the change stick?

A new directive does its work only when it displaces the old one in the places that matter. Follow these steps.

1. **Write the new version.** Use your state's formalities for witnesses or a notary. If you are unsure, read about [signing requirements for a living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary).
2. **Include a revocation sentence.** State that it replaces all earlier directives and name the date of the one you are replacing, if you know it.
3. **Collect the old copies.** Ask your agent, your doctor's office, your hospital and any relative who holds one to return or discard them.
4. **Distribute the new copies.** Give one to your agent and backup, your primary doctor, each hospital you use and your attorney.
5. **Update your HIPAA release.** If the person who can see your records changed, a new [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) is needed, and the old release should be revoked in writing.
6. **Check your medical orders.** A DNR or POLST is a separate physician order. Changing your directive does not change it. Ask your clinician to update it if your wishes shifted. See [DNR and POLST orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).
7. **Record where it is.** Update the wallet card, phone note or state registry entry.

The same logic applies to wills, where a later document can displace an earlier one, as in [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils). Healthcare documents are easier to change because the stakes are shorter in time but the circulation is wider, and the stale copy is the usual villain.

## What should the new version say differently?

Updating is a chance to fix what experience has shown you. People who have sat at a bedside often want sharper language. A few examples of the kind of change we see:

- **Moving from general to specific.** "No extraordinary measures" becomes a plain sentence about ventilators, feeding tubes and dialysis, each with the condition in which you would refuse it.
- **Adding a time limit.** Some people ask for a trial of treatment, say a few days on a ventilator, and then reassessment if there is no improvement.
- **Naming comfort priorities.** Pain control, being at home, music, a particular chaplain or no visitors.
- **Releasing the agent from guilt.** A sentence telling your agent that you trust their judgment helps when the facts do not fit the page.

Talk the changes through with your agent before you sign. A new instruction your agent has never heard is more likely to be second-guessed under stress.

## What if you can no longer make changes?

If capacity is gone, the existing document governs. Your agent must follow its instructions and use judgment where it is silent. If the agent believes the document no longer fits a situation no one foresaw, the agent works with your doctors and, if needed, the hospital's ethics service. A court can interpret the document or replace an agent who is not acting properly. Our article on [who can overrule your healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive) describes those limits.

If you have a financial power of attorney too, the same capacity rule applies, and the document may end for other reasons, as covered in [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).

## How state law changes this

State law governs how revocation works. Points that vary:

- **Oral revocation.** Most states accept it, with differences in who must hear it or whether it must be documented.
- **Effect of divorce.** Some states cancel a former spouse's role automatically; others require action.
- **Formalities for the new document.** Witness counts, who may witness, and notary requirements differ.
- **Registries.** A few states keep an advance directive registry, and a new filing should replace the old one.

Your [state guide](/estate-planning) lists the rules where you live, and the [Illinois healthcare power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) article shows how one state handles the agent role.
