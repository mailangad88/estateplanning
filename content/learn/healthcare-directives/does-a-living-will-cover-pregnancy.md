A living will says what treatment you do and do not want if you are dying and cannot speak. Most people sign one without thinking about pregnancy. For a woman who could become pregnant, that paragraph matters, and it surprises people when they find it on the form.

The answer depends on the state. This page covers the Illinois and New Jersey rules in plain terms, explains what they mean for how you write your documents, and lists the conversations to have. It is general information, and a woman who is pregnant, trying to conceive or in a high-risk situation should talk with an attorney and her doctor about her own documents. Our [living will overview](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) covers the basics.

## What does Illinois say about a living will during pregnancy?

Illinois puts a limit in the statute itself. Under the Living Will Act (755 ILCS 35/3), the declaration of a qualified patient who has been diagnosed as pregnant by the attending physician is given no force and effect as long as, in the physician's opinion, the fetus could develop to the point of live birth. In other words, the living will is set aside while a live birth remains possible.

Three details are worth noting.

- The rule turns on the physician's opinion, not on the patient's earlier wording.
- It applies only while that opinion holds. If the pregnancy ends, or the physician concludes live birth is no longer possible, the declaration is no longer paused by this section.
- It sits in the Living Will Act, which covers a patient with an incurable, irreversible, terminal condition. It is part of how that one document works.

The Act also sets the signing rules: you sign, or someone signs at your direction, with two adult witnesses. Those witnesses cannot be entitled to part of your estate or responsible for paying your care. Our page on [the Illinois healthcare power of attorney, living will and POLST](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) explains how the documents fit.

## What does New Jersey say?

New Jersey takes a different approach. The statute on advance directives (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-56) says a female declarant may include in her advance directive what effect it shall have if she is pregnant. So the law does not set a fixed answer. It invites you to write one.

That gives you more control and more responsibility. A short phrase such as "honor this directive even if I am pregnant" or "do not apply this directive while I am pregnant" is a legal choice, and a vague line leaves staff and family guessing. Our summary of [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives) shows where the form asks about this and how the proxy and instruction directives work.

## How the two states compare

| Question | Illinois | New Jersey |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a built-in rule? | Yes, in the Living Will Act | No fixed rule; the declarant may state her wishes |
| When does it apply? | While the physician believes live birth is possible | As you write it |
| Who decides if it applies? | The attending physician | Your document, then the agent or care team |
| What can you change? | You cannot override the statute in a living will | You choose the effect |

## Does it matter if I have a healthcare agent instead?

It can. A living will speaks only to a narrow case: a terminal or irreversible condition, usually when death is near. A healthcare agent can speak to almost anything, including a sudden event during pregnancy, such as a car accident, that a living will might never reach. For most healthy women, the agent is the more useful document, and the living will is a supplement.

How a pregnancy limit interacts with an agent's authority is a legal question that depends on the statute and the facts. Our attorneys read the documents together and tell you where the gaps are. ## What should I do if I might become pregnant?

Treat it as a planning step rather than a worry. Four things help.

1. **Read the pregnancy paragraph** on any directive form before you sign it.
2. **Decide what you want**, in a sentence, and say it to your agent in plain words. Our list of [questions to answer before writing a living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/questions-to-answer-before-writing-a-living-will) helps you find the words.
3. **Tell your doctor** what you decided, and ask that your chart be updated.
4. **Revisit the form** when you plan a pregnancy, and again afterward. The steps are in [how to change or revoke an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive).

### Example

Lena, 33, is expecting her first child and has a living will from five years ago. She lives in Illinois. Her husband Omar asks what would happen if a complication left her unable to decide and unlikely to recover. The honest answer is that her living will would not be the controlling document while a live birth was possible. She and Omar sit down with an attorney, name Omar as her healthcare agent with her sister as the alternate, and write down what she would want in each stage of pregnancy. Lena also asks her obstetrician to put the new documents in her chart. None of this takes long. What it removes is the argument at the bedside.

## What should a pregnancy clause actually say?

Where the law lets you write your own, specifics beat slogans. A useful clause names the situation, the stage if it matters to you, and who decides when the facts are unclear. For example: "If I am pregnant when this directive would otherwise apply, I want my agent to decide, after talking with my obstetrician, and my agent knows my wishes." That sentence does not decide the question in advance, but it names the person who will.

Three mistakes show up in forms we see.

- **Leaving the box blank** on a form that has one, so staff have nothing to follow.
- **Copying a stranger's wording** from an online template that was written for another state.
- **Never telling the agent.** A clause the agent has not heard about is a surprise at the worst time.

## What about someone who cannot consent?

A pregnant patient who lacks capacity and has no document at all falls under the surrogate rules of her state. In Illinois, that is the Health Care Surrogate Act, covered in [who makes medical decisions in Illinois if you have no directive](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides). The surrogate is supposed to decide as she would have chosen, but the surrogate is guessing without anything written down. Our article on [what happens without an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive) explains how that goes for families.

## Do other states handle it the same way?

No. Some states give a living will no effect during pregnancy, some limit it to certain stages, some let you decide, and some say nothing. If you move, or divide your time between states, read the form of the state where you will be treated. The article on [whether advance directives work in other states](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states) explains what travels with you and what does not.

## How state law changes this

Besides the pregnancy rule, states differ on the number of witnesses, the use of a notary, the form's wording and how a living will interacts with an agent.
