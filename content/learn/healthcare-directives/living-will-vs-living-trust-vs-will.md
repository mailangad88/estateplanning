Three of the most common estate planning documents have almost the same name. A living will, a living trust and a last will and testament all sound like they belong to the same family, and plenty of people sign one believing it covers what another one does. It does not, and that mix-up is one of the most frequent gaps we see.

This article explains each document in plain terms, adds the related papers that complete the picture, and gives you a way to decide what you actually need. For the medical side in depth, see our [guide to advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What is the one-line difference between each document?

Each document answers a different question, which makes the quickest comparison a short list.

- **Will:** Who gets my property after I die, who manages the estate, and who raises my minor children?
- **Living trust:** Who manages and receives my property, starting now and continuing through incapacity and death, without probate?
- **Living will:** What medical treatment do I want or refuse if I am terminally ill or permanently unconscious?
- **Healthcare power of attorney:** Who speaks for me on medical matters when I cannot?
- **Financial power of attorney:** Who handles my money and bills if I cannot?

Notice that only two of these deal with property after death. The rest are about you, while you are alive.

## What does a will do?

A will is a document that takes effect only at death and directs how your probate property is distributed. It names an [executor](/glossary/executor) to carry out your wishes and, if you have minor children, names a guardian for them. It does not control assets that pass by beneficiary designation or joint ownership, and it generally must go through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) to be used.

A will does nothing while you are alive. If you have a stroke at 70, your will is not consulted. Someone else has to manage your affairs, and that is the job of the incapacity documents below. Our article on [will vs. trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) goes deeper on when each fits.

## What does a living trust do?

A [living trust](/glossary/living-trust), also called a revocable trust, is a legal arrangement that you create while alive. You transfer assets into it, usually serving as your own trustee, and you name a [successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) who takes over if you die or become incapacitated. At death, the trust assets go to the people you named, generally without probate.

Two points trip people up. First, a trust works only for assets that are actually retitled into it, a step called [funding](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust). Second, a trust does not make medical decisions. If you become incapacitated, your successor trustee can pay your bills from trust assets, but nobody can consent to surgery on your behalf unless you also have a healthcare power of attorney. Most trusts come with a short companion will, called a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will), to catch anything left out.

Maria, 62, put her house and brokerage account in a living trust but never signed medical documents. When she had a serious fall, her son could keep her mortgage paid through the trust, yet the hospital had to follow the state's default surrogate list to decide on her treatment. The trust did exactly what it should. It simply was never designed for that.

## What does a living will do?

A [living will](/glossary/living-will) is a written statement of your wishes about life-sustaining treatment, such as ventilators, feeding tubes, CPR and dialysis, if you are terminally ill or permanently unconscious. It usually applies only in narrow circumstances that doctors must certify, not whenever you are sick. It has nothing to do with property, and it has no effect after death.

Despite the name, a living will does not name anyone. It gives instructions that your medical team and your family are meant to follow. Because no document can foresee every situation, most people pair it with an agent. Our article on [what a living will should say](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) covers the wording.

"Advance directive" is the umbrella term. In some states a single form combines the living will and the agent appointment; in others they are separate documents. The glossary entry on [advance directives](/glossary/advance-directive) explains the range.

## Which related documents complete the picture?

A complete plan has more than these three papers. These are the others, and the ones people forget.

- **[Healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney)** (also called healthcare proxy or medical power of attorney): names the person who makes medical decisions when you cannot.
- **[Durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney):** names someone to manage your accounts, pay bills and file taxes. It ends at death.
- **[HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization):** lets named people see your medical records and talk to your doctors.
- **[POLST or DNR](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst):** actual medical orders, signed by a clinician, that emergency crews follow. They are for people who are seriously ill, not a substitute for a directive.

## How do the documents compare side by side?

| Document | Covers | When it applies | Who it names | Needs probate? | Lawyer needed? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Will | Property, guardians for minors | After death | Executor, guardian | Yes, to be used | Not required, but common |
| Living trust | Property you transfer in | Now, through incapacity and death | Successor trustee | No, for funded assets | Strongly recommended |
| Living will | Life-sustaining treatment wishes | If terminally ill or permanently unconscious | No one | No | Often a state form is enough |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Medical decisions | When you cannot decide | Healthcare agent | No | Often a state form is enough |
| Financial power of attorney | Money and property decisions | Immediately or on incapacity | Financial agent | No | Recommended |
| POLST | Specific medical orders | Now, for serious illness | No one | No | No, completed with a clinician |
| HIPAA authorization | Access to medical information | When signed | Recipients of information | No | No |

## Which documents does a typical person need?

It depends on your life, so treat this as a starting list and not advice.

1. **Every adult, including a young adult at 18:** healthcare power of attorney, HIPAA authorization, financial power of attorney.
2. **Add a living will** when you want to state your wishes about end-of-life treatment, which most adults benefit from.
3. **Parents of minor children:** a will naming a guardian, or a trust with a will.
4. **Homeowners and anyone with significant assets:** consider a living trust, especially if you want to avoid probate or own property in more than one state.
5. **Anyone seriously ill or frail:** ask your doctor whether a POLST fits your situation.

## What mistakes come up most often?

- **Assuming a will covers medical decisions.** It does not take effect until death.
- **Assuming a living will names a decision maker.** It does not, so name an agent separately.
- **Assuming a trust covers everything.** A trust never replaces medical documents, and unfunded trusts hold nothing.
- **Signing once and forgetting.** Documents signed years ago in another state may need updating after a move or a family change.
- **Keeping documents where no one can find them.** Give copies to your agent and doctor, as explained in our [pillar on directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## How do state rules affect these documents?

Each state sets its own execution rules, forms and default surrogate lists. Some states offer a single statutory advance directive form; others have separate forms. Wills and trusts also follow state formalities, which are covered in [what makes a will valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid). If you split time between states, ask an attorney whether your documents will be honored in both. Start with your state's page, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), to see local specifics.
