If a hospital social worker handed you a MOLST and you live in Illinois or New Jersey, you may be wondering why the form uses a name your state does not. Or you may have a parent in New York who has one, and now they are moving near you.

The short answer is that MOLST and POLST are two names for one tool. This page explains how the names differ, what Illinois and New Jersey actually use, and what to do with a form from somewhere else. It is general education, not legal advice.

## What does MOLST stand for, and what is the form?

MOLST stands for Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment. It is a brightly colored form that a clinician signs after a conversation with a patient who has serious illness or frailty. Typical boxes cover whether to attempt CPR, how much intervention to give if breathing or the heart fails, whether to go to a hospital, and whether to use a feeding tube. Because it is a doctor's order, an ambulance crew or nursing home can act on it right away, without calling a family member or hunting for a lawyer's document.

That immediate effect is what separates it from the papers in a lawyer's file. A [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) states your wishes for the future. A MOLST says what the care team should do now. Our general article on [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) draws the line in more detail.

## How is a MOLST different from a POLST?

Mostly in the name and the paperwork. The version below is simplified, because forms change and each state's department of health controls its own.

| Name | What it stands for | Where you might see it |
|---|---|---|
| POLST | Physician (or Provider) Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment | Illinois, New Jersey and many other states |
| MOLST | Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment | A smaller group, including New York, Massachusetts and Maryland |
| POST or MOST | Physician Orders for Scope of Treatment; Medical Orders for Scope of Treatment | A few states with their own naming |

Differences you might notice between a MOLST and a POLST:

- **Who may sign.** Some states allow only physicians; others add nurse practitioners and physician assistants.
- **Whether the patient's signature is required.** Some forms need the patient or representative to sign; others only require a clinician's signature after consent.
- **How the form is stored and transmitted.** A few states keep an electronic registry and others expect a paper copy on the refrigerator door.
- **Which choices are listed.** Boxes for antibiotics or artificial nutrition differ.

None of these differences changes the main point: the form comes out of a medical conversation and is honored by clinicians.

## What do Illinois and New Jersey use?

Both use POLST. In Illinois, the state health department publishes the POLST form, and hospitals, long-term care facilities and emergency crews must honor a qualifying form that is apparent and immediately available. Our article on [Illinois POLST and DNR orders](/learn/illinois/illinois-polst-and-dnr-orders) covers the signing rules. In New Jersey, the form comes from the Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment Act, and a clinician and either the patient or a representative must sign it. See [New Jersey POLST: who qualifies and who signs](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-polst-form-who-qualifies-who-signs-and-how-it-works).

So there is no MOLST form to request in either state. If someone offers you one, ask whether they mean the state's POLST.

## What if I already have a MOLST from another state?

Do not discard it. Take it to your primary care doctor or a hospice nurse in your new state and ask them to review it and complete the local form. An out-of-state order is evidence of what you want, and some states formally honor out-of-state forms that meet their law. Our article on [whether advance directives work in other states](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states) explains the general rule for the directive documents. For orders, the safest course is a fresh local form.

Here is how it played out for Priya, 58, who lives in Evanston. Her father, Anil, 84, lived in Rochester, New York, with advanced Parkinson's disease. His MOLST said no CPR and no ventilator, but hospital transfer was allowed. When he moved into her home, she called his new neurologist. The neurologist saw him in his first week, read the MOLST aloud, asked Anil what had changed, and signed an Illinois POLST with the same choices. Priya put the new one on the refrigerator and put the old one in the file with a note. A month later a fall brought the paramedics, who looked at the POLST first.

## What are the steps to get a POLST (or MOLST) in your state?

1. **Ask yourself if the form fits.** It is meant for people with serious illness or frailty. If you are healthy, start with a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
2. **Book a visit about goals.** Ask your doctor, nurse practitioner or palliative care team. Bring your agent or a family member.
3. **Review each box.** Ask what each choice means in practice, not just the label.
4. **Sign with your clinician.** The patient, or the legal representative if the patient lacks capacity, signs and the clinician signs.
5. **Put copies where responders look.** The refrigerator door is the common spot. Give copies to your agent, your care facility and the home care agency.
6. **Review it after any hospital stay or change in diagnosis.** A form that no longer matches your goals is dangerous.

If your condition is moving toward comfort-focused care, the form interacts with hospice. Our article on [palliative care, hospice and comfort care orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/palliative-care-hospice-and-comfort-care-orders) explains how.

## What should you ask before you sign?

The form is short, but each box hides a real choice. Bring these questions to the visit.

- If my heart stops, what does "attempt CPR" involve, and what are the odds for someone with my diagnosis?
- If I am sent to the hospital, will the box I checked still allow a short trial of treatment, such as antibiotics or a few days on a ventilator?
- Who will keep my original, and does the clinic scan it into my chart?
- If my wishes change next month, who rewrites the form, and how do I cancel the old one?

People are often surprised that "limited treatment" can mean hospital care without intensive care. Ask for the plain-language version, then write it down in your own words and give it to your agent.

## What a MOLST or POLST cannot do

These forms do not name a decision-maker, so they do not replace a healthcare agent. They also do not cover finances, and they do not decide what happens after you die. If you have a MOLST but no agent, the next time your care changes, a hospital still needs a person to talk to. Under state surrogate rules that person might not be the one you would choose.

A common mistake our attorneys see is the reverse: a family member signs a POLST for an incapacitated parent, then discovers months later that the parent's written directive says something different. Where the two conflict, ask the clinician to reconcile them in writing.
