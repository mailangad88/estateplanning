A diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease or another dementia changes a family's life, and it also starts a clock. Legal documents require a person who understands them, and the time in which a person can sign is limited. The good news is that the window is often wider than people fear.

This article explains how capacity works, what to sign and in what order, how to document the signing so the documents are not challenged later, and what to do if you are past that point. It builds on our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives), and you may also want our [power of attorney overview](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## Does a dementia diagnosis end your ability to sign documents?

No. The law presumes that adults have capacity until shown otherwise, and a diagnosis does not remove it. Dementia is progressive, so in the early stages many people can still make decisions, manage some money and sign legal documents. Capacity is also specific: a person may be able to choose a healthcare agent while no longer able to manage an investment portfolio.

Capacity is judged at the moment of signing. A person who is confused at 5 p.m. may be lucid at 10 a.m. Lawyers often schedule signings for a person's best time of day.

## What does capacity mean for each type of document?

The legal standard differs by document. The following summary is general, and states vary.

| Document | What the person generally must understand |
|---|---|
| Healthcare power of attorney | Who they are choosing, what the person will decide and that they can revoke it |
| Living will | The kinds of treatment addressed and the consequences of choosing or refusing them |
| Financial power of attorney | What property they have, what authority they are giving and the risks |
| Will | Who their family is, what they own and how the will distributes it. See [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) |
| Contracts, gifts | A somewhat higher understanding of terms and effects |

In general, creating a will requires a lower level of capacity than making a complex contract, and many courts treat a power of attorney as requiring a level close to that of a contract. Because this is a legal, not medical, standard, a lawyer's assessment is as important as a doctor's.

## Which documents should you sign first?

Priorities depend on the person's situation. A sensible order is:

1. **Healthcare power of attorney,** naming a healthcare agent and backups. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
2. **Durable financial power of attorney,** with named successors. See the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) guide. Immediate rather than springing is usually better for dementia, since proof of incapacity is slow and contested.
3. **HIPAA authorization,** so family and agents can talk to doctors. See [HIPAA authorizations](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization).
4. **Living will,** with a values statement. See [what is a living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).
5. **Will or trust,** or updates to them, with correct beneficiary designations. A person with dementia can often still create or amend a will; see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).
6. **Documents about living arrangements,** such as preferences about assisted living or care at home.
7. **Digital access** and a list of accounts, so that someone can find things. Our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets) explains how.

Do not wait to gather all the documents before the first meeting. The first appointment should aim to sign the powers of attorney.

## What should the documents say about dementia specifically?

Standard forms are written for terminal illness, but dementia raises its own questions. A thoughtful directive might address:

- **Where you want to live,** and under what conditions you would accept a move to memory care.
- **Who should help with daily needs,** and whether you accept paid caregivers.
- **How you feel about feeding tubes** if you can no longer eat safely, since evidence shows they do not clearly prolong life or improve comfort in advanced dementia.
- **Antibiotics and hospital transfers** for infections late in the illness.
- **Priorities** such as comfort, dignity, recognition of family, and time at home.
- **Wishes about disclosure,** such as whether to be told the diagnosis in detail, and who should be told.
- **Money rules,** such as who pays bills, limits on gifts and what to do if you are targeted by scams.

Some advance directives written for dementia explicitly refuse life-prolonging treatment after a certain stage. The legal status of such directives differs between states and between facilities, because a person with advanced dementia may seem content in the moment, which complicates "refusal in advance" and makes the writing sensitive. Discuss it with an attorney and the care team. A durable agent who understands your values is often more flexible than a rigid document.

## How do you document capacity at signing?

The most common legal dispute after a dementia diagnosis is not whether the person signed but whether they were able to understand. Reasonable steps can reduce risk.

1. **Meet with the lawyer alone,** or at least have the lawyer talk with the person privately, away from beneficiaries who might influence them.
2. **Ask a physician for a letter** or assessment shortly before signing, addressing the specific decisions, if there is any doubt.
3. **Sign at a good time of day,** and delay if the person is unwell or confused that day.
4. **Have the lawyer keep notes** of the conversation, including the person's answers about family and property in their own words.
5. **Use disinterested witnesses** and a notary, not people who will benefit.
6. **Consider video,** though practices differ, and some lawyers avoid it because it can cut both ways.
7. **Make the choices consistent** with prior plans where possible, since a change that makes sense is harder to challenge. A sudden change that benefits a caregiver invites claims of [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence).

Consider Margaret, 74, diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment. Her daughter Tessa books an appointment with an elder law attorney, who meets with Margaret alone for half an hour, asks her to explain in her own words what each document does, and notes her answers. Margaret signs a power of attorney naming Tessa and a healthcare directive. Two years later, when her son questions the documents, the attorney's notes and a contemporaneous letter from her physician settle the question.

## What if capacity is already gone?

If the person can no longer understand the documents, they cannot sign them, and the family must look at other routes.

- **A court-appointed guardian or conservator,** discussed in [power of attorney vs. guardianship or conservatorship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).
- **Surrogate statutes,** under which many states allow close relatives to make healthcare decisions in a set priority order.
- **Existing documents,** which may be enough. Look for old powers of attorney or a trust, and read the documents carefully.
- **Joint accounts or representative payee** arrangements for benefits, as a stopgap.

These routes are slower and more costly than signing in advance, which is why early action matters. Where the person has lost the ability to sign but has not lost all understanding, ask a lawyer whether a limited document or simplified process is possible.

## How does dementia planning connect to long-term care costs?

Dementia care is expensive, and many families eventually face memory care or nursing home costs. Medicaid planning has a 60-month look-back period in most states, so planning early gives more options. Our [Medicaid planning guide](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) explains the rules. A power of attorney that expressly allows gifting and trust creation can be essential to this planning, while a power that lacks those clauses can leave the agent unable to act. Review this with an attorney before the diagnosis progresses.

## How do you support a parent through this?

If the person with dementia is your parent, approach the topic gently. Begin by asking about their wishes, not by presenting forms. Frame it as making sure their choices are followed. Involve siblings early to prevent suspicion. Offer to go with them to the lawyer. Our guide on [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) and on [talking to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) offer starting points.

Caregivers should also keep records, protect against scams and fraud, and look after their own health. Document important conversations. See our guide on [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) for warning signs.

## What common mistakes should you avoid?

- Waiting for a "bad enough" moment before seeing a lawyer
- Assuming a diagnosis means no more signing is possible
- Using springing powers that need a doctor's finding of incapacity
- Having the beneficiary child sit in on the signing
- Forgetting a HIPAA release
- Leaving no backup agent
- Using online forms that do not meet state rules
- Failing to update the plan as the disease progresses

State rules about capacity, witnesses and forms differ. For general state context, see our guides for [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) and [Arizona](/estate-planning/arizona).
