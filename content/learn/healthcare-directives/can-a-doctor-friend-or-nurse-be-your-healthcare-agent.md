Priya, 63, has no children and a sister two time zones away. Her closest friend, Denise, is a nurse at the hospital where Priya's cardiologist works. Priya wants Denise to speak for her if she is ever too sick to decide. She has two worries: is a friend allowed, and does Denise's job get in the way?

Those are the two questions we hear whenever someone chooses a healthcare agent, and the answers differ a little between Illinois and New Jersey. This page covers who is eligible, who is barred, and the practical limits that no statute spells out. If you are still deciding between candidates, our guide to [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) covers the judgment side. This page covers the legal side, and it belongs with the rest of our [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## Who is allowed to be a healthcare agent?

Any competent adult you trust. That is the default rule in nearly every state. Your agent does not need to be a relative, a lawyer, a state resident or a citizen. They do need to be at least 18 and able to understand what they are agreeing to, and they must sign or accept the role in the way your state's form describes.

Some people assume the agent must be a spouse or an adult child. They do not. Unmarried partners, close friends, a sibling, a niece, a faith leader or a trusted neighbor are all common choices. The law gives you wide freedom, and that freedom is the reason to put the choice in writing. Without a document, default rules favor blood and marriage relatives. See [what happens without an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive).

## Who is barred or restricted?

| Person | Illinois | New Jersey | Practical note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adult friend, partner or relative | Allowed | Allowed | Confirm they will accept |
| Person under 18 | Not allowed | Not allowed | Minors cannot serve |
| Your attending physician | Barred (755 ILCS 45/4-5) | Barred in practice; a doctor who is not your attending may serve | Name someone else |
| Other providers treating you | Barred | Barred if staff of the institution where you are a patient, unless related | Relatives are the exception |
| Doctor or nurse not treating you | Allowed | Allowed | Good fit if they know your wishes |
| Owner or staff of your care facility | Barred as a provider | Barred unless related | Applies to nursing and assisted living |
| Someone out of state | Allowed | Allowed | Name a local alternate |
| Your witness | Barred | Not advisable | Use someone else to sign |

The statutes use their own definitions, so read the form you are using. In Illinois the barred group is the attending physician and other health care providers or professionals caring for you. The Illinois Power of Attorney Act and its statutory form set the categories, and our page on [Illinois health care documents](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) lists the exact categories. In New Jersey the key rule is that an operator, administrator or employee of a health care institution where you are a patient or resident cannot serve, unless related by blood, marriage, civil union, domestic partnership or adoption (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-58). Details are on our [New Jersey advance directives page](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives).

## Can a doctor or nurse who is a friend serve?

Yes, provided they are not treating you. Priya's situation is the typical one. Denise is a nurse, but not Priya's nurse. She works on another floor. In Illinois she is eligible because she is not caring for Priya. In New Jersey the institution rule could matter if Priya is admitted to Denise's own hospital, so Priya should check with an attorney before choosing someone who works where she receives care.

A friend with medical training can be an excellent agent. They understand a prognosis, ask better questions and are less intimidated by hospital staff. They also carry risk. They may lean on their own professional view instead of your wishes, and a clinician agent can find it painful to decline treatment that they would offer themselves. Talk through your values, not just your diagnosis.

If you choose a clinician, add a successor agent who is not in medicine. That way, if the clinician is conflicted or unavailable, someone else is ready.

## Can I choose more than one healthcare agent?

You can name a primary agent and one or more successors, and this is what we recommend. Naming two people to act together sounds fair, but the hospital then needs both to agree in a crisis, and a disagreement at 3 a.m. delays care. The Illinois statutory form is built around an agent plus successors. If you want two people involved, tell your agent in writing to consult the other before major decisions. Name that person in the document as someone to be informed, and make sure both are covered by a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so the hospital can talk to them.

## How do you check that the person you want is eligible?

Work through this list before you sign.

1. Confirm the person is 18 or older and willing to serve.
2. Ask whether they treat you, work for your doctor's practice or work at the facility where you live.
3. If they do, ask an attorney whether the rule applies to them or whether a family exception covers it.
4. Name a successor who lives closer or has a different schedule.
5. Choose two witnesses or a notary who are not the agent. The Illinois statutory form needs one adult witness who is not the agent, your attending physician or certain other providers, and a New Jersey directive needs two adult witnesses or a notary.
6. Give the agent a signed copy and a copy to your doctor.

## Does your agent have to say yes?

No one can be forced to serve. Ask first, before you write the name down. Priya called Denise, explained what the job involves, and gave her a week to think. The job can mean sitting in a hospital hallway, hearing hard prognoses, and saying no to a relative who is crying. Some people discover they cannot do that, and it is much better to learn it now than in an intensive care unit. An agent who accepts should also be handed the practical items: a copy of the document, your doctor's name, a short list of your values, and your insurance card details.

An agent can usually resign later by telling you, if you have capacity, or by notifying your doctor and the successor. If you are worried about someone who might resign under pressure, say so in your conversation and name a firm successor.

## What if the agent you want cannot serve?

Pick someone else, and keep your wishes in writing. A good alternative is a person with the same temperament: calm, willing to push back on relatives, and able to follow your wishes. The person who is barred can still be part of your care. Priya might name her sister as agent, make Denise the successor, and write a letter explaining what she wants. If you cannot find anyone, our page on [incapacity with no family or no one to name as agent](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-with-no-family-or-no-one-to-name-as-agent) explains what happens.
