Tom named his wife Grace as his healthcare agent, and he stopped there. Grace was in the hospital herself last spring with pneumonia, and for two weeks nobody could have spoken for Tom if he had needed it. His daughter lives two hours away. The form was valid, but it had one name on it, and one name is a single point of failure.

This page answers the question people type before they sign: should I name an alternate healthcare agent? It also covers the question that comes later, which is whether you can remove the person you named. The short answers are yes, and yes, while you can still decide. Our [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives) explains how the main documents fit together.

## Do I need an alternate healthcare agent?

You should have one, because a first choice fails more often than people expect. The agent may be traveling, ill, divorced from you, unwilling when the moment arrives, or simply asleep with a phone on silent when the hospital calls at 3 a.m. Without a named alternate, the hospital falls back on the default surrogate rules of your state, and the person who ranks first may be exactly the relative you were trying to avoid.

In Illinois that list is set by statute and runs from a guardian through spouse, adult child, parent and sibling to a close friend (see [who makes medical decisions in Illinois if you have no directive](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides)). An alternate keeps the decision with someone you chose.

The statutory Illinois form has space for successor agents, and New Jersey's directive allows you to name alternates as well. Our summaries of the [Illinois healthcare power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives) show where on each form to write them.

## When does the alternate take over?

Only when the form says the first agent is not able to act. A good form lists the triggers, and good practice is to define them in everyday language rather than leave them to interpretation.

- The first agent has died.
- The first agent says in writing or by phone that they will not serve.
- The first agent cannot be reached after reasonable efforts, for example two calls within a few hours.
- A doctor finds the first agent is incapacitated.
- The first agent is no longer eligible, such as a former spouse after divorce.

The alternate is not a co-decider. If the first agent comes back and is willing, authority generally returns to that person. It helps to say so. Naming two people "jointly" is the common mistake, and our guide to [choosing a healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) explains why joint agents tend to deadlock at the worst time.

## How do I choose and word the order?

Pick people the way you would pick for a role: who answers the phone, who can say no to a doctor, who can say a hard sentence out loud. Then write the order as a numbered sequence on the form.

1. First agent: the person you trust most with the hardest choices.
2. Alternate: someone who lives in a different household or town from the first, so one storm or one illness does not take out both.
3. Second alternate: optional, often an adult child or close friend.

Before you sign, call each person. Tell them the order and show them the document. Ask whether they would carry out a refusal of treatment, because a person who cannot bear to do it is a poor choice even if you love them. Our article on [questions to answer before writing a living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/questions-to-answer-before-writing-a-living-will) helps you put your wishes into words they can use.

One more point on eligibility. In Illinois your attending physician cannot serve as your agent, and a New Jersey agent cannot act as a witness. These limits apply to alternates too, so check each name. Our page on [who can be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/can-a-doctor-friend-or-nurse-be-your-healthcare-agent) lists them.

## Can I remove my healthcare agent?

Yes, as long as you have capacity. You do not need the agent's permission, a court order or a lawyer. Illinois lets you revoke a health care power of attorney by destroying it, signing a written revocation, or stating the revocation aloud in front of an adult witness (755 ILCS 45/4-6). New Jersey does not require a formal act either, but a clear signed replacement avoids doubt.

Revoking by itself is not enough. Follow this list.

1. Sign a new directive naming the person you want, or a signed revocation if you are replacing the agent later.
2. Tell the old agent, in person or in writing, that the role has ended.
3. Collect or destroy old copies, including the one on file at your primary doctor's office and the hospital.
4. Hand out the new form to your new agent, alternates, doctor and family.
5. Update any online portal or registry where you uploaded the old version.

Our article on [how to change or revoke an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive) covers timing, and [where to keep your advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/where-to-keep-your-advance-directive) helps you retire old copies.

### Example: replacing a sibling

Ruth, 72, named her brother Alan as agent and her friend Joan as alternate. Alan and Ruth stopped speaking after an argument over their mother's house. Ruth signed a new directive naming Joan as the agent and her niece Becca as alternate, mailed Alan a short note that the role had ended, and asked her doctor's office to scan the new form and delete the old one. A year later, when Ruth needed surgery, the chart held a single form, and everyone on the care team called Joan.

## What if I can no longer revoke it?

Then the agent stays unless a court acts. If you lack capacity, a revocation you attempt may be questioned, and someone else cannot revoke for you in the ordinary way. Family who believe the agent is ignoring your wishes, abusing the role or has a conflict can petition the court to limit the agent or appoint a guardian of the person. It is slow, public and expensive. That is the main reason to choose carefully and to sign a replacement early, when relationships change, rather than wait.

Divorce is the clearest example. In New Jersey, divorce generally ends a spouse's role as healthcare representative unless the document says otherwise, and many states have a similar rule. Do not lean on it. Our article on [whether divorce cancels a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-divorce-cancel-a-power-of-attorney) explains the Illinois and New Jersey versions, and the health care form deserves the same attention as the financial one.

## What if the agent and my family disagree?

The agent you named ranks ahead of family. Hospital staff often invite everyone to a family meeting, because a disagreement at the bedside is hard on the team, but the decision remains the agent's as long as the agent acts in line with your wishes and the law. If a relative believes otherwise, the path is the court, not the nurses' station. We cover the limits in [who can overrule your healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive).

You can lower the odds of a fight. Tell your family who your agent is, in a short conversation, before there is a crisis. Put a sentence in the form that says you expect the agent to act even if relatives disagree.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether alternates must be named in a certain way, who can witness, whether a notary is optional, what happens on divorce, and how revocation works. Some forms offer a line for "successor" agents and others leave it to a rider. Read the signature page of your form, not just the first page.
