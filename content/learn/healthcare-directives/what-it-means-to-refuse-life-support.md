"I don't want to be kept alive on machines" is one of the most common things people say to their families. It is also one of the least precise. Machines do different things, they help in different situations, and a hospital cannot act on a sentence that vague.

This page defines the common life-sustaining treatments, explains what it means to refuse each, and shows how to write your choices so they are followed. It is part of our [guide to advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives), and it goes deeper than the treatment list in our article on the [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).

## The short version

- Life support is not one thing. It is a set of treatments, and you can say yes or no to each.
- Refusing a treatment does not mean refusing care. Comfort, pain relief and nursing remain.
- You or your agent can stop a treatment later if it is not helping.
- The most useful instruction describes the outcome you would not accept, not just the machine.
- Put the choice in a living will, name an agent who understands it, and ask your doctor about a medical order if you are seriously ill.

## What are the main life-sustaining treatments?

| Treatment | What it involves | Why people refuse it |
|---|---|---|
| CPR | Chest compressions, sometimes a shock to the heart, often a breathing tube | Recovery odds fall with advanced age or serious illness; ribs often break |
| Ventilator | A machine pushes air through a tube in the windpipe, usually with sedation | Fear of long dependence on the machine in a condition with no recovery |
| Feeding tube | Nutrition through the nose or the abdomen | May not extend meaningful life in advanced dementia or end-stage illness |
| Dialysis | A machine cleans the blood when the kidneys fail | Burden of long sessions for little gain when other illness is advanced |
| IV fluids, antibiotics, transfusions | Fluids, drugs and blood given through a vein | Treating infections can prolong a decline some people do not want extended |

## What does refusing CPR mean?

Refusing CPR means that if your heart stops, the team will not start chest compressions or a shock. It does not affect treatment before that point. You can still receive antibiotics, oxygen, surgery or any other care.

CPR works best on people whose heart stops suddenly while otherwise healthy. It is far less likely to restore a person whose heart stops at the end of a long illness, and survivors can face broken ribs, brain injury or a stay on a ventilator. That is why many seriously ill patients, after an honest talk with a doctor, decline it. Others want it fully, and that is a valid choice.

A living will states your wish, but it is a hospital's [DNR order](/glossary/dnr) that the team actually follows. In an emergency, paramedics usually need a specific out-of-hospital form, as we explain in [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).

## What is a ventilator, and what is a DNI order?

A ventilator is a machine that breathes for you through a tube placed in the windpipe. Placing the tube is called intubation. A person on a ventilator is usually sedated, and a longer stay may require a tracheostomy, a tube through the neck.

A do-not-intubate order, DNI, tells the team not to place the breathing tube. It is distinct from a DNR. You might want CPR if your heart stops but not a long dependence on a ventilator, or the reverse. The medical team may also offer a time-limited trial. That means accepting the ventilator for a defined period, say several days, to see whether you recover, and agreeing in advance to stop if you do not. People who dislike the idea of permanent machines but would try a short course often choose this wording.

Noor, 68, has emphysema. She writes: "I will accept a ventilator for up to a week if my doctors think I can recover. If I cannot breathe on my own after that, I want it withdrawn and I want to be kept comfortable." Her family, her agent and her pulmonologist can all follow that.

## What is artificial nutrition and hydration?

Artificial nutrition and hydration is food and fluid given by a tube. A short-term tube runs through the nose to the stomach. A longer-term feeding tube, often called a PEG tube, goes through the skin of the abdomen into the stomach. Hydration alone can be given through a vein.

The law treats this as a medical treatment, like a ventilator, so you may decline it. Families often find this one the hardest, because food feels like love. A few points help:

- In advanced dementia, major geriatrics organizations generally advise against feeding tubes and recommend careful hand feeding for as long as the person accepts it.
- At the very end of life, the body stops asking for food and fluid, and forcing them can cause discomfort.
- Some states set stricter rules for an agent stopping artificial feeding unless the person's wishes were clear. Be specific about it in writing.

Our article on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) covers how to write a directive for a condition where your capacity fades slowly.

## Can you stop a treatment once it has started?

Yes. The law and medical ethics generally treat withdrawing a treatment the same as not starting it. If you or your agent decide that a ventilator or tube is no longer helping, the team can stop it and focus on comfort. This matters when people hesitate to try anything for fear they cannot undo it. The right to refuse treatment is long established. In 1990 in the *Cruzan* case, the US Supreme Court assumed that a competent person may refuse life-sustaining treatment and said states may require clear evidence of an incapacitated person's wishes. That is why writing your choices down matters.

Withdrawal does not mean abandonment. Palliative teams manage pain, breathlessness and anxiety, and hospice care takes over where treatment stops. See [palliative care, hospice and comfort care orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/palliative-care-hospice-and-comfort-care-orders).

## How do you record your choice so it is followed?

1. **Think about outcomes, not machines.** Write what you would not accept, such as permanent unconsciousness or being unable to recognize loved ones.
2. **Address each treatment.** Say yes, no, or time-limited trial for CPR, ventilator, feeding tube and dialysis.
3. **Name a healthcare agent** and an alternate. Use our guide to [choosing a healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent). The agent speaks for you when the document does not fit the situation.
4. **Sign with the formalities your state requires.** Many states need two witnesses or a notary.
5. **Ask for medical orders if you are seriously ill.** Doctors write DNR, DNI or POLST orders. A living will alone does not bind an ambulance crew.
6. **Give copies to your agent, your doctor and your hospital,** and tell the people closest to you what you chose and why.

If your wishes differ from your family's, say so in the document. Their disagreement does not override a valid directive, a point we cover in [who can overrule your healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive).

## How state law changes this

States use different forms and wording, and some limit what an agent may do about artificial feeding or what a directive may say during pregnancy. Some recognize a physician's order for out-of-hospital DNR on specific forms only. Many accept directives from other states, though not all treat them identically, as in [do advance directives work in other states](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states). Start with your state's guide, such as [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) or [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey), and ask an attorney about local wording.
