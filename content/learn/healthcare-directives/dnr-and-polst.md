Many people sign a living will and assume that settles whether doctors and paramedics will attempt CPR. It does not always. Emergency crews respond to what is in front of them, and unless a medical order is in place, they are trained to try to save a life.

This article explains the difference between a living will, a DNR order and a POLST form, who needs which, and how to make sure the right paper is where it needs to be. They are part of the broader set of [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What is the difference between advance directives and medical orders?

Advance directives are documents you create, as a person, to state wishes or name a decision-maker. Medical orders are instructions written and signed by a clinician that other clinicians follow.

| Document | Created by | Type | Who follows it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) | You, with witnesses or a notary | Statement of wishes | Doctors, who interpret it |
| [Healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) | You | Names a decision-maker | Providers, who deal with the agent |
| DNR order | A physician or other authorized clinician, with you or your agent | Medical order | Hospital staff, and EMS if it is the out-of-hospital form |
| POLST | Clinician and patient together | Portable medical order | Emergency crews and facilities across settings |

Think of it this way. A living will tells your story. A DNR or POLST tells the team what to do right now.

## What is a DNR order?

A [DNR order](/glossary/dnr) is a medical order saying that if your heart stops or you stop breathing, staff should not attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). CPR includes chest compressions, electric shocks and breathing assistance, often with a breathing tube. Some states use related terms, such as DNAR (do not attempt resuscitation) or AND (allow natural death).

Important points:

- A DNR addresses only CPR. It is not a "do not treat" order.
- A hospital DNR typically applies inside the hospital. It may not follow you home or to the ambulance.
- Many states have a separate out-of-hospital DNR form, sometimes with a bracelet or wallet card, which paramedics honor.
- A DNR can be combined with a related order, a DNI (do not intubate).
- You, or your healthcare agent if you lack capacity, can revoke it at any time, by telling staff.

CPR can save lives, particularly when a healthy heart goes into a rhythm that can be shocked. For people with advanced illness or frailty, success rates are lower and the risk of rib fractures and brain injury is higher. Those facts are why a conversation with a doctor about your own situation matters.

## What is a POLST?

A [POLST](/glossary/polst) is Provider Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment, a portable, signed medical order that records a seriously ill person's choices about CPR and other treatment. Many states use the name POLST, while others use MOLST (medical orders), MOST (medical orders for scope of treatment), POST or similar. The National POLST organization tracks state programs, and some states have not yet adopted one.

A POLST commonly has sections covering:

1. **CPR:** attempt resuscitation or do not attempt resuscitation.
2. **Medical interventions:** full treatment, selective treatment (hospital but avoid intensive care and intubation) or comfort-focused treatment.
3. **Artificial nutrition:** feeding tube, trial period or none.
4. **Signatures:** a physician, nurse practitioner or physician assistant, depending on the state, and the patient or legal representative.

The form is typically printed on brightly colored paper, so it is easy for paramedics to spot, and it follows the patient between home, hospital and nursing facility. It is a voluntary tool and is not required for anyone.

## Who should have a POLST, and who should not?

A POLST is for people whose health condition makes it likely that they will face these decisions soon. Guidance commonly suggests it for someone with advanced illness or frailty for whom a clinician would not be surprised if they died within a year. Examples include a person with advanced heart failure, metastatic cancer, end-stage lung disease, or advanced dementia in a care facility.

For a healthy 40-year-old, a POLST is generally not recommended. They need a [living will and healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives), which can address possible future situations, and a POLST would be an order to doctors about a current condition.

Meet Frank, 81, who has advanced COPD and has been hospitalized three times this year. His doctor talks with him and his daughter about what he would want if his breathing failed. Frank decides he would accept hospitalization and antibiotics but does not want to be intubated or have CPR. His doctor completes a POLST, and they post it on the refrigerator. When Frank's breathing worsens at night, the paramedics see the form and take him to the hospital for treatment without attempting intubation.

## Why doesn't a living will cover emergencies?

A living will is written to guide doctors in conditions such as terminal illness or permanent unconsciousness. It requires interpretation. In an emergency at home, a 911 crew has seconds, no way to verify a document and no authority to interpret it. Protocols tell them to start treatment unless there is a valid out-of-hospital order. In many states, that is a DNR form or POLST.

So a living will and a POLST do different jobs. They also should agree. If your living will says you do not want ventilator support in a terminal state but your POLST says full treatment, staff may be confused. When you sign one, review the other.

## How do you get one?

1. **Talk with your doctor.** Ask whether a DNR or POLST fits your health situation. Many hospitals and hospice programs also have trained facilitators.
2. **Bring your healthcare agent** and your living will to the conversation.
3. **Decide each section** on the form, with the doctor explaining the effect of each choice.
4. **Have the clinician sign,** and sign yourself, or your representative if you lack capacity.
5. **Keep the original** where emergency crews will see it: usually on the refrigerator, plus a copy in a go bag and a wallet card.
6. **Give copies** to your agent, family, physician, care facility and hospital.
7. **Review it** whenever your health changes, when you move between settings or at least once a year.

Rules differ widely by state. Some states accept only their own official forms, some require specific colors, and some do not recognize POLST by statute but treat it as a standard of care. Ask a local provider or lawyer what is recognized where you live and travel.

## Can a family member sign for someone who can't?

A healthcare agent, or in many states a legal surrogate, can generally consent to a DNR order or POLST on behalf of a patient who lacks capacity, consistent with the patient's known wishes. This is another reason to name a healthcare agent and share your wishes in advance. Without one, a surrogate under state law may have to make the call, often under pressure. ## Can you change your mind?

Yes. You can revoke a DNR or POLST orally or in writing at any time, or by asking your clinician for a new one. If your health improves or your preferences change, ask for a new form, and destroy or mark the old ones. Tell family, caregivers and facilities. Many errors come from old forms still on the wall.

## What common misunderstandings should you avoid?

- **"A DNR means no treatment."** It does not. It refers to CPR only.
- **"My living will works in the ambulance."** Not usually.
- **"A POLST replaces my advance directive."** It does not; they complement each other.
- **"I can't change a POLST."** You can.
- **"A DNR is for old people."** It is for people whose medical situation and values make CPR a choice they would not want. Age alone is not the test.
- **"Family can override it."** A valid order should be honored, though disputes can happen if documents conflict, which is why consistency matters.
