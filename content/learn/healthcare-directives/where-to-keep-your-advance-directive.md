A perfectly drafted directive in a drawer is worth very little at 2 a.m. in an emergency room. The people deciding for you will have minutes, not days. If nobody can produce the paper, the team falls back on defaults, and defaults are what a directive is meant to avoid.

This page covers where the original should live, who needs copies, how to get the document into your medical record, and which documents emergency crews will actually follow. It belongs to our [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## The short version

- Original at home, in a known and unlocked place. Copies everywhere else.
- Your agent should have a copy on paper and on a phone.
- Ask each doctor and hospital to scan it into your chart and confirm it is there.
- Paramedics need a medical order form, not a living will.
- Replace old copies whenever you update the document.

## Where should the original go?

Keep the signed original in a folder at home that your agent knows how to reach, such as a labeled file in a desk or a document box. Tell your agent and your alternate where it is. A fireproof box is fine so long as someone else knows the key or combination.

Avoid a safe deposit box. Banks generally restrict access, so your agent may be unable to open it on a weekend, and the bank may not let anyone in until a court order or a signed access arrangement exists. For wills the original is often essential, but a directive works from copies, and a copy is generally as good as the original in a medical setting. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) for the different logic that applies there, and [how to organize your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents) for the full folder.

## Who should have a copy?

| Who | Why | How |
|---|---|---|
| Healthcare agent | Needs the authority and your wishes in hand | Paper copy and a photo on their phone |
| Alternate agent | Steps in if the first is unreachable | Same as above |
| Primary doctor | The record other doctors see | Hand-delivered or uploaded to the portal |
| Hospital you use | Admission staff look in the chart first | Ask the registration desk to scan it |
| Spouse or close family | Reduces conflict at the bedside | Copy plus a conversation |
| Your attorney | Backup and updates | Keep with the rest of your plan |
| Care facility | Needed if you move in | Provide at admission |

The best test is simple. If you collapsed tonight and your agent were at the airport, could someone else locate the paper? If the answer is no, add a copy.

## How do you get it into your medical record?

Most health systems make this easier than people expect.

1. Log in to your patient portal and look for an "advance directive" or "documents" section. Upload a scan or a clear PDF.
2. If there is no upload option, give a copy to the front desk or the nurse at your next visit and ask that it be scanned into the chart.
3. Ask your primary doctor to note in the record that you have a directive and who your agent is.
4. A week later, check that the document appears. Scanned files sometimes land in the wrong folder.
5. Repeat with every hospital system you use. Records at one system do not follow you to another unless they share an electronic record.

A [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) is a good companion paper. It lets your agent and others see your records without a fight over privacy, and it is a separate form from the directive.

## Is there a registry?

There is no single national government registry for advance directives. A few states run their own, and some private companies sell storage with an online account or a wallet card. Registries work only if the hospitals around you know to look, so ask your doctor or the hospital's social work office whether they use one before you pay or fill one in. A registry also adds one more place that holds an old version, so remember it when you update.

## What will paramedics follow?

Emergency crews generally follow medical orders, not a living will. A living will is a statement for your treating team, but an ambulance crew called to a collapse has seconds and wants a signed physician order. Depending on your state, that is an out-of-hospital DNR form, a POLST or a similar medical order form. We explain the difference in [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).

Imagine Margaret, 84, with advanced heart failure. She has a living will in her desk and a signed POLST on her refrigerator, which her state's crews know to check. When her husband calls 911, the paramedics find the form, read it and honor her choice. Without the POLST they would have started CPR. Where available, a bracelet or medallion tied to the same order can help when you are away from home, but it works only if the signed order exists.

## Does a hospital or nursing home have to give you a directive?

Hospitals, nursing homes and many home health agencies that accept Medicare or Medicaid must ask whether you have a directive, give you information about your rights under state law, and note the answer in your chart. They must not make care depend on whether you have one or sign one. Hospitals commonly offer forms and a social worker to explain them.

Signing during an admission is better than nothing. The downside is that you are often unwell and rushed, and the form may not match the choices you would make with time. If you can, prepare one before you get sick and hand it over when you arrive.

## What should a wallet card or phone note say?

Keep it to the facts a stranger needs. Write your full name, the words "I have an advance directive," your agent's name and phone number, your alternate's number and your primary doctor's name. Add the location of the full document, such as "copy in the patient portal, original in the blue folder in the hall desk." Most smartphones have an emergency information screen reachable without a passcode, which is a good home for the same details. A card or note does not replace the document, but it points a hurried nurse or paramedic toward your agent within seconds, and the agent can supply the rest.

## What about traveling, vacations and moves?

Carry a copy, or at least a photo on your phone and your agent's number. Most states honor directives made in other states, though not all in the same way, as we explain in [do advance directives work in other states](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states). If you move, update the copies in your new doctor's chart and your new hospital's records.

## Keep the set current

Every update creates a trap: the old version still exists somewhere. When you replace a directive, collect the old copies, send the new ones to the same list and ask each place to discard the former. The steps are in [how to change or revoke an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive).

A short checklist helps:

- Original stored at home, and agent knows where.
- Copies with agent, alternate, doctor, hospital and family.
- Portal upload confirmed.
- Wallet card or phone note with agent's name and number.
- Medical order form (DNR or POLST) on file if you are seriously ill.
- Old copies destroyed.

## How state law changes this

States control registry programs, the form of out-of-hospital orders, what bracelet programs exist and whether a copy is as good as an original. Hospital policies add their own habits on top. Check your state's page on [estate planning by state](/estate-planning), or ask your doctor which forms local emergency services accept.
