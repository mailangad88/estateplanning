Tom, 61, ticked the donor box on his driver's license thirty years ago and has not thought about it since. Last month he signed a living will that says he does not want to be kept alive on machines. His daughter read both and asked a fair question: if something happened, which one wins?

The two documents do different jobs, and they can work together if the wording is right. This article covers how to become a donor, what a directive should say about it, and the one conflict that catches careful people. It sits within our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## How do you become an organ donor?

You become a donor by registering your decision with your state. The usual routes are the donor question at the motor vehicle office when you get or renew a license or state ID, the state's online registry, or a mark on a health insurance or health-system profile. In Illinois the registry runs through the Secretary of State's driver services, and in New Jersey through the Motor Vehicle Commission and a state donor registry. Either way the sign-up takes a few minutes.

Nearly every state follows a version of the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act. Under it, an adult's own signed decision to donate is a legal gift. That is called first-person authorization. The law does not make the family a co-signer.

Registration covers what you can give: organs such as kidneys and heart, tissue such as corneas and skin, or both. Many registries let you choose. If you want to limit the gift, say so there and in your directive.

## Where does your advance directive come in?

Your directive tells your agent and the hospital what you want, in your own words, at a moment the registry may not reach. A registry entry is a yes or no. A directive can add the practical detail around it.

Four things are worth putting in writing:

1. Your choice to donate, naming organs, tissue, or both, and any limits.
2. A statement that you accept temporary medical support, including a ventilator, if it is needed to keep donation possible.
3. Who may speak for you about donation if the hospital has questions. Usually this is your healthcare agent.
4. What you want done if you cannot donate for medical reasons, for example whether you would consent to research use.

Most state forms include a donation section. A free state form is fine for this. The wording below it matters more than the form.

## Why can a living will seem to block donation?

A living will usually refuses ventilators, breathing machines and other life support when recovery is not expected. Organ donation often requires the opposite for a short time. A patient who has died, or who is declared brain dead, may have to stay on a ventilator for hours or a day or two so the organs keep receiving oxygenated blood until the transplant team is ready.

If your directive says "no ventilator, no artificial support, ever," the hospital may read that literally. Then it has to choose between your refusal and your donation. Most hospitals will call the agent. A poorly drafted directive just makes a hard day harder.

The fix is one honest sentence. For example: "I want to be an organ and tissue donor. I consent to ventilator support and other measures for the time needed to carry out donation, even though I have otherwise refused life support." That does not undo your refusal of treatment while you are alive and dying. It addresses the specific period around a declared death.

| Situation | What can go wrong | What fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| Registry says yes, living will refuses all machines | Staff are unsure which to follow | A sentence in the directive allowing short-term support for donation |
| Registry silent, directive says donate | Donation team may not see it quickly | Join the registry as well and tell your agent |
| Registry says yes, family objects | Delay, distress, rare cancellation | Tell family now; agent confirms your choice |
| You want to donate but also have a POLST | Orders may stop resuscitation before the donation process starts | Ask the doctor completing the POLST to note your donor status |

If you have a [POLST or DNR order](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst), mention donation to the clinician who completes it. A DNR order says nothing about donation. Being a donor and declining resuscitation are separate decisions and most patients make both without trouble.

## What can your healthcare agent do about donation?

An agent can usually authorize or refuse donation only where you left no instruction. If you registered and wrote it into your directive, the agent's job is to confirm what you chose, not to reopen it. If you were silent, many state laws let the agent consent, often followed in order by a spouse, adult children or parents.

Whether the agent's authority continues after death is a legal question. Medical decisions end at death, but several states extend the agent's power to anatomical gifts, autopsy and disposition of remains. Our article on [healthcare agent authority after death](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-agent-authority-after-death) goes through that handoff. Funeral decisions are often a separate legal track. In New Jersey see [who controls funeral and burial decisions](/learn/new-jersey/who-controls-funeral-and-burial-decisions-in-new-jersey), and in Illinois the [Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act).

## What is the difference between organ donation and body donation?

Organ and tissue donation are for transplant, and the recovery happens in a hospital soon after death. Whole-body donation sends the body to a medical school or research program, and it needs its own enrollment in advance. Programs often decline a body if organs were recovered, and many decline for other reasons too, such as an autopsy or certain infections. If you want both, ask the program how it handles that before you register.

Neither one is a funeral plan. If you want a particular service after donation, write that down as well. See [how to plan your own funeral in advance](/learn/basics/plan-your-own-funeral-in-advance).

## What to do this week

1. Join your state registry, or check that your current record is still correct.
2. Open your advance directive and find the donation section. If it is blank, fill it in.
3. If your directive refuses a ventilator, add the short-term support sentence above.
4. Tell your agent, your backup and one other relative what you decided.
5. Put copies of the directive where the hospital can find them, as described in [where to keep your advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/where-to-keep-your-advance-directive).

A conversation matters here more than any wording. Marta registered as a donor but never told her husband. At the hospital he heard a nurse mention donation and thought it was a pressure tactic, until a chaplain asked whether she had a card. The conversation took ten minutes and he agreed. It would have taken two minutes a year earlier.

## How state law changes this

States use different forms, different registries and different default lists of who may consent when you said nothing. Some states let teenagers register, some require a parent to consent for anyone under 18. Rules on how an agent's authority carries past death also differ. Check your own state's form and your motor vehicle agency's registry page, then compare what you see against your directive.
