You signed your healthcare documents last spring. Today you are fine, and the papers sit in a folder. When, exactly, do they start to matter? Does your agent have a say over your doctor appointments next week? What has to happen before someone opens that folder?

The short answer is that the document works the moment you sign it, but it does not take control of anything while you can still decide. This article explains the triggers, who decides they have been met, and the odd cases where a directive turns on and off. It is part of our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## The short version

- You are in charge as long as you can understand your situation and communicate a choice.
- Your agent's authority begins when a physician finds you cannot decide.
- A living will may need a second condition, such as terminal illness or permanent unconsciousness.
- Capacity can return, and when it does, authority returns to you.
- A directive nobody can find starts nothing, so distribution matters as much as drafting.

## When does a healthcare power of attorney start?

A healthcare power of attorney is valid from signing and active from incapacity. Those are separate moments, and confusing them causes real worry.

At signing, the document exists and can be relied on. Hospitals will honor it if it meets your state's formal rules. At incapacity, your agent begins to make medical decisions, because you no longer can. The agent's authority is not a second vote alongside yours. If you can express a choice, your choice wins.

Take Dolores, 71, who names her son Paul as agent. She goes in for a hip replacement, and the surgeon walks her through the consent form. She signs it herself. Paul has no role. After surgery she develops a severe infection and is sedated on a ventilator. The attending physician records that she cannot participate in decisions. From that point, Paul consents to antibiotics, changes in sedation and a transfer to the intensive care unit. When Dolores wakes up and understands her situation, the decisions go back to her.

The glossary entry on the [healthcare proxy](/glossary/healthcare-proxy) uses the same idea, and our article on the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) explains the agent's job in more detail.

## When does a living will take effect?

A living will usually takes effect when two conditions are both true: you cannot decide for yourself, and your medical condition falls within the situations your document describes.

Most living wills use named conditions. Common ones include:

- A terminal condition with limited life expectancy
- Permanent unconsciousness, sometimes called a persistent vegetative state
- An end-stage condition, such as advanced dementia, in which you cannot recognize family or communicate

Many states require that one or two physicians confirm the condition. If your document says "two physicians," one is not enough in a hospital that follows it strictly. A broken ankle, even with surgery under anesthesia, never triggers a living will, since the condition is not in the list.

This is why vague phrases such as "no heroic measures" cause trouble. The trigger is unclear, and a clinician may ask your agent to interpret it. Spelling out the condition and the treatment, such as "if I am permanently unconscious, do not use a feeding tube," narrows the room for argument. Our article on the [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) covers wording in more depth.

## Who decides you have lost capacity?

Your attending physician, in most cases. Many states also accept or require a second clinician. The test is functional, not diagnostic. Having dementia, a mental illness or a brain injury does not automatically mean you lack capacity. The question is whether, for this decision, at this moment, you can do four things.

1. Understand the information about your condition and options
2. Appreciate how it applies to you
3. Reason about the choices
4. Communicate a choice

Capacity is decision-specific. A person with early Alzheimer's may be able to choose between two simple treatments yet unable to weigh a complex surgery. This is why our guide on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) urges families to sign while capacity is clearly present, and why an agent should expect to share decisions with a loved one for a while before taking over.

## What if your capacity comes and goes?

Directives can behave like a switch. Delirium, medication, infection and some psychiatric conditions produce stretches of clear thinking and stretches without. When you lack capacity, the agent acts. When you regain it, you do.

Practically, three things help:

- **The agent keeps you in the conversation.** Even when the agent decides, an agent who explains and asks for your input respects your dignity and often gets a clearer picture.
- **Staff document each assessment.** Ask that the chart note when capacity was lost and regained.
- **Disagreement with the agent is a signal.** If you are lucid and object to what the agent decided, staff should treat your objection seriously and reassess.

## Healthcare triggers are different from financial triggers

People often mix up healthcare and financial documents. A financial power of attorney can be written to start right away or only on incapacity, and the latter is called a springing power of attorney. The choice has real tradeoffs, covered in [springing vs. immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney). Healthcare documents almost always follow the incapacity trigger, because medical decisions are personal and no one needs to act for you while you can speak.

| Document | Valid when | Active when | Who confirms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Healthcare power of attorney | Signed and witnessed or notarized | You lack capacity | Physician, sometimes two |
| Living will | Signed and witnessed or notarized | You lack capacity and meet the condition listed | Physician, often two |
| HIPAA authorization | Signed | Immediately, unless you set a later start | Not applicable |
| Financial power of attorney | Signed | Immediately or on incapacity, as written | Depends on document |

## What can go wrong at the moment it matters?

Most failures at activation are practical. The document is in a safe deposit box nobody can open at 2 a.m. Or it names an agent who is on a flight. Or no one is willing to write the incapacity finding, so the agent is stuck in a gap between authority and proof.

A short checklist reduces these risks.

1. Put a copy in your medical record at every hospital and clinic you use.
2. Give your agent and one backup a copy and a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so they can see your records without delay.
3. Tell your primary doctor who your agent is.
4. Keep a wallet card or phone note naming your agent and where the documents live.
5. Review the trigger language with an attorney if you want it narrower or broader than your state's default.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how incapacity is determined, how many physicians must agree, whether the agent's power starts immediately by default and whether a living will is limited during pregnancy. Some state forms include a built-in definition of the triggering conditions. Others leave it to you. Check your [state guide](/estate-planning), and note that a hospital will usually follow its own policy for who must sign off.

If you have no directive when the moment arrives, the default rules in [what happens without an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive) apply instead.
