Most people have an opinion about how they want to die, and almost no one has put it in a place anyone can find. Ask a room of adults whether they want to be on a ventilator for months, and nearly everyone says no. Ask how many have said so in writing, to the person who would be making the call, and the room goes quiet.

This article shows how to turn those opinions into documents and conversations that hold up. It supplements our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives) and covers a topic that guide touches only briefly: where each kind of wish should be recorded.

## What counts as an end-of-life wish?

End-of-life wishes are any preferences about the last stage of your life, and they fall into four groups. Each one has its own best home.

| Type of wish | Examples | Where to record it |
|---|---|---|
| Medical treatment | CPR, ventilator, feeding tube, dialysis, comfort care | [Living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) or advance directive; [POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) if seriously ill |
| Decision-maker | Who speaks for you | Healthcare power of attorney |
| Body and donation | Organ and tissue donation, whole-body donation, burial or cremation | State donor registry, directive, written instructions |
| Personal and ceremonial | Where you die, who is present, music, funeral, messages | Letter of instruction, Five Wishes, conversation |

Mixing them up causes trouble. A treatment wish in a will arrives too late. A funeral wish in a living will is ignored because it is not a treatment decision.

## Step one: figure out what you actually want

Many people do not know. A useful way to start is to ask yourself specific questions rather than "do you want to be kept alive."

- If I could not recognize my family and had no prospect of recovering, would I want a feeding tube?
- Would I accept a breathing machine for a short trial if doctors thought I might improve? For how long?
- Where do I want to be if I am dying: at home, in a hospital, in a hospice?
- Are there things I would endure for time with family, and things I would not?
- Is there a religious ritual or person I want near?

Write down your answers, even loosely. Your doctor can help you understand what a ventilator or feeding tube involves in your own medical situation, and that information often changes what people say they want. Medicare and many private plans cover advance care planning visits.

## Step two: have the conversation

The document is a backup. The conversation does the real work. Tell your healthcare agent, your spouse or partner, your adult children and your doctor what you have decided and why. Use specifics. "I do not want to be kept alive on machines" is open to interpretation. "If I have had a severe stroke and the doctors do not expect me to wake up or recognize anyone, I want comfort care only" is not.

Expect some pushback. Adults who love you may say "don't talk like that." Hold the line gently and say you need them to know. Our article on [talking to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) offers phrasing for difficult talks, and the one on [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) covers what to tell your agent.

## Step three: sign the documents

1. **Advance directive.** Name an agent and backup, and record treatment preferences. Follow your state's signing rules, covered in our piece on [whether a living will needs a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary).
2. **HIPAA authorization.** So your agent and family can get information. See [the HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization).
3. **Medical order, if appropriate.** Healthy adults do not need a POLST. If you have advanced illness or frailty, ask your clinician about it.
4. **Organ donation registration.** See below.
5. **Letter of instruction.** Funeral wishes, personal messages, account locations. See [the letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction).

## How does organ donation fit in?

Organ donation is a separate decision with its own record, though your directive can reinforce it. In the United States you can register as a donor through your state's donor registry, often at the motor vehicle office or online, and the registration is a legal consent in most states. A heart on a driver's license is a prompt for the registry entry, not the whole record.

Several details trip people up:

- **Organ donation happens only in specific circumstances.** Donation after death usually requires a death in a hospital, which is why most people who die at home cannot donate organs, though they may be able to donate tissue such as corneas.
- **Machines may be needed briefly.** To keep organs viable while the family is told and the process is arranged, a donor may be kept on a ventilator for a short time. If your living will refuses ventilators in all cases, say in the directive that you accept them for donation purposes only.
- **Your family may still be asked.** If you are not registered, families are often approached in the hospital. A registered decision tends to settle the matter.
- **Whole-body donation is different.** Giving your body to medical education requires arrangements with a specific program in advance, and the program may decline if the body has been through certain procedures. Check before relying on it.
- **Directives vs. registry.** Many state forms have a donation section. If it conflicts with your registry entry, the most recent one is the safest, so keep both consistent.

None of this is a reason to donate or not. It is a reason to write your choice down in the right places.

## Funeral and burial wishes

Write them down separately from your will. A will is often not found or read until after the funeral, and in some places the person who controls the body is determined by a state statute that ranks your spouse first, then adult children, then others. If you want cremation and your children want burial, the hierarchy decides, not the will.

To make sure your preference is followed:

- Say so in a signed letter, and give copies to those most likely to make the arrangements.
- Consider a pre-need contract with a funeral home, and keep the paperwork.
- If your state allows it, name an agent for disposition of remains in a short form, and tell that person.
- Include the name of your preferred funeral home, cemetery plot details and whether you hold a veteran's burial benefit.

## Example: Helen's folder

Helen, 78, has heart failure. She signs an advance directive naming her son Marcus as agent and her daughter Ruth as backup, completes a POLST with her cardiologist, and registers as a tissue donor. She writes a one-page letter saying she wants a short service at her church, cremation, and that the family should play a specific hymn. She puts all four documents in a folder, labels it "If I am sick," and tells both children where it is. She also uploads copies to her hospital's patient portal. Two years later, when she is hospitalized with pneumonia and cannot speak, the staff find the POLST in the chart and Marcus has the folder in his car. Nothing about it was dramatic. It simply worked.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Leaving the only copy in a safe deposit box or a home filing cabinet nobody knows about.
- Writing wishes only in a will.
- Changing your mind but never telling your agent.
- Failing to account for religious or cultural expectations in your family, then leaving your agent to explain the decision.
- Skipping digital accounts and passwords; see [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access).
- Assuming a hospice will have your documents. Bring them.
