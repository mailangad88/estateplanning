People use these three terms as if they were interchangeable, and hospital paperwork does not help. A nurse hands you a packet titled "Advance Directive," and inside are a living will section and a proxy section. A friend says she "has a proxy." Your brother says he "did a living will online." Are those the same thing? Not quite, and the difference matters the day someone has to use them.

This article sorts out the vocabulary, shows the three side by side, and explains how to decide what to sign. It belongs to our wider guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## How the three terms fit together

Think of "advance directive" as a folder. The living will and the healthcare proxy designation are the papers that go inside. An [advance directive](/glossary/advance-directive) is any written instruction, signed ahead of time, that tells doctors and family how to handle your care once you cannot speak for yourself. The documents that qualify fall into two types: instructions about treatment, and the appointment of a decision-maker.

| | Living will | Healthcare proxy / medical POA | Advance directive |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Written treatment wishes | Appointment of a person | Umbrella term, or a combined form |
| Answers the question | What do I want? | Who decides? | Both, if combined |
| Who acts on it | Doctors read and apply it | Your named agent speaks to doctors | Doctors and your agent |
| Handles surprises | Poorly, it only covers what it lists | Well, the agent can weigh new facts | Depends on whether it names an agent |
| Typical trigger | Terminal illness, permanent unconsciousness, or similar, as the form defines it | Your doctor finds you lack capacity | Same as the document inside |
| Needs witnesses or notary | State specific | State specific | State specific |
| Works while you can decide for yourself | No, you decide | No, you decide | No, you decide |

The last row surprises people. None of these documents takes your voice away. While you can understand your choices and communicate them, your word controls, and the paper sits in a drawer.

## What a living will does

A [living will](/glossary/living-will) records your preferences about life-sustaining treatment in situations where recovery is not expected. Typical topics include breathing machines, feeding tubes, dialysis, CPR and pain control. It speaks for you in the first person: "If two physicians agree that I am permanently unconscious, I do not want..."

Its strength is clarity. A doctor reading a specific instruction signed by the patient has something solid to point to. Its weakness is that life rarely matches the form. Maria, 71, writes that she wants no feeding tube if she is terminally ill. Then she has a stroke, is not terminally ill, but cannot swallow, and her doctors suggest a feeding tube for a few weeks. Her living will says nothing about that. Someone has to decide, and the document cannot. Our article on the [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) goes through the wording in more detail.

## What a healthcare proxy does

A [healthcare proxy](/glossary/healthcare-proxy) names a person, the agent, to make medical decisions when you cannot. The agent can consent to or refuse treatment, choose among doctors and facilities, and read your records. The agent's duty is to apply what you would have wanted, not what the agent would choose.

The strength is flexibility. The agent can ask the surgeon questions, hear that the stroke is likely to improve, and say yes to a short trial of a feeding tube while the family waits. The weakness is the mirror image: a proxy with no guidance is guessing. A person who has never heard your views, however loving, may freeze or may impose their own. The full article on the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) covers the legal mechanics.

Naming the right person is its own decision. We cover that in [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent).

## Why the labels vary by state

The words differ because states wrote their own laws, often decades apart. You may see healthcare proxy, healthcare agent, healthcare surrogate, patient advocate, durable power of attorney for healthcare or medical power of attorney. Directive-style documents appear as living will, declaration, natural death directive, or "instructions" section of a larger form.

A few practical consequences follow:

- A form titled differently in your state is not a problem. Read the substance, not the label.
- Some states make the two documents separate statutes with separate signing rules. Others give you one printed form with parts one and two.
- Witness rules vary. Many states bar your agent, relatives and heirs, and employees of your care facility from witnessing. Our piece on [whether a living will needs a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary) explains the typical rules.
- Your state may have its own statutory form. Using it removes doubt about whether a hospital will accept it.

Your [state guide](/estate-planning) lists the local rules in more depth, and if you live in Illinois see [the Illinois healthcare power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## When each one makes the most sense

**A living will alone** can fit someone with a long-standing, well-defined view, for example a person with a progressive condition who has decided exactly which treatments she will refuse. Even then, we would want a proxy to enforce the choice, because hospitals respond to a person standing at the bedside.

**A proxy alone** fits a younger, healthy adult who has not thought through the details but knows exactly whom they trust. Naming the person is quick, and the conversation can follow. Priya, 34, names her sister, tells her "no machines if I am not going to wake up, and don't let anyone argue you out of it," and has done more than most people her age.

**Both together** is the right answer for nearly everyone else, and it is what a good estate plan includes. The living will informs the agent; the agent covers the gaps. If the two ever conflict, the agent is generally expected to follow your written instructions, which is why the instructions should not be vague.

## A simple order of operations

1. Decide who your agent and your backup agent will be, and ask each whether they are willing.
2. Talk through the situations you fear most: long-term ventilation, severe dementia, permanent unconsciousness.
3. Fill in your state's form, or have an attorney draft one that includes both parts.
4. Sign with the required witnesses or notary.
5. Give copies to your agent, backup, doctor and anyone who will be at the hospital.
6. Add a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so those people can actually receive your medical information.
7. Review the documents after a diagnosis, a divorce, a move or a death in the family.

If your goals include emergency orders such as no CPR, a different type of document applies. [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) are signed by clinicians, not by you alone.

## Mistakes we see

- Treating the terms as synonyms and signing one document when the family believed there were two.
- Naming a proxy but never telling them anything about your wishes.
- Naming one person, with no backup, who lives across the country or is older than you.
- Writing a living will so absolute ("no treatment of any kind") that doctors cannot tell when it applies.
- Assuming a financial power of attorney covers medical decisions. It does not, and the reverse is also true. See [the durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
- Keeping the only copy in a safe deposit box nobody can open at 2 a.m.
