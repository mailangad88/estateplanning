Some people know from experience that a mental health crisis can arrive with little warning. During one, judgment and insight can fail, and the person who can usually explain what helps and what harms may not be able to. A mental health advance directive is written in the good weeks, for the bad ones.

It is a document most people have never heard of, and many doctors rarely see one. This article explains what it contains, how it differs from the standard directive in our [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives), what the law will and will not let it do, and how to write one that is actually used.

## What is a mental health advance directive?

It is a written statement, made while you have capacity, about how you want to be treated if a psychiatric condition leaves you unable to make decisions. It is also called a psychiatric advance directive, or PAD. People who live with bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, severe depression or PTSD write them, and so do people with early dementia who expect episodes of confusion or agitation.

A PAD usually does three things. It states preferences about treatment. It names a person, an [agent](/glossary/agent), who can speak for you. And it gives practical instructions, such as who should care for your child or your dog while you are in a hospital.

It resembles a [living will](/glossary/living-will), but the subject matter is different. A living will mostly addresses life support near the end of life. A PAD deals with treatment that may happen many times over a lifetime, where the goal is recovery.

## What can a mental health advance directive cover?

The more specific the document, the more useful it is. Typical topics include:

- **Medications.** Drugs that have worked, drugs that caused bad reactions, and drugs you refuse. This is the single most useful section for a treating doctor.
- **Facilities.** Hospitals you prefer, and ones you want to avoid.
- **Interventions.** Your views on restraint, seclusion, electroconvulsive therapy and emergency injections.
- **Early warning signs.** What a crisis looks like for you, such as not sleeping for three nights, so that others recognize it.
- **What helps.** A quiet room, a particular person, music, a short walk.
- **Who to contact and who not to.** Including family members you do not want informed.
- **Practical arrangements.** Care for children, pets, and a house; who pays the bills.

Some states provide a statutory form. Others accept a custom document that is signed and witnessed like a healthcare power of attorney. Look at your state's rules, and read our page on [advance directive requirements and other-state validity](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states) if you move or travel.

## How is it different from a healthcare power of attorney?

They overlap, and many people need both pieces in one document or two coordinated ones.

| | Standard healthcare power of attorney | Mental health advance directive |
|---|---|---|
| Main focus | Medical decisions of any kind | Psychiatric treatment and crises |
| Typical trigger | You cannot decide | You cannot decide because of a psychiatric condition, sometimes defined in the document |
| Can name specific treatments | Often general | Yes: medicines, facilities, therapies |
| Statutory restrictions | Some states limit psychiatric admission by an agent | Many states have a separate statute |
| Who reads it | Hospitals, doctors | Psychiatrists, crisis teams, hospitals |

In many states, an agent under an ordinary healthcare power of attorney cannot admit you to a mental health facility or consent to certain treatments unless the document expressly says so. That is why generic forms often fall short here. Our guide to the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) explains the general rules.

## What is a Ulysses clause?

A Ulysses clause is a provision in which you agree in advance to treatment that you expect to refuse when ill. The reference is to the hero who ordered his crew to tie him to the mast so he could hear the sirens and survive. The clause might say: "If I am admitted and stop taking my medication, I consent to hospitalization for up to seven days even if I object at the time."

This is the most debated part of a PAD. A directive that only refuses treatment is easy to honor. A directive that overrides your own objection raises hard questions about autonomy and safety. States answer them differently. Some enforce a self-binding clause with conditions. Some allow you to revoke the directive even during a crisis. Some limit how long a clause can last. The honest answer is that a Ulysses clause may be useful to your treating team as strong evidence of what you want, even where it is not strictly enforceable.

Think carefully before including one. Discuss it with your psychiatrist, your agent and an attorney, and revisit it after any episode.

## What are the limits of a PAD?

A PAD is not a shield against all involuntary treatment. Three limits come up often.

1. **Emergency and commitment laws.** If a court orders treatment, or a doctor invokes an emergency, the directive may be set aside. Most statutes say so explicitly.
2. **Availability.** A hospital can decline a directive that requires a service it does not offer, such as a particular medication or a bed on a given date.
3. **Medical judgment.** Providers may override a refusal if they find it unsafe in the circumstances, and some statutes allow this when you are a danger to yourself or others.

A good PAD takes this into account. Rather than refusing everything, it says what you prefer and why, and gives the team alternatives. A document that offers a workable path tends to be followed. One that simply prohibits is more likely to be ignored.

## Who decides when it takes effect?

The document should say. Many PADs start when a physician, sometimes two, or a psychiatrist determines that you cannot understand or communicate treatment decisions. Others name a trusted person who can trigger it earlier. Our page on [when a healthcare directive takes effect](/learn/healthcare-directives/when-does-a-healthcare-directive-take-effect) describes the usual approaches, including why a switch-style trigger matters for conditions that come and go.

Capacity is also decision-specific. You may be unable to decide on an admission and still fully able to choose your agent. If you have a cognitive condition as well, see [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

## How do you write one that works?

Follow these steps in order.

1. **Talk with your treatment team first.** Ask your psychiatrist or therapist what they would find useful, and whether they will sign or witness your document where the law allows.
2. **Choose your agent carefully.** You need someone steady, available and willing to disagree with relatives. Read how to [choose a healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent), and name an alternate.
3. **Write your preferences in concrete terms.** Name medications and doses where you can. Describe warning signs in your own words.
4. **Sign it correctly.** Follow your state's witness and notary rules. Many states bar your doctor or a family member from being a witness. See our note on [notarization](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary).
5. **Sign a HIPAA release.** Your agent and treatment team need to talk to each other. A [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) matters here, because mental health records often have extra privacy protections.
6. **Distribute copies.** To your agent, alternate, psychiatrist, primary care doctor, the hospital you prefer and a trusted family member. Keep one in your wallet or phone.
7. **Review it.** After each hospitalization or medication change, ask whether it still matches reality.

## Who is a PAD for?

Anyone with a history of episodes that impair judgment, anyone newly diagnosed with a condition that may do so, and caregivers of someone who is. It is not only for people with a serious mental illness. Someone who has had severe postpartum depression, or who has had a bad reaction to a psychiatric medication, can have good reason to write one.

A short example. Joel, 34, has bipolar I disorder. Two years ago he was hospitalized during a manic episode, given a medication that made him ill and held in a facility three hours from home. He now has a directive naming his sister, Rena, as agent. It lists the medication to avoid, the hospital he prefers and the symptoms Rena should watch for. It tells staff to call his employer only through Rena. It also says to feed his cat. When the next episode starts, Rena has the document in her hands and a doctor who has read it. The episode is still hard. The decisions around it are his own.
