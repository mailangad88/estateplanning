Few decisions are harder than those made at the end of life, and fewer still are made well in a hospital hallway under stress. A living will lets you decide in advance, calmly, and write down what matters to you so that your family does not have to guess.

This article explains what a living will is, what it should say, how to sign it, and where it falls short. It is one of the documents in our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

For how a living will differs from a trust and a will, see [living will vs. living trust vs. will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will-vs-living-trust-vs-will); for signing rules, see [does a living will need a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary).

## What is a living will?

A living will is a written statement of your wishes about medical treatment if you cannot speak for yourself, and it is one type of [advance directive](/glossary/advance-directive). The [living will](/glossary/living-will) takes effect only when you lack the capacity to decide and your condition fits the conditions you described. The name is confusing, since it has little in common with a last will. States use other names, including "declaration," "healthcare directive," "natural death act directive" or "medical directive."

Typical situations a living will addresses are:

- A terminal illness, where death is expected within a limited time even with treatment
- Permanent unconsciousness, such as an irreversible coma or persistent vegetative state
- In some forms, advanced dementia or an end-stage condition where you could not recognize your family

## What decisions can a living will cover?

The most useful forms cover a short list of treatments in plain language. You decide, for each, whether you would want it, never want it, or want it only for a limited time trial.

| Treatment | What it is | Questions to think about |
|---|---|---|
| CPR | Chest compressions and electric shock to restart a stopped heart | Would you want it if your chances of recovery to your current abilities were slim? |
| Mechanical ventilation | A machine that breathes for you through a tube | Would you accept it short term for recovery, but not long term? |
| Artificial nutrition and hydration | Feeding by tube | Would you want it if you could never eat on your own again? |
| Dialysis | Machine that cleans blood when kidneys fail | Would you want ongoing dialysis if you were permanently unconscious? |
| Antibiotics and transfusions | Treating infections and blood loss | Would you want treatment for infections if the underlying illness is terminal? |
| Pain and comfort care | Medication and hospice | Do you want comfort prioritized even if it hastens death or causes drowsiness? |
| Organ and tissue donation | Donation after death | Do you want to donate, and are you registered? |

Be careful with absolute words. "No heroic measures" and "I never want to be kept alive on machines" sound clear, but doctors cannot act on them without interpretation. A better form says what you want to avoid and what you want to protect.

## How do you write one that actually helps?

Concrete values help doctors and family much more than a checklist alone. Consider writing a short paragraph in your own words. For example:

"What matters most to me is being able to recognize my family and communicate with them. If I am permanently unconscious or in a condition where I cannot recognize my children and there is no reasonable expectation of recovery, I do not want treatments whose main effect is to prolong dying. I want to be kept comfortable and, if possible, at home."

Then answer the specific questions. A few tips:

1. **Think about outcomes, not procedures.** Some people would accept a ventilator for two weeks but not two months. Say so.
2. **Address uncertainty.** If a doctor is unsure about your prognosis, do you want a time-limited trial?
3. **Address pain.** Many people want comfort care even if it makes them drowsy.
4. **Mention religion or spiritual wishes** if they affect your decisions, including specific rites or a chaplain.
5. **Say where you hope to be,** such as home or hospice, if possible.
6. **Pair it with a healthcare agent** who knows your values. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## What does a living will not do?

A living will has limits, and it helps to know them in advance.

- **It is not a medical order.** A living will tells your doctors about your wishes, but it is not an order that emergency crews follow in the field. For that you need a [DNR order or a POLST form](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).
- **It cannot foresee every scenario.** Real illnesses rarely match the form. That is why a person who can interpret it matters.
- **It cannot require doctors to do something unlawful or medically inappropriate.** A doctor who objects on conscience grounds generally must help transfer your care.
- **It does not cover money or property.** That is the role of a [financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) and your will or trust.
- **It does not give your family access to your medical records,** unless you also sign a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization).
- **It is not a substitute for conversation.** Written words are strongest when your family has already heard them.

## How do you sign a living will?

Each state sets its own rules, but a common pattern is that you must be a competent adult and sign in front of two adult witnesses or a notary. Some states put limits on who may witness. A witness commonly cannot be your healthcare agent, a relative, someone who inherits from you, or an employee of your care facility, though the details vary. Because the details differ, use a form designed for your state or have a lawyer prepare it.

Here are the steps:

1. Talk with your doctor about the treatments and your health conditions.
2. Choose your healthcare agent, and a backup, if your state's form combines the two documents.
3. Complete the form, adding your personal statement.
4. Sign in front of the required witnesses or a notary.
5. Give copies to your agent, your family, your primary doctor and your hospital.
6. Ask for the document to be added to your medical chart.
7. Keep the original somewhere your family can find it quickly. Do not lock it in a safe deposit box. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) for general storage principles, though the same advice about accessibility applies to medical documents.
8. Review it every few years, or after a major diagnosis or life change.

Some states keep registries where you can file a directive so that hospitals can find it. Ask your state health department or your lawyer whether your state has one.

## Can you change or cancel a living will?

Yes, as long as you have capacity. You can revoke it by tearing it up, signing a new one, telling your doctor, or in many states, simply stating your wishes. The safest method is a signed written revocation and a new document, with copies sent to everyone who had the old one. If your wishes change, update the agent, doctor and any registry at the same time.

## What happens if you do not have one?

Without a living will, decisions fall to a surrogate under state law. Many states have a priority list, such as spouse, adult children, parents, siblings, and then close friends, while others require a court to appoint someone. The surrogate has to guess what you would have wanted. Families can disagree, doctors may hesitate, and a dispute can end up in court. Take Henry, 68, who has a severe stroke. His wife believes he would want everything done; his two sons believe he would not. Without Henry's written wishes, the hospital ethics committee gets involved, and the family spends weeks in conflict at his bedside.

A short, thoughtful document and a conversation avoid that outcome.

## Who should have a living will?

Every adult, not only the elderly or the ill. Accidents and sudden illness do not check birthdays. A young adult may have a simple version, naming an agent and stating a basic preference for comfort care in a permanent coma, while someone with a chronic condition may want a detailed one. If you are in the early stages of dementia, timing matters; see [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

If you have a serious illness now, talk to your doctor about whether a POLST form, which is a medical order rather than a personal statement, is appropriate.

## How does a living will fit with the rest of your plan?

A complete plan has a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney), a living will, a HIPAA authorization, a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), and a will or trust. Our [core documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) shows how they work together. Some states combine the healthcare agent and living will into one form called an advance directive, which is fine so long as it is signed correctly.

Rules vary by state, including the form, witness rules and what happens with pregnancy provisions. For an overview of your own state's context, see our guides such as the [Illinois estate planning page](/estate-planning/illinois) or [Washington page](/estate-planning/washington).
