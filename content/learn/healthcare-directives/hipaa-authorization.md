When someone is hospitalized, relatives often show up at the desk asking how their parent is doing, and hear, "I'm sorry, I can't share that." It sounds like a rule written to frustrate them. In reality, it is a privacy law applied cautiously, and a short form can fix the problem.

This article explains what the federal medical privacy rule actually says, who can see your information, and how to prepare an authorization that works in a crisis. It belongs with the rest of your [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What is HIPAA, and why does it block families?

HIPAA is the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996, and its Privacy Rule governs how healthcare providers, health plans and clearinghouses (called covered entities) use and disclose your protected health information. The rule exists to keep your records private, including from relatives.

The rule is not as rigid as many people think. It permits providers to share information with family and friends involved in your care, if you agree, if you have a chance to object and do not, or if you are unable to agree and the provider decides sharing is in your best interest. But providers are risk-averse. Hospital privacy officers train staff to say no unless there is paperwork on file. The result is that a spouse may learn very little while a patient is unconscious. Fear of penalties, not the text of the law, drives the refusal.

Real situations show the problem:

- A woman in her 40s is in the ICU after a car accident and her parents cannot get a status update.
- A man with early dementia is seeing a neurologist and his daughter, who manages his finances, cannot learn the diagnosis she needs to put his accounts under her authority.
- An adult child needs billing information to resolve a dispute with an insurer for a parent.

## What does a HIPAA authorization do?

A [HIPAA authorization](/glossary/hipaa-authorization) is your written, signed permission to disclose specified health information to named persons. Federal rules require it to have certain elements:

1. A specific description of the information to be disclosed
2. The name of the person or class of persons authorized to disclose
3. The name of the person or class of persons to receive it
4. A description of the purpose, which can be "at the request of the individual"
5. An expiration date or event
6. A statement of your right to revoke it in writing
7. A statement about whether treatment can be conditioned on signing
8. A statement that information disclosed may be redisclosed and no longer protected by HIPAA
9. Your signature and date

A form missing these elements may be rejected. Many hospitals have their own form, and some insist on it, so ask them whether they will also honor yours.

## How is it different from a healthcare power of attorney?

They solve different problems and work best together.

| | HIPAA authorization | Healthcare power of attorney |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Access to information | Authority to decide |
| Who it names | Anyone you want to receive records | The person who decides for you |
| Effective | Usually immediately | When you lack capacity (often) |
| Can name many people | Yes | Usually one at a time, with backups |
| Legal basis | Federal privacy rule | State law |

Under HIPAA, a person with legal authority to make healthcare decisions for you, such as a healthcare agent, is generally treated as your personal representative and can receive your information as you could, but only within the scope of that authority, and sometimes only after it is triggered. See our article on the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney). A separate authorization avoids arguments about whether the agent's authority has started and covers family members who are not agents.

## Who should you name?

Name every person who might need information in an emergency. At minimum:

- Your healthcare agent and backups
- Your spouse or partner
- Adult children
- Your financial agent, especially if your power of attorney is springing
- Your executor or successor trustee, in case they need records after death
- Your attorney, if you want them to be able to confirm capacity

You can name people individually, so that a particular sibling is excluded. You can also limit categories, for example by excluding psychotherapy notes or substance use treatment records, which have extra protection under separate rules. Federal rules treat psychotherapy notes and certain substance use disorder records specially, and most states have their own rules for mental health, HIV status and genetic information, so a lawyer should tailor the form if these matter to you.

## Why does it matter for a springing power of attorney?

A springing power of attorney starts when a physician certifies incapacity. For the physician to speak to your agent and give that certification, you need to give permission in advance. Without it, the agent cannot get the document that proves the trigger. A HIPAA release should be signed at the same time as the power of attorney, and the power of attorney should refer to it. Our article on [springing vs. immediate powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) describes the delays that occur when this is overlooked.

## How do you set one up?

The process is short.

1. **Decide whom to name** and what to share.
2. **Choose a form.** Your lawyer can include a HIPAA authorization with your estate planning documents. Hospitals and doctors also have their own forms.
3. **Make it effective immediately.** A release that waits for incapacity can bring back the same delay problem.
4. **Choose a duration.** Federal rules require an expiration date or event. Common choices are a number of years or "until revoked in writing." Some providers reject open-ended forms, so be ready to update.
5. **Sign and date it.** Witnesses are not required by federal law, but a notary can help, and some providers prefer it.
6. **Give copies** to each named person, your primary doctor and any other doctors you see regularly, and ask them to add it to your chart.
7. **Review it** every few years and after any change in relationships.

Where there are multiple providers, you may have to deliver the form to each one. Patient portals also let you add authorized contacts.

## What are the limits?

An authorization is helpful but not magic.

- It only controls what covered entities may share. It does not require them to talk, though most do once they have a valid form.
- It does not give authority to make decisions.
- It may not cover records held by non-covered entities, such as some employers or life insurers.
- Some states and facilities have stricter rules for certain types of information.
- It does not undo past disclosures if revoked.
- It does not replace a state-specific surrogate statute that gives family members authority.

## Can you revoke it?

Yes. You can revoke a HIPAA authorization at any time by written notice to the provider. The revocation applies going forward, not to information already shared. Send it to every provider that holds your form, and tell the named people. If you divorce or have a falling out with someone you named, make revoking the release part of the update.

## What happens after death?

HIPAA continues to protect your health information for 50 years after you die. The person with authority to act for your estate, such as an executor or administrator, is generally treated as a personal representative and can request records. In some situations, such as family members who were involved in care, providers are allowed to share information relevant to that involvement unless you had expressed a contrary wish. An executor who needs records, for example to support a life insurance claim, should bring letters of authority. Our guide on [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) covers the usual paperwork.

## What common mistakes should you avoid?

1. Assuming a healthcare power of attorney is enough by itself
2. Making the release effective only on incapacity
3. Forgetting to add the financial agent
4. Signing a release with a short expiration and never renewing it
5. Naming only one person
6. Not giving copies to the hospital
7. Overlooking separate rules for mental health or substance use records
8. Failing to update after divorce or estrangement
