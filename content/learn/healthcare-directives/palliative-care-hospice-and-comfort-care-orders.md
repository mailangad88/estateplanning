Families hear three words in the same hospital conversation and often take them to mean one thing: palliative, hospice and comfort care. A doctor says "I'd like to bring in palliative care," and a daughter hears "she's dying." A nurse asks about "comfort measures," and a husband thinks he is being asked to give up.

The words overlap, but they are different services with different timing, different rules and different decisions behind them. Knowing the difference lets you write a directive that says what you mean. It also lets your family say yes to help without feeling they have said yes to the end. This guide fits with our overview of [healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What is palliative care?

Palliative care is care for symptoms, pain and stress that comes with a serious illness. It is provided by a team that can include doctors, nurses, social workers and chaplains, and it works alongside your regular doctors.

The key point is timing. You do not have to be dying. A person with heart failure, cancer, lung disease or advanced kidney disease can receive palliative care for years while also receiving treatments meant to extend life. The team helps with pain, breathlessness, nausea, anxiety, and with hard conversations about what matters most.

## What is hospice?

Hospice is a program of comfort-focused care for people near the end of life. Under the Medicare hospice benefit, which many private plans resemble, a doctor certifies that the patient is expected to live about six months or less if the illness runs its normal course. The patient then chooses comfort care rather than treatment aimed at curing the terminal illness.

Hospice usually comes to the patient, whether at home, in a nursing facility or in a dedicated hospice unit. A team covers nursing, aides, medication for symptoms, equipment, counseling and support for family. Choosing it is not permanent. A patient who improves, or who decides to pursue treatment again, can leave hospice and return later if still eligible. A prognosis is an estimate, and some people live longer than six months in hospice.

## What is a comfort-care-only order?

This is where the confusion tends to start. "Comfort care only," sometimes written as "comfort measures only," is a medical order or a description of the care plan in a hospital or nursing facility. It tells the team to focus on comfort and not to start treatments meant to prolong life. It may mean no ventilator, no intensive care transfer and no resuscitation, though the details differ between hospitals.

Because it is an order, a clinician writes it. Your [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) and your agent can ask for it, but the order itself comes from a doctor or advanced practice clinician after a conversation. That is also true of a do-not-resuscitate order, covered in our page on [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).

## How do they compare?

| | Palliative care | Hospice | Comfort-care-only order |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A service and a team | A program and a benefit | A medical order or care plan |
| When it can start | Any stage of a serious illness | Typically the last months, with a physician certification | Whenever the patient or agent and the clinician agree |
| Alongside curative treatment? | Yes | Generally not for the terminal illness | Usually no life-prolonging treatment |
| Who decides | Patient and care team | Patient or agent, with the hospice physician | Patient or agent, and the clinician who signs |
| Can it be reversed? | Yes | Yes, by leaving hospice | Yes, by a new order |

## What is a code status?

A code status is the resuscitation order in your chart. Full code means that if your heart stops or you stop breathing, staff will attempt CPR and related measures. A do-not-resuscitate order, or DNR, means they will not. A separate do-not-intubate order, or DNI, means they will not place a breathing tube. The orders are independent, so a person can choose one without the other.

Code status is discussed at hospital admission, and it is a sensible moment to check that the chart matches your directive. If you are at home, an out-of-hospital DNR or a POLST form is what emergency medical staff look for. A directive on its own, tucked in a drawer, will usually not stop an ambulance crew.

## What should your directive say about these choices?

You do not need to decide, today, that you will enter hospice. But you can write what you would want in plain language, so your agent is not guessing. Useful statements include:

- Whether you want palliative care involved early if you are seriously ill, even while you are still being treated.
- At what point, if any, you would prefer comfort over life-prolonging treatment, described by outcomes rather than diagnoses. For example: "If I cannot recognize my family and doctors do not expect me to recover, I want comfort care only."
- Where you want to be, such as at home, if that is possible.
- Whether you want pain control to take priority even if it makes you sleepier.
- Who should be allowed to ask for a hospice evaluation if you cannot.

Phrases like "no heroic measures" sound clear and are not. Two doctors can interpret them differently. Specific is better, as we explain in [how to document your end-of-life wishes](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-document-your-end-of-life-wishes).

## What does Medicare cover?

Medicare covers a voluntary advance care planning conversation, where a doctor or qualified provider helps you discuss and complete a directive. It is also part of the annual wellness visit, with no cost to you when the provider accepts Medicare's payment terms. Outside the wellness visit, ordinary cost-sharing may apply, so ask the office first.

Medicare also covers the hospice benefit for those who qualify, including the care team, medication related to the terminal illness and equipment. Rules for room and board in a facility, and which medications count as related, are specific. Read the hospice's admission papers carefully and ask what is not included. For nursing home costs more broadly, see our [elder care guide](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## Can an agent choose hospice for you?

If you cannot speak for yourself, a healthcare agent named in your [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) can usually consent to a hospice evaluation and to comfort-focused orders, subject to your written instructions and state law. If you have no agent, a surrogate under your state's default rules may be asked, which is a worse outcome for everyone. Our page on [what happens without an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive) describes that order.

## A worked example

Hannah, 72, has advanced lung disease. Her pulmonologist adds a palliative care team. Hannah keeps her treatments and gets help with breathlessness and a plan for bad nights. She still hopes for time with her grandchildren. Her directive states that she wants palliative care involved now, that she wants to stay at home if it is safe, and that if she needs a ventilator with little hope of recovery she would rather not have one. It names her son Andre as agent and Maya, her daughter, as alternate.

Eight months later, an infection puts her in the hospital. Andre has the directive. He asks that she not be intubated, and the team writes a DNI order that matches Hannah's words. Later, her doctor says her prognosis is under six months. Andre, following what she wrote, asks for a hospice evaluation. Hannah goes home with hospice. Nobody had to guess, and nobody was surprised.
