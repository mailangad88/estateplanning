You can write a perfect living will and still have a bad outcome if the wrong person is standing at the bedside. When you cannot speak, your healthcare agent speaks for you. That person will talk with surgeons, sign consent forms, and sometimes have to say no to a doctor, a sibling or a crying parent. The job is harder than it looks on the form, and the name you write in the box deserves more thought than most people give it.

This article offers a way to choose, what to ask the person, and what to tell them. It builds on the basics in our article on the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and the wider guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What does a healthcare agent actually do?

A healthcare agent makes medical decisions for you once your doctor finds you unable to make them yourself. The agent can consent to or refuse treatment, pick a hospital or nursing facility, approve or stop medications, access medical records, and in many states decide about organ donation or autopsy if you have not addressed it. The agent follows your instructions first. Where you were silent, the agent decides based on what you would have chosen, and if that is truly unknown, on your best interests.

The legal term is [agent](/glossary/agent), and in some states the role is a [healthcare proxy](/glossary/healthcare-proxy). The role is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary) one, meaning the agent must put your interests ahead of their own.

## Six questions that sort candidates

Do not start with a list of relatives. Start with these questions and see who is left.

1. **Will this person do what I say, even if they disagree?** An agent who thinks you are making a mistake may quietly override you. You want someone who will say, "It is not what I would choose, but it is what she told me."
2. **Can this person take pressure?** Hospitals can be loud, fast and emotional. A good agent asks questions without panicking and can hold a position when a relative is shouting.
3. **Can this person get there?** Many decisions happen within hours. An agent who lives three time zones away can decide by phone but cannot sit with the doctors, and that has a cost.
4. **Does this person know my values?** If not, they can learn. If they will not listen, that answers the question.
5. **Is this person likely to be healthy and alive when I need them?** An 82-year-old spouse is a loving choice and a risky one if you are 80. Younger backups matter.
6. **Will this person get along with the rest of the family?** Not essential, but a calm agent who can keep siblings informed prevents years of resentment.

Notice what is not on the list: who is oldest, who is closest, who feels hurt if skipped. Those are family questions, not decision questions.

## Common candidates, honestly assessed

| Candidate | Often strong when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse or partner | Knows you well, will be present, first in line by default | May be too grieved to refuse treatment; may be frail |
| Adult child | Lives nearby, calm, shares your views | Siblings may feel passed over; a child may feel guilty making an end-of-life call |
| Sibling | Same generation, long shared history | Often close to your age, so health and availability matter |
| Close friend | Understands you better than your family does | Some hospitals defer to relatives unless the document is clear; name them clearly and carry a copy |
| Clergy or trusted advisor | Shares spiritual framework | May have conflicts, or no standing with your relatives |
| Professional fiduciary | No family in the picture | Costs money and the person will not know you personally |

If you are unmarried or estranged from relatives, the naming matters even more. Without a document, state law decides who speaks for you, and a partner of twenty years may rank behind a sibling you have not seen in a decade. Our article on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) covers why.

## Example: two siblings, one decision

Daniel is 66, widowed, with two daughters. Anna lives ten minutes away and is a nurse. Beth lives overseas and is the more affectionate of the two. Daniel's instinct is to name Beth because he does not want Anna to feel she is "always the one stuck with it." Then he thinks about it from the hospital's side. The decision-maker has to be reachable, steady and comfortable asking medical questions. He names Anna as agent and Beth as backup, tells Beth why, and asks Anna to keep Beth on every update call. Both daughters know the plan. Neither is surprised.

The point is not that nurses make better agents. It is that Daniel made the choice by function, then did the family work separately.

## How to ask

Do it in person if you can. A request by text invites a casual yes.

- Explain what the job involves, including that it could mean refusing a treatment the agent hopes you will accept.
- Offer an easy exit. "If you do not want to do this, tell me now and I will be fine."
- Ask whether they are willing to follow your instructions even if someone else in the family disagrees.
- Ask who else they would want involved.

If they say yes, put the name in the form with their full legal name and contact details. If they hesitate, believe the hesitation.

## What to tell them once they say yes

The form is a half-measure without the conversation. Cover these topics, in plain language, and then write a few lines down and give a copy to your agent.

- What matters most to you: independence, being at home, recognizing family, avoiding pain, time with a particular person.
- Treatments you would never want, and ones you would try for a limited time.
- How you feel about being kept alive on a ventilator or feeding tube if recovery is unlikely.
- Where you want to be at the end, and whether hospice appeals to you.
- Any religious or cultural practices that should be followed.
- Where the documents are and who has copies.

For help with the harder questions, see [the end-of-life wishes guide](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-document-your-end-of-life-wishes). If you or a parent is facing cognitive decline, the earlier these talks happen the better, as discussed in [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

## Backup agents and joint agents

Always name at least one alternate. The first agent might be unreachable, ill, divorced from you, or unwilling when the moment comes. Without an alternate, the hospital goes to your state's default list of surrogates, and the person at the top may be exactly who you did not want.

Naming two people to act together sounds fair and often fails. If they disagree, the hospital has no clear decision-maker, and a delay is the likely result. If you want two people involved, name one as agent and ask that person to consult the other. Some states do not let co-agents act independently at all.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Naming a person without asking them.
- Choosing a spouse who is also your only caregiver and may be in the next hospital bed.
- Naming all your children "equally" with no tiebreaker.
- Listing an agent who has moved, changed names or died since you signed.
- Giving the agent no copy, so they cannot prove their authority when asked.
- Forgetting the [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization), which lets providers share information with the people you name.

Check your choice again after any divorce, family move, diagnosis or conflict.
