You want a living will before the next surgery or the next birthday. Before you pick a path, you want to know the price. Do you need an attorney, or will a form from the hospital do?

We do not quote exact prices on this page. Fees differ by firm and place, and an invented average helps nobody. What we can do is show what you pay for in each route, so you can compare a quote when you get one. This page belongs to our [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives), and the document itself is explained in the [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) article.

## How much does a living will cost?

It ranges from no charge to a flat attorney fee, depending on the route. The four common ones:

| Route | What you pay | What you get | Where it falls short |
|---|---|---|---|
| State or hospital form | Usually nothing | A valid statutory form | You supply the choices and the signing |
| Five Wishes or a nonprofit booklet | A small fee for the booklet, if any | Plain-language values questions | Must still meet your state's signing rules |
| Online document service | A fixed price | Guided questions and a print-out | No review of your family situation |
| Attorney | A flat fee, often bundled | Drafting, signing supervision, copies | Highest cost; worth it for complex families |

The honest answer for most healthy adults is that the form is cheap and the thinking is the work. Our comparison of [free forms, Five Wishes and an attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/advance-directive-forms-five-wishes-or-a-lawyer) goes deeper on that choice.

## What are the real costs besides the form?

Four small items add up.

1. **Witnesses or a notary.** Illinois requires two witnesses for a living will. New Jersey requires either two adult witnesses or a notary or other authorized official (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-56). Neighbors and coworkers do this for free. A notary charges a modest fee that state law caps, and some banks do it for customers at no charge.
2. **Copies.** Print enough for your agent, your alternate, your doctor, your hospital and yourself.
3. **Time.** A hospital social worker can walk you through the form in an hour. An attorney visit takes longer but adds drafting.
4. **A health care power of attorney.** If you only budget for a living will, you have bought the weaker document. The agent named in a power of attorney handles everything the living will does not foresee. Illinois' health care power of attorney needs one adult witness and no notary, and the New Jersey proxy directive uses the same two-witness-or-notary rule.

Notice the signing rules. A free form that is signed in front of the wrong witness is a document that a hospital may question. See [whether a living will needs a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary) for the details.

## Who pays for a lawyer to prepare healthcare directives?

You do, unless you qualify for free legal help. A health care directive is a personal document that you sign, and there is no insurer that covers drafting it. But you have routes that cost little:

- **Legal aid and senior legal services.** Many serve people over 60 or with low income and prepare directives at no charge.
- **Bar association and law school clinics.** These are sometimes available on set days.
- **Hospital and hospice social workers.** They give forms free and answer basic questions. They cannot give legal advice.
- **Medicare's annual wellness visit.** In many cases it includes an advance care planning conversation with your doctor, which is not the same as legal drafting but gets your wishes clear.

If you hire an attorney, ask for a flat fee in writing, and ask whether it includes the health care power of attorney, a HIPAA release, signing supervision and certified copies. A directive drafted as part of a full plan is usually cheaper than drafted alone. Our guide to [how much estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost) shows how fees are structured.

## When is paying a lawyer worth it?

Not always. Here is a rule of thumb from our attorneys.

**A free form is usually enough when:**
- You are healthy and your family agrees about who should speak for you.
- Your wishes are simple, such as no prolonged life support if you are permanently unconscious.
- You live in one state and will sign with proper witnesses.

**An attorney is worth the fee when:**
- Your family is divided, blended or estranged, and you worry about who would challenge your agent.
- You have a serious diagnosis, early dementia or an upcoming surgery.
- You split time between states or own property in two.
- You want specific instructions, such as a refusal of a feeding tube, that the form does not handle cleanly.
- You want the directive to fit with a will, trust and power of attorney.

## An example

This is invented, and the figures are placeholders for the shape of a decision, not prices. Naomi, 63, lives in Hoboken, New Jersey. She gets a hospital form for free and has two neighbors witness it. Cost to her: a few dollars in printing. Her husband is her representative and her brother is the alternate.

Her friend Elliott, 58, has an estranged adult son and a partner of ten years, and he wants his partner to decide. He asks an attorney for a flat-fee quote that includes the directive, a power of attorney and a HIPAA release, and his partner is named as health care representative with the son's possible objection addressed in writing. Elliott pays more than Naomi. He is paying for the plan to hold up when someone challenges it.

## Why do free forms sometimes fail?

The form is not what fails. The signing is, or the content is. We see four patterns. The document was signed with only one witness where two are required. A witness was also the named agent, which most state forms forbid. The form was from another state and the hospital asked questions. Or the instructions were so general that a doctor could not tell whether they applied to the situation in the hospital bed. Each of these is cheap to avoid and expensive to fix once the person cannot sign again.

If you use a free form, read the witness section first. In New Jersey the person you name as representative or alternate cannot witness, and each witness confirms you signed voluntarily and appear to be of sound mind. In Illinois the property and health care forms each carry their own witness rules, so use the form for the right document. After signing, hand copies to your agent and your doctor and ask for yours to be placed in your chart, as described in [where to keep your advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/where-to-keep-your-advance-directive).

## What does a quote usually include?

Use this checklist to compare a quote.

1. Drafting of a living will and a health care power of attorney.
2. A HIPAA authorization so your agent can see records now. See [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization).
3. Supervision of the signing with witnesses or a notary.
4. Copies and instructions for where to keep them.
5. An update if the law changes, or at a stated interval.
