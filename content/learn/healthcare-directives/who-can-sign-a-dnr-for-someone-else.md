Your father is 86 and in the hospital with pneumonia. He is confused and cannot follow a conversation. A doctor comes in and asks whether the family wants a DNR on his chart. You and your sister look at each other. Who is supposed to answer, and does it have to be unanimous?

It does not have to be unanimous, but it does have to be the right person. This page, part of our [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives), explains who has authority to sign a do-not-resuscitate order for another adult, what the Illinois and New Jersey laws add, and what to do when relatives disagree.

## Who can sign a DNR for someone else?

A DNR is a medical order. It always needs a clinician's signature, and for a patient who cannot decide, it also needs consent from someone with legal authority to speak for that patient. In rough order, that person is:

1. **The patient, if they can understand the decision.** Capacity beats everyone else's wishes.
2. **A health care agent named in the patient's power of attorney for health care.** This is the person the patient chose.
3. **A court-appointed guardian,** where one has been named.
4. **A surrogate recognized by state law,** which is the next of kin in a priority order when the patient has no agent or guardian.

Doctors and hospitals check this chain whether the family does or not. If the patient had signed a directive naming an agent, that person's word controls, even over a spouse or an older child who would otherwise have a say. Our guide to the [health care power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) explains what that agent can do.

### What does Illinois say?

Illinois' POLST law lets consent for a POLST or DNR-type order come from the individual, a person acting at the individual's direction, a guardian, a health care agent or a surrogate (755 ILCS 40/65). Section 20(h) of the Health Care Surrogate Act lets a surrogate sign a POLST to forgo life-sustaining treatment, but only if the patient lacks decisional capacity and has a qualifying condition. That means a terminal condition, permanent unconsciousness, or an incurable or irreversible condition, certified in writing by the attending physician and at least one other qualified practitioner.

The surrogate should follow what the patient would have wanted and use best interests if those wishes are unknown. The Act does not apply if the patient objects, unless the surrogate is a court-appointed guardian. For the priority list, see [who decides under the Illinois Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).

### What does New Jersey say?

New Jersey uses the POLST as the portable order. A form needs the patient's signature if the patient has capacity, or the representative's signature if not, plus the signature and date of the attending physician, physician assistant or advance practice nurse. It does not require a notary or two witnesses. Our article on the [New Jersey POLST](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-polst-form-who-qualifies-who-signs-and-how-it-works) walks through the form.

## Can family members cancel a DNR?

Family members cannot cancel a valid order merely because they disagree. Who can change it depends on who holds the authority.

| Person | Can sign a new DNR? | Can revoke one? |
|---|---|---|
| Patient with capacity | Yes | Yes, at any time |
| Health care agent | Yes, within the directive's terms | Yes, if the patient lacks capacity and the directive allows |
| Guardian | Yes, with court rules for end-of-life | Yes, within the same limits |
| Surrogate (Illinois) | Yes, if the Act's conditions are met | Subject to the same conditions |
| Adult child without authority | No | No |
| New Jersey representative | Yes | Only if the patient authorized it on the form |

In New Jersey, if the patient has signed a newer directive that conflicts with the POLST, the clinician must honor the more recent document. In Illinois, the POLST applies when the patient cannot speak for themselves or has no pulse; a patient who can speak can accept or decline care differently from the form.

## What happens if the family disagrees?

The usual path is an ethics consultation inside the hospital. Both states allow facilities to resolve disputes about capacity or about how a form applies through their own procedures, and a court is the final step. In practice, the process runs like this.

1. Ask the attending physician to explain the patient's condition in plain terms, with the specific question the order is deciding.
2. Ask the nurse or social worker who the legal decision-maker is and request a copy of the directive on file.
3. If relatives dispute the agent's authority, ask for an ethics committee meeting.
4. If a dispute about authority cannot be resolved, a guardianship petition may be needed.

A court case is slow and rarely helps in a crisis. Planning helps more. Our article on [who can overrule your healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive) lists what to put in the document to keep the issue from arising.

## An example

Dolores, 79, lives in Trenton, New Jersey. She has advanced heart failure. Two years ago her doctor completed a POLST with her that says no CPR. Her daughter Anita is her named representative. Her son Kevin lives out of state and is upset when he hears about it.

During an admission, Dolores is too sleepy to speak. Kevin tells the nurse to take the DNR off. The nurse says Anita is the representative. Kevin has no authority to revoke the order and Dolores did not authorize any representative to change it. The team offers an ethics consultation instead. Kevin joins by phone, hears Dolores's own words from the form and the conversation Anita recalls, and the order stays. Two weeks later Dolores is alert and confirms it herself.

## What should you ask the doctor before consenting?

A signature on a DNR is easier to give, and to live with, when you know what the doctor expects. Ask these before you sign.

- What is the diagnosis, and what is the realistic outcome of CPR for someone in this condition?
- Would CPR be likely to cause injury, such as broken ribs, and would a breathing machine follow?
- Does the order apply only to the heart stopping, or does it also limit intubation or transfer to intensive care?
- Is there a medical order that will follow the patient home or to a nursing home?
- What did the patient say before, in writing or aloud, and is a living will on file?

Write the answers down, with the date and the doctor's name. If you are a surrogate or agent, you are expected to speak for the patient, not for yourself. A decision built on the patient's own past words is easier to defend later than one made from fear or guilt. It helps to say to the family, "this is what Dad told me in 2023." Hospital chaplains and palliative care teams often join these conversations and can lower the heat in the room.

## How do you set it up before a crisis?

1. Name a health care agent and an alternate.
2. Talk with them about CPR, ventilators and where you would want treatment to stop.
3. If you are seriously ill or frail, ask your doctor to complete a POLST with you.
4. Put copies where responders will see them and in your doctor's chart.
5. Review it yearly and after any hospitalization.

For a healthy adult, the first step is the agent, not the DNR. See [where to keep your advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/where-to-keep-your-advance-directive) and [hospice and comfort care](/learn/elder-care/assisted-living-memory-care-and-hospice-who-pays) for related decisions.
