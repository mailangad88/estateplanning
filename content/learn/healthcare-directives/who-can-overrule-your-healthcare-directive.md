You have signed the papers. You have named your daughter as agent. Then you picture the worst day: a doctor who disagrees, a brother who arrives from out of town with opinions, or a hospital staffer who says, "We do not have anything on file." Can any of them set your wishes aside?

The honest answer is not "never" and not "anytime." This article explains who has the power to override a directive, who does not, and what to do when it is ignored. It is part of our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## The short version

- Family members have no veto over a valid directive or your named agent.
- A hospital must generally respect your directive but may decline particular instructions for conscience or medical reasons and then must help you transfer.
- Directives fail in practice because they are missing, outdated or unclear, far more often than because someone defied them.
- A court is the final referee and can remove an agent who misuses the role.
- Your best protection is clear wording, an agent who knows your views, and copies in the medical record.

## Who can overrule a healthcare proxy?

No one has an automatic right to overrule your healthcare agent. While you cannot speak for yourself, the agent steps into your shoes for medical decisions, within the limits of your written instructions and state law. Hospital staff are expected to deal with the agent as they would deal with you.

That does not mean the agent is beyond challenge. Four routes exist for someone who believes the agent is wrong.

1. **Ethics committee.** A relative or clinician asks the hospital's ethics service to review. The committee advises on whether the agent's decision is consistent with your wishes and your best interests.
2. **Court petition.** A relative, a doctor or the hospital can ask a court to review the agent's authority or to appoint a guardian. Courts take these cases seriously and generally require evidence, such as proof that the agent is acting against the directive, has a conflict of interest, or is neglecting the role. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) for how courts view the two.
3. **Revocation by you.** If you regain capacity, you can revoke or change the appointment at any time.
4. **Adult protective services.** In cases of suspected abuse or exploitation, a report to your state's adult protective services agency can lead to investigation.

A relative who simply disagrees with the decision, even strongly, is not in a position to override it. Imagine Joan, 80, who names her son Eric as agent and declines a feeding tube in her directive. Her daughter Lisa wants everything done. Lisa can speak, plead and ask for an ethics review. She cannot direct the staff. Eric decides, guided by Joan's document. That is precisely what the document is for.

## Can a hospital ignore a living will?

A hospital cannot lawfully ignore a valid directive, but it can fail to find it, question it, or decline a specific instruction. Those are different things.

| Situation | What usually happens |
|---|---|
| Directive is not in the chart | Staff treat as default, often with full treatment, until it is produced |
| Directive is unclear or does not fit the facts | Clinicians ask the agent or family; an ethics review may follow |
| Clinician objects on conscience grounds | In many states, must disclose and transfer or help transfer |
| Instruction asks for care doctors consider medically inappropriate | Hospital policy and state law apply; the hospital may refuse and offer transfer |
| Doubt about validity (signature, witnesses, capacity) | Staff may ask for verification; court review is possible |
| Family demands a different course | Staff follow the directive and agent; escalate to ethics if the conflict persists |

Note the third and fourth rows. A directive protects you from unwanted treatment more reliably than it compels wanted treatment. A request such as "keep me on a ventilator no matter what" is not assured in the same way, because clinicians are not required to provide care they consider medically inappropriate. State law and hospital policy shape the outcome, and the details vary by state.

## What to do if your directive is being ignored

If you are the agent, or a family member trying to help, here is the order we would follow.

1. **Put the document in front of the right person.** Ask for the attending physician, not just the nurse. Hand over a copy and ask that it be scanned into the chart.
2. **Say who you are.** Introduce yourself as the healthcare agent, and give the date of the document.
3. **Ask for the plan in writing.** Many problems resolve when staff have to record their reasoning.
4. **Ask for the patient advocate or ethics service.** Most hospitals have both, reachable through the main switchboard.
5. **Ask about transfer.** If the provider will not honor the document, ask to be moved to one who will.
6. **Call an attorney.** If the situation is urgent, a lawyer can seek emergency court relief in some places, though timing is uncertain.
7. **Report persistent violations.** Your state health department or medical board takes complaints.

Keep notes: names, times, what was said. They help in any later review.

## How state law changes this

States differ on the rules that matter here, and the differences are not small.

- **Default surrogate lists.** If no agent is named or available, most states list who may decide: typically spouse, adult children, parents, siblings. Some states do not recognize unmarried partners or friends on that list, which is another reason to name an agent.
- **Conscience and transfer rules.** Most states allow a provider to decline certain instructions on moral grounds, and require notice and transfer. The details differ.
- **Interstate recognition.** Many states honor a directive validly executed in another state; others require it to be consistent with local law. Our article on [signing requirements for a living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary) explains how witness rules differ. If you split time between states, ask an attorney to check.
- **Statutory forms and presumptions.** Some states give a special presumption of validity when a statutory form is used.
- **Pregnancy exclusions.** Some states restrict the effect of a living will during pregnancy. Read your state's form.

Start with your [state guide](/estate-planning). For an example of one state's approach, see the [Illinois healthcare power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## How to make your directive hard to override

- **Use clear language.** "If two doctors find that I am permanently unconscious, do not provide a feeding tube" is better than "no heroic measures."
- **Name a backup agent.** Otherwise your default surrogates may take over when your first choice is out of reach.
- **Brief your agent.** An agent who has heard you say it can speak with confidence.
- **Use the right medical order.** For CPR and emergency situations, a [POLST or DNR](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) is what paramedics follow.
- **Sign properly.** Witnesses, notary and capacity as your state requires.
- **Share it.** Give copies to your doctor, hospital and agent, and keep the original accessible. Add a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so the people you name can get information.
- **Review it.** Update after a diagnosis or family change.

Not having any document is the weakest position of all, as we describe in [what happens when you are incapacitated without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney).
