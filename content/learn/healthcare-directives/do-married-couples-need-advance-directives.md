Ray and Dolores have been married for forty-four years. When Ray's heart doctor asked about a healthcare directive, they both said the same thing: "She's my wife, she'll decide." The nurse nodded and did not push. It is a sensible answer and an incomplete one.

Marriage does solve a lot. This article shows where it does not, and what a married couple should sign so that the default does not have to carry the whole weight. It is part of our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## Does marriage give your spouse authority to decide?

In most states, a spouse sits first or nearly first on the list of people a hospital turns to when a patient cannot decide and has named no agent. Illinois puts a guardian of the person first, then the spouse, then adult children, parents, siblings, grandchildren and close friends under the Health Care Surrogate Act. Our guide to the [Illinois surrogate order](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides) covers that list in detail. New Jersey lets you name a spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner as a health care representative in a proxy directive, so the paper is where the authority comes from.

In daily practice, a spouse at the bedside is rarely turned away. The trouble starts in the cases below.

## Where does the spousal default break down?

Five situations come up often enough that every couple should check them.

| Situation | Why the default struggles |
|---|---|
| Both spouses are in the same accident or hospital | There is no healthy spouse to decide, and the next relatives on the list may not know either of you well |
| Spouses are separated but not divorced | Some state lists skip a legally separated spouse, and hospitals hesitate |
| A second marriage with adult children from the first | Children may challenge the new spouse or demand a say |
| The spouse is frail, ill or has dementia | The default names a person who cannot do the job |
| The decision is large and contested | Hospitals often want a document before honoring a refusal of life support |

The first problem is the one people forget. Couples of the same age often get sick in the same decade. If Ray has a stroke while Dolores is recovering from a hip replacement, the person in the room might be their son, whom neither has briefed. A backup agent named in advance avoids that.

## What does the default not tell the doctors?

Authority and instructions are different things. A spouse by default has the right to be asked. The default does not say whether you would want a feeding tube after a stroke, or whether you would accept a ventilator for two weeks but not two months. Most spouses think they know. Relatives guess wrong more often than they expect, and the burden of guessing falls on the person who loves you most.

A [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) puts your own answers on paper, and a conversation with your spouse puts the reasons behind them. Our [list of questions to answer before writing a living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/questions-to-answer-before-writing-a-living-will) is a good place to start together.

## What each spouse should sign

Each spouse needs their own documents. They are not joint, and one cannot sign for the other.

1. **A healthcare power of attorney.** Name your spouse first if that is right for you, then a backup, ideally someone younger and from a different household. See the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) article.
2. **A living will.** State your wishes on life support, feeding tubes and comfort care.
3. **A HIPAA authorization.** Name your spouse and your backup so providers can discuss your care. Our article on the [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) explains why the privacy rule still matters inside a marriage.
4. **A POLST or DNR order, if you are seriously ill.** These are medical orders signed with a clinician, not documents a couple signs at a kitchen table. See [DNR and POLST orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).

Sign them separately. It is fine to meet with the attorney together, but each person's choices should be their own. A spouse who feels pressure to name the other as agent, or to match the other's choices on life support, has not signed a real directive.

## How do you have the conversation?

Most couples never have it because it feels like inviting bad luck. Make it small. Pick one scenario, such as a stroke that leaves one of you unable to speak or swallow, and each say aloud what you would want done at two weeks, at two months and at a year. Then swap. You will probably find a gap somewhere. Dolores learned that Ray would accept a feeding tube for a few weeks but not for good, which was not what she had assumed for forty-four years.

Write down the gaps, not just the agreements, and let the attorney or the form turn them into wording. Share the result with your backup agent and with any adult children, so the first time they hear your wishes is not in a corridor.

## What happens when spouses disagree with the rest of the family?

Hospitals tend to defer to a spouse over other relatives, and a signed agent over everyone. If a brother or an adult child challenges the spouse, staff may call the ethics service or ask for a court order, which is slow. A signed directive and healthcare power of attorney shorten that process because the hospital can point to your own words. See our article on [who can overrule a healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive) for the routes a dispute can take.

## Second marriages and blended families

The default list is blind to family history. A new spouse of five years may rank above three adult children who have known the patient for fifty. That can be exactly right, or it can be a source of fights. If you have children from an earlier marriage, talk to them about who your agent is and why, before the hospital does it for you. Our [blended family planning guide](/learn/blended-families) covers the wider issues.

Take Sam, 70, who remarried at 66. His daughters like his wife Joyce but fear she will withhold treatment too early. Sam names Joyce as agent, a daughter as backup, and writes his instructions clearly so that neither has to guess. Everyone knows what the paper says. The document does its job even when the family is not in agreement.

## Does divorce or separation change anything?

A divorce usually ends your ex-spouse's role as agent, but not in every state and not for every document. New Jersey's law, for one, provides that a spouse's designation ends on divorce, separation or dissolution of a civil union unless the directive says otherwise. Legal separation is treated differently from state to state. If your marriage is under strain, sign a new directive now rather than assuming the old one adjusts itself.

## A note on same-sex and unmarried couples

For an unmarried partner there is no spousal default in many states. That makes the document more important, not less. Our article on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) walks through the full picture.
