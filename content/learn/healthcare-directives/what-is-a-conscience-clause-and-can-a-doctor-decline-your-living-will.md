Marguerite, 79, has a living will that says no feeding tube if she is permanently unconscious. Her daughter just took her to a hospital with a cross on the sign. Will the staff follow the document?

Usually yes, or at least they will help move her to a place that will. A conscience clause is the rule that makes that "or at least" possible. This page explains what a conscience clause is, how far it goes, and what you can do in advance. It is general education for Illinois and New Jersey readers, not legal advice. For the broader set of conflicts, see our article on [who can overrule your healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive).

## What is a conscience clause in health care?

It is a legal or policy protection that lets a doctor, nurse or institution decline to take part in something they find morally or religiously unacceptable. Many states, and federal law, have versions. Illinois has the Health Care Right of Conscience Act, which protects both individuals and institutions that decline to provide certain services based on conscience. New Jersey's advance directive law and its POLST law each address what a provider must do when it objects to an instruction.

The clause is a shield for the provider. It is not a license to override the patient. Most of these laws say that a provider who will not comply must tell the patient or agent and must not abandon care. Think of it as a polite refusal with a duty to hand off, not a veto.

## What can a doctor or hospital decline, and what can't they?

In practice the disputes involve a small group of decisions.

| Instruction in your document | Can a provider decline on conscience grounds? | What should follow |
|---|---|---|
| Withdraw a ventilator when recovery is unlikely | Sometimes, depending on the facility's policy | Prompt notice and a transfer plan |
| Stop artificial nutrition and hydration | Sometimes, especially at religiously affiliated facilities | Prompt notice and a transfer plan |
| Provide comfort-focused sedation near death | Rare, and mostly a question of how it is described | Palliative care team review |
| Give medication for pain even if breathing slows | Generally no; this is accepted comfort care | Standard care |
| Withhold CPR under a DNR or POLST | Individual clinicians may object, but the facility is bound | Another clinician honors the order |
| Provide a treatment you requested that the doctor deems medically inappropriate | Not a conscience question; hospital policy governs | Ethics review and possible transfer |

What the clinician cannot do is pretend your document does not exist, give treatment you refused without telling you, or stop caring for you until a transfer happens. The patient stays a patient. If a hospital will not honor the document and cannot move you, take that as a signal to call an attorney the same day.

## How do religious hospitals handle living wills?

A religiously affiliated hospital or nursing home may have written rules on end-of-life care that are stricter than your state's default. Catholic hospitals commonly follow the Ethical and Religious Directives for Catholic Health Care Services, a public document. In many cases these facilities will honor a living will that refuses burdensome treatment, since the church distinguishes between ordinary and extraordinary means. Where they may draw a line is around withdrawing artificial nutrition and hydration in some cases, or around certain sedation practices.

New Jersey's POLST Act says that a religiously affiliated institution may decline to follow a policy contrary to its written rules, but it must disclose those policies and help transfer a patient if a conflict cannot be resolved. That duty to disclose is the useful lever. If the facility has a policy, it must be written down, and you are entitled to ask for it.

## How do you check before you are admitted?

You do not want to learn the policy at 2 a.m. Here is a short checklist.

1. **Identify the hospital or nursing home you are likely to use.** Where do your doctors have privileges? Where does the ambulance usually take you?
2. **Call patient relations or the ethics office.** Ask for the written policy on life-sustaining treatment, artificial nutrition and palliative sedation.
3. **Ask what happens if your wishes conflict with the policy.** The right answer is that they will tell you promptly and help transfer.
4. **Ask which other facilities nearby accept transfers.** Capacity matters; a bed has to exist.
5. **Add a line to your directive.** For example: "If any provider will not follow these instructions, my agent is authorized to arrange my transfer to one who will."
6. **Tell your agent.** The person you name should know that this may mean ambulance calls and paperwork, not just a bedside vigil.

## What can your healthcare agent do if a provider objects?

An agent has authority to act for you under the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney), and that includes arranging a different provider. The steps look like this.

1. Ask the physician to state the objection and record it in the chart.
2. Request the ethics consult and the patient advocate.
3. Ask the case manager to identify a receiving facility and confirm the date of transfer.
4. Keep notes of every name and time.
5. If a transfer is not happening and the patient is in distress, call an attorney, and in Illinois ask whether the surrogate rules in the [Illinois Health Care Surrogate Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides) affect who is making the decision.

Here is an example. Tomas, 71, has advanced heart failure and a POLST that says no intubation. He is admitted to a community hospital where one of the on-call physicians objects to withdrawing a ventilator after an emergency intubation that happened before the POLST was found. Tomas's daughter, who is his agent, asks the charge nurse for the patient advocate. Within the day, a second physician reviews the case, and the hospital arranges a transfer to its sister facility. Nobody had to go to court. The daughter had a copy of the POLST in her bag, and she asked for each step by name.

## What mistakes do families make?

Three come up again and again. The first is assuming that "a Catholic hospital will never honor a living will." Many do honor refusals of burdensome treatment, and treating the facility as an enemy before you have asked costs you goodwill. The second is leaving the document at home, so the staff cannot weigh it at all. The third is naming an agent who hates conflict. A person who will not argue with an attending physician is a poor choice for the one job where arguing, politely and repeatedly, is the job. Our guide to [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) covers that choice.

## What changes your odds?

A clear, current document with a named agent and an alternate does most of the work. Courts and ethics committees take specific instructions more seriously than general ones. "I do not want a feeding tube if I am permanently unconscious" is stronger than "no heroic measures."
