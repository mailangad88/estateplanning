Advance directive paperwork is one of the few legal documents that ordinary people sign on their own with good results. A free form from your state, filled in carefully and witnessed correctly, is a valid document. Plenty of families never need more.

That said, "valid" and "enough" are different questions. This article compares the three common routes, free state forms, the Five Wishes document and attorney-drafted directives, so you can pick the one that fits your life. It sits within our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What is an advance directive form, exactly?

An advance directive form is a pre-printed document that lets you name a healthcare agent, record your wishes about life-sustaining treatment, or both. Each state sets the legal requirements: how many witnesses, whether a notary is allowed or required, who may not witness, and sometimes the specific wording a hospital must honor. States that publish a statutory form usually say that using it creates a presumption the document is valid.

If you want the vocabulary first, read [advance directive vs. living will vs. healthcare proxy](/learn/healthcare-directives/advance-directive-vs-living-will-vs-healthcare-proxy).

## The three routes at a glance

| | Free state or hospital form | Five Wishes | Attorney-drafted directive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost | Free | A small fee per copy, with free sample use in some programs | Fee varies; often included in a full plan |
| Style | Checkboxes and short blanks | Plain-language booklet in five sections | Tailored text for your facts |
| Names an agent | Yes | Yes | Yes, with backups and instructions |
| Covers personal and spiritual wishes | Rarely | Yes, extensively | As you direct |
| State compliance | Built for that state | Accepted in many states; check yours | Drafted to your state |
| Handles complex family situations | Poorly | Moderately | Well |
| Best for | Simple wishes, supportive family | People who want to express values, not just medical choices | Complicated families, large estates, out-of-state ties |

Price and acceptance change, so check current details with the organization or your state before you rely on any of them.

## Free state and hospital forms

These are the default choice and a good one. They are designed to meet the state's signing requirements, and hospital staff know them on sight. A typical form asks you to name an agent and alternates, check boxes about CPR, ventilation, tube feeding and comfort care, and leave space for personal instructions.

Their limits are practical. The checkboxes are blunt, the space for notes is small, and the form assumes you understand terms like "persistent vegetative state" without explaining them. If you check a box you did not fully understand, your agent may be stuck defending an answer you did not mean.

If you use one, ask your primary care doctor to explain any item that seems unclear. Doctors do this routinely, and some visits include advance care planning at no extra charge to the patient under many insurance programs.

## Five Wishes

Five Wishes is a booklet-style document published by a national nonprofit. It is widely used by hospices, churches and employers. It differs from a state form in tone and scope. The five wishes address the person who will make decisions for you, the medical treatment you want or do not want, the comfort you want, how you want to be treated, and what you want your loved ones to know.

Many people find it easier to fill in because it asks about meaning rather than procedures: whether you want music playing, whether you want someone holding your hand, whether you want to be forgiven or to forgive. A family reading it later learns what you valued. That is useful.

The caution is that legal compliance depends on the state. Many states accept it, sometimes with instructions on how to sign it. Some states have formal requirements it may not satisfy. The prudent approach is to check the state-specific instructions printed with it, and if there is any doubt, also sign your state's form. Both can sit together, with the state form handling the legal appointment and Five Wishes giving personal color.

## When an attorney adds value

You do not need a lawyer to be valid. Here is when we would suggest one:

- **A divided or blended family.** If your spouse and your adult children from a prior marriage may disagree, the directive should be written to prevent a fight, not just to state a preference.
- **Property in more than one state.** You may spend winters in Florida or own a cabin elsewhere. An attorney can check that the document will be honored where you receive care. See [do advance directives work across states](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive) for the general rules.
- **A serious diagnosis.** If you have early dementia, Parkinson's disease, cancer or heart failure, you need tailored instructions, not standard boxes. For dementia in particular, see [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).
- **A religious or philosophical preference** you want honored precisely, such as refusing blood transfusions.
- **A larger estate plan.** If you are already doing a will or trust, the directive and the [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) should be drafted together and coordinated with your financial power of attorney.
- **An agent you worry will be challenged.** Careful drafting and a conversation with counsel can reduce the risk.

Pricing for attorney help varies. Our guide to [how much estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost) explains how flat fees usually work.

## A DIY path that works

Here is a sequence we would trust for someone who wants to do this alone.

1. **Get your state's form.** Use one from a state agency or your hospital, not a random website.
2. **Read the whole thing first.** Do not start checking boxes.
3. **Choose your agent and backup** and ask them before you write names down. See [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent).
4. **Talk to your doctor** about any treatment choice you do not understand.
5. **Sign with the right witnesses.** In many states, relatives, heirs and your agent cannot witness. Some states require a notary instead. See [whether a living will needs a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary).
6. **Make copies** and give them to your agent, your backup, your doctor and anyone who may be with you in an emergency.
7. **Put it in your medical record.** Ask your doctor and your hospital to scan it in. Many patient portals let you upload it.
8. **Review it** every few years and after any major event.

## Mistakes with any route

- Signing without witnesses because the form "looked official."
- Using a form from a different state because it was easier to find.
- Crossing out or editing printed language, which can raise doubts.
- Storing the only signed copy in a lockbox.
- Forgetting to tell the agent they were named.
- Assuming the directive covers money or property. It does not; that is the job of a [financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
- Skipping the doctor's conversation and later learning a checkbox meant something different than you thought.

For emergency medical orders such as no CPR at home, a signed form is not enough; see [DNR orders and POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).

## Example: three people, three choices

Carol, 52, is healthy, married, with two adult children who get along. She downloads her state's form, her husband and a neighbor witness it, and she is finished in an hour. That is a good outcome.

Walter, 74, was diagnosed with early Parkinson's. He and his late wife's children from her first marriage do not agree on much. He meets with an attorney, names his niece as agent, writes detailed instructions for a late-stage scenario, and includes a short letter to his stepchildren explaining the choice.

Nina, 63, dislikes forms. She completes Five Wishes with her sister at the kitchen table and has two witnesses sign. Her state accepts it, and the booklet leads to the first real talk about death her family has had.

None of them chose wrongly. They matched the tool to the situation.
