Rosa and Gene spend six months in Wisconsin and winters in Arizona. Their directives were signed in Wisconsin, years ago. A friend recently asked, "Would the Arizona hospital even look at those?" They did not know.

The honest answer is "probably, and you should not rely on probably." This article explains how states treat each other's directives, what travelers and movers should do, and which medical orders behave differently. It is part of our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## The short version

- Many states honor directives signed elsewhere, but statutes and conditions vary.
- A clear, properly signed document is accepted more readily than a vague or informal one.
- When you change your permanent home, sign a new directive under the new state's rules.
- If you split the year, ask for one document that satisfies both states.
- Portable medical orders, such as a POLST, need separate attention.

## Will another state honor your advance directive?

Often it will, but the rule is a patchwork. Each state sets its own law on healthcare directives. Many states have a provision that recognizes a directive valid in the state where it was made, or valid under the law of the state where it is used. Some say this in the statute. Others say nothing, and hospitals follow policy and risk judgment.

Two different questions are tangled in this topic.

- **Legal recognition.** Does the state's law say the out-of-state document counts? In many states, yes, if it was valid where signed and does not conflict with local public policy.
- **Practical acceptance.** Will the staff on shift accept it, quickly, without calling legal counsel? A document with clean signatures, witnesses and a notary seal makes that easier.

A directive from another state is more likely to be a problem when it has an unusual form, leaves questions open, or was signed without the formalities the first state required. Then the hospital must wonder whether it is valid anywhere. Our article on [signing requirements for a living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary) explains why notarization is cheap insurance.

## What differs from state to state?

Most healthcare directive law fits a common pattern, but these details change from one state to the next.

| Feature | How it can differ |
|---|---|
| Witnesses | Number required, and who is barred, such as relatives, heirs or your doctor |
| Notary | Required in some states, optional or alternative in others |
| Names of documents | Healthcare proxy, medical power of attorney, durable power of attorney for healthcare, living will, declaration |
| Trigger language | Definitions of terminal condition or permanent unconsciousness |
| Feeding tubes and hydration | Some states require express language to refuse them |
| Pregnancy | Some limit a living will during pregnancy |
| Default surrogate list | Who decides when no agent is available |
| Medical orders | POLST programs, out-of-hospital DNR forms and recognized colors or formats |

None of this means your document fails. It means that the closer your document is to local language and practice, the fewer questions staff ask.

## What should you do if you move?

Treat the move as a reason to redo your paperwork, much as you would check a will, as shown in [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan). Follow this order.

1. **Do not tear up the old directive yet.** Keep it valid until the new one is signed.
2. **Sign a new directive in your new state.** Use its statutory form or have an attorney adapt it, with the witnesses and notary the state calls for.
3. **Say it replaces earlier ones.** The revocation language is explained in [how to change or revoke an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive).
4. **Check your agent.** If your agent lives in the old state, ask whether someone nearer should be the backup. An agent two time zones away can still decide, but cannot sit in the room.
5. **Reissue your HIPAA release.** See the guide to [HIPAA authorizations](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization).
6. **Get local medical orders.** If you have a DNR or POLST, ask your new clinician to issue one under local rules.
7. **Update the copies.** New doctor, new hospital, new pharmacy.

Moves also affect wills, trusts and financial powers of attorney, covered in our [guide on how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) and the pillar on [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney). Treat the healthcare documents as one part of the same checkup.

## What does a hospital actually check?

In an emergency room, nobody sends your document to a lawyer. A nurse or social worker looks for three things: your signature, a date, and either witness signatures or a notary stamp. Then they look at what it asks. Is there an agent with a phone number? Are the treatment instructions specific enough to apply?

A document that clears those checks is usually scanned into the chart and followed. A document that is missing a page, has an agent with no contact information, or reads like a general essay on values is more likely to prompt a call to risk management. That call can cost hours. Put your agent's phone number on the first page, and initial each page if your state allows it.

## What if you live in two states?

If you split the year, you are the person most at risk of a mismatch. There are two sound approaches.

- **One document meeting both states' formalities.** An attorney can draft a directive that uses witnesses and a notary acceptable in both states, and uses language familiar to both. This works for most people.
- **Two documents, each local.** Another route is to sign one in each state, each saying it does not revoke the other, with the same agent named. That adds paperwork, but it can ease acceptance in a hospital that wants its own statutory form.

Either way, keep a copy with you, in both homes, and on your phone. Name an agent who can reach both places, and a backup who lives in each. State guides such as our [Illinois healthcare power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) article show how much one state's rules can shape the form.

## Do medical orders travel the same way?

Not always, and this catches people. A directive is a legal document you sign. A POLST or out-of-hospital DNR is a medical order signed by a clinician. Many states have programs that recognize orders from other states, and others expect their own forms. Emergency crews follow the orders they recognize, and they are trained to act in minutes, not to analyze a foreign form.

If you are seriously ill and travel, ask your clinician how your order will be seen where you are going, and carry the original. Our article on [DNR and POLST orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) explains the difference between the order and the directive. The question of who can set aside a document in practice is addressed in [who can overrule your healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive).

## How state law changes this

The relevant variation is mostly about recognition and formalities. Some states honor out-of-state directives expressly. Some honor them only if they comply with local law, and a few are silent. Your [state guide](/estate-planning) is the best place to see your state's approach, and an attorney can confirm. Do not assume that a form from an online site written for another state will meet your local requirements.
