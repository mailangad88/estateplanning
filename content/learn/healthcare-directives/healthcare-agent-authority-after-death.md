When someone you love is in a hospital bed, the healthcare agent is the person everyone looks to. Then the monitors go quiet, and a new set of questions arrives within hours. Who talks to the funeral home? Who approves an autopsy? Does anyone get to say yes to the organ donation team? Is the agent still in charge?

The short answer, covered across the wider [healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives) library, is that the agent's role mostly ends, and other people step in. Knowing who, in advance, saves a family from guessing in the worst hour.

## The short version

- Medical treatment decisions end when a doctor declares death.
- A few narrow tasks can continue if your document or state law allows them: organ and tissue donation, autopsy consent, and sometimes the disposition of your body.
- Your executor, named in your will, takes over money and paperwork.
- Without a named person, state law decides who handles the funeral, usually the closest relatives in a set order.

## Does a healthcare power of attorney end at death?

Yes, in the sense that matters most. A healthcare power of attorney exists to make medical decisions for you while you are alive and unable to speak. Once you die there are no treatment decisions left to make, and the document has done its job. The same is true for most [financial powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end), which end at death and hand off to the executor.

Think of Walter, 74, who names his daughter Carol as his healthcare agent. Walter has a stroke and Carol approves a feeding tube, then later stops it after the doctors explain that he will not recover. When Walter dies on a Tuesday, Carol's treatment authority is finished. She is still his daughter and probably his next of kin, which gives her a role in many of the steps below, but the role comes from family law and from other documents, not from the healthcare proxy.

For how the document works while you are alive, see [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## What decisions come up right after death?

Here is who usually has the say in each, and where your own documents change that.

| Decision | Who usually decides | What you can do ahead of time |
|---|---|---|
| Organ and tissue donation | You, through a donor registration; otherwise your agent or next of kin | Register, mark your license, and put it in your directive |
| Autopsy | Medical examiner if required; otherwise next of kin or authorized agent | Say yes or no in your directive |
| Burial, cremation, funeral | A person you named, the executor, or next of kin in an order set by state law | Name an agent for remains; prepay a plan |
| Medical records | Personal representative | Name who may see them |
| Estate, bills, accounts | Executor named in your will | Sign a will and name a backup |

## What happens with organ donation and autopsy?

Donation is time sensitive. If you registered in your state or on your license, the donation team generally proceeds on that authorization, and in most places your family cannot undo it. If you did not register and your directive is silent, the people allowed to consent are listed in state law, and a healthcare agent often sits at or near the top of that list.

Autopsy works differently. A medical examiner or coroner can order one in cases of unexpected, violent or unexplained death, and no one's consent is needed. In other cases a hospital may ask the family. A directive that says "no autopsy unless required" or "yes, please" settles the question before anyone has to ask. See [how to document your end-of-life wishes, including organ donation](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-document-your-end-of-life-wishes) for the registry steps.

One wrinkle: if your living will refuses ventilators, a donor can still be kept on temporary support long enough for donation to occur. Say how you want those two instructions to fit together, or the hospital may have to ask your agent in a short window.

## Can a healthcare agent decide on funeral arrangements?

Not automatically, and this surprises families. The authority over a body is a different legal right from the right to make medical decisions. Many states have a statute that lets you sign a form naming a person to control your disposition. If you name nobody, the statute usually sets a ranking, often the spouse first, then adult children, parents, and siblings.

Where this goes wrong is easy to picture. Maria dies and leaves a spouse who has been estranged for years, plus an adult daughter who cared for her. Without a document, the law may give the spouse the final say on cremation versus burial. A one-page designation, signed ahead of time, avoids that fight.

You have three practical choices.

1. **Name your healthcare agent for remains too.** This works well when you trust the same person for both jobs.
2. **Name a different person for funeral choices.** Useful if your agent is overwhelmed or lives far away.
3. **Prepay a plan.** A written, funded contract with a funeral home states what you want and takes the payment off your family. Funeral costs and who covers them are discussed in [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral).

## Who takes over after the agent's role ends?

Your executor, if you have a will. The executor gets court authority, often called letters testamentary, then pays bills, handles accounts and distributes property. The executor is also usually the "personal representative" who can request your medical records after death. If you are not sure who needs which role, [power of attorney versus executor](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-executor) lays it out.

Many people name the same person as healthcare agent and executor, and that is fine when it fits. Others split the roles because the best decision-maker at a bedside is not always the best administrator. Sorting out paperwork takes months. Bedside courage takes an hour.

## Does divorce or remarriage change who the agent is?

It can. In many states a divorce automatically cancels a former spouse's appointment as your agent. Other states require you to revoke it yourself. That uncertainty is why we tell clients to sign new documents after a divorce and hand copies to every doctor and hospital. Our guide to [how to change or revoke an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive) covers the steps.

## What should I put in writing now?

A short checklist you can finish in an afternoon:

- Your healthcare power of attorney, naming an agent and a backup.
- A line in that document stating whether the agent may consent to organ donation and autopsy.
- A signed designation for the disposition of remains, or your wishes for burial or cremation.
- Your donor registration, confirmed on your license or in the state registry.
- A short letter saying where documents are, and who the funeral home and executor are.
- A [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) that lets your agent and your executor see records.

Keep copies where your family can find them, as covered in [where to keep your advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/where-to-keep-your-advance-directive).

## What if my family disagrees?

Disagreement is most common when wishes were never written. A directive and a designation reduce it. If a conflict remains, many states allow an interested person to ask a court to decide, and funeral homes will often wait for agreement among the people with legal authority before proceeding. It is better to have the conversation while you can still join it.
