The call comes after a fall or a hospital stay. The discharge planner says your mother cannot go home alone and needs skilled nursing care. A stack of forms arrives, and one line asks who is signing. If your mother named you as her healthcare agent, you may be the one holding the pen.

This guide, part of our [healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives) library, covers what that role allows, what it does not, and the clauses that quietly turn a signature into a personal obligation.

## The short version

- A healthcare agent may usually consent to admission to a nursing home when the principal cannot decide.
- Sign in your capacity as agent, and cross out or decline any language that makes you personally liable.
- The agent's power is medical. Money powers come from a separate financial document.
- If the person fights the move or the unit is locked, the legal path may be different.

## Can a healthcare agent consent to a nursing home admission?

In most states, yes. The agent's power is to make health care decisions, and where a person lives for medical care is part of that. The directive has to be in effect, which normally means a doctor has found the person unable to make or communicate decisions. If your parent still has capacity, the choice is theirs, even if the agent made the phone calls. See [when a healthcare directive takes effect](/learn/healthcare-directives/when-does-a-healthcare-directive-take-effect) for how that finding is made.

Some documents are more precise than others. A strong form says the agent can arrange admission to a hospital, nursing home, assisted living facility or hospice, and can hire and fire caregivers. If yours is silent on facilities, state law often fills in the gap, but it is better to confirm with the document itself.

For an overview of what the role includes, read [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## What are you actually signing?

The admission packet is long, and different pieces have different weight.

| Document | What it does | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Consent to admission and treatment | Authorizes care in the facility | Fine to sign as agent |
| Admission agreement | Sets rates, services, rules, discharge terms | Read it; ask for a copy; do not sign as responsible party |
| Arbitration clause | Waives court for disputes | Federal rules generally bar making it a condition of admission |
| Financial responsibility line | Makes the signer liable for payment | Decline; a facility cannot lawfully require a family member's personal promise to pay |
| Resident rights acknowledgment | Confirms rights were explained | Keep your copy |
| Consent for psychotropic medication | Authorizes specific drugs | Ask why and for how long |

The line that causes the most harm is "responsible party." Many facilities use it routinely. It sounds like a courtesy, such as the person who gets the mail or the calls. In some contracts it is a promise to pay. Sign as "Jane Doe, as agent for John Doe," never in your own name alone, and cross out or ask to revise any language that makes you liable personally. Our page on [whether a nursing home can make your children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) goes through the details.

## What can a healthcare agent not do?

Three limits cause most of the confusion.

1. **Pay the bills.** Writing checks, moving money and applying for Medicaid belong to a financial agent. If your parent never signed a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), a healthcare agent may be stuck at the cash register. A facility cannot be forced to wait while the family works it out, so this gap can push families to court.
2. **Override a clear instruction.** If the directive says no feeding tube, the agent cannot decide otherwise because the family is not ready. See [who can overrule a healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive).
3. **Bypass special rules.** In many states some placements, such as a secured dementia unit or a psychiatric facility, have extra safeguards, especially when the person objects.

The agent also owes a duty to act in the person's interest. Using the role to get a parent into a facility that suits the children's schedule is a conflict worth avoiding.

## What if my parent does not want to go?

This is where a lot of families stall. Mr. Alvarez, 82, has dementia, wanders at night, and says he will never live in "that place." His daughter, the agent, believes he is unsafe. What are her options?

In many states an agent can place a resident who lacks capacity, even over verbal objection, when the placement is the least restrictive way to keep him safe. Other states treat an objection as a reason to ask a court. Locked memory care units trigger special scrutiny in some places because they restrict movement.

When an agent is unsure, these are the sensible steps.

1. Get the doctor's capacity finding in writing.
2. Ask the facility what its policy is for a resident who objects.
3. Try a respite or short-term stay first, which is easier on the person and gives you time.
4. Talk to an attorney about your state's rule before signing a secured-unit admission.
5. If a court is needed, a guardianship petition, with its notice and hearing, may follow. See [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

The guide on [when a parent refuses help or is not safe alone](/learn/elder-care/when-a-parent-refuses-help-or-is-not-safe-alone) covers the stage before admission.

## Who can serve, and does it matter if the agent is a caregiver?

A cautious rule applies in many states: a person's treating doctor, and the owner or an employee of the facility where they live, cannot serve as agent unless they are related. The point is to prevent the person who profits from the placement from also approving it.

A family caregiver who is paid by the person is a different case and usually allowed. It can still cause trouble if siblings suspect the payments. The page on [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) shows how to document that. And [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) has the broader picture on choosing and backing up the role.

## What if there is no healthcare agent at all?

Then the hospital or facility looks to state law. Many states have a surrogate list that ranks the spouse, adult children, parents and siblings. A few do not. Without a list or a document, a family may have to petition for guardianship just to place a parent, which takes time and money and removes some privacy. [What happens without an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive) walks through the usual order.

## How should I prepare before a crisis?

Prepare now, while the person can still talk about it.

- Make sure there is a signed healthcare directive with a backup agent.
- Pair it with a financial power of attorney and a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization).
- Talk about what kind of facility they would accept, how far from family, and what they cannot stand.
- Keep a short file with insurance cards, a medication list, and the name of the primary doctor.
- Know in advance how care will be paid for. The [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) helps when the timeline matters.
