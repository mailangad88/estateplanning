Most people put off a living will because the form feels like a quiz they have not studied for. Do you want CPR? A ventilator? A feeding tube? Under what circumstances? Those are medical questions with moral weight, and a checkbox does not help you think.

The better order is to think first and sign second. This page gives you the questions to work through, a way to write the answers down, and some practical advice on talking to your family, including a parent who would rather not. It pairs with our guides to the [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) and to [documenting your end-of-life wishes](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-document-your-end-of-life-wishes), and it belongs to our wider [healthcare directives pillar](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## The short version

- Start with values: what do you most need from life to want it to continue?
- Move to outcomes: which conditions would you refuse treatment to avoid?
- Then treatments: CPR, breathing machines, feeding tubes, dialysis.
- Choose your agent and say who should not decide.
- Tell people, in person, and write it down.

## Which questions help you decide what you want?

Take these in order. You do not need to answer all of them in one sitting.

**Your values**

1. What are the things you could not give up and still feel like yourself? Recognizing your family? Walking? Being at home? Speaking?
2. What worries you more, dying sooner or living in a condition you dislike?
3. What do you remember about someone else's final illness that you want to repeat or avoid?
4. Does your faith, culture or philosophy shape how you think about suffering or about prolonging life?

**Your outcomes**

5. If doctors said you would never wake up, would you want treatment continued? For how long?
6. If you could no longer recognize your children, would you want antibiotics for a pneumonia?
7. Would you accept a hard but time-limited treatment, like a ventilator for a week, for a real chance of recovery?
8. Where do you want to be at the end: home, a hospital, a hospice?

**Your decision makers**

9. Who understands you well enough to speak for you even if it hurts them?
10. Is there anyone who must not decide, such as a relative who cannot accept loss or who would argue with the doctors?
11. Who is the second choice if the first cannot serve?

Questions 1 to 4 matter more than most people expect. A doctor can treat a particular condition without any directive, but only you can say what a life worth living looks like.

## How do you write the answers down as a values history?

A values history is a page or two in your own words. It is not a legal form, and you do not need special language. Open with the sentence "What matters most to me is..." and then write a few lines under headings such as these:

| Heading | Example line |
|---|---|
| What I value | "Being able to talk with my grandchildren and to sit outside." |
| What I would not want | "Long-term life in a facility where I cannot recognize anyone." |
| Treatments I would try | "A short ventilator trial if doctors expect recovery." |
| Treatments I would refuse | "A feeding tube if I have advanced dementia." |
| Who decides | "My sister Dana, then my son Luis. Please listen to Dana." |
| Comfort and setting | "Music, family around me, home if possible." |

Attach it to your directive, or keep it in the same folder. Forms leave gaps, and an agent who has read your page can fill them in the way you would have.

Take Walt, 71, who fills in a standard form and finds a box for tube feeding but none for his actual worry, which is a stroke that leaves him alert but unable to speak or swallow. His page says: "If I can understand people but can never speak or eat again, I would not want a feeding tube for more than a few weeks." That sentence does more for his daughter than the whole form.

## How do you turn answers into a signed document?

1. Pick the form your state recognizes, or have an attorney prepare it. Our article on [Five Wishes or a lawyer](/learn/healthcare-directives/advance-directive-forms-five-wishes-or-a-lawyer) compares the options.
2. Name your agent and alternate in a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
3. Complete the living will sections using your answers to the outcome questions.
4. Sign and witness it according to your state's rules.
5. Add a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so your agent can read your records.
6. Tell people and hand out copies.

## What should you tell your family?

Tell them three things: who your agent is, the main outcomes you would refuse to live with, and that you have thought it through. Say the reason, not only the decision. A relative who hears "Mom doesn't want a feeding tube" may fight it. One who hears "Mom said she would rather have a few good weeks than months in a bed" is more likely to accept it.

If someone will disagree, say it ahead of time and name your agent clearly. The record of a calm talk beats a hospital-room argument. Our article on [what happens when nobody knows your end-of-life wishes](/learn/what-if/nobody-knows-my-end-of-life-wishes) shows how quickly families split when no one has been told.

## How do you start this conversation with a parent?

Do not hand a parent a form and expect a response. Try an approach that opens the topic gently:

- Use a story. "A friend's father was in the ICU last month and the family did not know what he wanted. It made me wonder what you would want."
- Ask for guidance, not permission. "I want to get it right for you. Can you tell me what matters most?"
- Choose the setting. A walk, a drive or a kitchen table works better than the hospital waiting room.
- Start with who, not what. People find it easier to name a decision maker than to speak about dying.
- Keep it short and come back. Three small talks are better than one long one.
- Write down what they say, read it back and ask if it is right.

If your parent has already begun to lose memory, move quickly. Capacity to sign a directive can fade, and our article on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) explains how to handle that stage. For general tips on family conversations about plans, see [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan).

## When should you review your answers?

Your answers will change. People who have watched an illness up close often revise them. Review the whole set after a new diagnosis, a death in the family, a change in your agent's availability, a divorce or a move. The mechanics of replacing a document are in [how to change or revoke an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive).

## How state law changes this

States differ on the form's name and wording, the number of witnesses, who cannot witness, and which treatments a directive can address. Some add special rules for pregnancy or for stopping artificial feeding. The values history is not a legal document anywhere, so it supports your signed directive and does not replace it. For a state-specific example, see our guide to [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives).
