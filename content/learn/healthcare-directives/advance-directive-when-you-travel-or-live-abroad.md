Paul and Ines, both in their sixties, have booked three weeks in Portugal. Paul has a heart condition. Before the trip he asks his daughter whether his living will, signed in New Jersey, would do anything in a Lisbon hospital. She does not know, and neither did the travel agent.

The honest answer is that it does less than you would hope and more than nothing. This article explains why, and gives you a short checklist for trips and for longer stays. It builds on our article on [directives across state lines](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states) and our wider guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## Does a US advance directive have legal force in another country?

Generally no. A directive is valid because a US state's statute says so. Another country's courts and hospitals are bound by their own law, and the US state's statute has no reach there. Some countries have a living will or a "patient's representative" scheme with their own formalities. Others rely on the treating doctors and the family. Many say little about refusing treatment, and a few treat it very differently from US practice.

That does not make your document worthless. A hospital abroad is made up of doctors who usually want to know what you would choose. A clear, signed directive tells them. It also tells the person next to you what to ask for. But it is evidence and guidance, not an order.

## What does it still do for you?

Three things.

1. **It names your agent.** A doctor can phone a named person and discuss options. The agent speaks with your voice even when the country's law does not require the doctor to accept the decision.
2. **It records your wishes.** If you have said "no prolonged ventilation" in writing, it shapes the conversation.
3. **It guides the evacuation decision.** The most serious decisions on a trip are often about whether to move you home. Your agent and your insurer's assistance team make that call, and the directive helps them.

## What should you carry on a trip?

A short trip needs a short kit. The point is that anyone helping you can find it in thirty seconds.

| Item | Why | Where to keep it |
|---|---|---|
| Copy of your healthcare power of attorney and living will | Evidence and contacts | Phone (a photo and PDF) and one paper copy in luggage |
| One-page summary in plain English, and the local language if you know it | Fast reading by staff | Top of the paper copy |
| HIPAA authorization | Lets US doctors and insurers speak to your agent | With your agent, and on your phone |
| Agent and backup phone numbers with country codes | Staff may call from a hospital line | Wallet card and phone |
| Insurance policy and the assistance line number | Authorizes care and evacuation | Wallet and phone |
| Medication list and diagnoses | Often asked first | Same page as the summary |

Do not rely on a cloud account you cannot open abroad. Save the files offline.

If you have a POLST or an out-of-hospital DNR order, know that these are US medical orders, tied to US emergency systems. Paramedics abroad will not know the form. Treat them as information, not instructions. Our article on [DNR and POLST orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) describes what they do at home.

## Is your agent really reachable?

Plan for the time difference. A healthcare agent who sleeps while the hospital in Bangkok needs an answer is not much help. Name a backup who lives in a different time zone from your agent, and tell both exactly when you will be away. If your first-choice agent is travelling with you, the backup becomes the key person. Choose accordingly. Our article on [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) covers how to name a backup.

Make sure your agent has a calling plan or an app that works internationally, and that your agent knows who your insurer is. Many travel insurers will speak to a named family member at once.

## What if you live abroad part of the year or permanently?

Short trips are one problem. A long stay is another. If you winter in Mexico or retire to Spain, you may spend months where your US document does not apply and your family is far away. Ask a local lawyer, or the embassy's list of local lawyers, whether the country has a form for naming a healthcare representative and recording treatment wishes. Keep your US directive current at the same time, because you may still return for serious care or treatment.

Joanne, 68, moved to Portugal for good. She kept her Illinois directive and added a Portuguese document drawn up by a local lawyer. When she had a fall, the hospital worked from the local form and her daughter used the Illinois documents with her US insurer. Neither was ideal alone.

The same split appears in other parts of your estate plan. A will written in a US state can be challenged or may need a local companion will, covered in our article on [moving states or living abroad with a will](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad). A financial power of attorney faces similar limits, which we cover in [whether a power of attorney is valid abroad](/learn/power-of-attorney/is-a-power-of-attorney-valid-in-another-state-or-abroad).

## What changes if you are flown home?

Everything, in your favor. Once you land in a US hospital, your state directive and your agent's authority apply again, subject to that state's rules and the hospital's usual checks. The difficult stretch is the middle one, the days in a foreign hospital before a transfer is approved. Doctors abroad often see a transfer as a medical judgment, and insurers want records before they pay. Your agent can request those records quickly if you have signed a release. Without one, a daughter in Ohio may spend two days arguing with a clerk in another time zone.

This is also where the conversation about your wishes matters. If you would not want to be transferred for a long, uncertain recovery, say so now and write it into your instructions.

## What about travel inside the United States?

Between US states the picture is calmer. Most states honor an out-of-state directive that was valid where it was signed, or that follows their rules, though a hospital may ask for a local form. Our article on [advance directives across states](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states) covers that in full.

## A pre-trip checklist

1. Confirm your agent and backup are willing and reachable for the dates you will be away.
2. Photograph your documents and save them offline.
3. Write a one-page summary of your wishes and your medical history.
4. Add your agent's number to your insurer's emergency contact record.
5. Buy a policy that includes medical evacuation, and read what it requires before it will authorize a move.
6. Tell your agent your return date and your hotel or hospital contact if you can.
