Helen, 74, lives alone and has three doctors, each with a different app. Her son Paul drives her to appointments but cannot see any of the results. Helen texts him her password for one portal "just this once." Then she wonders if that was a bad idea, and what she should have done.

This is one of the most common questions we hear from older clients and from adult children. The answer is that three different documents each solve one piece, and a password solves none of them properly. It is part of our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What does HIPAA let a family member see?

HIPAA is the federal privacy rule for healthcare providers and insurers. It lets a provider share information with a family member who is involved in your care when you agree, when you do not object, or when you cannot speak and the provider judges it to be in your interest. That is permission, not duty. A cautious front-desk worker will often say no.

The rule is about what providers may disclose. It does not stop you from sharing your own records with anyone, and it does not stop your relative from reading what you give them. Our article on the [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) explains the form that turns "may" into "will" for most offices.

## Why is a shared password a poor solution?

A shared password feels simple and creates four problems.

- Most portals prohibit sharing logins in their terms of use.
- Your relative appears in the record as you. If a message goes out or a refill is requested, nobody can tell who sent it.
- The login often does not let a second person see everything, and messages from your relative may be treated as coming from you.
- When your relative needs to talk to the office by phone, the staff still cannot confirm they are allowed to.

It is a workaround that fails on the day you need it most. There are better tools.

## What are the three ways to give access?

| Tool | What it gives | Where you get it | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Portal proxy access | Their own login to your portal | Each provider's form, often at the front desk or in the portal settings | Works for that provider only |
| HIPAA authorization | Permission for providers to release records and talk to named people | A signed form, kept by each office | Does not give decision power |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Authority to make medical decisions when you cannot | A signed document with witnesses as your state requires | Usually active only when you lack capacity |

Proxy access is the portal fix. It creates a separate account tied to your relative's identity, with your consent. Some systems let you limit what the proxy sees, such as appointment scheduling and billing but not clinical notes.

A HIPAA authorization works across offices that accept it. Name your adult child, state what they may receive, and set an end date or "until I revoke." Many people sign one for each provider and keep a master copy in the file.

A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) is the decision tool. It names who consents to treatment if you cannot, and if you name your adult child as agent they will want to hold the other two documents as well.

## How do you set up proxy access?

1. List every provider who has a portal: primary care, every other doctor you see, labs, the hospital you use, your pharmacy.
2. Ask each practice for its patient proxy or "authorized representative" form. Most are one or two pages.
3. Decide the access level. Common options are full read access, scheduling and billing only, or messaging only.
4. Sign in person if the office asks. Some require your government ID and your relative's.
5. Have your adult child create their own account and confirm they can see what you intended.
6. Record the date, and put a reminder on your calendar to review in a year.

The form is usually free. Expect some systems to have a different process for adult patients than for minors, because proxy features for children were built for parents.

## What about someone who cannot set it up?

If a parent already has dementia, or is in the hospital and cannot sign, the options narrow. A parent who still understands the request can usually sign the portal form or a HIPAA release even with a memory diagnosis. Capacity is about understanding the specific decision. Our article on [the caregiver who cannot get medical or bank records](/learn/what-if/caregiver-cannot-get-medical-or-bank-records) covers what to do when the signature window has closed.

If you hold a power of attorney for property, it is not the same thing. A financial agent can often deal with billing and insurance, but medical records access normally comes through a HIPAA release or a healthcare agent. See how financial agents and online accounts interact in [power of attorney and digital accounts](/learn/digital-assets/power-of-attorney-and-digital-accounts).

## What if the family member is a spouse, a friend or a sibling?

The same tools work for anyone you trust. A spouse often already has the strongest informal access, but a spouse who is also elderly may need a younger backup on the forms. A friend or partner who is not a relative usually has no default standing in a hospital, so a written release matters even more. Names matter: spell the person's full legal name, and list a backup.

Anna, 66, is unmarried and has a close friend, Mark, who handles her appointments. Her sister lives out of state. Without paperwork, the office would speak to the sister, a relative she rarely talks to, and refuse Mark. Anna signed a release and a proxy form for Mark, and named her sister as backup. The sister agreed.

## Illinois and New Jersey

Both states follow federal HIPAA for privacy of records and add their own rules for sensitive records, such as mental health and substance-use treatment, which may need a specific consent beyond a general release. For decisions rather than records, see the [Illinois health care power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney) and the [New Jersey advance directive](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives) guides.

## Does the portal show everything a relative might worry about?

Not always. Portals display what the provider chooses to post, and some content never appears: psychotherapy notes, certain lab results held for a review period, and records from outside systems. A relative who sees a clean portal should not assume nothing is happening. Visit summaries are often the better window, and a short call with the nurse after an appointment fills gaps the screen leaves.

Think too about what you do not want shared. An adult child who sees every result may start to call after each one. Some parents set proxy access to scheduling and billing only, and share the rest in conversation. That is a fair choice, and you can change it later. Say it kindly and say why.

## Keep it current

Access outlasts your memory of it. Once a year, open each portal's settings and check who is listed. Remove a former spouse, a relative you have fallen out with, or someone who has moved away. A divorce can end a spouse's role as healthcare agent in some states automatically, but it will not remove a portal login.
