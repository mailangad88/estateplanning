Most families never talk about medical wishes until a crisis forces the question. By then the patient often cannot answer, and relatives are left to guess while doctors wait for a decision. Advance directives move that conversation to a time when you can think clearly and speak for yourself.

This guide explains each document, how the pieces fit, what to discuss with your family and doctor, and what to do for specific situations such as a serious diagnosis or dementia. Each section points to a more detailed article. For the financial side of incapacity planning, see our companion pillar on [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## What are advance healthcare directives?

Advance healthcare directives are documents prepared ahead of time to guide medical care if you cannot make or communicate your own decisions. The glossary definition of an [advance directive](/glossary/advance-directive) includes two main types: instructions about treatment, and the appointment of a person to decide.

They matter because incapacity is common. Illness, injury, anesthesia, infections and the final stage of most serious illnesses all leave people unable to speak. In these moments, doctors need to know who has authority and what the patient would want, and in the absence of paperwork they follow state law that may not match your wishes.

## What are the core documents?

A complete set has four layers. Each answers a different question.

| Layer | Question it answers | Article |
|---|---|---|
| Healthcare power of attorney | Who decides for me? | [Healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) |
| Living will | What do I want, or not want? | [What is a living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) |
| HIPAA authorization | Who can see my medical information? | [HIPAA authorizations](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) |
| DNR or POLST | What should emergency staff do right now? | [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) |

Each one has weaknesses by itself. A living will with no agent leaves no one to interpret it. An agent with no HIPAA release may struggle to get information. A POLST without a conversation may not reflect what you really want. The combination is stronger than any single piece.

## Who decides for you: the healthcare power of attorney

The most important document is the one that names your healthcare agent, also called a healthcare proxy or surrogate. The [healthcare proxy](/glossary/healthcare-proxy) can consent to or refuse treatment, choose providers, arrange hospice and speak with doctors when you cannot.

Choose the person who will carry out your wishes, even if they would choose differently, and who can stay calm under pressure. Name a backup. Talk to them before you sign. Our article on the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) has a checklist of traits and a list of topics to discuss. Many of the same questions apply to choosing a financial agent; see [how to choose your agent under a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent).

Without an agent, most states rely on a default list of family members, and when relatives disagree, a court may appoint a guardian. Unmarried partners and close friends are often left off the list. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) for how burdensome that route is.

## What you want: the living will

A [living will](/glossary/living-will) states which treatments you would accept or refuse in conditions such as terminal illness or permanent unconsciousness. It commonly addresses CPR, ventilators, feeding tubes, dialysis, antibiotics and comfort care.

The strongest living wills pair a list of choices with a short statement of values, such as "being able to recognize my family is essential to me." That helps your agent and doctors interpret a situation the form did not foresee. Our article on [what a living will should say](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) explains how to write one, how to sign it and what it cannot do.

A living will is not the same as a last will. It governs medical care while you are alive and has nothing to do with property. Our overview of [wills](/learn/wills) covers the other kind.

## Who can see your information: the HIPAA authorization

Federal privacy rules allow providers to share information with family involved in your care in certain cases, but providers frequently decline without a signed form. A [HIPAA authorization](/glossary/hipaa-authorization) names the people who may receive your records and speak to your doctors. It should be effective immediately, not only on incapacity, and should name your agent, spouse, adult children and financial agent.

Our guide on [HIPAA authorizations](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) covers required elements, mental health and substance use records, duration and revocation. It also explains why a springing financial power of attorney depends on this form. See [springing vs. immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).

## What emergency crews will follow: DNR and POLST

Advance directives are written for hospital teams, not for paramedics. Emergency medical crews generally attempt CPR unless they see a valid out-of-hospital order. A DNR order and a POLST form are signed medical orders that fill that gap.

A POLST is intended for people with serious illness or frailty, and is completed with a clinician after a conversation. It is not recommended for healthy adults. The form, name (POLST, MOLST, MOST) and legal status vary by state. Our article on [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) explains who needs each, who signs them, and where to keep them.

## What about dementia?

Dementia creates a time-limited opportunity. A person in the early or middle stages can often still sign documents if they understand what they are signing, and a diagnosis alone does not remove legal capacity. Documents signed at that point should be well documented to withstand a challenge, and should address dementia-specific questions such as where to live, feeding tubes and hospital transfers. If capacity is already gone, a court-supervised guardianship may be the only route.

Our article on [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) covers capacity, signing order, documentation and long-term care implications, including how powers of attorney connect to [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

Our article on [living will vs. living trust vs. will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will-vs-living-trust-vs-will) untangles three similarly named documents, and [does a living will need a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary) explains witness and notary rules before you sign.

## How do you start the conversation with your family?

The paperwork is the easy part. The conversation is harder and more valuable. A few suggestions:

1. **Start with values, not procedures.** Ask what a good day looks like, and what would make life not worth living.
2. **Use a story.** "When Dad was in the ICU, I wished we had known what he wanted."
3. **Talk to your agent first,** then tell the others who it is and why.
4. **Include your doctor.** Ask what treatments would be likely in your condition.
5. **Write down what you say.** Memories fade and conflict is common.
6. **Revisit it.** Opinions change after a diagnosis or life event.

Parents, in particular, may respond better to a conversation about "making things easier for your children." Our guides on [talking to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) and [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) can help.

## What should you do, step by step?

1. Decide who your healthcare agent and backup will be, and ask them.
2. Think through your values and the treatments that matter to you.
3. Choose forms that meet your state's rules, or have a lawyer prepare them.
4. Sign with the witnesses or notary required.
5. Sign a HIPAA authorization effective immediately.
6. Give copies to your agent, family, doctors and hospital, and ask that they be added to your chart.
7. If you are seriously ill, ask your doctor about a POLST or out-of-hospital DNR.
8. Keep the originals accessible, and add a card to your wallet.
9. Review everything after a diagnosis, divorce, move or the death of a named agent.
10. Coordinate with your financial documents, as shown in our [documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist).

## What does a hospital do when there are no directives?

When a patient cannot speak and nothing is on file, the care team looks first for a person with legal authority. In most states, that means working down a statutory list of surrogates: spouse or registered partner, adult children, parents, adult siblings and sometimes other relatives or close friends. The surrogate is expected to decide based on what the patient would have wanted, and where that is unknown, on the patient's best interests.

This works smoothly when one close relative is available and the family agrees. It breaks down in common situations. Adult children may disagree about whether to continue treatment. A long-term partner who is not married may have no standing. An estranged spouse may hold priority over the person who has actually provided care for years. Hospitals then turn to an ethics committee, and, if the dispute is not resolved, to a court. Each of these steps adds days or weeks of delay at the bedside.

Take Carmen, 45, who is unmarried and has lived with her partner Dev for ten years. Carmen has a serious car accident. Her parents arrive from out of state, and under the default list in her state they outrank Dev. They want aggressive treatment; Carmen had often told Dev she would not want to be kept on a ventilator without hope of recovery. Because nothing was written down, Dev has no authority and no document to point to. A one-page healthcare power of attorney naming Dev would have prevented the conflict.

## How do directives work for different life stages?

The right documents depend on where you are in life.

| Stage | Priority documents | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Young adult, age 18 and older | Healthcare power of attorney, HIPAA authorization | Parents lose automatic access at 18, so a short document naming them keeps them involved |
| Parents of young children | Directives plus [guardian nominations](/learn/guardianship) | The agent and the guardian are different roles |
| Midlife, healthy | Full set: agent, living will, HIPAA | Review after major life events |
| Chronic or serious illness | Full set plus a conversation with your doctor about a POLST | Directives should reflect your actual diagnosis |
| Early dementia | All documents, signed soon, with capacity documented | See [dementia planning](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) |
| Aging parent | Check existing documents; update if they name someone who has died | A copy should be in the doctor's chart |

Young adults deserve a special mention. On a child's 18th birthday, a parent generally loses the legal right to see medical records or make decisions, even if the parent still pays the insurance premium. College students with no signed documents have been in the hospital while parents were turned away at the desk. A healthcare power of attorney and HIPAA authorization signed by an 18-year-old is a small task with large consequences.

## What differs from state to state?

State law controls the name of the documents, the number of witnesses, who may not witness, whether a notary is allowed or required, the default surrogate order, pregnancy restrictions, the recognition of out-of-state documents and the availability of POLST programs. Some states provide official forms or registries. A document signed in one state is often honored in another, but not always, and hospitals tend to follow what they recognize, so update if you move. For examples of how local law shapes planning, see our guides for [California](/estate-planning/california), [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), [New York](/estate-planning/new-york) and [Texas](/estate-planning/texas).

## Which mistakes come up most often?

- Having a living will but no named agent
- Naming an agent without discussing wishes
- Keeping the only original in a safe deposit box
- Forgetting the HIPAA authorization
- Assuming a living will will be followed by paramedics
- Using an old document that names an ex-spouse
- Failing to give a copy to the hospital or doctor
- Using forms that conflict with each other
- Waiting until a diagnosis, when capacity may be shrinking
- Believing directives are only for the elderly

## How do directives fit with financial planning?

Healthcare directives handle medical decisions and information. The [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) handles money. A will or [trust](/learn/trusts) handles property after death. Together they form a complete incapacity and legacy plan. If you have not yet put these in place, our guide on [what estate planning is](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning) shows how to start. Parents of minor children should also consider [naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship), and families of a loved one with disabilities may want to read our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).
