If you were in an accident tonight and could not speak, someone would be asked what you would want. That person could be a spouse who has not discussed it with you, a sibling you have not spoken to in years, or no one at all. A healthcare power of attorney lets you choose the person, not leave it to chance or state default rules.

This article explains how the document works, who to pick, what to discuss and how it interacts with your other directives. It is part of our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What is a healthcare power of attorney?

A healthcare power of attorney is a document that appoints someone to make healthcare decisions for you if you cannot. The person is called a healthcare agent, healthcare proxy, healthcare surrogate or patient advocate, depending on the state. Our glossary defines a [healthcare proxy](/glossary/healthcare-proxy) and an [advance directive](/glossary/advance-directive).

It is a separate document from the [financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney), though the law of agency is similar. A person can be your healthcare agent and not your financial agent, and vice versa. Some states combine the healthcare agent appointment and the living will in a single form.

## What can a healthcare agent decide?

Within the limits of state law and your document, a healthcare agent can make nearly any decision you could make about your medical care. This usually includes:

- Consenting to or refusing tests, surgery, medication and other treatment
- Choosing or changing doctors, hospitals and care facilities
- Deciding about life-sustaining treatment, including CPR, ventilators and feeding tubes
- Arranging hospice or palliative care
- Accessing your medical records and talking with your providers
- Making decisions about organ donation and autopsy, if you allow it
- Authorizing transfer to another state or facility

Some decisions carry special rules in many states, such as admission to a psychiatric facility, sterilization, experimental treatment or, in some places, withdrawing artificial nutrition. If any of these matter to you, ask a lawyer to address them in the document.

An agent cannot make financial decisions and cannot override clear instructions you gave, and a court can step in if the agent appears to act against your wishes.

## When does the agent's authority begin?

For most people, the authority begins when you lack the capacity to make a particular medical decision. Usually the treating physician decides, sometimes with a second opinion. Capacity can come and go: someone with a urinary infection may be confused for days and then recover. The agent steps in for those moments and steps back when you can decide again.

Doctors always try to involve the patient as much as possible, even a patient with dementia, in the decision. An agent does not replace you in everything just because you have a diagnosis. See [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) for how timing and capacity work for people with progressive conditions.

## Who should you choose?

Pick the person who will best carry out your wishes. That is not always the person who loves you most. A person who cannot bear to let go might override your instructions at the end. Think about these qualities:

1. **They know and respect your values,** and will follow them even if they would choose differently.
2. **They can handle stress.** The role often requires making hard decisions at the bedside with little time.
3. **They can speak up to doctors** and ask questions without being intimidated.
4. **They are available.** Someone in the same city, or willing to travel, is better than someone who cannot be reached.
5. **They can stand up to family pressure.** Siblings sometimes disagree, and the agent needs to hold firm.
6. **They are willing.** Ask directly. Some people say yes out of obligation.
7. **They are an adult and legally eligible** under your state's rules.

If you have difficulty choosing, our article on [choosing an agent under a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers questions that apply equally here. Always name at least one backup. If neither can serve, many states fall back to the default order of relatives, which may include someone you would not have picked.

Consider Priya, 52, who is divorced with two adult children. Her son lives nearby but becomes distressed in hospitals, while her daughter lives three hours away and is a nurse. Priya names her daughter as agent and her son as backup, tells both why, and explains her wishes to her son so he will not feel excluded.

## What should you tell your agent?

The document is only the start. A real conversation matters more. Cover these topics:

- Your overall priorities: for example, independence, being at home, time with family, avoiding pain or being able to recognize loved ones
- Specific treatments you would or would not want in different situations
- How you feel about being kept alive on machines for a limited trial
- Where you would want to receive care at the end of life
- Religious or spiritual preferences
- Who you would want involved or excluded
- Your attitude toward organ donation
- What you worry about most

Give your agent a copy of the signed document, and encourage them to ask questions. Ideally, they should meet your doctor. For prompts and suggested conversation starters, use a values worksheet from a reputable source or your lawyer. You can also add a written statement to your [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).

## How do you make a healthcare power of attorney valid?

State law sets the requirements, and a form that is valid in one state is not always valid in another. Typical requirements include a signed document and either two adult witnesses or a notary. Many states bar certain people from serving as witnesses, such as your agent, relatives, or heirs, or employees of your healthcare facility. A few states have both options. The steps are:

1. Choose your agent and backups.
2. Get a form for your state, or have a lawyer prepare one.
3. Fill it out, adding any specific instructions or limits.
4. Sign with the proper witnesses or a notary.
5. Give copies to your agent, backups, doctors and hospital.
6. Sign a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) as well so providers can share information.
7. Store the original where people can find it. Consider a phone photo and a card in your wallet naming your agent.
8. Review every few years or after a significant change.

## How is it different from a living will, DNR and POLST?

These documents are related but have different jobs.

| Document | Who it names or what it states | When it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Healthcare power of attorney | A person to decide for you | Whenever you cannot decide |
| [Living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) | Your written treatment wishes | Terminal illness or permanent unconsciousness, usually |
| [DNR or POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) | A medical order signed by a clinician | Emergencies and serious illness |
| [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) | Who may access your health information | Always |

The combination matters. A living will without an agent leaves no one to interpret it, and an agent without guidance leaves them to guess.

## What happens if there is no agent?

Most states have a surrogate statute that lists, in order of priority, who may decide for you: often spouse or domestic partner, adult children, parents, adult siblings and sometimes close friends. Hospital staff follow the list. If family members disagree, no one is available or a decision requires court authority, a guardian may be appointed. Our article on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains how burdensome that can be. Unmarried partners and close friends are especially at risk, since default lists often leave them out. A signed document is the only reliable way to ensure they can act.

## Can the agent be held responsible or removed?

An agent who acts in good faith under the document is generally protected from liability for decisions made. An agent who acts against your known wishes or in bad faith can be challenged. Interested persons can petition a court to remove or limit an agent in many states. You can also revoke the appointment yourself at any time while you have capacity, and in many states divorce automatically revokes a spouse's appointment, though a new document is still wise.

## What common mistakes should you avoid?

- Naming one agent with no backup
- Choosing someone because of their title rather than their fit
- Never discussing your wishes
- Using a form from another state without review
- Failing to give copies to the hospital and doctors
- Forgetting HIPAA authorization
- Leaving an ex-spouse named after a divorce
- Locking the only copy in a safe deposit box
- Assuming a spouse will automatically be allowed to decide in every state

For examples of how local rules differ, see our guides for [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) and [California](/estate-planning/california), both of which have their own forms and surrogate rules, and consider whether your state's form uses the term healthcare surrogate, proxy or agent.
