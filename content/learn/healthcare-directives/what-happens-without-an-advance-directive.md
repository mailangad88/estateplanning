Nobody in the room knows what you would want. You are in a hospital bed, unable to answer, and the nurse is asking your sister whether to proceed with surgery. Your sister is not sure she is even allowed to say yes. This is what happens when there is no advance directive, and it happens to capable, well-organized people every week.

This article walks through who decides, how those decisions get made, and where the system strains. It belongs to our guide to [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives), which covers the documents that prevent the problem.

## The short version

- Emergency treatment starts without any paperwork; the presumption is that you want to live.
- After the first hours, the hospital looks for a surrogate under your state's default statute.
- The surrogate is supposed to decide as you would, not as they would, but they have nothing in writing to go on.
- Conflict, absence or an unlisted loved one can push the case toward a court-appointed guardian.
- A signed directive removes the guesswork. Without one, your family carries the weight.

## Who makes decisions for you when there is no directive?

The person who decides is whoever your state's default surrogate law says it is. These laws are sometimes called family consent statutes. Most use a ranked list, and the hospital works down it until it finds someone available and willing.

A typical order looks like this, though the exact list and ranking differ by state.

| Rank | Who | Common wrinkle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Court-appointed guardian, if one exists | Rare unless a court already acted |
| 2 | Spouse | Some states require that you are not legally separated |
| 3 | Adult children | Usually a majority of reachable children, in states that say so |
| 4 | Parents | Often ranked above or below siblings, depending on the state |
| 5 | Adult siblings | May be asked to agree among themselves |
| 6 | Other relatives or a close friend | Only in some states, and sometimes only with a statement of familiarity |

Consider Marcus, 52, divorced, with two adult daughters and a long-time partner named Elena. He collapses at work and cannot communicate. Under a typical statute, his daughters rank ahead of Elena. If Marcus always said "Elena knows what I want," that wish carries no legal weight, because nothing is signed. Elena may be consulted, but she may not be the one who decides.

## How do doctors decide what treatment to give?

Doctors fall back on two standards. First, if the surrogate can say what you would have chosen, that is the preferred standard, often called substituted judgment. Second, if no one knows, the team uses a best interests standard, weighing benefit, burden and likely recovery.

Neither standard is precise. A son might remember his father saying he never wanted to be kept alive on machines. A daughter might remember him fighting hard through a prior illness. Both memories are real. Without a document, the team has to weigh competing recollections, and the default course in a close call is to keep treating.

That default matters. A living will says when to stop. Without one, stopping requires a surrogate who is willing to make the request and a medical team that is comfortable honoring it. Some surrogates are reluctant to say the words even when they believe they are right. A written instruction takes that burden off them, and our article on the [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) explains what such a document can cover.

## When does a lack of directive end up in court?

Court is not the usual outcome, but it is a real one, and it tends to arrive in four situations.

- **No one on the list is available.** A person with no close family, or a family that cannot be found, leaves the hospital without a surrogate.
- **Relatives of equal rank disagree.** Three siblings, two votes for continuing treatment, one against. Statutes in many states do not say what happens next.
- **The surrogate is unfit.** Staff may suspect a conflict of interest or neglect and ask a court to step in.
- **The decision is outside the surrogate's authority.** Some states limit what a default surrogate can decide, such as certain mental health treatment or placement in a long-term care facility.

A guardianship petition requires notice to family, a hearing, often a medical evaluation, and sometimes appointment of a lawyer for the patient. It can take weeks, and the record is public. It also may produce a guardian you would not have picked. Our guide to [guardianship](/learn/guardianship) and the article on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) show why planners work hard to avoid it.

## What to do if someone you love has no directive

If you are the relative in that hospital hallway, these steps help.

1. **Ask who the legal surrogate is.** The social worker or case manager can tell you how the hospital applies your state's list.
2. **Gather what you know.** Write down conversations, religious beliefs, past medical choices and anything the person said about machines, nursing homes or pain.
3. **Check for documents anywhere.** Look in the home file, with the primary doctor, in a lawyer's office and in any state registry. An old directive may exist that nobody remembered.
4. **Agree as a family first.** Even a short call among siblings can avoid a dispute in front of staff.
5. **Request a family meeting.** Ask the attending physician to explain the prognosis in plain terms to everyone who has a voice.
6. **Use the ethics service early.** It is free, and it works best before positions harden.
7. **Consult an attorney if no surrogate exists or a fight is brewing.** Emergency guardianship may be the only route, and the choice of petitioner matters.

If the person is still able to understand and express wishes, even partly, it may not be too late to sign a directive. The question is capacity, which our article on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) discusses in detail.

## How state law changes this

States differ in who is on the surrogate list, how it is ordered and what the surrogate may decide.

- **Who is included.** A minority of states name unmarried partners or close friends. Others do not address them at all.
- **How children share authority.** Some states let a majority of adult children decide. Others require unanimity or leave it unclear.
- **Limits on authority.** Several states restrict default surrogates from withdrawing certain treatment unless the patient's condition meets defined criteria, such as terminal illness or permanent unconsciousness.
- **Whether the statute exists.** Most states have one. In states without a statute, hospitals rely on custom and policy.

Your [state guide](/estate-planning) is the place to start. For one example of how a state structures the agent role, see the [Illinois healthcare power of attorney](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## What a directive replaces

A short package does the work: a healthcare power of attorney naming your agent, a living will if you have specific wishes, and a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so your agent can see your records. With those signed, the default list never comes into play. Your agent steps in, as described in our article on [the healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).

The financial side has the same gap. Without a durable power of attorney, bank accounts can freeze, as shown in [what happens if you are incapacitated without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney).
