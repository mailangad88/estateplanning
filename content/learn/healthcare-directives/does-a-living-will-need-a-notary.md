You have found a state form or worked through a questionnaire, and now you are staring at the signature page. Do you need a notary? Who is allowed to watch you sign? Getting this wrong does not usually make a living will worthless, but it can give a hospital a reason to hesitate at the moment you need it.

This article explains the usual rules for witnesses and notaries, the common ways people slip up, and what to do after signing. It is part of our [guide to advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

## What is the short answer?

The rule is set by your state, and states fall into a few patterns. The table shows how they generally divide, without naming every state, because statutes change and you should confirm yours.

| Pattern | What it means | How common |
|---|---|---|
| Witnesses only | Two adult witnesses sign; no notary needed | The most common |
| Witnesses or notary | Either one satisfies the statute | Common |
| Witnesses and notary | Both are required | A minority |
| Special rules for facility residents | Extra witness or ombudsman requirement if you live in a nursing home | Appears in several states |

Even where only one is required, signing in front of two witnesses and a notary is a widely used belt-and-suspenders approach. It costs little and makes the document easy for hospitals, other states and a skeptical relative to accept. State pages such as [California](/estate-planning/california) and [New York](/estate-planning/new-york) summarize local practice, and our attorneys can confirm the current rule for you.

## Who can be a witness, and who cannot?

A witness must usually be an adult who watches you sign or hears you acknowledge your signature, and who is not a person with something to gain. Typical disqualifications include:

- Your relatives by blood, marriage or adoption
- Anyone who would inherit from you under a will or by law
- The person you name as your healthcare agent
- Your doctor or other treating provider, in some states
- Anyone responsible for paying for your care
- Employees of the hospital or nursing home where you live, in some states, with limited exceptions

The reasoning is simple. The law wants someone neutral to confirm that you signed willingly and appeared to understand what you were doing. Choose two friends, neighbors or coworkers who are not in your will. Some states require that at least one witness be unrelated to you and not a beneficiary, while allowing the other more leeway, so read your form's instructions carefully.

Walk through an example. Daniel, 71, signs his living will at his daughter's kitchen table. His daughter, who is his agent, and his son-in-law sign as witnesses. In many states, that document would fail: both witnesses are disqualified. Had Daniel asked two neighbors to step over, the problem would not exist.

## When does a notary come in?

A notary public confirms your identity and that you signed voluntarily. In states that accept a notary in place of witnesses, a notarization alone completes the document. In states that require witnesses, the notary is an extra layer and not a substitute.

Notarization is most valuable for two reasons. It makes the document easier to use in another state, and it makes later disputes less likely, since a family member would struggle to say you never signed. Banks, many law offices and some hospitals offer notary services, and a notary should not give legal advice or fill in your form for you.

Notarization does not fix a poorly chosen witness. If your state requires witnesses and one of yours is disqualified, a notary stamp does not repair that on its own.

### A note on nursing homes and hospitals

If you sign while living in a nursing home or while admitted to a hospital, some states add a rule: a facility employee cannot witness, and a patient advocate or ombudsman may need to be one of the witnesses. People often sign directives during an admission, which is exactly when these rules apply, so ask the social worker which form and witnesses the facility will accept before you sign.

## What happens if the formalities are wrong?

The consequences vary. A document that misses a statutory requirement may be treated as invalid, as simply persuasive evidence of your wishes, or honored anyway by a hospital acting in good faith. Many states protect providers who follow a directive that seems valid on its face, which means a hospital may follow a defective form, but you should not count on it.

The better your formalities, the less your family must argue about them. If you realize an old document had a problem, the simplest fix is to sign a new one correctly and give copies to everyone who has the old version. Our article on [what a living will should say](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) can help you decide whether to rewrite the content at the same time.

## Can you sign remotely or electronically?

Some states now allow remote witnessing or remote online notarization for advance directives, often using live video and identity verification. Others require physical presence. Rules differ on who must be physically present, whether a recording is kept and whether the notary must be in your state. Because rules and technology change, confirm with an attorney before relying on an online signing for a medical document, and ask whether the hospital or facility will accept the result.

## What if you move or travel?

Many states honor an advance directive that was validly signed in another state, and some say so in their statutes. Others honor it only if it substantially complies with local rules, and hospitals tend to follow the path of least resistance. If you spend winters in one state and summers in another, consider signing documents that meet the strictest formalities, or one set for each state. Your [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) is the document most likely to be questioned, so give it the most attention. See also our [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) for local variation, such as Texas.

## What should you do after signing?

A signed living will helps only if someone can find it. Use this checklist.

1. Make several copies of the signed original and keep the original somewhere reachable, not in a safe deposit box.
2. Give copies to your healthcare agent and an alternate.
3. Give a copy to each doctor, and ask that it be scanned into your medical record.
4. Upload it to your hospital's patient portal if one exists, and to a state registry if your state has one.
5. Pair it with a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so your agent can actually obtain your records.
6. Tell your family where it is. A secret directive is no help.
