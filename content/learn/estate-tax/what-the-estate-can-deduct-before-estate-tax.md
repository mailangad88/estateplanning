Walter died at 81, owning a house, a brokerage account, an IRA and a small rental property, all worth a bit over $16,000,000 together. His daughter Ines, the executor, assumed the estate would owe a large tax bill on the amount above $15,000,000. Then the accountant asked for the funeral bill, the mortgage payoff on the rental, the attorney's invoice and a letter from the university Walter had left money to. Each one reduced the number.

The [estate tax](/learn/estate-tax) is not charged on everything a person owned. It is charged on what is left after a defined list of deductions. This article explains the list, what each item needs in order to qualify, and a few traps. The broader mechanics are in our guide to the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption), and you will find the planning side in [how to reduce estate taxes](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-reduce-estate-taxes).

## How does the calculation work?

The return starts with the gross estate: everything the person owned or controlled at death, including life insurance they owned, retirement accounts, and the share of jointly held property. It then subtracts the deductions. What remains is the taxable estate. Taxable lifetime gifts are added back, and the tax is computed on the total. The first $15,000,000 in 2026 is covered by the basic exclusion.

| Step | Walter's estate |
|---|---|
| Gross estate | A little over $16,000,000 |
| Subtract funeral costs | Small |
| Subtract debts (rental mortgage, final bills) | Moderate |
| Subtract administration expenses and legal fees | Moderate |
| Subtract gift to the university | Substantial |
| Taxable estate | Possibly below $15,000,000 |

Walter's rough numbers show why the deductions matter. An estate that looks $1,000,000 over can turn out to owe nothing, or can still owe tax on only a fraction of that sum. The tax rate on the amount above the exemption tops out at 40%, so every deductible dollar saves up to 40 cents.

## Which deductions are allowed?

### Funeral and burial costs

The reasonable cost of the funeral, the headstone and the burial plot is deductible when the estate pays it. If a relative paid the bill from personal funds and the estate reimburses them, the reimbursement counts. If the relative never gets reimbursed, it does not. Our article on [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral) covers how to handle that in practice.

### Debts

Anything the person owed at death qualifies: credit cards, unpaid income tax, medical bills, car loans and mortgages. Two limits matter.

First, a debt must be a genuine obligation. A promissory note a parent signed in favor of a child shortly before death, with nothing received in exchange, will be looked at skeptically.

Second, a mortgage on property included in the estate is deducted only if the full value of that property is counted. If the estate counts only the net equity, the debt has already been subtracted, and it cannot be deducted twice.

### Administration expenses and legal fees

The costs of settling the estate are deductible: court fees, appraisal fees, the attorney's fee, executor compensation, accountant fees and the cost of preserving and selling assets. The fee has to be reasonable and actually paid or payable. A bill for work that benefits an heir personally, or that was not necessary to settle the estate, can be challenged.

An executor who is also an heir may take a fee or skip it. If the fee is paid, it is deductible to the estate but taxable income to the executor. Many family executors skip it for that reason, but the estate loses the deduction. The right choice depends on who the heirs are and what tax rates apply, and it is worth a short conversation with the accountant.

### The marital deduction

Everything left to a surviving spouse who is a U.S. citizen passes free of estate tax, with no dollar limit. It has to pass in a form that qualifies: outright, in a qualifying trust, or by beneficiary designation. A spouse who is not a citizen gets the deduction only through a special trust, described in the article on a [QDOT for a non-citizen spouse](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot). The deduction postpones the tax rather than removing it, because the assets are then in the survivor's estate. [Portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) helps with that.

### The charitable deduction

Property passing to a qualified charity under a will, trust or beneficiary form is deductible in full. If Walter's will leaves $1,000,000 to the university, the taxable estate drops by $1,000,000. Partial interests, such as gifts to charity through a [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust), are deductible only for the charity's share, and the rules are specific.

Naming charity as the beneficiary of a traditional IRA is often the smartest way to give, because the charity pays no income tax on it and the family gets other assets with a stepped-up basis.

### State death taxes

If the estate paid a state estate tax or inheritance tax, that amount can be deducted from the federal taxable estate. States such as Washington and Massachusetts charge estate tax at much lower thresholds than the federal level, and some states charge inheritance tax on beneficiaries. Our article on [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) lists the pattern.

## Can the estate deduct an expense twice?

No, and this is the most common error. Administration expenses and certain losses can be deducted either on the estate tax return or on the estate's own income tax return, but never on both. Which one is better depends on the numbers.

- If the estate owes no estate tax, the income tax return is usually the better place. The estate's income tax bracket climbs quickly, so deductions are valuable there.
- If the estate owes estate tax at 40%, the estate tax return is usually better, since the rate is higher than most income tax rates.

The executor makes the choice by how the expenses are claimed. A good preparer will model both. This is one more reason to keep a simple ledger of every payment the executor makes. Our overview of [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) explains the other returns the executor may need to file.

## What does not qualify?

- **Costs the heirs pay for themselves.** Fees for a fight among beneficiaries, or for planning their own taxes.
- **Funeral costs that go beyond what is reasonable,** for example, a lavish memorial far outside what the family usually spends, if the estate cannot show it was a legitimate expense.
- **Gifts to individuals,** including family members other than a qualifying spouse.
- **Debts the estate does not actually owe,** or debts that are unenforceable.
- **Charitable gifts to groups that do not qualify,** such as an individual's fundraising page or a foreign charity that is not recognized under the tax code.

## Do deductions matter if the estate is under $15,000,000?

For federal tax, often not, because no return is due unless the gross estate is above the exemption. But three situations keep them relevant.

1. **The state tax.** Several states have their own estate or inheritance tax at far lower thresholds, and their deductions can differ from the federal list.
2. **A portability election.** A surviving spouse who files a return to capture the unused exemption still needs the numbers to be right.
3. **Income tax.** The estate's income tax return can use the expenses even when no estate tax return is needed.

If you are unsure whether a return is required, the article on [Form 706](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) lists the filing triggers and due dates.
