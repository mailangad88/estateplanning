The basic step-up rule is easy to say: inherit something, and its tax basis resets to its value at death. The trouble starts when the asset had two owners. A house in both spouses' names, a brokerage account held jointly with a sibling, a farm bought during marriage in California. Each of those gets a different answer, and the difference can be tens of thousands of dollars in capital gains tax.

This article covers the cases the main guide to the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), part of our [estate and gift tax library](/learn/estate-tax), does not go into: shared ownership, community property, and how you prove the number once you have it.

## The short version

- Spouses in a common-law state: half of a jointly owned asset steps up when the first spouse dies.
- Spouses in a community property state: usually both halves step up.
- Joint owners who are not married: the portion counted in the deceased owner's estate steps up.
- Cash, life insurance proceeds and retirement accounts are different. Cash has nothing to step up; retirement accounts are taxed as income.
- Keep a date-of-death valuation. You will need it when you sell.

## How does the step-up work for a house owned by a married couple?

In most states the answer is half. Under federal tax rules, when spouses own property together as joint tenants or tenants by the entirety, one half is treated as belonging to the first spouse to die, regardless of who paid for it. That half is included in the estate, so it takes a basis equal to its fair market value at the date of death. The other half keeps its old basis.

### Example: Joan and Ray

Joan and Ray bought their house in 1990 for $140,000. Ray dies in 2026 when it is worth $540,000. Joan inherits.

| | Original basis | New basis |
|---|---|---|
| Ray's half | $70,000 | $270,000 |
| Joan's half | $70,000 | $70,000 |
| Joan's total | | $340,000 |

If Joan sells for $540,000, her taxable gain is $200,000. She may be able to exclude a large part of it under the home sale exclusion if she meets the ownership and use tests, which makes the number far less frightening, but it does not remove the need for records.

A second home or a rental is harder. There is no home sale exclusion to soften the result, so the half that did not step up leaves real gain behind.

## What changes in a community property state?

Community property states treat most things earned or bought during a marriage as owned half and half by each spouse. When one spouse dies, federal tax law lets the whole property take the date-of-death value, including the survivor's half, as long as at least half the property was community property.

Using Joan and Ray's numbers in a community property state, Joan's basis would be $540,000 and her gain on a sale at that price zero. Nine states have this system, and a few others let couples opt in with a community property trust. The rules for what is community and what is separate property, and how a trust affects it, differ by state. Our guides for [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) show how two of those states handle it.

## What about joint owners who are not married?

Siblings, parents and children, and unmarried partners can all hold joint accounts or property with survivorship rights. The rule is different from the spousal one. Generally, the whole property is included in the estate of the first to die, except the part the survivor can show they paid for or contributed. That included portion gets the step-up.

So if Mother adds her son Dev to a house she paid for entirely, and she dies, the entire house is included in her estate, and Dev takes the house at its full date-of-death value. If she had instead deeded him a share years earlier, that share would have been a gift and would carry her old basis. The article on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) walks through the arithmetic, and the [lady bird deed](/learn/probate/lady-bird-deed-explained) is the usual way to avoid the problem.

## Does a deed transfer before death lose the step-up?

Yes, if it is a completed gift. A deed that gives the child ownership now means the child takes your basis for that portion. A deed that only takes effect at your death, such as a transfer-on-death deed or a lady bird deed, leaves the property in your estate for tax purposes, and the child inherits at the stepped-up value. Whether you also want a trust to hold the property is a separate question that depends on your state and your family.

## How do you find the stepped-up basis of what you inherited?

Work through these steps.

1. Identify the date of death. That is your valuation date, unless the executor elected the alternate date six months later on a Form 706.
2. Get a value for each asset. Bank accounts: the statement balance. Stocks and funds: the average of the day's high and low prices, which most brokers will report for you. Real estate: a retrospective appraisal by a licensed appraiser, or the value on Form 706 if one was filed.
3. If a Form 706 was filed, ask for your copy of the Form 8971 statement. When an estate tax return is required, the executor reports each beneficiary's values, and your basis cannot be higher than the number on it.
4. Write the values down with the source and date. Keep the appraisal.
5. Give the figure to your tax preparer when you sell.

Cryptocurrency follows the same logic; see [how inherited crypto is taxed](/learn/digital-assets/how-inherited-crypto-is-taxed). If you plan to sell a house, read [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) for the practical steps.

## What does not get a step-up?

Traditional IRAs, 401(k)s and annuities do not. They carry deferred income tax that comes out when the beneficiary withdraws. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years. Assets you gave away during life, and property in most irrevocable trusts, are also outside the rule.

## A mistake to avoid

People sometimes retitle everything into joint names to "save probate" and only learn about the basis cost at a sale. If you are considering it, run the numbers first. The estate tax is not the issue for most families; the capital gains tax is.

## What if the surviving spouse remarries or sells quickly?

Remarriage does not undo a step-up. The new basis belongs to the property from the date of death, and the survivor can sell the next week without owing tax on growth that happened before. Gain or loss is measured only from the date-of-death value. Inherited property is treated as held long term, whatever the actual holding time, so a quick sale does not push a gain into the higher short-term rate.

A loss works the same way. If Joan sold the house for $500,000 against a $540,000 basis, she would have a $40,000 loss on the date-of-death value. For a personal residence that loss is generally not deductible, but for an investment property or inherited stock it can be.

## Why the paperwork matters more than people expect

Basis disputes happen years later, when the person who knew the facts is gone. A sale of the family farm in 2036 is audited on a basis figure someone wrote down in 2026. The fix is dull: one folder with the appraisal, the closing statement for improvements, and a one-page note of how the basis was calculated, kept with the deed. Our attorneys often add a copy to the trust binder so the next generation does not have to reconstruct it.
