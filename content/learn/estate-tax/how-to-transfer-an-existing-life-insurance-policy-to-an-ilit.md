Neil, 66, owns a $3 million permanent life insurance policy that he bought 25 years ago. He has an estate of roughly $4.6 million, a house, an IRA, and a brokerage account. He lives in Illinois, where the state estate tax starts at $4 million, and he has just learned that the policy he thought was "outside" his estate is inside it. His agent says an irrevocable life insurance trust could fix that. Neil wants to know how to move the policy he already has.

This article explains how to transfer an existing life insurance policy to an ILIT, step by step, and when buying a fresh policy inside the trust is the safer route. For what an ILIT is and who needs one, see our overview of the [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust). The wider subject sits in our [estate and gift tax pillar](/learn/estate-tax), and ways to cut a taxable estate are in [how to reduce estate taxes](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-reduce-estate-taxes).

## Why move a policy at all?

Life insurance is income tax free to the beneficiary, but it is part of the owner's taxable estate if the insured owns it or holds incidents of ownership such as the right to change the beneficiary or borrow against it. A $3 million policy added to a $1.6 million estate is a $4.6 million estate. Federal tax is not an issue for most families at $15,000,000 per person in 2026. State tax can be. In Illinois, the policy can be the dollar that crosses the threshold, and the [Illinois estate tax guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) shows how steeply the tax rises above it.

Moving the policy to a trust the insured does not control takes the proceeds out of the estate, if the timing works.

## What are the steps to transfer a policy to an ILIT?

Moving a policy is a paperwork process, but order matters. Do it in this sequence.

1. **Sign the ILIT first.** The trust must exist, be irrevocable and have a trustee other than the insured, with its own taxpayer number and bank account.
2. **Ask the insurer for its forms.** Request the assignment of ownership and a beneficiary change form, plus a valuation statement for gift tax purposes (insurers commonly use Form 712 for this).
3. **Assign ownership to the trustee.** The owner signs; the trustee accepts. Change the beneficiary to the trust at the same time.
4. **Report the gift if needed.** The value of the transfer is a gift. If it is more than the annual exclusion, which is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, file a gift tax return. It will use part of the lifetime exclusion. Our article on [Form 709](/learn/estate-tax/form-709-gift-tax-return-gift-splitting-and-net-gifts) explains the return.
5. **Fund future premiums by gifts to the trust.** Each year the insured gives the trustee cash, and the trustee pays the insurer.
6. **Send Crummey notices.** Every time you add money, the trustee sends each beneficiary a letter stating that they can withdraw their share for a short window, typically 30 days. That right makes the gift qualify for the annual exclusion. Keep copies.
7. **Update the file.** Tell your agent, your attorney and your executor where the policy and trust records are.

## What is the three-year rule?

If the insured dies within three years of transferring a policy, the proceeds are pulled back into the taxable estate. Congress wrote this rule to stop deathbed transfers. After three years, the proceeds are out.

Neil is 66 and in good health. If he transfers the policy and lives past age 69, the $3 million stays out of his estate. If he dies at 68, the policy is counted as though he still owned it, and the trust and the tax sit side by side. The trust still pays his family. The tax simply is not saved.

An alternative is to have the trustee apply for a new policy. A policy the trust buys from the start was never owned by the insured, so the three-year rule does not apply. The cost is that the insured needs to pass underwriting and the new policy is priced at today's age. For a person in poor health, the old policy may be the only one available, and the three-year rule is a risk worth taking.

| | Transfer existing policy | New policy bought by the trust |
|---|---|---|
| Three-year rule | Applies | Does not apply |
| Underwriting | None, but the insurer values the policy | Required |
| Gift on transfer | Yes, based on policy value | Only premiums |
| Premium | What you already pay | Based on current age and health |
| Best when | Health is poor or the old policy is hard to replace | Health is good and a new policy is affordable |

## Which kinds of policy transfer easily, and which do not?

A policy that has a large cash value is a bigger gift than a term policy near its end. A few cases need extra care.

- **Policy loans.** If the policy has a loan larger than what the owner paid in premiums, moving it to a trust can be treated as a part sale and can create income tax. Repay the loan first if you can.
- **Group term life through an employer.** Often assignable, but the rules differ by plan, and the employer's form controls.
- **Term policies.** Check whether the policy can be converted to permanent coverage before the conversion window ends. The trustee may be able to own the term policy and convert it later.
- **Second-to-die policies.** These are common with married couples. The three-year rule still applies to the transfer by either spouse.
- **Policies with a surrender charge or special rider.** Ask whether an ownership change affects the policy's stated benefits.

Ownership change requests are not the same as [changing a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-to-change-the-beneficiary-on-a-401k-ira-or-life-insurance-policy). If you only change the beneficiary to the trust and leave yourself as the owner, nothing leaves your estate.

## What mistakes do attorneys see most often?

The same four mistakes come up again and again.

- **Paying premiums directly.** If Neil writes the check to the insurer, instead of gifting to the trust first, the paper trail breaks and the IRS can argue the insured still controls the policy. Gift to the trust, then let the trustee pay.
- **Skipping the Crummey notices.** Without them, each premium gift may be a taxable gift, not one that qualifies for the exclusion.
- **Naming the insured as trustee.** The insured should not serve as trustee or have the power to change the beneficiary. A sibling, a child or a corporate trustee can serve.
- **Forgetting the old beneficiary.** If the insurer keeps the previous beneficiary on file, the proceeds can end up in the wrong hands. Confirm the change in writing.

If a trust is badly set up, our article on [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) explains why fixing it later is hard.

## Does the transfer use up part of the lifetime exemption?

Often it does, but usually only a little. The gift is measured by the policy's value, not by its death benefit. For a term policy, that is roughly the unearned premium. For a permanent policy it is based on the reserve and premiums paid, which the insurer reports on its valuation form. Neil's $3 million policy might be valued at a fraction of that, and if the value is above the annual exclusion, he reports the excess on a gift tax return and subtracts it from his $15,000,000 lifetime exclusion. No tax is due unless he has already used the exclusion, which is rare. If a spouse is a beneficiary of the trust, the two of them can split the gift. The [gift tax annual exclusion article](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) explains the yearly limit that the premium gifts rely on.

## What does it cost?

Costs fall into three buckets: attorney fees to draft the trust, an accountant for any gift tax returns, and the annual administration of Crummey notices and records. For a policy that might save six or seven figures in estate tax, the amounts are usually small, but they are not nothing. In an estate that will never be taxed, the work may not pay.
