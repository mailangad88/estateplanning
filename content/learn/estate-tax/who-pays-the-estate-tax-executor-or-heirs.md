Marguerite died in March with an estate of about $18 million. Her son Jonas is the executor. A friend told him that the children will each get a tax bill. Another said the estate pays it before anyone is paid. Jonas wants to know whose money the check will come from and whether he is on the hook if he gets it wrong.

If you are asking who pays the estate tax, the executor or the heirs, the short answer is that the estate pays and the executor handles the payment. The harder question is which beneficiaries absorb the cost, and that is a drafting question. This page covers both. For the tax itself, read our [overview of the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) and the [estate and gift tax pillar](/learn/estate-tax).

## Who actually pays the estate tax?

The estate pays federal estate tax, from estate assets, and the executor signs the return and sends the payment. Form 706 and the tax are both due nine months after death. The tax is figured on the whole taxable estate in excess of the exclusion, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, at a top rate of 40%.

Beneficiaries do not write a check to the IRS. They receive what is left after the tax, and in practice the executor sells assets or uses cash and investments in the estate to cover it. The [Form 706 article](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) describes the return itself.

## Who bears the cost: the will, the trust or state law?

Who ultimately bears the cost is a separate question from who writes the check. Three layers answer it, in this order.

1. **A tax clause.** A will or trust can say that all estate taxes are paid from the residue, or that they are divided among the recipients in proportion to what each receives. This is the most important sentence in the document for a large estate.
2. **State apportionment law.** If the document is silent, state law decides. Some states spread the tax among everyone who receives taxable property. Others put it on the residuary estate first. Your attorney can tell you which rule applies in your state.
3. **Federal rights of recovery.** For certain assets, federal law lets the estate recover a share of the tax from the recipient, even if the will is silent. These include life insurance, property over which the decedent held a power of appointment and certain trust interests, unless the will clearly says otherwise.

Jonas's mother left a house worth $1.5 million to Jonas, $2 million to a church, and the rest equally to Jonas and his sister Elise. The gift to the church is deductible and carries no tax. A clause that charges taxes to the residue means Jonas and Elise share the cost. Without any clause, and in a state that follows the apportionment approach, Jonas might bear tax on the house as well, while the church pays none.

## What does a worked example look like?

This example is for illustration only and ignores prior taxable gifts, deductions and state tax. Suppose a single person dies in 2026 with an $18 million taxable estate. The excess over the $15 million exclusion is $3 million. At 40%, the federal tax is about $1,200,000.

| Will provision | Who bears the $1.2 million |
|---|---|
| Taxes paid from the residue | Residuary beneficiaries only, usually the children |
| Taxes apportioned among all recipients | Each taxable recipient in proportion to value; charities and a spouse pay none |
| No clause, state follows apportionment | Same as the second row, by state statute |
| No clause, state charges the residue | Residue pays; a specific gift to a friend arrives untaxed |

The tax can also change the size of a charitable gift. A residue split between children and a charity, with taxes charged to the residue, can cause a formula problem that shrinks the charity's share and increases the tax. That circular calculation is a reason for an attorney to draft the clause.

## Is the executor personally liable?

Yes, in certain circumstances. Federal law makes a fiduciary personally liable if the estate pays other debts or distributes property before the government's claim is satisfied, up to the amount paid out. For an executor, that means two rules in practice:

- Do not make final distributions until the estate tax is paid or a closing letter confirms the matter is settled.
- Keep documentation of every valuation and payment, so you can show you acted reasonably.

The IRS also holds a lien on estate property for a number of years, so a buyer may not get clear title to an asset until the tax is paid. If the executor feels exposed, our page on [whether a trustee or fiduciary can be personally liable](/learn/after-a-death/can-a-trustee-be-personally-liable) describes how courts view that risk, and [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) lists the steps.

## What should an executor do before paying anyone?

Before the first distribution, an executor can follow a short sequence that protects both the estate and the executor.

1. List every asset and its value on the date of death, and add up the likely tax. The [guide to how assets are valued](/learn/estate-tax/how-assets-are-valued-for-estate-tax) explains what appraisals the IRS expects.
2. Read the will and trust for a tax clause, and note which beneficiaries it charges.
3. Set aside a reserve in cash or easily sold investments equal to the estimated tax plus a cushion for interest.
4. File the return or request the extension on time, and pay what is due.
5. Distribute only the part that is clearly beyond the tax, and keep the rest until the IRS accepts the return or issues a closing letter.

Heirs often push for early distributions. A written explanation of why the reserve exists, with the numbers, usually calms the room faster than an argument does.

## What if the estate does not have the cash?

Estates with a house, a business or retirement accounts often face a gap between the tax and the liquid assets. The executor can sell assets, borrow against them or, where a closely held business makes up a large share of the estate, ask for an installment arrangement. The estate-tax elections article, [alternate valuation, deferral and farm valuation](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-elections-alternate-valuation-deferral-and-farm-valuation), explains deferral. Planning ahead with life insurance held outside the taxable estate is the cleaner fix.

## How does this work with a state estate or inheritance tax?

State taxes follow their own rules. Illinois imposes an estate tax on estates above a $4 million threshold, paid by the estate, and the Illinois return is the executor's job. The [Illinois estate tax guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) has details. New Jersey has no estate tax for deaths since 2018, but it charges an inheritance tax on certain heirs, figured on each heir's share, and the executor generally files and pays it from the estate or collects from the heirs. See [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

An estate can owe both a federal and a state tax, so a tax clause should address each by name. The broader overview, [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes), shows how the states compare.
