Nora, 78, owns 2,000 shares of a company stock she bought in 1995 for $40,000. They are worth $240,000 now. She has two children, and her son Greg wants help with a down payment. Nora wonders whether she should give the shares to Greg today or leave them to him in her will, and which way leaves him with less tax.

For this stock the answer is clear, and it surprises many people: waiting costs Greg less. This page explains why, when that reverses, and what Illinois and New Jersey add. It is part of our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## Is it better to give assets to your children now or leave them at death?

For appreciated assets such as stock and real estate, leaving them at death is usually cheaper for the child in income tax. For cash, the timing does not change the child's tax. The difference comes from the basis, which is the number the child subtracts from the sale price to find the taxable gain.

| | Gift during life | Inheritance at death |
|---|---|---|
| Child's basis | Your original cost (carryover) | Value on the date of death (step-up) |
| Built-in gain | Passes to the child | Mostly erased |
| Gift tax return | Form 709 if over the annual exclusion | Not applicable |
| Counts against exemption | Yes, if over the annual exclusion | Yes, at death if the estate is taxed |
| Medicaid | 60-month look-back penalty risk | No look-back |
| Control | You lose it now | You keep it for life |

## What is carryover basis, and why does a gift cost more?

When you give property, the child takes your basis. If Nora gives Greg the shares and he sells at $240,000, his basis is her $40,000 and he reports a $200,000 gain. At the long-term federal rates that apply to most families, the tax on that gain can be tens of thousands of dollars, before any state tax.

Compare the inheritance. If Nora keeps the shares and dies owning them, Greg inherits at the date-of-death value, say $240,000. If he sells right away, his gain is close to zero. The same shares, the same sale price, and one route produces a large tax bill while the other produces almost none. Our page on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains the rule and its limits.

Here is that comparison for Nora's shares, assuming a sale at $240,000.

| | Gift now | Inheritance |
|---|---|---|
| Greg's basis | $40,000 | $240,000 |
| Taxable gain on sale | $200,000 | $0 |
| Greg's federal tax | A capital gains tax on $200,000 | None on this sale |

A gift is not a sale, so Nora herself owes no tax on the gift.

## When does giving make more sense?

Giving wins in these cases:

1. **Cash.** Cash has a basis equal to its value, so there is nothing to step up. A gift can help a child now at no income tax cost, within the annual exclusion.
2. **An estate that may owe estate tax.** If Nora's estate were $22 million, appreciation counted in the estate would be taxed at up to 40% above the exemption. Moving a fast-growing asset out of the estate early removes its future growth from the taxable total. The extra income tax the child pays is lower than the estate tax saved. See [use your exemption now or wait](/learn/estate-tax/use-your-estate-tax-exemption-now-or-wait).
3. **An asset that has fallen in value.** For a loss asset, the child's basis for a loss is the lower of your basis or the value at the gift. Giving a loser is rarely worth it. Selling it yourself and giving the cash lets you use the loss.
4. **Help that cannot wait.** A down payment, tuition or medical bill is worth more to the child at 30 than at 60. A lifetime gift also shows you how your child manages money.
5. **Growth under your control.** A grantor trust or family partnership can transfer growth and still preserve some flexibility. These are covered under [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies).

## How much can you give without paying gift tax?

You can give $19,000 per recipient in 2026 without any report. A married couple can give $38,000 to each child by splitting. Anything above that is a reportable gift, filed on Form 709, but no tax is due until your lifetime total exceeds the $15,000,000 exclusion. At that point the rate is up to 40%. The full rules are in the article on the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion), and the page about [whether lifetime gifts count toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax) shows how the totals add up.

Most families will never pay federal gift or estate tax. The decision for them is about income tax, control and fairness, not the exemption.

## What about the house?

Adding a child to the deed is a partial gift. If Nora adds Greg to her house deed, he takes her old basis on his share and she may need a Form 709 for the value. The house is also exposed to his divorce and judgments, and she may lose the right to sell without him. Leaving the house through a will, a living trust or a [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) keeps the step-up and her control. The article on [joint property and the step-up](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis-for-married-couples-and-joint-property) covers how it works for spouses.

Gifts also invite family friction. If one child receives the house early, the others may feel shortchanged. See [what happens when a parent gave money or a house to one child](/learn/after-a-death/parent-gave-money-or-a-house-to-one-child-before-death).

## How do Illinois and New Jersey treat gifts?

Both states look back at lifetime gifts, in different ways.

- **Illinois.** The state estate tax applies to estates above $4 million, and its calculation takes certain lifetime gifts into account. A large gift does not necessarily save Illinois estate tax. See the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) guide.
- **New Jersey.** The state has no estate tax but charges an inheritance tax to some heirs. Close family members, such as children and grandchildren, are generally exempt, while siblings and non-relatives pay. Certain gifts made shortly before death can be pulled into the inheritance tax, so a late gift to a sibling or friend is not a safe way around it. See the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

Neither state changes the basis rule, which is federal.

## Does Medicaid change the answer?

Yes, and for families facing nursing home costs it can outweigh tax. A gift made inside the 60-month look-back in most states triggers a penalty period, and a taxable gift is not required for that penalty. The half-a-loaf and caregiver child rules are in the article on [gifting to children before Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid). If you may need care within five years, ask before moving any asset.

## A short decision checklist

- Is the asset cash? Give within the annual exclusion if you can afford to.
- Is it appreciated stock or real estate in a family under the estate tax exemption? Hold it and leave it at death, or use a trust.
- Is the estate near or above the exemption? Talk to an attorney about gifts of growth assets.
- Might you need nursing home care in five years? Do not give before checking the look-back.
- Is the child in a risky marriage, or in debt? Use a trust instead of an outright gift.
