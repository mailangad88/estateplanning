Anika, a U.S. citizen living in Ohio, was born in India. When her mother died in Mumbai, she learned that she and her brother would inherit an apartment and a savings account. A cousin told her the American government would take "half". A different cousin said she did not need to tell anyone. Neither is right.

Cross-border estates cause more confusion than almost any other area of tax, because three questions get tangled together: who is inheriting, who died, and where the property sits. This page separates them. It covers a U.S. citizen inheriting from abroad, a nonresident alien who owns U.S. property, and Americans living overseas. For the domestic baseline, see our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) and the article on [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## Which situation are you in?

| Who died | Who inherits | What the U.S. cares about |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign person, assets abroad | U.S. citizen or resident | Reporting the receipt; reporting foreign accounts; later income and gains |
| Foreign person, owned U.S. property | Anyone | U.S. estate tax on the U.S. property, with a small exemption |
| U.S. citizen or resident living abroad | Anyone | U.S. estate tax on worldwide assets, with the full exemption |
| Former citizen or long-term resident | Anyone | Special expatriation rules for a number of years |

Start by finding the row that matches. Everything else follows from it.

## Is a foreign inheritance taxable income in the U.S.?

For the person receiving it, generally no. The United States does not have a federal inheritance tax, and the tax code excludes gifts and inheritances from income. That is true whether the money comes from a cousin in Ohio or a mother in Mumbai. The exclusion applies to the inheritance itself.

Three things are taxable after you receive it:

1. **Income the asset produces.** Interest on the savings account, dividends on shares and rent from the apartment are income, whether paid to a foreign bank or not.
2. **Gain on a later sale.** The cost basis is generally the value at the date of death, measured in U.S. dollars at the exchange rate that day. That is the foreign counterpart of the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). Keep the valuation and the rate.
3. **Currency movement.** In some situations a gain or loss from exchange rates is separately recognized, especially on a foreign bank account that you close.

The other country may tax too. Some countries have an inheritance tax, an estate tax or a transfer fee on the property. Those payments often reduce your U.S. tax through a credit or a treaty, but the coordination is not automatic.

## What is Form 3520, and when do you file it?

Form 3520 is an information return. It tells the IRS that a U.S. person received a large gift or inheritance from a foreign individual or foreign estate. No tax is calculated on it. The point is that the IRS wants to know when money crosses the border.

- **Threshold.** The duty starts when the total received from foreign individuals in a year goes over the amount in the current form instructions. The threshold is for the year, not per person, so several small inheritances from the same source may add up.
- **Deadline.** The form is due with your income tax return for the year of receipt, including extensions. It is filed separately from the return.
- **Penalty.** A late or missing form can bring a penalty based on a percentage of the amount received. That is far more than the cost of filing.
- **Foreign trusts.** If the inheritance comes from a foreign trust, the form asks more, and the rules about distributions and accumulated income are harsher.

If you realize you missed a filing from earlier years, do not ignore it. Procedures exist for filers who correct the omission voluntarily, and the outcome is usually better than waiting for a notice.

## Do inherited foreign accounts need extra reports?

Yes, and this part catches many families. A U.S. person with a financial interest in, or signature authority over, foreign accounts must file the FBAR, a form for the Treasury's financial crimes unit, if the combined balance in the accounts exceeded the threshold at any point in the year. A second form, attached to the income tax return, applies above a different threshold. Both focus on the account existing, not on income.

A mother's savings account in Mumbai becomes yours at death in practice, even while the bank is still processing the paperwork. If you are a joint holder or have signing power, the duty may begin right away. Ask the preparer to check both forms.

## Does U.S. estate tax apply to people who live abroad?

It depends on who the decedent was.

**A U.S. citizen or resident abroad.** The estate tax follows the person. A citizen who lives in Lisbon is treated like one who lives in Dallas: the worldwide estate is counted, and the exemption is $15,000,000 for 2026. A treaty or a foreign tax credit may avoid double tax, but the U.S. return is still due for a large estate. Our article on the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) explains the calculation, and the piece on [wills when moving states or living abroad](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) explains why a local will matters.

**A nonresident alien.** Only property with a U.S. location is counted. That includes U.S. real estate, shares of U.S. corporations, and tangible property here such as art kept in an American home. Bank deposits and certain portfolio debt are usually left out, and so is life insurance on the person's life. The exemption is much smaller than the one for citizens and residents unless a treaty says otherwise, so even a modest U.S. holding, such as a vacation condo, can create a taxable estate. The estate files a nonresident return and pays the tax before U.S. property can be transferred.

Planning can help. A foreign investor might hold U.S. property through a foreign corporation or a trust, or choose life insurance, depending on the home country. These steps have costs and pitfalls, and they must follow both countries' rules.

## What about a spouse who is not a citizen?

The unlimited marital deduction is available only if the surviving spouse is a U.S. citizen. If the spouse is not, the estate generally needs a qualified domestic trust to defer the tax. The article on the [QDOT](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot) covers that route, and also gifts to a non-citizen spouse, which have a yearly cap.

## What if the property abroad needs a local court?

Often it does. A foreign apartment is dealt with under the law of the country where it sits, and the process there may involve a notary, a court order or a certificate of inheritance. This is similar to [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) between states. The foreign proceeding does not replace the U.S. one, but you will need a local lawyer for it. Our attorneys can coordinate with them, but we do not practice foreign law.

## What should you do first?

1. **Get a certified valuation** of the property in the foreign currency at the date of death, and record the exchange rate used.
2. **List every account and asset** that now belongs to you or that you can sign for.
3. **Check the reporting forms:** the information return for the receipt and the account reports.
4. **Ask whether a treaty applies** between the United States and the country involved.
5. **Keep records** of foreign taxes paid, because they may be creditable.
6. **Do not move the money in odd ways** to avoid reporting. Structuring transfers is a separate legal problem.
