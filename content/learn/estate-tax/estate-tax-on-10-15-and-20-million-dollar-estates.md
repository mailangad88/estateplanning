Most people who ask this question have a number in mind, often from a house, a retirement account and a life insurance policy added together, and they want to know what the government would take. The answer for the great majority of families is nothing. For the few who are over the line, the math is simpler than the tax table suggests.

Below are four worked examples at different sizes, then an explanation of why the "graduated" rates behave like a flat rate in practice. Every figure is the 2026 federal rule. For the broader picture, start with our guide to [estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## The short version

- Each person has a $15,000,000 basic exclusion in 2026. Married couples can use two of them, $30 million, with [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples).
- Tax applies only to the amount above the exclusion, at a top rate of 40%.
- Assets left to a U.S.-citizen spouse are deductible, so the first death usually triggers no tax at all.
- Gifts you made over the annual limit during life reduce the amount left at death.
- Your state may tax you at a far lower level. Check that separately.

## How much is the tax at $10 million, $15 million and $20 million?

Here is the arithmetic for a single person who made no large lifetime gifts and has no deductions beyond the basics.

| Taxable estate | Amount over $15,000,000 | Federal estate tax |
|---|---|---|
| $10,000,000 | None | $0 |
| $15,000,000 | None | $0 |
| $20,000,000 | $5,000,000 | About $2,000,000 |
| $35,000,000 | $20,000,000 | About $8,000,000 |

The "about" is there because a real return adjusts for prior taxable gifts, state death taxes that can be deducted, and the exact size of the credit. For a quick estimate the 40% shortcut is dependable.

### Example: Walter at $10 million

Walter, 71, a widower, has a $2.4 million house, $5 million in investments, a $1.8 million retirement account and $800,000 of other property. He owes no federal estate tax. His real exposure is his state, and income tax on the retirement account for whoever inherits it.

### Example: Denise at exactly $15 million

Denise's estate is $15 million today. She owes nothing, but she has zero margin. If her portfolio grows 5% a year and she makes no gifts, she is over the line in a few years, and the exclusion is only adjusted for inflation. Our article on [planning when you are near the exemption](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-planning-when-you-are-near-the-exemption) covers what to do in her position.

### Example: Paul at $20 million, and the married version

Paul, 68, is single, with a $20 million estate made up of a business interest, real estate and securities. Five million dollars sits above his exclusion. His family would owe about $2 million within nine months of his death, which is a cash problem if most of the estate is a business or land. Our article on [what happens when the estate cannot pay the tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-if-the-estate-cannot-pay-the-estate-tax) explains the options.

Now take Marcus and Elena, married, with the same $20 million together. If Marcus dies first and leaves everything to Elena, the marital deduction removes the tax. His unused exclusion moves to her through portability if the executor files a timely Form 706. Elena then has up to $30 million of shelter against a $20 million estate. Same assets, no tax. The only difference is marriage and one filing.

## Why are the rates "graduated" if it works like a flat 40%?

The statute contains a table of brackets that starts at 18% on the first dollars and rises to 40% on amounts over $1 million. Nobody pays the low brackets on a large estate, because of how the credit is built.

The unified credit equals the tax the table would produce on $15,000,000. So the tax on the first $15 million is calculated and then cancelled in full. What is left is the tax on the slice above $15 million, and every dollar in that slice is already past the top bracket. The result: 40% on the excess, nothing below.

This is also why small increases above the line are costly. An executor who finds an extra $100,000 of property in an estate that is already over the exclusion adds $40,000 of tax.

## What counts as the taxable estate?

The taxable estate is the gross estate less deductions. The gross estate is wider than most people expect. It includes the house, bank and brokerage accounts, retirement accounts, the death benefit of life insurance you own, the value of a revocable trust, and your share of jointly owned property. Our article on [what counts toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-counts-toward-the-estate-tax) lists the pieces.

Then the executor subtracts debts, funeral and administration costs, charitable gifts and amounts passing to a spouse, as described in [what the estate can deduct](/learn/estate-tax/what-the-estate-can-deduct-before-estate-tax). Finally, adjusted taxable gifts from earlier years are added in. The rates and credit are applied to that last figure.

## How do lifetime gifts change the number?

They use up the same exclusion. The credit is unified, so a $3 million taxable gift in 2024 leaves $12 million of exclusion at death in the 2026 tables. Gifts within the $19,000 per recipient annual exclusion do not count at all. See [do lifetime gifts count toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax).

## How to estimate your own number

1. List everything you own, including life insurance on your own life and retirement accounts.
2. Subtract mortgages and other debts.
3. Add taxable gifts you reported on Form 709 in earlier years.
4. Subtract $15,000,000 (or $30 million for a married couple planning with portability).
5. Multiply anything left by 40%.
6. Look up your state's threshold separately.

## What about my state?

A federal bill of zero does not end the discussion. Some states set their threshold in the low millions. Our [state estate and inheritance tax guide](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) explains the difference between an estate tax and an inheritance tax, and the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) shows where yours falls. A family at $10 million can owe a six-figure state bill and no federal tax at all.

## Will these numbers change?

Congress can change the exclusion and the rate, and it has done so many times. As of 2026 the $15,000,000 figure has no scheduled sunset, which is different from the past. That is not a promise it stays. We usually advise people near the line to build a plan that works at a lower number, rather than guess.
