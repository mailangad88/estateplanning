Imagine your mother bought her house for $50,000 in 1985. Today it is worth $450,000. If she gives it to you now, and you sell, you could owe capital gains tax on $400,000. If you inherit it instead, you might owe almost nothing. That difference is the step-up in basis, and it shapes some of the most important choices in family planning.

This article explains what basis is, how the step-up works, when it does not apply, and how it compares with gifting. For the full picture of related taxes, see our guide to [estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## What is basis?

Basis is the starting number used to figure a gain or loss when you sell something. Usually it is what you paid, plus improvements. If you buy shares for $10,000 and sell for $25,000, your gain is $15,000, and capital gains tax applies to that $15,000.

Basis changes depending on how you acquire the asset. If you buy it, basis is the cost. If you inherit it, basis generally is the fair market value at death. If you receive it as a gift, basis generally carries over from the giver.

## How does the step-up in basis work?

Under federal tax law, property that passes to someone because of the owner's death generally receives a new basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death. Estates can sometimes elect a value six months later, called the alternate valuation date, but only if that lowers both the estate's value and the estate tax. For heirs, the key point is that the clock for built-in gain essentially starts over.

An heir who sells soon after inheriting often reports little or no taxable gain. Inherited assets are also treated as long-term holdings automatically, which means any gain after the date of death gets long-term capital gains rates, whatever the holding time.

### Example: Marcus and the family home

Ruth bought a home for $90,000. When she died it was worth $360,000, and her son Marcus inherited it.

| | Inherit | Gift while alive |
|---|---|---|
| Marcus's basis | $360,000 | $90,000 |
| Sale price | $370,000 | $370,000 |
| Taxable gain | $10,000 | $280,000 |

Marcus would report a $10,000 gain in the first column. In the second, he would report $280,000, which could mean tens of thousands in federal tax and state tax on top. Over time a family can save a significant sum by letting property pass at death instead of giving it away.

## Which assets get a step-up?

Most assets included in the decedent's gross estate for tax purposes get one:

- Homes and land
- Stocks, bonds, and mutual funds in taxable accounts
- Business interests
- Collectibles and art
- Assets in a revocable living trust, because they are included in the grantor's estate
- Assets in POD and TOD accounts, and on a transfer-on-death deed
- The deceased spouse's half of jointly owned property, and for community property, both halves

The tax law that applies is based on inclusion in the estate, not on whether the estate owes tax. An estate of $2 million owes no federal estate tax in 2026 (the exclusion is $15,000,000 per person), yet its assets still get a step-up.

## Which assets do not get a step-up?

- **Traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, and annuities.** These are taxed as income when withdrawn. The tax is deferred, not gained, so there is no basis to adjust. The inherited IRA rules are covered in [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).
- **Lifetime gifts.** The recipient takes over the giver's basis. See [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).
- **Assets in most irrevocable trusts that were completed gifts.** If you transferred the asset to an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) and kept no control, it is not in your estate and gets no step-up. Some irrevocable trusts are designed to keep a step-up, which an attorney can explain.
- **Items that count as income in respect of a decedent,** such as unpaid wages or deferred compensation.

## How does community property change things?

In community property states, when one spouse dies the surviving spouse's half of community property can also receive a stepped-up basis, giving a "double step-up." In other states, only the deceased spouse's half (or the share included in the estate) is adjusted. This is a significant advantage for couples who own a long-held home or investments, and it is one reason families in these states hesitate to retitle assets. Our glossary has more on [community property](/glossary/community-property).

## What about a step-down?

If an asset lost value, basis resets lower. A stock bought at $100,000 and worth $60,000 at death has a basis of $60,000. The heir cannot use the original owner's $40,000 loss. In that case, it can be better for the owner to sell during life and use the loss. This is a niche point, and tax advisers can look at specifics.

## Does the step-up affect gifting decisions?

Yes. Before giving away an appreciated asset, compare:

1. **Estate tax saved.** If your estate is below the $15,000,000 exclusion (2026) and your state has no estate tax, gifting saves no estate tax at all.
2. **Capital gains tax created.** The recipient takes your low basis and may owe tax on the full gain.
3. **Loss of control.** A gift cannot be reversed.
4. **Medicaid.** Gifts may trigger a penalty in the look-back period.

For many families, keeping appreciated property until death is the more tax-efficient path. The exception is large estates facing a 40% estate tax, where lifetime gifts, even without a step-up, can still come out ahead. Whether gifting makes sense is a calculation to do with an advisor, not a rule of thumb.

If your goal is to help a child with a home, consider whether a transfer-on-death deed or a trust leaves the step-up intact; our article on the [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) explains how the owner keeps title until death. For heirs thinking about what to do with a house they've inherited, see [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house).

## What should heirs do after inheriting?

1. Get a date-of-death appraisal of any real estate and other hard-to-value assets. This protects the new basis if the IRS asks.
2. Collect date-of-death statements for stocks and funds.
3. Keep records of any improvements made after death.
4. Give your tax preparer the documents before you sell.
5. If an estate tax return was filed, the value reported there is generally what you must use for your basis, and you may receive a statement of the value from the executor.

## Can a trust or deed change the step-up?

Sometimes the way an asset is held decides the outcome. Putting a house in a revocable trust or naming a beneficiary on a transfer-on-death deed generally preserves the step-up, because you keep ownership until death. Adding a child to the deed during your life does not, since it is a gift of part of the home. Some irrevocable trusts include a provision that lets the grantor swap assets or gives a trustee power to include appreciated assets in the estate so they receive a new basis. Because these designs have trade-offs for creditor protection and tax, they should be set up by an attorney who works with your tax advisor.

Donors who hold highly appreciated assets and also want to support charity may find a [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust) or a [donor-advised fund](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds) a better route than a gift to family.
