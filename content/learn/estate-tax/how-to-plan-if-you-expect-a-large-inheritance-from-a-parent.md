Karen, 54, lives in Naperville with her husband and two teenagers. Her parents are in their eighties, own a paid-off house, a brokerage account and a farm parcel, and Karen suspects she and her brother will one day split a few million dollars. She has no idea whether she should do something about it, or whether doing anything would be presumptuous.

This is a different planning problem from the usual one. You are not the person with the large estate yet, and you may never be. But the decisions you make before the money arrives affect how much of it you keep. This article covers what to do now, what to leave alone, and the tools that matter if the inheritance shows up. For a broader view of the tax rules, read our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## Does an expected inheritance count as part of my estate?

No. An inheritance you have not received is only a hope. Your parent can sell the house, spend the account on care, remarry, change the will or leave everything to someone else, and you have no claim to stop any of that. For tax purposes and for creditors, nothing exists until the parent dies and the estate distributes assets to you.

That has a practical consequence. Do not borrow against it, do not plan your retirement around it, and do not tell your spouse it will pay for college. Plan as if it may be smaller, later or absent, and treat what arrives as a bonus.

## When does an inheritance become part of my estate?

The moment you receive it, it is yours, and it is counted in your estate when you die. That is what changes the math. Karen's own assets are about $2.4 million today. If she inherits $3 million, her estate becomes $5.4 million.

| Your estate after the inheritance | Federal estate tax in 2026 | Illinois estate tax |
|---|---|---|
| Under $4 million | None | None |
| $4 million to $15 million | None | Possible |
| Over $15 million | 40 percent on the excess | Possible |

For a married couple, the federal exclusion is $15 million each and unused exclusion can carry to the survivor through portability. Illinois does not carry over the exclusion between spouses, so couples there often use a trust. New Jersey has no estate tax, and a child inheriting from a parent is in the exempt class for New Jersey inheritance tax, though a parent living in another state may have property that is taxed there.

Karen's case: at $5.4 million, she has no federal exposure, but she does have exposure under Illinois law, because her estate would exceed $4 million. See [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) for how the tax is figured. Your numbers will differ, and the [estate tax estimator](/tools/estate-tax-estimator) can give you a quick read.

## Can I refuse the inheritance? How does a disclaimer work?

Yes. A qualified disclaimer lets you decline all or part of an inheritance so it passes to the next beneficiary, as though you had died first. Karen could disclaim part of her share so it passes to her children, using her parents' tax exclusion instead of adding to her estate.

The rules are strict:

1. It must be in writing and signed.
2. It must be delivered to the executor or trustee within nine months of the parent's death.
3. You cannot have accepted the property or any benefit from it, including income or using the house.
4. You cannot direct where the property goes. It follows the plan or the law.

A disclaimer is a tool for someone who does not need the money, and who knows who would receive it instead. If you disclaim and the next in line is your sister, she gets it. See our glossary entry on [disclaimers](/glossary/disclaimer) and [disclaiming an inheritance in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-new-jersey). If you receive Medicaid or SSI, disclaiming can be treated as a transfer, so ask an attorney first.

## Can my parent leave my share in trust instead of outright?

Yes, and it is often the most useful conversation you can have. A parent can leave your share in a lifetime trust for you, with a trustee who can distribute income and principal under terms the parent writes. Why do families do this?

- **Creditors and lawsuits.** If you are sued, a well-drafted discretionary trust is harder for a creditor to reach than cash in your account.
- **Divorce.** A trust that you do not commingle is more likely to stay separate property.
- **Estate tax.** In some designs the trust is not included in your estate even if you benefit from it, which keeps the growth out of a taxable estate. The terms matter, and a trust that gives you full control will be counted.
- **Your kids.** The trust can say where the money goes when you die, which keeps it in the bloodline.

The trade-off is control. You may not get unlimited access to the principal. Many people find that acceptable. Parents who want this need to write it in their documents, which is a conversation with their attorney, not with you. If you want to raise it, our article on [how to talk to your parents about their estate plan](/blog/how-to-talk-to-your-parents-about-their-estate-plan) offers some phrases.

## What should I not ask my parents to do?

Do not ask them to give you appreciated property now to save estate tax. Karen's parents bought the farm parcel decades ago for a small fraction of what it is worth. If they give it to her, she takes their low basis and pays capital gains on the whole increase when she sells. If she inherits it, she takes a stepped-up basis equal to its value at death, and much of that gain disappears. See [the step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) and [giving your house to your child](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs).

Since your parents are below the federal threshold unless their estate exceeds $15 million each, a lifetime gift to save federal estate tax usually costs more than it saves. If their estate is larger, that is a different conversation for them and their attorney. Read [do lifetime gifts count toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax).

## What can I do now to prepare?

You cannot control what your parents do, but you can control your own paperwork. A short list:

- **Update your will and beneficiary forms.** If you inherit $3 million and die with a will that was written for a smaller estate, your family may pay more tax or lose control. Our attorneys often add trust provisions for children at this point.
- **Check your life insurance.** If your estate grows, insurance held outside a trust is counted in it.
- **Mind the nine months.** If a disclaimer is possible, do not take, spend or use anything until you decide. Once you do accept, see [what to do with an inheritance of cash in the first year](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-with-an-inheritance-of-cash-in-the-first-year).
- **Keep it separate.** If you are in a second marriage, our guide on [keeping an inheritance separate in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/keeping-an-inheritance-separate-in-a-blended-family) covers how not to turn separate property into joint property.
- **Ask who the executor is** and where the documents are, without asking for amounts.

## What about retirement accounts and inherited IRAs?

If a parent leaves you an IRA, you will probably have to empty it within 10 years, and the withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. That means a $1 million IRA is not worth $1 million to you. Plan for the tax bill, and consider the timing of withdrawals around your own income. Our article on [the 10-year rule for inherited IRAs](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) explains how to schedule them.
