Maybe your husband died three years ago and nobody mentioned an estate tax return. Maybe you are an executor, the nine months passed while you were dealing with the house, and now a letter has arrived. The deadline for Form 706 is firm, but a missed date is not always a disaster, and there are separate answers depending on whether tax was owed.

This article is the "it's already late" companion to our main guide to [Form 706](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return), one of the topics in our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax). Start by working out which of two situations you are in.

## The short version

- If tax is owed and the return is late, file as soon as you can and pay what you can. Interest runs from the due date.
- If no tax is owed and you just want portability, you may have up to five years from the date of death.
- If the estate was required to file, the simplified portability route does not apply.
- Keep a record of why you were late. Reasonable cause matters.

## When is the Form 706 due, and how long is the extension?

The return and any tax are due nine months after the date of death. Form 4768 requests an automatic six-month extension to file, and it must be filed by the nine-month date. The tax itself is still due at nine months. Pay an estimate with the extension request and you avoid most of the cost of delay. The extension to pay is a separate request that needs a showing of reasonable cause, and the estate's circumstances, such as a closely held business, may support one. See [what happens when the estate cannot pay](/learn/estate-tax/what-if-the-estate-cannot-pay-the-estate-tax).

| Situation | Deadline | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Return required, tax owed | 9 months, extendable 6 months to file | Form 4768, pay an estimate |
| Return required, no tax owed | Same dates | File anyway, penalties are tied to tax |
| Portability only | 9 months, or up to 5 years under Rev. Proc. 2022-32 | Complete return, labeled for the revenue procedure |

## What is the penalty for filing late?

Two penalties exist. One applies to a late return and one to late payment, and both are a percentage of the tax that is unpaid, building month by month to a cap. Interest runs on top. Because both are tied to unpaid tax, an estate that owes nothing has little to be penalized on.

Reasonable cause can erase the penalty. The IRS looks at what a prudent executor would have done. An executor who was seriously ill, who relied on a professional who gave wrong advice, or who could not get a hard-to-value asset appraised in time, has a real argument. Not knowing the form existed is a weaker one. Write the reasons in a short statement attached to the return.

## How do you elect portability years after a death?

Portability is the transfer of the first spouse's unused exclusion to the survivor. Normally the election is made on a timely Form 706. Rev. Proc. 2022-32 gave a simplified path for estates that were not otherwise required to file.

1. Confirm the first spouse died within the last five years and the estate was below the filing threshold when the person died. Your own dates and totals have to be checked, because gifts count toward the threshold.
2. Confirm that no earlier Form 706 was filed for the estate.
3. Prepare a complete and properly prepared Form 706, with values for the assets, even though no tax is due. Marital deduction property can sometimes be reported with estimated values.
4. Write at the top of the form that it is filed under Rev. Proc. 2022-32 to elect portability.
5. Have the executor sign and send it, with proof of mailing.

If there is no appointed executor, the person with possession of the property may have to file. Our article on [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) explains why the unused amount is worth chasing, especially for a surviving spouse whose own assets have grown.

For example, Nadia's husband died four years ago. His estate was about $2 million, all to her, and nobody filed. Her own assets have since grown to $11 million. With a late election she can still add his unused exclusion to hers. Without it, she has $15,000,000 at 2026 levels against an estate that is rising. The cost of the filing is small compared to what it could save a decade out.

## What is Form 8971 and who gets it?

Form 8971 is a basis reporting statement. When an estate files a Form 706 because it must, the executor sends the IRS a Form 8971 and gives each beneficiary a Schedule A within 30 days after the return is filed. It lists the property that beneficiary receives and its value for estate tax purposes. The beneficiary's basis cannot generally exceed that value.

A return filed only for portability does not trigger the Form 8971. If you are a beneficiary and the estate was large enough that a 706 was required, ask for your Schedule A before you sell anything. It is the proof you will need on your own tax return, as discussed in [step-up in basis for joint property](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis-for-married-couples-and-joint-property).

## Who should prepare the return?

You can legally prepare it yourself, and some executors with a simple estate do. We would not. The return has schedules for real estate, stock, insurance, jointly owned property, deductions and elections, and a mistake can lead to a late-filed portability election being treated as invalid. Valuation also needs support. A qualified appraisal is generally needed for real estate, closely held business interests and unusual assets. See [how assets are valued](/learn/estate-tax/how-assets-are-valued-for-estate-tax).

An estate planning attorney or a CPA who handles estate returns is the usual choice. Ask what the fee covers, whether appraisals are separate, and who will respond if the IRS writes back.

## What records should the executor keep?

Appraisals, account statements at the date of death, the will or trust, proof of debts and funeral costs, correspondence with the IRS, a copy of the filed return and proof of mailing. The IRS can examine a portability return when the surviving spouse later files their own, because the unused amount is verified then. Keep copies for as long as the surviving spouse lives, and a few years beyond.

## What should an executor do this week?

Do the quick things first. They cost little and they limit the damage.

1. Find the date of death and write down the nine-month and fifteen-month dates.
2. Decide which question you are answering: tax owed, or portability only. Add up the gross estate and any lifetime taxable gifts against $15,000,000.
3. If tax may be owed and you are still inside nine months, file Form 4768 and pay an estimate.
4. If you are past the date, gather appraisals now. The slowest part of any late return is valuation, and the IRS gives more weight to a return that arrives complete.
5. Call the person who will prepare it before you call the IRS, so that any contact is coordinated.

## Does a surviving spouse lose anything by waiting?

Possibly. Portability is only useful if the survivor's estate is likely to exceed the exclusion, or the law changes. The unused amount is not adjusted for inflation, and a survivor who waits until the fifth year has less time to use it for gifts. Filing early keeps more options open. A survivor with the amount confirmed can use it for gifts right away, while a late filer spends years not knowing whether it exists.

Another point is cost. Preparing a portability return on a modest estate is a smaller job than a full taxable return, but it still requires date-of-death values. Memories fade and brokerage statements get lost, so the sooner the better.
