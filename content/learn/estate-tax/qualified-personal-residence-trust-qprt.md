For a family with a large estate, the house is often the asset most likely to grow and the one nobody wants to sell. A qualified personal residence trust, or QPRT, is built for that situation. You hand the house to your children now, at a discount for tax purposes, and keep living there.

This article explains how a QPRT works, why the survival requirement matters, what it does to the children's tax basis, and who should skip it. It sits in our broader [estate tax library](/learn/estate-tax) next to other ways to shrink a taxable estate.

## How does a QPRT work?

You create an irrevocable trust and transfer your home to it. The trust document says you may live in the house for a term, say ten or fifteen years. When the term ends, the house belongs to your children or goes into a continuing trust for them.

Giving away a house you keep using seems like it should not count as a gift, but it does, just a smaller one. The gift is not the whole home. It is the children's right to receive the home at the end of the term. That right is worth less than the house today, because they must wait for it. The gift is valued by tables that depend on the term, the home's value, and interest rates set by the IRS.

You report the gift on Form 709 and it uses part of your [lifetime exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption), $15,000,000 per person in 2026. If the house grows in value during the term, the growth happens outside your taxable estate.

### The steps

1. Choose the home and confirm it qualifies as a personal residence.
2. Pick a term. Longer terms make the taxable gift smaller but raise the chance you do not outlive them.
3. An attorney drafts the trust and a trustee is named. You can serve as trustee during the term in most cases.
4. You sign a deed moving title to the trust and record it where required.
5. An appraiser values the house, and you file Form 709 for the year of the transfer.
6. You live in the house, pay the usual expenses, and keep the paperwork tidy.
7. At the end of the term the home passes to the beneficiaries, and you either move or begin paying rent.

## What is the catch if you die early?

The whole benefit depends on surviving the term. If you die before it ends, the house is generally brought back into your gross estate, at its full date-of-death value. The family does receive the step-up in basis in that case. The QPRT then simply failed to save tax, and you paid fees for it.

That makes the choice of term a judgment call. A longer term shrinks the gift more, so a healthy 62-year-old might choose fifteen years. A 78-year-old with heart disease might pick a shorter one, accepting a larger gift to improve the odds.

### Example: Gloria and the beach house

Gloria, 66, owns a beach house worth $2.4 million that she expects to grow. She creates a QPRT with a twelve-year term for her two children. The taxable gift is a fraction of the house value. She lives there as always. She reaches the end of the term at 78, the house is now worth more, and the growth went to her children without gift or estate tax. She begins paying a market rent, which moves further cash to them. Had she died at 74, the house would have returned to her estate.

Notice that Gloria's estate may not have owed federal tax in 2026 at all. The strategy matters most for owners whose estates are near or above the exemption.

## What does a QPRT do to the step-up in basis?

It takes it away. Your children receive the home with your original basis, plus improvements, not its value on your death. If the house cost $300,000 and is worth $2 million at the end of the term, the children carry a $1.7 million built-in gain. A later sale could trigger capital gains tax well above any estate tax saved.

Our article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) shows the arithmetic. As a rule of thumb, the QPRT works only when the estate tax avoided is clearly larger than the capital gains tax created, and for federal purposes most families are well under the exemption, so the answer is often no.

## Can you stay in the house after the term?

Yes, if you pay fair market rent. The children own the house, and you are a tenant. The rent should be supported by comparable rentals and paid on a regular schedule. If you simply stay for free, the IRS may argue you retained an interest, which pulls the house back into your estate.

Some families like this arrangement, because the rent moves more money out of the estate. Others dislike the feeling of paying rent in their own home. Talk it through with the children before you sign, since they become the landlords.

You can also write the trust so that you can buy the house back at fair price, though some restrictions apply. A qualified attorney should draft that language.

## What does a QPRT cost to set up and run?

Expect legal fees for drafting, an appraisal, a deed, and a gift tax return in the year of the transfer. After that the costs are the ones you already pay as an owner. Under the trust you generally keep paying property taxes, insurance and upkeep, since you are the one living there. Those payments do not count as extra gifts. Ask your attorney whether the trust should say who pays for major repairs, because a new roof in year nine is a common point of friction.

The legal fees are modest compared with the tax at stake for the right family and a waste for the wrong one. That is one more reason to run the numbers before you sign anything.

## Who should consider a QPRT?

| Situation | QPRT likely helps? |
|---|---|
| Estate well above the federal exemption, home expected to grow | Often worth studying |
| Estate under $15 million, no state estate tax | Rarely |
| Lives in a state with a low estate tax threshold | Sometimes |
| In poor health or over 80 | Usually not |
| Wants to qualify for Medicaid | Not a Medicaid tool; ask about other options |
| Children plan to sell soon after term | Basis problem; weigh carefully |

A QPRT is one of several tools. A [grantor retained annuity trust](/learn/estate-tax/grantor-retained-annuity-trust) uses the same retained-interest idea for investments, and [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) as a family explain the general trade-offs. Families who just want the house to pass without probate often do better with a living trust or a [lady bird deed](/learn/probate/lady-bird-deed-explained), which keep the step-up.

## How state law changes this

Some states with their own estate tax have thresholds far below the federal one, which makes a QPRT worth a look for owners there. A few states treat a transfer into the trust differently for property tax reassessment, and some count gifts made shortly before death. Because rules differ so much, check your [state guide](/estate-planning/california) and ask an attorney to confirm how a transfer affects the property tax bill before you sign.
