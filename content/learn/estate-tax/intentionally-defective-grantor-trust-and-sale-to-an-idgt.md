The name is the first obstacle. Nobody wants to hear that their lawyer is recommending a "defective" trust. It is a term of art that sounds worse than it is: the trust works exactly as designed, and it is one of the more common tools for people whose estates are above the federal exclusion or growing toward it.

This article explains the double life of an intentionally defective grantor trust, why a sale to one can move so much growth out of an estate, and where the plan can go wrong. For the larger picture, our [estate tax pillar page](/learn/estate-tax) lists the other tools, and our piece on [how to reduce estate taxes](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-reduce-estate-taxes) compares them.

## What does intentionally defective mean?

The trust is defective only for income tax. The tax code has a group of rules, the grantor trust rules, that say when the person who created a trust is still treated as its owner for income tax. A drafter can trigger them on purpose, for instance by giving the grantor the right to swap assets of equal value in and out of the trust.

That produces two results at once.

- **For income tax,** the trust is ignored. Its income, gains and deductions appear on the grantor's personal return, and the grantor pays the tax.
- **For estate and gift tax,** the trust is respected. The assets belong to the trust, are not in the grantor's gross estate, and the growth is not taxed at death.

The mismatch is the whole point. Paying the trust's income tax from your own funds is not treated as another gift to the trust. It is a way of making additional transfers that are free of gift tax. The trust keeps compounding without being reduced by tax, and your estate gets smaller by the tax you pay.

## How is an IDGT different from a regular irrevocable trust?

Most [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) keep the assets out of your estate. What makes this one different is the income tax treatment and the way it is funded. The table compares four common choices.

| Trust | Outside your estate? | Who pays income tax | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revocable living trust | No | You | Probate, incapacity |
| Irrevocable life insurance trust | Yes | Varies | Life insurance proceeds |
| Grantor retained annuity trust | Yes if you outlive the term | You | Short-term growth transfer |
| IDGT | Yes | You | Gift plus sale of growing assets |

Compare it with a [grantor retained annuity trust](/learn/estate-tax/grantor-retained-annuity-trust), which also moves growth out of an estate but depends on surviving a fixed term. An IDGT has no term. It can last for decades and can be written to benefit children and grandchildren.

## How does a sale to an IDGT work?

A sale to an IDGT turns your future growth into someone else's property while you keep the value you have today. These are the typical steps.

1. **Create the trust.** An attorney drafts an irrevocable trust with an independent trustee, the grantor trust powers, and beneficiaries such as children.
2. **Seed the trust.** You make a gift to the trust, using part of your lifetime exclusion, so that it has some value of its own. Planners often seed with roughly a tenth of the sale price. That is a practice, not a legal rule.
3. **Value the asset.** A qualified appraiser values what you plan to sell, such as shares in a closely held company. Appraisals may include discounts, which the IRS can challenge.
4. **Sell for a note.** You sell the asset to the trust in return for a promissory note. The note bears at least the applicable federal rate, which the IRS publishes monthly.
5. **The trust pays you back.** Income from the asset services the note, interest-only for some years and then the principal.
6. **Growth stays in the trust.** Anything the asset earns or gains above the note's interest rate stays with the trust, outside your estate.

Because you and the trust are treated as one person for income tax, the IRS has long taken the position that you do not recognize a capital gain when you sell to it, and the note interest is not income to you. That position is the reason this technique is worth the paperwork.

Take Marcus, 66, who owns a distribution company worth $12 million that has doubled in value every eight years. He sells a minority stake to an IDGT for a note. If the company reaches $24 million, the growth on that stake belongs to his children's trust, not to his estate, and he never paid a tax on the sale itself. If the company flops, the note still needs to be paid by the trust, which is a risk for the trust and the children, not an extra tax for Marcus.

## What are the risks and trade-offs?

The technique has costs and failure modes that a good attorney will put in front of you early.

- **No step-up in basis.** Assets in an IDGT are generally not stepped up at your death, because they are not in your estate. The IRS said so in a 2023 ruling. When an asset has a very large built-in gain, the lost [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) can cost the heirs more than the estate tax saved. Some trusts include a power to swap appreciated assets back before death to capture the step-up.
- **Dying with the note outstanding.** If you die before the note is repaid, the unpaid balance is part of your estate. The plan still usually works, but the outcome is weaker.
- **The income tax bill is yours.** If the trust holds a business that throws off large profits, you owe the tax on them whether or not any cash reaches you.
- **Valuation fights.** A sale at an appraised value with discounts is the part the IRS looks hardest at. An independent, well documented appraisal matters.
- **Permanence.** Once assets are in an irrevocable trust, you cannot take them back. If you might need the money, a plan where you keep no access is the wrong fit.
- **Rules can change.** Proposals to limit grantor trusts have been discussed in Congress. None is law as of this writing, but anyone planning around one should ask what happens if it changes.

## Who is an IDGT for?

It is aimed at people with substantial wealth in assets that will grow. In 2026 the federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person and $30 million for a married couple using [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples), so for most households no sophisticated technique is needed. The people who consider an IDGT tend to fall into three groups:

- Owners of a closely held business or real estate partnership whose value will clearly keep rising.
- Families already near the exclusion who expect to cross it, covered in our article on [estate tax planning near the exemption](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-planning-when-you-are-near-the-exemption).
- Residents of a state with an estate tax that starts well below the federal level.

For smaller estates, the same trust costs real money to set up and run, files its own return each year, and may never save a dollar. Our piece on [using your exemption now or waiting](/learn/estate-tax/use-your-estate-tax-exemption-now-or-wait) is a good place to test whether a lifetime transfer makes sense at all.

## How does an IDGT compare with the other options?

A spousal lifetime access trust keeps a spouse as beneficiary, as discussed in [spousal lifetime access trusts](/learn/estate-tax/spousal-lifetime-access-trust-slat). A life insurance trust holds a policy. A QPRT moves a residence. An IDGT is the flexible container: it can hold almost any growing asset, take a gift, take a sale, and last for generations. It is also the one that most depends on careful drafting and a record that holds up if reviewed.
