Life insurance feels like it should sit outside your estate. It pays a named beneficiary, skips probate, and usually arrives tax-free for income purposes. But for federal estate tax, a policy you own is counted at its full death benefit. A $3 million policy on top of a house, retirement accounts, and a business can push an estate over a line no one expected.

An irrevocable life insurance trust, or ILIT, is the standard tool for fixing that. This article explains how it works, what it costs in control and effort, and, just as important, why most families should not bother with one.

## What is an ILIT, in plain terms?

An ILIT is an [irrevocable trust](/glossary/irrevocable-trust) whose main asset is a life insurance policy on your life. Four roles matter:

- **The grantor** creates and funds the trust. This is you, the person insured.
- **The trustee** owns the policy, pays premiums, and later collects and distributes the death benefit. The grantor should not serve, because that would defeat the purpose.
- **The beneficiaries** receive the money, usually a spouse and children, often in trust for years rather than in a lump sum.
- **The insurer** issues a policy that lists the trust as owner and beneficiary.

Because the trust, not you, owns the policy, you hold none of the "incidents of ownership" (the right to change beneficiaries, borrow against the policy, cancel it, or choose how benefits are paid). That is what keeps the death benefit out of your gross estate.

## Why can life insurance be taxed in your estate?

Under the federal estate tax rules, life insurance is included in your gross estate if the proceeds are payable to your estate, or if you held any incident of ownership at death. Naming your child as beneficiary does not change this when you still own the policy. Our article on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) covers how the forms work; the tax rule is separate.

For a married couple using the unlimited [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction), the tax is simply postponed until the second death. The problem tends to appear then, when the survivor owns everything, including policies, and no deduction is left.

## How does an ILIT work step by step?

1. **Draft and sign the trust.** An attorney writes the trust agreement. It names the trustee, the beneficiaries, and how and when they receive distributions.
2. **Get a tax ID.** The trust obtains its own employer identification number and a bank account.
3. **Buy the policy through the trust.** The trustee applies for new coverage, with you as the insured. This is the cleanest route.
4. **Fund the premiums.** You make gifts to the trust, and the trustee pays the insurer. Each gift is a transfer to a trust, so it needs to qualify for the [annual exclusion](/glossary/annual-exclusion) ($19,000 per recipient in 2026) or you report it against your lifetime exclusion.
5. **Send withdrawal notices.** Gifts to a trust are normally "future interests," which do not qualify for the annual exclusion. The fix is a Crummey withdrawal power: each beneficiary gets a limited window, often 30 days, to withdraw their share of the gift. The trustee sends a written notice every time. Beneficiaries ordinarily let the right lapse, and the money stays in the trust to pay the premium. Skipping notices is the most common ILIT failure.
6. **Collect the death benefit.** After your death the insurer pays the trustee, who follows the trust terms.

Here is an example. Daniel, 62, is widowed with a $13 million estate in a state with an estate tax that begins at $2 million. He adds a $2 million policy through an ILIT for his three children. Federally, nothing is owed on $13 million. But because the policy sits outside his estate, the state tax base is $2 million lower, and the children receive cash right when they need it to settle the estate.

## What is the three-year rule?

If you transfer an existing policy into an ILIT and die within three years, the death benefit is generally included in your estate. The rule, found in section 2035 of the Internal Revenue Code, closes a loophole that would let people move a policy to a trust on their deathbed. It does not apply when the trust buys a new policy itself, so most planners prefer that route.

Transferring an old policy can still be worthwhile if you are healthy and the policy has value, but you take on the three-year risk and the transfer may count as a gift of the policy's value. If your health is poor, buying new coverage may not be possible, which makes this a time-sensitive conversation.

## Who might benefit from an ILIT?

An ILIT is worth discussing when at least one of these is true:

- **Your estate is near or above the exclusion.** The 2026 federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, indexed for inflation after 2026. Anyone approaching that amount, particularly when the exclusion might be changed by future law, may want policy proceeds outside the estate. See [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).
- **You live in a state with a lower threshold.** Several states tax estates well below the federal level. Our article on [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) lists them.
- **Your estate is illiquid.** A family farm, a business, or real estate may leave heirs without cash to pay taxes or equalize shares among children. A policy owned by an ILIT creates liquidity. Business owners should also read about [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).
- **You want to control the payout.** A trust can pay out over time, protect a beneficiary from creditors or a divorcing spouse, and set conditions. A similar design appears in [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).
- **You are paying for a large permanent policy and have a clear sense you will keep it.**

## Who probably does not need one?

Most readers. If your estate, including insurance, is comfortably below both the federal exclusion and any state threshold, an ILIT adds cost and paperwork for little tax benefit. Term insurance that you expect to outlive, or a modest policy meant to replace income for a young family, is typically better handled by naming a beneficiary, or by naming a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) so the money is managed for minor children. Note that a revocable trust gives you management and probate benefits but no estate tax savings. See [does a trust avoid taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes).

## What are the costs and trade-offs?

- **You cannot undo it.** The policy and the trust terms are locked in. Choose the trustee and beneficiaries carefully.
- **Administration.** Someone has to keep a bank account, pay premiums on time, track gifts, and send notices. Many families hire a corporate trustee or an attorney's office to manage this.
- **Gift tax filings.** Premium gifts that rely on the annual exclusion are often reported on Form 709 to document the Crummey powers.
- **Premium commitment.** If gifts stop and the policy lapses, the trust has no purpose.
- **Law changes.** The 2025 tax law set the exclusion at $15,000,000 for 2026 with inflation indexing and no scheduled sunset. Congress can still change it, and a trust built for a lower exclusion may be left holding a policy the family no longer needs for tax reasons. It still serves control and creditor goals.

Check how the insurance is paid, too. A trust that holds a policy with cash value can run into complications if the trustee borrows against it.

## How does an ILIT compare with other options?

| Option | Estate tax exposure | Control | Complexity | Typical reader |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name a person as beneficiary | Included in your estate if you own the policy | You keep ownership | Low | Most households |
| Name a revocable trust as beneficiary | Included in your estate | You keep ownership, trust manages money | Moderate | Parents of minors |
| ILIT owns a new policy | Generally excluded | You give up ownership | High | Estates near or over the limit |
| ILIT receives an existing policy | Excluded only if you survive three years | You give up ownership | High | Healthy owners of permanent coverage |
| Adult child owns the policy | Generally excluded if you hold no incidents | Child controls it | Moderate | Families who trust the child |

A spousal lifetime access trust is another alternative that can hold policies and give a spouse indirect access to the funds, with its own risks if the couple divorces.

## What about married couples and second-to-die policies?

Couples sometimes buy a joint "second-to-die" policy that pays only after both have died. Because the marital deduction defers tax until the second death, that is when the cash is needed. An ILIT usually owns such a policy. The estate tax exposure is still tested at the second death, and portability may reduce it, so review [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) first.
