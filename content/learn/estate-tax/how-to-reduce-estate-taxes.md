Most advice about reducing estate taxes assumes you owe them. Before spending money on trusts, it helps to know if you do. In 2026 the federal tax starts only above $15,000,000 per person, and the top rate on the excess is 40%. For many readers of this page the honest answer is "you probably will not owe federal estate tax, but check your state."

If your numbers are closer to the line, or you live in a state that taxes smaller estates, this guide walks through what lawfully lowers the bill and what each approach costs you. It sits alongside our [guide to estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## Do you actually need to reduce estate taxes?

Start by adding up what counts. The taxable estate includes your home, bank and investment accounts, retirement accounts, business interests, life insurance you own, and assets in a revocable trust. Debts and funeral and administration costs are subtracted.

Then compare the total with the right yardstick.

| Your situation | Yardstick | Typical next step |
|---|---|---|
| Single, estate under $15 million, no state tax | Federal exclusion | Usually no tax planning needed |
| Married, combined under $30 million | Two exclusions with portability | Elect portability when the first spouse dies |
| Any estate in a state with an estate tax | A lower state threshold | Check your state with our state death tax checker or an attorney |
| Estate above the exclusion | Federal exclusion plus 40% rate | Lifetime gifts, trusts, charitable planning |

Try the estate tax estimator below before you decide anything, or read how the tax is figured in [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

## Which deductions lower the taxable estate?

The law lets you subtract two big items from the taxable estate.

**The marital deduction.** Anything you leave to a spouse who is a U.S. citizen is deductible without limit. That is why a married couple rarely owes tax at the first death. The deduction defers the tax, because the survivor's estate will later include what was inherited. It works best paired with portability or a credit shelter trust. If your spouse is not a citizen, a special trust may be required; see [QDOTs](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot).

**The charitable deduction.** Gifts to qualified charities are deductible with no cap. A reader who plans to leave money to a church, school or foundation anyway can structure it so the estate tax falls on the family's share less. A [donor-advised fund](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds) or a [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust) can add income or income tax benefits for you in life.

## Can lifetime gifts reduce the estate?

Yes, and they are the most widely used tool. Each gift removes an asset, and all future growth on it, from your estate.

There are three layers:

1. **Annual exclusion gifts.** You can give $19,000 per recipient in 2026 with no return and no use of your lifetime exclusion. Married couples can give $38,000 together.
2. **Direct payments of tuition and medical bills.** These are not gifts for tax purposes if paid straight to the school or provider.
3. **Larger gifts using the lifetime exclusion.** A gift above the annual amount is reported on Form 709 and reduces your $15,000,000 exclusion. No gift tax is due until it is used up.

The catch is basis. A gift carries your original basis, while property inherited at death generally gets a step-up to its value at death. Giving a $50,000 stock holding now worth $400,000 to a child can create a large capital gains bill when the child sells. For families under the exclusion, that tax can easily exceed the estate tax saved, which is none. Read [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) and [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies) before giving appreciated assets. Gifts can also create problems with Medicaid's look-back, which is 60 months in most states.

## Do trusts reduce estate taxes?

Some do, and some do not. This is where the most confusion lies.

A **revocable living trust** is a tool to avoid probate and plan for incapacity. You keep control, so the assets are still yours for tax. It does not lower the tax. Our article on [whether a trust avoids taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes) explains that in detail.

Certain **irrevocable trusts** can take assets out of the estate because you no longer own them. The common ones:

- An [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) owns a policy so that the death benefit is not in your estate.
- A grantor retained annuity trust passes growth to heirs while you receive payments; see [how a GRAT works](/learn/estate-tax/grantor-retained-annuity-trust).
- A credit shelter trust uses the first spouse's exclusion and keeps growth out of the survivor's estate; see [bypass trust vs portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability).
- A charitable remainder trust gives income to you and the remainder to charity.

The price is control. Once you move an asset into an irrevocable trust, you generally cannot take it back or change the terms. Our overview of [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) covers what you give up.

## Does life insurance count?

If you own the policy when you die, the death benefit is part of your taxable estate. That surprises people who think of insurance as tax-free. It is free of income tax to the beneficiary, but not automatically free of estate tax. Moving ownership to a trust, or to an adult child, can fix that, though a transfer made within three years of death is pulled back in. Work through this with the policy's owner and the insurer.

## What about the states?

A reader whose federal estate is safe may still owe a state estate tax or inheritance tax. As of 2026, about a dozen states and D.C. tax estates, and five states tax inheritances. Some thresholds are far below $15,000,000. Strategies like using both spouses' state exemptions or gifting during life work differently by state, and some states add back gifts made shortly before death. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes), and if you live in Illinois, [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).

## An example of putting it together

Gerald, 68, and his wife Lorraine, 66, live in a state with an estate tax that starts at $2 million. They own a $1.6 million house, $2.4 million in investments and $600,000 in retirement accounts, and Gerald's life insurance is $500,000. The total is $5.1 million, well below the federal level, but well above their state's.

Their attorney suggests three things: wills with credit shelter trusts so each spouse's state exemption is used; moving the life insurance into a trust; and annual gifts to their three children. Together these can reduce or remove the state tax, at a cost of some legal work and a bit of control. A plan that worked for the $15 million federal question would not have touched their real exposure.

## What are the mistakes to avoid?

- Giving away the family home to cut tax, then losing the step-up and the homestead protections.
- Paying for complex trusts when the estate is well under any threshold.
- Naming the estate as the beneficiary of a retirement account, which hurts income tax planning.
- Forgetting that the exclusion is indexed after 2026 and may change.
- Not updating the plan after a move, sale, inheritance or change in law.
