Elena and Paul own a house in the Chicago suburbs, a small manufacturing company and investments, and a financial adviser told them last month they "should think about estate tax." The adviser did not say what that would cost or whether it would save them anything. They have a more useful question than the adviser's: what will I pay, and what do I get for it?

This article gives you a way to price estate tax planning without quoting fees that vary by firm and county. It covers what drives the fee, what the work includes, what it costs to keep a plan running, and a simple test for deciding whether the tax at stake justifies the spending. Start with our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) if you are not sure whether the tax applies to you at all.

## What does estate tax planning cost?

It costs more than a will and less than a lawsuit. The fee depends on how many documents you need and how complicated your assets are. Law firms quote it one of two ways, and the way matters as much as the number.

| Pricing method | How it works | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat fee for a defined package | One price for named documents (will, trusts, powers of attorney) | A clear scope and no surprises | Anything outside the package billed separately |
| Hourly | You pay for the time spent | Open-ended or uncertain work, such as business valuations | A bill that grows if you change your mind |
| Hybrid | Flat fee for drafting, hourly for later reviews or tax filings | Plans with a long tail | Ask what triggers hourly billing |
| Ongoing retainer | A yearly fee for review, filings and updates | Families with trusts that need yearly work | Make sure you know what the retainer covers |

We do not print a price range here because it would be wrong for someone. County, firm and the work involved shift it too much. What you can do is get a written quote for a defined scope from more than one firm and compare scope first, price second.

## What drives the price up or down?

Five things move the fee more than anything else:

1. **Which tools you need.** A will and a portability filing cost less than an irrevocable life insurance trust, a [grantor retained annuity trust](/learn/estate-tax/grantor-retained-annuity-trust) or a [spousal lifetime access trust](/learn/estate-tax/spousal-lifetime-access-trust-slat). Each trust requires drafting, funding and sometimes a gift tax return.
2. **Your assets.** Brokerage accounts are easy to value. A family business, farm, rental portfolio or art collection is not.
3. **Valuations.** Appraisals for real estate, business interests and discounts add cost. Our article on [how assets are valued for estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/how-assets-are-valued-for-estate-tax) explains which assets need them.
4. **Number of heirs and states.** Property in two states, a second marriage or a trust for a child with a disability each adds drafting.
5. **Tax filings.** A [gift tax return, Form 709](/learn/estate-tax/form-709-gift-tax-return-gift-splitting-and-net-gifts), a trust's annual income tax return and eventually a [Form 706](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) each carry preparation fees.

## What costs show up after the documents are signed?

Most of the long-run cost is not drafting. A trust that holds assets, or a life insurance policy that pays a premium every year, has recurring costs.

- **Trust income tax returns.** Each irrevocable trust with income typically files its own return and pays tax at compressed rates, so many trustees hire a CPA.
- **Trustee fees.** A family trustee often serves for free. A bank or trust company charges a percentage of assets or an hourly rate.
- **ILIT upkeep.** An [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) needs the grantor to gift the premium to the trust each year and the trustee to send notice letters to beneficiaries. Skipping the notices can undo the gift tax treatment.
- **Appraisals at death.** The executor may need fresh valuations, which also reset the basis for income tax.
- **Reviews.** A plan written before the 2025 tax law may be built around an exemption that no longer applies.

When you compare quotes, add these up for ten years. A cheap setup with an expensive upkeep can cost more than a pricier setup that is simple to run.

## How do you decide if it is worth it?

Compare the tax at stake with the total cost of the plan, then ask whether there are cheaper ways to capture most of the savings. Federal estate tax is 40 percent of the amount above the exclusion, which is $15 million per person in 2026, or $30 million for a married couple using portability.

Here is an invented example. Dana, a widow in Illinois, has an estate of $17 million and no unused exclusion from her late husband. The federal exclusion is $15 million, so the exposed amount is $2 million, and the tax at 40 percent is $800,000 before any planning. Illinois adds its own estate tax, since its exclusion is $4 million. If planning cost $25,000 (a made-up figure) and reduced the exposed amount by half, the saving would dwarf the fee. If Dana's estate were $6 million, federal planning would save nothing, because no federal tax is due, and the question would be Illinois, where the answer turns on how much a trust and a marital deduction strategy can shelter.

The mirror case is the family at $2 million that is sold a trust for tax reasons. Federal tax does not apply, Illinois does not either, and in New Jersey the estate tax was repealed in 2018, though an inheritance tax can apply to some heirs. These families can have good reasons to use a trust, but "estate tax" is not one of them. Our article on [planning near the exemption](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-planning-when-you-are-near-the-exemption) helps you judge where you fall, and the [estate tax estimator](/tools/estate-tax-estimator) shows rough exposure in a couple of minutes.

## What is the cost of not planning?

For a family whose estate is above the exclusion, the cost of not planning is a bill, due nine months after death, that the executor may have to pay by selling assets. That is hardest on a family with a business, a farm or illiquid real estate. See [what happens if the estate cannot pay the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-if-the-estate-cannot-pay-the-estate-tax).

For a family below every threshold, the cost of not planning is different: a missed portability election, a stale beneficiary form or a trust no one funded. These are cheaper to fix and usually fall into a review, not a tax strategy.

## Which pieces are cheap, and which are optional?

Some estate tax steps cost little and capture most of the benefit. Others are worth it only when the numbers are large.

- **Usually inexpensive:** annual exclusion gifts of $19,000 per recipient in 2026, a portability election on the first spouse's death, and updating beneficiary forms.
- **Moderate:** a revocable trust with tax-sensitive language, or a charitable gift through a [donor-advised fund](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds).
- **Larger commitments:** lifetime gifts using the exemption, a SLAT, a GRAT, an installment sale to a defective grantor trust, and family partnerships. These involve valuation work and tax returns every year.

## What should you ask before you hire anyone?

Bring these to the first meeting. The answers tell you more than the price.

1. Is the fee flat or hourly, and what exactly does it include?
2. Does the quote include funding the trusts, such as retitling accounts?
3. Who prepares gift tax returns and trust tax returns, and what do they cost?
4. What does it cost each year to keep the plan running?
5. Is a review included if the law changes?
6. Can I do part of this myself, such as gathering account statements, to lower the cost?

If you are weighing a do-it-yourself filing for the estate tax return, read [can you file Form 706 yourself](/learn/estate-tax/can-i-file-form-706-myself-or-do-i-need-an-attorney) first.
