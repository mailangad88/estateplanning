Most married couples never think about citizenship when they plan their estates. But if one spouse is a U.S. citizen and the other is a green card holder or visa holder, a quiet rule in the tax code changes the plan. The usual promise that everything can pass to a spouse tax-free at death does not automatically extend to a spouse who is not a citizen.

This article explains the rule, what a qualified domestic trust (QDOT) does about it, and how gifts and retirement accounts are affected. It is general education about U.S. estate tax, not immigration advice, and an immigration attorney should handle any question about status or citizenship.

## Why does citizenship change estate planning?

For married couples where both are citizens, property left to the surviving spouse qualifies for the unlimited [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction). No federal estate tax is due at the first death, and the tax is postponed until the second. That postponement works because the property is expected to be taxed in the survivor's estate.

Congress worried that a non-citizen survivor could take the property abroad and leave the U.S. before death, so the property would never be taxed. To close that gap, the marital deduction is generally denied for property passing to a non-citizen spouse, unless it goes into a QDOT, which keeps the property tied to the U.S. tax system.

This matters only when the estate is large enough for tax to be a question. The federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026 (see [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption)). A couple with $6 million has no federal issue, but a state estate tax, discussed below, can still create one. Because a QDOT interacts with the unused exclusion, it also helps to understand [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) before choosing a plan.

## What is a QDOT and what must it contain?

A QDOT is a trust that meets the requirements of the Internal Revenue Code for a qualified domestic trust. Its main features:

- **A U.S. trustee.** At least one trustee must be an individual U.S. citizen or a domestic corporation, such as a U.S. bank.
- **Right to withhold tax.** No principal distribution can be made unless the U.S. trustee has the right to withhold the estate tax due on it.
- **Security requirements.** Depending on the size of the trust, Treasury rules may require that a U.S. bank serve as trustee or that the trustee post a bond or letter of credit. Larger trusts face stricter rules, and an attorney needs to check the current thresholds.
- **A proper election.** The executor must elect QDOT treatment on the federal estate tax return (Form 706). A late return can cause the election to be lost if it is more than a year past the due date, so do not treat the filing as optional.
- **U.S. governing law and administration,** with the trust set up under the law of a state or the District of Columbia.

A QDOT can be created in the will or a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust). It can also be created after death, if the document did not include one, under a statutory rule allowing the surviving spouse to transfer property to a QDOT before the return is filed. Many couples include a standby QDOT clause in their plan, so it is ready if needed.

## When is the tax due on a QDOT?

The estate tax is deferred, not forgiven. Tax is generally due when:

- **Principal is distributed to the spouse,** except for certain hardship distributions. The trustee withholds tax from the distribution.
- **The surviving spouse dies,** and the balance is taxed as if it were part of the first spouse's estate, using the first spouse's rates and exclusion.
- **Other triggering events occur,** such as the trust ceasing to qualify.

Income the trust pays to the spouse is generally not subject to this tax, although it is ordinary taxable income. Hardship distributions, for an immediate and substantial financial need, are available without tax in limited cases, and the trustee must make the call.

Here is an example. Carlos, a citizen, dies with a $20 million estate, leaving it in a QDOT for his wife Ana, a green card holder. His $15 million exclusion offsets part of the estate, and the marital deduction through the QDOT would cover the rest. The QDOT pays Ana income each year. If the trustee distributes $500,000 of principal for her to buy a vacation home, the trustee withholds estate tax on that distribution. At Ana's death, the balance is taxed in Carlos's estate.

## What is the citizenship exception?

A QDOT is not needed if the surviving spouse becomes a U.S. citizen before the estate tax return is filed, has been a U.S. resident at all times after the decedent's death and before becoming a citizen, and receives the property outright or in a qualifying marital trust. For couples already in the naturalization process, this can save the cost of a QDOT. Timing questions, including the executor's filing date and extension, belong with the estate attorney and an immigration attorney together.

## How do lifetime gifts to a non-citizen spouse work?

Gifts between citizen spouses are unlimited. For a non-citizen spouse, the unlimited deduction is replaced by a larger-than-usual annual exclusion. For 2026 the first $194,000 in gifts to a non-citizen spouse is not treated as a taxable gift. The amount is indexed for inflation each year, and you can confirm the current figure in the IRS's annual inflation notice.

Gifts above that amount are reported on Form 709 and reduce the giver's lifetime exclusion, so no tax is due until the exclusion is used up. This is separate from the ordinary $19,000 annual exclusion that applies to everyone else. Our [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies) article covers how the pieces fit, and the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) article explains Form 709.

| Spouse status | Unlimited marital deduction at death | Annual gift limit to spouse (2026) | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| U.S. citizen | Yes | Unlimited | None needed |
| Non-citizen | No | $194,000 | QDOT, or lifetime gifts |
| Non-citizen who becomes a citizen before the return is filed | Yes, if conditions are met | Unlimited after citizenship | Confirm timing |
| Non-citizen with a QDOT | Yes, deferred | $194,000 for lifetime gifts | Use the QDOT at death |

## What about retirement accounts?

A 401(k) or IRA left to a non-citizen spouse can raise the same issue. The marital deduction is denied unless the account is made payable to a QDOT, or the spouse becomes a citizen in time. The plan's rules, the trust language, and income tax deferral all interact, so naming a trust as beneficiary requires careful coordination. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) and [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries). Because inheriting spouses usually have valuable rollover options, how to preserve them is a reason to see an attorney.

## What happens to jointly owned property?

For citizen spouses, half of jointly owned property is included in the estate of the first to die, no matter who paid. That rule does not apply the same way to a non-citizen spouse. Generally, the portion included depends on who contributed to the purchase, and a surviving non-citizen spouse may not benefit from the usual marital deduction for the transfer. A house titled in joint names, which seems simple, can complicate matters. Review [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) and the glossary entry on [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy).

## What about state estate and inheritance taxes?

A state with its own estate tax may require a state-level QDOT-type election or may follow the federal one. Several states tax estates at far lower thresholds than $15,000,000, and the state rules for a non-citizen spouse are not uniform. If you live in or own property in such a state, read [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and check your [state guide](/estate-planning/new-york).

## What about property and accounts in other countries?

U.S. citizens are taxed on worldwide estates. A non-citizen who lives abroad may be taxed only on U.S. assets and has a much smaller exclusion. A couple with homes or accounts in two countries needs both U.S. and foreign legal advice, since a foreign will or inheritance rule may conflict with a U.S. plan. We handle U.S. estate plans, and foreign counsel should address foreign assets.

## A planning conversation checklist

1. Confirm each spouse's citizenship and residency status.
2. Total your assets, including retirement accounts, life insurance, and property abroad.
3. Check your state's estate tax threshold.
4. Decide whether a standby QDOT clause belongs in each spouse's will or trust.
5. Choose a U.S. citizen or institution as trustee, and a backup.
6. Review beneficiary forms on retirement accounts and insurance.
7. Decide whether to make annual gifts up to $194,000 to equalize ownership.
8. Revisit your plan if citizenship status changes.
