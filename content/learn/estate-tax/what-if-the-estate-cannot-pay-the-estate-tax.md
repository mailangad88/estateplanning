Most families who hear the words "estate tax" never face it. A few do, and the ones who do often find the real problem is not the size of the bill but where the money will come from. Picture a farm worth many millions with a modest checking account. The tax is due in cash, and the cash is not there.

This article explains what the executor can do when an estate owes federal estate tax and has little cash on hand. It also covers the deadline, the extensions people confuse, and the personal risk executors carry. For the bigger picture on who owes the tax at all, start with our [guide to estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## The short version

- The tax is due nine months after the date of death, the same day the return is due unless an extension to file is requested.
- Only estates above the exemption owe anything. In 2026 that is $15,000,000 per person, and the top rate on the excess is 40%.
- An estate with no cash can sell assets, borrow, or ask for more time. Each has a price.
- Executors should not distribute the whole estate until the tax question is settled.

## When does the tax have to be paid?

The federal estate tax is due nine months after death. The executor files [Form 706](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) and pays the tax by then. If the executor needs more time to assemble valuations, Form 4768 gives an automatic six-month extension to file.

That extension to file is where many executors go wrong. It does not move the payment date. Tax still unpaid after the nine-month mark generally picks up interest from that day. The sensible practice is to estimate the bill and send a payment with the extension request, even if the final number will change later.

A separate request, made for reasonable cause, can extend the time to pay. That is a different application, with a different standard, and the IRS expects the executor to explain why paying on time would cause undue hardship or why the estate cannot pay yet. Having no cash, with the main asset not sellable in a reasonable time, is the type of facts that supports it.

## How do estates raise the cash?

Here are the common routes, roughly in the order executors try them.

1. **Use the liquid assets.** Bank accounts, brokerage accounts, and Treasury bills in the decedent's name are the simplest source. Retirement accounts can also be tapped by the beneficiary, but withdrawals carry income tax, so they are rarely the first choice.
2. **Collect life insurance.** Proceeds paid to the estate can be used for the tax. Proceeds paid to a named beneficiary or an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) belong to those people, not the estate, although the trustee can sometimes lend to the estate or buy assets from it.
3. **Sell something.** Real estate, a stock position, or a business interest. Because inherited assets get a new basis at death under the [step-up rule](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), a prompt sale often produces little capital gain.
4. **Borrow.** A bank loan secured by the real estate or the business is common. So is a loan from a beneficiary who has cash and will take a larger share later.
5. **Ask the IRS for time.** Reasonable-cause extension, or installment payment where the rules allow.

### Example: Walt and the orchard

Walt, 81, died owning an orchard and packing shed valued at several million dollars above the exemption, plus about $200,000 in cash. His daughter Denise, the executor, learned that the tax would be well beyond the cash. She asked an attorney early. The estate sold a parcel of land the family had long considered surplus, took a loan against part of the orchard, and applied for installment treatment on the part of the tax tied to the business. The orchard stayed in the family. It worked because Denise started in month two, not month eight.

## What is installment payment?

When a closely held business or farm is a large share of the estate, federal law lets the executor elect to pay the part of the tax attributable to that business over a period of years instead of all at once. Interest is charged, usually at a favorable rate on a portion of the tax and at a market rate on the rest, and payments may start with interest only.

There are conditions. The business must be an active one, not just a pile of investments. The size threshold is set by statute, and the election must be made on a timely filed return. If the family sells most of the business during the payment period, the remaining installments can come due early. Our article on [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) explains why the valuation fight comes first, and [farm succession planning](/learn/business-owners/farm-succession-planning) covers the family side.

## Can the executor be personally liable?

Yes, and this is the part that surprises people. Federal law gives the government a claim against the executor personally when the executor pays other debts first or distributes property to heirs while the estate tax is unpaid. The exposure is generally limited to what was paid out improperly, but that can still be a lot.

The practical habits that follow:

- Do not make final distributions until the estate tax picture is clear.
- Keep a reserve for the tax and for the cost of an IRS review.
- Document appraisals and the way each value was reached.
- If heirs press for money, explain the personal-liability rule. It usually ends the argument.

Our overview of [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) lists the other tasks that sit alongside this one.

## What if the estate really cannot pay?

It happens, though rarely, since estates large enough to owe tax usually hold something that can be sold or borrowed against. When it does, the order of events usually looks like this:

1. The estate files on time and pays what it can.
2. The executor applies for an extension of time to pay, or elects installments if eligible.
3. The IRS may put a lien on estate property to secure the unpaid balance.
4. If the debt is still unpaid after years, the IRS can pursue the property, and beneficiaries who received assets may have to return what they received, up to its value.

Heirs sometimes ask whether they can simply refuse the inheritance to avoid trouble. A formal [disclaimer](/glossary/disclaimer) is possible but has strict timing rules and does not erase the tax, which is owed by the estate regardless of who ends up with the assets.

## How do you plan so the problem never arises?

Families with a big illiquid asset can do a handful of things while they are alive.

| Strategy | What it does | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Life insurance in an irrevocable trust | Creates cash outside the taxable estate | Premiums must be paid for years; the policy must be set up correctly |
| Buy-sell agreement funded by insurance | Gives a business partner or family member cash to buy the interest | Needs a clear price formula |
| Lifetime gifts of business interests | Moves growth out of the estate | Loses the step-up on gifted shares |
| Keeping a liquid reserve | Lets the executor pay without a fire sale | Cash earns less than the business |

Which of these fits depends on the estate. See [how to reduce estate taxes](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-reduce-estate-taxes) for the wider list.

## How state law changes this

A few states levy their own estate or inheritance tax, often with a much lower threshold than the federal one and its own due date. The state bill can arrive when the federal one does not. Check our guide to [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) to see whether yours is one. Each state also sets its own rules for how estate assets may be sold during administration.
