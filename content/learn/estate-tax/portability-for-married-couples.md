When a married person dies, nearly everything usually passes to the surviving spouse, and no estate tax is due because of the unlimited marital deduction. The catch is that the first spouse's own $15,000,000 exclusion is not used at all. Portability is the rule that stops that exclusion from going to waste.

This article explains how portability works, how to elect it, where it falls short, and when other tools make sense. For more on the tax itself, see our guide to [estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax) and the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

## What is portability?

Every person has a federal estate and gift tax basic exclusion, which is $15,000,000 in 2026. When the first spouse dies and leaves everything to the survivor, the marital deduction means no tax is paid and none of the first spouse's exclusion is used. Without portability, that $15,000,000 would disappear.

Portability, enacted in 2010 and made permanent in 2012, allows the survivor to claim the unused amount, called the deceased spousal unused exclusion (DSUE). It is added to the survivor's own exclusion, so a couple can shelter up to $30 million. See our glossary entry on [portability](/glossary/portability).

### Example

Dana and Paul have a combined estate of $12 million. Paul dies in 2026 and leaves everything to Dana. No tax is due. Paul used none of his $15,000,000 exclusion, so with portability Dana can add $15,000,000 to her own, for $30 million. If Dana's estate grows to $20 million by the time she dies, it is still below the combined exclusion and owes no federal estate tax. Without the election, her exclusion would be $15,000,000 and about $5 million could be taxed at up to 40%.

## How do I elect portability?

Portability is not automatic. It must be elected, and that takes a federal estate tax return.

1. **The executor gathers information.** Values are needed for every asset the first spouse owned or shared, as of the date of death.
2. **The executor files Form 706** for the first spouse. Filing is required even if no tax is due. For estates below the filing threshold, some valuation details can be reported more simply.
3. **The return is filed on time.** The general deadline is nine months after death. A six-month extension is available by filing Form 4768.
4. **Portability is elected on the return.** The election is made by filing a timely, complete return, unless the executor opts out.
5. **The IRS may later examine the return.** The IRS can review the DSUE calculation when the survivor's estate is audited, even if the first spouse's return is old, so keep the records.

If the nine-month deadline has passed and the estate was not otherwise required to file, an IRS revenue procedure (Rev. Proc. 2022-32) gives a simplified path to elect portability up to five years after death. There are conditions, so seek advice quickly.

The person filing is the executor, or if none was appointed, another person in charge of the property. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) for the other returns that arise after a death.

## What are the limits of portability?

### It does not cover the GST tax

The DSUE amount applies to estate and gift tax only. The generation-skipping transfer tax exemption is not portable, so a first spouse's GST exemption is lost if it is not used. That matters if you plan to leave money to grandchildren. See [generation-skipping transfer tax](/glossary/generation-skipping-transfer-tax).

### It is not indexed

The DSUE is a fixed amount, set by the exclusion in effect when the first spouse dies. The survivor's own exclusion is indexed for inflation after 2026, but the DSUE does not grow. Assets placed in a credit shelter trust, by contrast, can grow outside the survivor's taxable estate.

### Remarriage can change the amount

A surviving spouse can use only the DSUE from the "last deceased spouse." If they remarry and the new spouse dies, the new spouse's DSUE replaces the earlier one. If the survivor made gifts using the first DSUE before the second death, that amount is used first.

### Most states do not allow it

Some states levy their own estate tax at much lower thresholds, and most of them do not allow the unused exemption to pass to the surviving spouse. A couple in such a state may lose the first spouse's state exemption unless the plan uses a trust. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

### Growth after the first death

Because the DSUE is frozen, large appreciation after the first death can push a survivor's estate above the combined exclusion even though the starting numbers looked safe.

## Portability or credit shelter trust?

Another way to use the first spouse's exclusion is a credit shelter trust (also called a bypass or family trust). At the first death, assets up to the exclusion go into a trust for the survivor and others rather than outright to the survivor.

| | Portability | Credit shelter trust |
|---|---|---|
| Needs a trust to be drafted | No | Yes |
| Federal estate tax exclusion preserved | Yes, via election | Yes, by using it at first death |
| Growth shielded from survivor's estate | No | Yes |
| GST exemption preserved | No | Yes, if allocated |
| State exemption preserved | Usually not | Yes |
| Basis step-up at second death | Yes, on all assets | Not on trust assets |
| Complexity | Low | Higher |
| Control over who inherits after survivor | Survivor decides | First spouse decides |

For many couples under $15 million with no state estate tax, portability is simple and enough. A credit shelter trust is useful for couples in states with an estate tax, for those with a blended family who want to protect the first spouse's children, and for those with very large estates. See our article on [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) for a related tool in second marriages.

Some plans combine the two: a disclaimer trust that the survivor can choose to fund within nine months, plus a portability election as a safety net.

## What should a surviving spouse do?

1. Confirm that an executor will be named and appointed and tell them about portability early.
2. Collect statements and appraisals for date-of-death values.
3. Ask an attorney or CPA whether to file Form 706, even if you think no tax is due.
4. Mark the nine-month deadline, and the extension date if needed.
5. Keep a copy of the filed return permanently, and give it to the executor of your own estate.
6. Read our guide on [estate planning after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies) for other updates to make.

## Can a surviving spouse use the DSUE before dying?

Yes. The survivor can use the DSUE amount to make lifetime gifts, and the IRS treats gifts as using the DSUE first, before the survivor's own exclusion. That can be a smart move when assets are likely to grow, since a gift moves future growth out of the estate. Claire, widowed at 70, has a $4 million DSUE on top of her own exclusion and gives her children a $2 million lake house. The gift is reported on Form 709 and applied to the DSUE first. If she remarries and her new spouse dies, the earlier DSUE could be lost if she has not used it, so the order matters. These strategies are worth discussing with a tax professional before acting.
