The federal estate tax only affects estates above $15 million per person in 2026, so many families assume that taxes on death are not their problem. A few states see it differently. Some tax estates starting at $1 million, and some tax the people who inherit, even when the amounts are small.

This article explains how state taxes work, which states have them as of 2026, and what you can do about them. For the federal rules, see the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption), and for the broader topic start with our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## What is the difference between an estate tax and an inheritance tax?

An estate tax is a tax on the right to transfer property at death. The executor calculates it on the total value of the estate and pays it from estate funds before heirs receive their shares. An [inheritance tax](/glossary/inheritance-tax) is a tax on the right to receive property. Each heir owes it on their own share, and the rate normally depends on the relationship between the heir and the person who died.

| | Estate tax | Inheritance tax |
|---|---|---|
| Who pays | The estate (executor files and pays) | Each heir who receives assets |
| Based on | Total value of the estate | Amount each heir receives |
| Rate depends on relationship | No | Usually yes |
| Spouse | Typically deductible | Typically exempt |
| Where it applies | Where the person lived, plus real property located in the state | Where the person lived, plus often real property and tangible property in the state |

The federal government has no inheritance tax. Our article on [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) covers what a beneficiary actually has to do.

## Which states have an estate tax?

As of 2026, these states and the District of Columbia levy an estate tax:

- Connecticut
- Hawaii
- Illinois
- Maine
- Maryland
- Massachusetts
- Minnesota
- New York
- Oregon
- Rhode Island
- Vermont
- Washington
- District of Columbia

Exemption amounts differ widely, and they change as legislatures act and as inflation adjusts them. A few reference points as of 2026 for planning: Oregon's exemption is about $1 million, among the lowest in the country. Illinois, Minnesota, and Vermont, and Maryland sit in a range of roughly $3 million to $5 million. Several other states, including New York, Maine, Hawaii, and Washington, have higher exemptions that are adjusted from time to time. Connecticut has tied its tax to the federal exemption amount. For every state, confirm the current number with the state revenue department or an attorney before you rely on it.

Top rates generally run from about 10% to 20%, and several states apply graduated rates.

### The "cliff" in some states

A few states, notably New York, have a "cliff": if the estate exceeds the exemption by more than a small margin, the exemption can be lost and the tax is calculated on the full estate rather than only the excess. For an estate just above the threshold, the effective tax rate on the last dollars can be very high. Estates near the line are often worth planning around, for example with lifetime gifts or charitable giving, though gifting rules differ by state.

## Which states have an inheritance tax?

As of 2026, five states levy an inheritance tax:

- Kentucky
- Maryland
- Nebraska
- New Jersey
- Pennsylvania

Iowa phased out its inheritance tax, and it no longer applies to deaths in 2025 and later. Maryland is the only state that has both an estate tax and an inheritance tax.

Inheritance tax rules usually sort heirs into classes:

- **Spouses:** exempt in all five states.
- **Children, grandchildren, and other direct descendants:** exempt in some states and taxed at low rates in others (Pennsylvania, for instance, taxes them at a low rate).
- **Siblings, nieces, nephews:** often taxed at moderate rates, sometimes with small exemptions.
- **Other relatives, friends, and unrelated heirs:** generally taxed at the highest rates.
- **Charities:** usually exempt.

Rates and exemptions are set state by state. Kentucky, Nebraska, and others have changed their rules over the years, so check the state revenue department for current figures.

## Can a state tax me if I do not live there?

Yes, for real estate and tangible property located in the state. If you live in Florida (no state estate tax) and own a cabin in a state with an estate tax, that state may tax the cabin's value, usually in proportion to its share of your total estate. The same applies to inheritance taxes: some states tax heirs who receive property located there, even when the person who died lived elsewhere.

Out-of-state real estate can also require a separate court process, called [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate). Putting the property into a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) typically avoids that court step but not the tax. Our guide to [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) goes into more detail.

## How does portability work at the state level?

Federal [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) lets a surviving spouse use the unused exclusion of the first spouse to die. Most states with an estate tax do not offer portability, so the first spouse's state exemption can be lost if everything passes to the survivor. A couple in a state with a $3 million exemption could lose $3 million of shelter at the first death. Some families use a credit shelter trust, or a "disclaimer trust," to use the first spouse's state exemption. A few states offer state-level portability, so check how yours works.

## Example: Tom and Gwen

Tom and Gwen, both 72, live in a state with a $4 million estate tax threshold. They own a $1.5 million house, $1 million in brokerage accounts, and $2.5 million in retirement accounts, for $5 million combined. Federally, they owe nothing. If Tom dies first and leaves everything to Gwen, no tax is due at his death because of the marital deduction. But Gwen's estate will be $5 million, over the state threshold, and the state tax could apply when she dies. Their attorney suggests using a trust at Tom's death to use his $4 million state exemption. That changes the state tax picture without affecting what Gwen can use during her life.

## What can I do about state estate and inheritance tax?

1. **Find out if your state, or the state where you own property, has a tax.** Ask your attorney or look at the state revenue department site.
2. **Estimate your estate.** Include life insurance you own, retirement accounts, real estate, and business interests.
3. **Check the spouse and heir rules.** Learn which heirs are exempt and which are taxed.
4. **Consider planning tools.** Credit shelter trusts, lifetime gifts (where allowed), charitable gifts, and changing how assets are titled can reduce state tax. Some states claw back gifts made shortly before death.
5. **Coordinate with your beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts and life insurance count toward the estate. See [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).
6. **Review when you move.** A move between states can change both the tax and the rules for your will and trust. Our [state guides](/estate-planning/new-york) summarize what each state is like.

State tax rules are among the most frequently changed parts of estate law, which is another reason to review your plan every few years.
