Many people want a charity to be part of their legacy and are unsure how to do it cleanly. Should you set up a fund now, give from your IRA, or leave a gift in your will? Each tool has a different tax effect, a different deadline, and a different trap.

This article walks through three ways to give, how donor-advised funds and qualified charitable distributions work, and how to name charities so your wishes are followed.

## What are the three ways to give in an estate plan?

| Tool | Who it suits | Tax effect | Control after death | Typical trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Donor-advised fund | Donors with appreciated stock or a large one-year gift | Income tax deduction when you contribute | Sponsor rules; successor advisors | Assuming heirs inherit the account |
| Qualified charitable distribution | IRA owners age 70 and a half or older | Excluded from income, up to the annual limit | None; the gift is finished | Check payable to you, or sent to a DAF |
| Bequest or beneficiary designation | Anyone, at death | Estate deduction; no income tax on IRA to charity | You keep control until death | Forms that conflict with the will |

## How does a donor-advised fund work?

A DAF is an account held by a sponsoring charity, such as a community foundation or a financial firm's charitable arm. You contribute cash, stock, or other assets and receive an income tax deduction in the year you give, subject to limits. The money then grows tax-free in the account. When you are ready, you recommend grants to qualified charities, and the sponsor reviews and sends them.

The key legal point is ownership. Under the IRS description of donor-advised funds, the sponsoring organization has legal control over the assets. Your role is to advise. That is why a DAF is not part of your family's inheritance, and it is why it usually does not appear in your probate estate. Read the IRS overview on [donor-advised funds](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations/donor-advised-funds) for the agency's definition and warnings.

A DAF works well when you want a large deduction in a high-income year, or when you hold appreciated stock. Giving the stock directly generally avoids capital gains tax on the growth, and you may deduct its fair market value, subject to limits.

## What happens to a DAF when you die?

The sponsor's rules decide. Most sponsors allow you to name:

- **Successor advisors,** who take over recommending grants. These can include your children, which lets them keep giving in your family's name.
- **Successor charities,** which receive the remaining balance on your death.
- A combination, such as advisors for ten years and then a named charity.

If you name nobody, many sponsors follow a default policy, which may send the balance to the sponsor's general fund. Look at your account's agreement and update the designation, much as you would on an [IRA or retirement account](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries). Do not assume the will controls it.

Consider Margaret, 74. She holds a DAF worth $200,000 and wants her two daughters to keep making grants. She names both as successor advisors, and a local food bank as the charity if the daughters decline. Her will does not mention the fund, and it does not need to.

## How does a qualified charitable distribution work?

A QCD is a transfer from your IRA directly to a qualifying charity. To qualify:

- You must be at least **70 and a half** when the transfer is made. The age has not moved with the later required-distribution ages.
- The money must go **directly from the IRA trustee** to the charity. If a check is made out to you, it is an ordinary distribution.
- The charity must be an eligible public charity. **Donor-advised funds and supporting organizations are excluded.**
- The amount is excluded from your taxable income, up to an annual limit. The original limit was $100,000, and it has been indexed for inflation since 2024. As of 2026 it is $111,000 per person, so a married couple with separate IRAs may each give that amount.

A QCD can count toward your required minimum distribution for the year, which helps if you do not need the income. Because it is excluded rather than deducted, it also helps those who take the standard deduction and who could not otherwise benefit from charitable deductions. A QCD typically does not raise adjusted gross income, which can matter for Medicare premiums and the taxation of Social Security.

A limited, one-time election lets certain QCD amounts go to some split-interest arrangements, including a charitable remainder trust or a charitable gift annuity. That election has its own indexed cap, and the rules are narrow. See our article on [charitable remainder trusts](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust) before considering it. Check the IRS notice for the current year, and Publication 590-B, for the exact figures, since they change.

## Should you leave an IRA to charity?

Often this is the most tax-efficient way to leave money to a cause. Individual heirs generally pay income tax on withdrawals from an inherited traditional IRA, and most must empty it within 10 years. A charity pays no income tax on the same funds. If you plan to give a portion of your estate to charity, it can make sense to leave the pretax IRA to the charity and other assets, such as a home or Roth account, to family. The family often receives a step-up in basis on assets like stock and real estate. Read [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) for why.

To do it, you complete the IRA custodian's beneficiary form. Name the charity by its full legal name and, if possible, its tax ID number, and name contingent beneficiaries in case the charity no longer exists. See [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) and [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

A split is also common: 50% to a charity and 50% to a child, with each share stated as a percentage. Do not name a trust and a charity jointly without legal advice, because the trust's terms may affect the payout period.

## How do charitable bequests in a will or trust work?

A will or [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can make three kinds of gifts:

- **Specific bequest:** "$25,000 to the Riverbend Animal Shelter."
- **Percentage:** "10% of my estate to my church."
- **Residuary:** whatever is left after other gifts, which can go to charity if no family remains.

Leaving a fixed dollar amount is clear but can be eroded by inflation. A percentage rises and falls with the estate. For a deeper look at those terms, see the [glossary entry on bequests](/glossary/bequest) and [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate).

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- Assuming heirs inherit a DAF.
- Sending a QCD to a DAF, or letting the check be payable to you.
- Giving the IRA gift too early in the year, then discovering the required minimum distribution was already taken, since a QCD cannot be applied retroactively.
- Naming the charity inaccurately, or naming a charity that has merged or closed.
- Changing the will without changing the beneficiary forms.
- Forgetting that charitable bequests may reduce an estate subject to [estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption), which can help larger estates.

## What do state rules change?

States mostly follow the federal tax treatment of QCDs and DAFs, but a few treat retirement distributions or charitable deductions differently, and community property states handle beneficiary rights of a spouse in an IRA in their own way. Consult your [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) and check the rules where you file.
