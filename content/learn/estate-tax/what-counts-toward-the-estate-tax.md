Most people count their estate the way a probate court does: the things that pass under the will. The IRS counts differently. It starts with everything you owned or controlled at death and works down from there, whether or not a will or a probate judge ever touches it.

That gap catches families off guard. (Our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) covers the rest of the topic.) A widow who thinks of her "estate" as the $700,000 house may not realize the IRA, the life insurance and the brokerage account all go into the same pile. This article walks through each major asset, shows what is included and what is not, and explains why most families still owe nothing.

## What is the gross estate?

The gross estate is the total fair market value of property you own or control at death, plus certain transfers you made earlier that the tax code pulls back in. The federal tax is figured on that total after subtracting debts, expenses, charitable gifts, and anything passing to a surviving citizen spouse. For the details on deductions, see our guide to [what the estate can deduct](/learn/estate-tax/what-the-estate-can-deduct-before-estate-tax).

Then comes the exemption. In 2026 the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) is $15,000,000 per person, and the 2025 tax law indexes it for inflation after that with no scheduled sunset. Only the amount over that line is taxed, at a top rate of 40%.

Take Walter, a widower with an $18,000,000 estate after debts and expenses. About $3,000,000 sits above the exemption, so the federal tax would be roughly $1,200,000 before any credits or planning. If his estate were $9,000,000, the federal tax would be zero, and nobody would file a tax return unless a state or a portability election required it.

## Which assets count?

The short answer is almost all of them. This table covers the ones people ask about most.

| Asset | Counted in the gross estate? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| House you own | Yes, at market value | Mortgage is a deduction, not an exclusion |
| Bank and brokerage accounts | Yes | Includes payable-on-death accounts |
| 401(k), IRA, annuity | Yes, at full value | Passes by beneficiary form, not the will |
| Life insurance you own | Yes | Counted even if a family member is the beneficiary |
| Life insurance in a properly built ILIT | Generally no | Three-year rule applies to transfers |
| Revocable living trust assets | Yes | You kept control, so the trust is ignored |
| Business and farm interests | Yes | Valued at what a willing buyer would pay |
| Jointly owned property | Half or all, depending on who owns it | See the joint property section below |
| Personal property, vehicles, art | Yes | Appraisals needed for valuable items |
| Assets in a completed gift to an irrevocable trust | Generally no | Gift tax rules still apply |

### Your house

Your primary home counts at what it would sell for on the date of death, not what you paid. A house bought for $120,000 in 1994 that is worth $850,000 today adds $850,000. If a lender holds a mortgage, the loan balance is subtracted as a debt.

One quirk: a house owned in another state counts for the federal calculation too. Property in a state with its own estate or inheritance tax can face a second tax. Our guide to [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) lists how that works.

### Life insurance

Many people assume life insurance proceeds are free of every tax. They are free of income tax for the beneficiary. They are not automatically free of estate tax. If you owned the policy, or held what the tax code calls "incidents of ownership" such as the right to change the beneficiary or borrow against it, the death benefit counts.

That is why a $2,000,000 policy on a person with a $14,000,000 estate can push the total over the line. An [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) can keep the proceeds out, but only if it is set up correctly. If you transfer an existing policy to the trust and die within three years, the proceeds are pulled back in.

### Retirement accounts

A traditional IRA or 401(k) counts at its full balance, even though no one has paid income tax on it yet. Then the heirs pay income tax as they withdraw it. The same dollars can be hit twice, once by the estate tax if the estate is large, and again by income tax, though the heir may get a deduction for the estate tax that was paid on those dollars.

Retirement accounts also pass by beneficiary designation, not by will. Your [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) decide who gets them and how fast they must empty the account.

### Jointly owned property

Joint ownership changes the count in three ways.

1. **Spouses who own property together.** Half of the value is generally included in the first spouse's estate, no matter who paid. The half that passes to a surviving citizen spouse is then deducted.
2. **Non-spouse co-owners.** The full value is included in the estate of the first owner to die, unless the survivor can prove they contributed some of the purchase money. A mother who adds her adult son to the house deed has not reduced her estate. See [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) for the other problems this causes.
3. **Joint bank accounts.** The same rule applies. If you add your daughter to your savings account, the whole balance counts in your estate, absent proof she put money in.

### Trusts

A revocable living trust is a probate tool, not a tax tool. Because you can change it or cancel it, everything in it counts. An irrevocable trust is different. If you gave the assets away completely and kept no control, the trust is generally outside your estate, though the gift may use some of your lifetime exemption.

## What do lifetime gifts do?

Gifts you made while alive do not simply vanish. Taxable gifts above the yearly limit are added back in the calculation, so they use up part of your $15,000,000. Gifts within the annual exclusion, $19,000 per recipient in 2026, are ignored. Our article on [whether lifetime gifts count toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax) explains the arithmetic.

Appreciation is the interesting part. If you gave away stock worth $100,000 that later grew to $400,000, the growth is not in your estate. That is the main reason gifting is a real tool for larger estates.

## What stays out of the estate?

Several things are left out of the total.

- Property you gave away completely during life, though the gift itself may use part of your exemption.
- Property in a properly drafted irrevocable trust you do not control.
- Life insurance owned by someone else, such as an ILIT.
- Social Security survivor benefits, which are paid to the family and are not part of the estate.
- Most pensions that stop at death with no survivor benefit
.
Do not read that list as a way to avoid anything. Each item has strings attached, and the IRS looks closely at transfers made shortly before death.

## Does the estate tax apply to a surviving spouse?

Not directly. A U.S. citizen spouse can inherit an unlimited amount free of federal estate tax under the [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction). The deduction postpones the tax; it does not erase it. When the second spouse dies, everything they own, including what they inherited, goes into their gross estate.

That is where [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) matters. If the first spouse's unused exemption is claimed, the survivor can shelter up to $30 million combined in 2026. Without that election, the first spouse's exemption can be lost. A noncitizen spouse is a different situation; see [the QDOT guide](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot).

## What about the heirs?

Inheritors do not file an estate tax return for what they receive, and the tax is paid out of the estate before distribution. Heirs may face other taxes. Income tax can apply to retirement accounts, and capital gains tax applies to later growth on inherited property, measured from the [stepped-up basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). A few states charge an inheritance tax to the person receiving the property.

## A short checklist before you decide you are safe

1. Add up the house, all accounts, retirement plans, business interests and the face value of every life insurance policy you own.
2. Subtract mortgages and other debts.
3. Add taxable gifts you made above the annual exclusion.
4. Compare the total to $15,000,000, or $30,000,000 for a couple who preserves portability.
5. Check whether your state taxes estates at a lower threshold. Several do.

If the number lands above about $10,000,000, or if you hold a large business or farm, a conversation is worth having. If you are well under, your planning energy probably belongs elsewhere.
