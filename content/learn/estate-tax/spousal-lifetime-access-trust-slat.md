Some couples reach the point where the house has gone up, the investments have done well, and a financial planner starts mentioning a trust with an acronym. The acronym is SLAT. It lets a couple give away a large amount of property without fully giving up the feeling of having it.

That feeling is the catch, and this article is mostly about it. You will see how a SLAT works, why it can reduce estate tax, what goes wrong if one spouse dies or the marriage ends, and when a simpler tool does the job better. It sits within our wider guide to [estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## What is a SLAT?

A SLAT is an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) created by one spouse, the donor, for the benefit of the other spouse and usually the couple's children. The trustee, who is often a trusted adult child or a corporate trustee, can distribute income or principal to the beneficiary spouse for health, education, maintenance and support. The donor does not control the trust and cannot take the property back.

Because the property has been given away, the donor can no longer touch it directly. Yet the family, through the spouse, still can. When the spouse needs money, the trustee can make a distribution that the couple spends together.

## How does a SLAT save estate tax?

The mechanism is a completed gift. Maria funds a SLAT with $4,000,000 of stock. For tax purposes, that is a gift, reported on a gift tax return. It uses $4,000,000 of her lifetime exemption, which stands at $15,000,000 per person in 2026. No gift tax is paid, because the exemption absorbs it.

From that day, the stock and everything it earns belongs to the trust, outside Maria's estate. If the stock grows to $9,000,000, the extra $5,000,000 is never counted. At 40%, that growth could have cost the family around $2,000,000 in federal tax if their combined estate was above the line. The trust is also drafted so it stays out of her husband's estate when he dies, which is the benefit that separates a SLAT from simply giving property to him.

The tax saving is only worth the trouble when the couple's combined estate is large enough to be taxed. If your total wealth is $6,000,000 and your state has no estate tax, a SLAT adds complexity without tax benefit. The [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) article explains where the lines fall.

## Are SLATs a risk if my spouse dies first?

Yes. This is the question that decides whether a SLAT is right for a particular couple.

The donor's access exists only because the beneficiary spouse can receive distributions. When that spouse dies, the trust usually continues for the children or other beneficiaries named in the document. The donor spouse is not a beneficiary and cannot be added back later. The money the couple was counting on as a cushion is no longer reachable.

Consider Daniel, 66, who funds a SLAT for his wife Priya with $5,000,000. Priya dies four years later. The trust now belongs to their two children. Daniel keeps his house and the $2,000,000 he held outside the trust and lives comfortably. But if he had put almost everything in the trust, he would be asking his own children for money. That is the picture a good plan prevents.

Planning around it usually includes these points.

- **Fund only what you can truly spare.** Many couples keep at least enough outside the trust to support the survivor for life.
- **Give the trustee broad discretion.** Power to make distributions to descendants as well as the spouse keeps the family flexible.
- **Consider a younger spouse as the beneficiary.** The spouse expected to live longer is the safer beneficiary.
- **Review life insurance.** Coverage on the beneficiary spouse can replace lost access to the funds.

## What if we divorce?

A SLAT does not automatically unwind in a divorce. The former spouse may remain a beneficiary, because the trust is irrevocable and the donor gave the interest away. Some trusts include a clause that ends the spouse's interest at divorce, but it must be written in at the start and works only if the state allows it. Couples in a second marriage should read our article on [SLATs in blended families](/learn/blended-families/spousal-lifetime-access-trust-blended-family) before going further.

## Can both spouses create SLATs?

They can, but the law watches for a trap. If each spouse creates nearly identical trusts for the other, a court can "uncross" them under the reciprocal trust doctrine. Each trust is then treated as if the donor were the beneficiary of their own trust, and the property returns to the donor's estate. To lower the risk, couples vary the trusts' terms, funding amounts, timing and powers. Attorneys do not treat this as a form-filling exercise.

## How does a SLAT compare with other tools?

| Feature | SLAT | GRAT | Relying on portability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who is it for | Married couple | One person, any marital status | Married couple |
| What leaves the estate | Whole gift plus all growth | Only growth above an IRS interest rate | Nothing leaves |
| Donor keeps access | Indirectly, through spouse | Fixed payments back to donor | Full ownership |
| Uses lifetime exemption | Yes | Little, if structured well | Not applicable |
| Main risk | Spouse dies first, divorce | Donor dying during the term | Growth is taxed later |
| Step-up in basis at death | Generally no | Not if donor survives the term | Yes |

A [grantor retained annuity trust](/learn/estate-tax/grantor-retained-annuity-trust) is the better fit for a single person or for a couple that cannot afford to give up the asset, because the donor gets the principal back over a few years. Our guide to [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) covers the simplest option, which asks nothing of the couple except a timely return after the first death.

## What are the tax costs people forget?

Three come up again and again.

1. **A gift tax return.** The donor files Form 709 for the year of the gift, even when no tax is due. Skipping it leaves the value of the gift open to question later.
2. **Lost step-up in basis.** Assets in a SLAT generally do not receive a new basis at the donor's death. Stock bought for $500,000 and now worth $4,000,000 carries a large built-in gain to the children. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) for how the trade-off works.
3. **Trust income tax.** A SLAT is usually written as a grantor trust, so the donor pays the income tax on its earnings. That payment is itself a quiet extra gift to the trust, and many families treat it as a feature. It does reduce what the donor has to live on.

## Who should consider one?

A SLAT tends to make sense for a couple with a combined estate comfortably above the exemption, or one living in a state with a lower estate tax threshold, where one spouse holds an appreciating asset and the other is in good health. It makes less sense when the marriage is new, when the couple cannot spare the asset, or when a simple credit shelter plan would do. The question to ask is not "can we save tax," but "what would we do if the trust turned out to be one spouse's only safety net." If that answer is uncomfortable, adjust the amount before signing.
