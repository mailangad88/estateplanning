Giving money to your children or grandchildren while you are alive can feel good, and it can reduce the size of a future taxable estate. It also comes with rules: an annual limit, a reporting form, and tradeoffs on taxes and care eligibility that surprise many families.

This article explains the annual exclusion, what it means to gift beyond it, and when gifting may be a mistake. For the larger picture, start with our guide to [estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## What is the gift tax annual exclusion?

The annual exclusion is the amount you can give to any one person in a calendar year without filing a gift tax return or reducing your lifetime exclusion. For 2026 it is $19,000 per recipient. There is no limit on the number of recipients, so you could give $19,000 each to three children, their spouses, and five grandchildren in the same year, if you chose.

The [annual exclusion](/glossary/annual-exclusion) applies to gifts of a "present interest," meaning the recipient can use the money now. A gift into a trust the recipient cannot touch yet may not qualify unless the trust is structured to allow it.

### Gift splitting for married couples

If you are married, you and your spouse can treat a gift as made half by each of you. That doubles the exclusion to $38,000 per recipient in 2026. Gift splitting requires filing Form 709, even if no tax is due.

| Giver | Annual exclusion per recipient (2026) |
|---|---|
| One person | $19,000 |
| Married couple, split gifts | $38,000 |

## What counts as a gift?

Any transfer for less than full value. Common examples include:

- Cash, checks, or transfers
- Stocks, real estate, or other property
- Forgiving a loan
- Adding a child's name to a deed (a gift of part of the property)
- Selling something for far less than it is worth

A gift is measured by value on the date it is made. Ordinary birthday and holiday gifts are part of the annual exclusion in theory, though no one reports them in practice when they are small.

## What gifts are always excluded?

Some gifts do not count against the annual exclusion or the lifetime exclusion at all:

- **Tuition paid directly to a school.** Payments made straight to the institution for another person's tuition have no limit.
- **Medical expenses paid directly to the provider.** The same applies, including health insurance premiums.
- **Gifts to a U.S. citizen spouse.** These are unlimited.
- **Gifts to a qualified charity.** These are deductible and also unlimited.
- **Political organization contributions** in many cases.

## What happens if I give more than $19,000?

You file Form 709 by April 15 of the following year, and the amount above $19,000 reduces your lifetime exclusion. For 2026 that exclusion is $15,000,000. You owe no gift tax until the cumulative total exceeds it.

### Example

Joyce, 70, gives her son $119,000 in 2026 to help with a home purchase. She files Form 709. The first $19,000 is covered by the annual exclusion. The remaining $100,000 reduces her lifetime exclusion from $15,000,000 to $14,900,000. She pays no gift tax. When she dies, that $100,000 is added back to her estate tax calculation, but the exclusion that remains is already reduced, so she and her estate get the same total shelter either way.

Since most people never reach the $15 million threshold, filing a gift tax return mainly keeps a record. The risk comes when someone does not file at all and has no evidence of the exclusion used.

## Does the gift tax get paid if I go over the limit?

Only after you use up the lifetime exclusion. Until then the answer is no. If you do exceed the lifetime exclusion, the top rate is 40%, and tax is due on the gift in the year it is made.

## What about 529 plans?

A 529 college savings plan has a special rule. You can put up to five years of annual exclusions into a 529 at once, which is $95,000 per beneficiary in 2026 (or $190,000 for a couple), if you file Form 709 and treat the gift as spread over five years. If you die during that period, part of the amount can be pulled back into your estate. A 529 also keeps you as the account owner, so you decide how the money is used.

## What are the downsides of gifting?

### Loss of step-up in basis

Property given during your lifetime keeps your original tax basis. If your daughter receives stock you bought for $10,000 that is now worth $110,000, her basis is $10,000 and a sale triggers capital gains on $100,000. Had she inherited the stock instead, her basis would generally reset to $110,000. This difference can be larger than any estate tax saved. Our article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains how to weigh it.

### Medicaid look-back

If you may need nursing-home care and Medicaid, gifts can cause a penalty period. In most states, Medicaid reviews transfers made in the 60 months before you apply. See the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period). The tax rules and the Medicaid rules are separate, and a gift that is fine for tax purposes can still cause a penalty.

### Loss of control and security

Money given away cannot be taken back, and it becomes the recipient's, including their creditors' and a spouse's in a divorce. If you give too much, you may not have enough for your own care.

### Gifts to minors

Gifts to children usually go into a custodial account under UTMA or a trust. A custodian manages the account until the child reaches age 18 to 21, depending on the state. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) for the options.

## Is gifting right for me? A checklist

1. Do I have enough left to live on, including long-term care costs?
2. Is the gift cash or an appreciated asset? Appreciated assets often do better if inherited.
3. Could Medicaid eligibility be a concern in the next five years?
4. Does my state tax estates or gifts? Some states count recent gifts back into the estate.
5. Is the recipient financially secure, and is their relationship stable?
6. Do I need to file Form 709 for this gift?
7. Would paying a bill directly to the school or provider work better than giving cash?

For larger gifts, an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) can add protection and control, though it adds cost and permanence.

## How do I report a gift on Form 709?

Form 709 is filed with your personal tax return timeline, by April 15 of the year after the gift. You list each gift, the recipient, the date, and the value. If you gave property, you describe it and explain how you valued it, and for real estate or closely held business interests you will usually need a written appraisal. The return shows how much of your lifetime exclusion the gifts used. You attach nothing to your income tax return, and you file Form 709 separately, even when no gift tax is due. Married couples who split gifts each file a return. Keep copies permanently, because your executor will need the totals when settling your estate.
