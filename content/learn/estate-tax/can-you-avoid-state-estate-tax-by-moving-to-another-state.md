For years you have heard that people leave Illinois and New Jersey for Florida, Arizona or the Carolinas to save taxes. If your estate is large enough that the state would take a share at your death, the idea is worth thinking about. It can also go badly if you treat it as a paperwork trick.

This page covers how state estate tax actually follows you, what you cannot move, and how states check whether a move is real.

## Does moving really change state estate tax?

A real change of domicile generally moves most of your estate out of the old state's reach. Domicile is your permanent legal home, the place you intend to return to and treat as home. State estate taxes and inheritance taxes are built on it. An Illinois resident's estate is tested against the Illinois threshold. A person who is a Florida resident at death, and Florida has no estate tax, is not.

The federal tax stays the same wherever you live: $15,000,000 per person in 2026, with a top rate of 40%. So moving only matters when the problem is a state tax that begins at a much lower level. Our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) lists which states have them.

The two states we serve work differently. [Illinois has an estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) that reaches estates well below the federal level and gives a surviving spouse no portability. [New Jersey has an inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) on what certain heirs receive, with children and spouses exempt. For New Jersey families whose heirs are children, moving rarely changes the bill, since there often is none.

## What can you not take with you?

Real estate, mostly. A state can tax real property inside its borders no matter where the owner lives, and Illinois and New Jersey both do. So the Lake Michigan condo or the shore house stays in the old state's net even after you change your driver's license.

| Asset | Taxed by old state after a real move? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bank and brokerage accounts | Generally no | Intangibles follow domicile |
| Retirement accounts | Generally no | Follow domicile |
| Business interests | Depends on the entity and assets | An LLC that owns local real estate can be looked through |
| Real estate in the old state | Yes | Taxed where it sits |
| Tangible items in the old state (art, vehicles, furniture) | Often yes | Taxed where located |
| Real estate in the new state | Under the new state's rules | Taxed where it sits |

Planning options exist: sell the old property, give it to heirs during your lifetime where the state does not add gifts back, or hold it through an entity that fits the state's rules. Each has income tax, basis and gift tax consequences. Gifts forfeit the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), so the tax saved at death has to be weighed against capital gains later.

## How do states decide where you really live?

States look at the facts, not the label. When the estate is big, the old state's revenue office or attorney general can examine the move, and they have seen every version of the story. Common factors:

- Where you hold your driver's license and voter registration.
- Which state you name as your home on income tax returns, and whether you file as a part-year resident.
- Where you spend more than half the year, with records to prove it.
- Where your primary doctors, accountants, lawyers and bank accounts are.
- Which home is the "near and dear" one, meaning where the sentimental items, pets and family photos are.
- Whether you kept your old home, and how you use it.

Take Frank, 76, a widower in Naperville with a $9 million estate. He buys a condo in Naples, Florida, gets a Florida license, but keeps the Naperville house, spends the summer and fall there, and uses his Illinois doctors. When he dies, the Illinois Attorney General can argue his real home was Illinois, and the paper trail will cut that way. The state does not have to prove bad faith. It only needs to show that his life stayed in Illinois.

A cleaner move looks different. Frank sells the Naperville house, spends most of each year in Florida, switches his doctors, banks, and voter registration, files a declaration of domicile there, and keeps a log of days spent in each state. If he wants a summer place in Illinois, he rents.

## A domicile move checklist

1. **Confirm the numbers first.** Estimate your estate, including life insurance and retirement accounts, and compare it to the old and new states' thresholds. If it is near or below the old state's threshold, a move may not help.
2. **Check the new state's taxes.** Some destinations have an inheritance tax or an estate tax of their own.
3. **Decide what to do with the old property** before you move, not after.
4. **Change the legal anchors:** license, vehicle registration, voter registration, homestead filing in the new state.
5. **Update your documents.** A will and trust signed in one state generally still work in another, but they should be reviewed by an attorney in the new state. See [moving states or living abroad with a will](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad).
6. **Keep a day log** and keep it for years.
7. **Handle the exit tax return** if the old state has a part-year resident filing.

## What does the income tax side look like?

Estate tax is only one of the taxes that follow a move. Illinois and New Jersey both tax income, and a new state may or may not. A retiree who leaves for a state with no income tax usually saves more each year than the estate tax would ever cost, which is why some people move for income tax and treat the estate tax as a bonus. The reverse also happens. A move to a state with a lower estate tax but a higher property tax can lose money overall. Add up the whole picture with your accountant, and use the federal numbers in our [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) page as the baseline. The [estate and gift tax library](/learn/estate-tax) has more on the planning tools.

## Does the new state's probate change things?

Yes, in two ways. Probate will happen under the new state's courts, and if you keep property in the old state, your family may also face [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) there. A revocable trust for the old property can avoid that second court case even though it does not avoid the tax.

## What if you cannot or will not move?

Many people do not want to leave their grandchildren. In that case the planning tools stay the same: credit shelter trusts that use the state exemption at the first death, lifetime gifts where the state does not add them back, charitable giving, and life insurance owned outside the estate. Our guide on [how to reduce estate taxes](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-reduce-estate-taxes) walks through them. The practical choice is often to stay and plan, rather than move.
