Most married couples never write an estate tax check at the first death, and the reason has a name: the marital deduction. It is the single biggest rule in the federal estate tax, and also one of the most misread. People hear that spouses "don't pay estate tax" and assume the problem has been solved. For many families it has only been moved.

This page explains what the deduction does, which transfers qualify, what it leaves for the second death, and where couples make mistakes. If you want the broader picture first, start with our guide to [estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## What does the marital deduction actually do?

It subtracts whatever passes to your spouse from your taxable estate. The federal estate tax starts with everything you own at death, then allows deductions. Property left to a surviving spouse who is a U.S. citizen can be deducted in full, with no limit. A $20 million estate left outright to a spouse becomes a $0 taxable estate, and no tax is due.

The same rule applies to gifts during life. You can give a citizen spouse any amount at any time without gift tax. That is why moving a house or investment account into joint names creates no tax problem between spouses, though it may affect other things such as [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis-for-married-couples-and-joint-property).

The glossary defines the term: [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction).

## It defers the tax, it does not remove it

The deduction postpones the tax to the second death. Whatever your spouse still owns when they die is part of their estate, and it is taxed if it exceeds what their exclusion covers. As of 2026, the basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person.

Consider Gloria and Henry. Their combined estate is $22 million. Henry dies first and leaves everything to Gloria. Nothing is taxed. Gloria now owns $22 million and has her own $15 million exclusion. At her death, about $7 million is over the line, and at the top rate of 40 percent the federal bill is roughly $2.8 million. Henry's unused exclusion would have covered that, but only if the couple took a step to keep it.

## What transfers qualify?

Not every gift to a spouse counts. The IRS requires three things.

1. **The spouse is a U.S. citizen.** If not, the unlimited deduction is lost unless the property goes to a qualified domestic trust. See [non-citizen spouse and the QDOT](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot).
2. **The property is included in the deceased spouse's gross estate.** You can only deduct what is counted in the first place.
3. **The spouse receives it in a qualifying form.** Outright ownership qualifies. So does property that passes by joint title, a beneficiary designation, or a will.

Trusts can qualify too, but they must follow strict rules. The best-known is the qualified terminable interest property trust, or QTIP. The spouse gets all the income for life, no one else may receive anything from the trust while the spouse lives, and the executor makes an election on the estate tax return. At the spouse's death, the trust is taxed in the spouse's estate, and the remainder goes to the people the first spouse chose, often children from an earlier marriage. Our guide to [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) walks through a blended family example.

| Way the spouse receives it | Marital deduction | Who controls the end result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outright by will or beneficiary form | Yes | The surviving spouse |
| Joint title with survivorship | Yes, for the part included in the estate | The surviving spouse |
| QTIP trust | Yes, if the election is made | The first spouse, who names the final beneficiaries |
| Trust ending when the spouse remarries, with no QTIP election | Usually no | The first spouse |
| Gift to a spouse who is not a citizen | Limited yearly amount | Depends on the form |

## Where do couples go wrong?

**They leave everything to the survivor and forget the first exemption.** If Henry's estate pays no tax and no one files the right return, his $15 million exclusion disappears. The fix is portability, which carries over the unused amount, but it only works if the executor files a timely estate tax return. See [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples). If the estate cannot meet the deadline, our article on [missing the Form 706 deadline](/learn/estate-tax/missed-the-form-706-deadline) covers relief.

**They assume a trust is needed.** For a couple whose combined estate is under the exclusion, a trust is not needed to avoid federal estate tax. The marital deduction and the exclusion together cover it. Trusts may still make sense for other reasons, such as protecting children from a first marriage. See [bypass trust versus portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability) for when each tool fits.

**They forget the state.** Federal numbers do not set state numbers. Illinois taxes estates over $4 million and does not offer portability, so a couple with $5 million can face an Illinois estate tax at the second death even though the federal tax is zero. Our guide to the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) explains the math. New Jersey has no estate tax but does have an inheritance tax, and a surviving spouse is exempt from it. See the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article.

**They ignore a spouse who is not a citizen.** The unlimited deduction is not available, and the first estate may owe tax at once unless the assets go into a qualified domestic trust.

**They deduct what is not theirs.** Jointly owned property between spouses is generally counted as half belonging to each, whatever the contribution. Property left to a former spouse after divorce, or to a new spouse the will never anticipated, may not qualify as you expect.

## How can a couple use the deduction well?

A couple with an estate comfortably under $15 million each can use the marital deduction with simple outright gifts and file for portability if the survivor might someday exceed the exclusion. A couple closer to the line, or one with children from earlier marriages, often adds a trust.

One practical approach is a disclaimer plan. The survivor receives everything, but has nine months to disclaim, or refuse, part of it. The disclaimed share passes to a trust for the children or the family, using up the first spouse's exclusion. If the numbers look tight, the survivor disclaims. If not, the survivor keeps all. The article on [how to disclaim an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) explains the rules for refusing property without it counting as a gift.

If your estate is near the exemption, read our article on [planning when you are near the exemption](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-planning-when-you-are-near-the-exemption). You can also run your own numbers with the [estate tax estimator](/tools/estate-tax-estimator).

## What should you check this year?

- Is every account titled and named the way you intend? A beneficiary form can override a will.
- Is your spouse a U.S. citizen? If not, speak to an attorney about a QDOT.
- Does your plan say what happens when your spouse remarries?
- If one of you has children from an earlier marriage, who gets the property after the survivor dies?
- Have you looked at your state's estate or inheritance tax, as well as the federal one?
