Some people hold a large asset that has grown far beyond what they paid for it: a block of stock, a rental building, a share of a family company. Selling it would trigger a big capital gains bill. Leaving it to children would give them a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) but nothing to live on in the meantime. If charity is already part of your plans, a charitable remainder trust can turn that asset into an income stream for you and a gift for a cause later.

This article explains the moving parts, the tax effects, and the people it does not fit.

## What is a charitable remainder trust and how does the money move?

A CRT is an [irrevocable trust](/glossary/irrevocable-trust) with two kinds of beneficiaries. Income beneficiaries, usually you and perhaps your spouse, receive payments first. The charity receives whatever is left at the end, called the remainder.

The steps look like this:

1. You sign the trust agreement, naming a [trustee](/glossary/trustee), the income beneficiaries, the payout method, the term, and the charity.
2. You transfer assets. Cash and publicly traded stock are the simplest. Real estate and business interests are possible but need valuation, environmental review, or other care.
3. The trustee sells appreciated assets inside the trust. Because the trust is tax-exempt, it does not pay capital gains tax on the sale, so the full value can be reinvested.
4. The trustee pays you the required amount each year.
5. When the term ends or the last income beneficiary dies, the remainder goes to the charity.

You can serve as trustee in many cases, though a bank or a professional trustee may be better for real estate or complex holdings. Name a successor in case you cannot continue.

## What is the difference between a CRAT and a CRUT?

The two types differ in how the annual payment is figured.

- **CRAT (annuity trust).** Pays a fixed dollar amount, set as a percentage of the starting value, every year. The amount never changes, and you cannot add assets later. A CRAT suits someone who wants predictable income.
- **CRUT (unitrust).** Pays a fixed percentage of the trust's value, revalued annually. Payments grow if the investments grow and shrink if they fall. You can add assets later. Variations let the trust pay only what it earns, which helps with hard-to-sell property such as land.

For both, federal rules set guardrails. The annual payout must be at least 5% and no more than 50%. The trust can last for the life or lives of the income beneficiaries, or for a term of up to 20 years. And the charity's projected remainder must be at least 10% of the initial value, which limits how high a payout can be for younger donors. The IRS publishes sample trust forms and guidance on its [charitable remainder trust page](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-remainder-trusts).

Consider Walter, 68, and his wife Elaine, 66. They own stock worth $1 million that cost $150,000. Selling it outright would produce an $850,000 gain. They put it in a CRUT paying 5% for the rest of both lives. The trust sells without paying capital gains tax, reinvests the $1 million, and pays them about $50,000 in the first year. The payment moves up or down as the portfolio does. They also receive a partial income tax deduction, which depends on their ages and the payout rate. At the second death, what is left goes to a hospital foundation.

## What are the tax effects?

There are three, and each deserves attention.

**A partial income tax deduction.** When you fund the trust, you may deduct the present value of the charity's remainder, which is calculated using IRS tables and interest rates. The deduction is subject to percentage-of-income limits and carryforward rules, so a tax advisor should run the numbers first.

**Deferred capital gains.** The gain is not wiped out. It is spread through the payments you receive. Distributions are taxed in tiers: ordinary income first, then capital gains, then other income, then tax-free return of principal. The character of what the trust earned each year controls what you pay.

**Estate inclusion.** If you keep an income interest for life, the value of the trust is generally included in your gross estate, offset by a charitable deduction for the remainder. Estate tax is rarely the reason to build a CRT, since the 2026 exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, but the rule matters for large estates. See [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

The trust files Form 5227 each year, and the trustee reports income to you on a Schedule K-1.

## Who is a CRT a good fit for?

- **Owners of appreciated, low-yield property.** If an asset has a large gain and pays little income, a CRT lets you convert it into a higher income stream.
- **Business owners planning a sale.** Moving shares into a CRT before a sale agreement is signed can defer gain on proceeds. Timing is critical, and the trust cannot be a mere conduit for a sale already agreed to. See [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).
- **People who already intend to give.** The best reason to use a CRT is that charity is a goal, with the tax effects as a bonus.
- **Donors with no children counting on the asset.** Without heirs depending on the property, the cost of giving up the remainder is small.

## Who is a CRT not a good fit for?

- **Families expecting an inheritance from the asset.** The remainder goes to charity, so children get nothing from it.
- **Small gifts.** Legal drafting, annual returns, and trustee costs make small trusts inefficient.
- **People who need access to principal.** You cannot take money back out of the trust beyond the required payments, and you cannot change the payout.
- **Anyone who is unsure about charitable goals.** If you may change your mind, a more flexible vehicle may be better.

## What does it cost, and what compliance is required?

Expect legal fees to draft the trust, a trustee who tracks the tier system, investment management, an annual Form 5227, and possibly state filings. Real estate adds appraisals and sometimes environmental checks. Mistakes, such as adding assets to a CRAT or paying out the wrong amount, can disqualify the trust, so careful administration matters.

## Can life insurance replace what goes to charity?

Yes, this is sometimes called wealth replacement. The donor uses part of the CRT income or the tax savings to pay premiums on a policy that benefits the children, often held in an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust). The family receives roughly what they would have inherited, and charity receives the CRT remainder. This only works if you can buy the insurance, so it is better planned before health changes.

## What alternatives are there?

| Tool | Irrevocable | Income to you | Tax deduction timing | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CRAT | Yes | Fixed amount | Year of funding | High |
| CRUT | Yes | Percentage of value | Year of funding | High |
| Donor-advised fund | Yes | None | Year of funding | Low |
| Charitable gift annuity | Yes | Fixed, from the charity | Year of funding | Moderate |
| Bequest in a will or trust | No, until death | Not applicable | Estate deduction at death | Low |

The [donor-advised funds and QCDs article](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds) covers the simpler options. If you only want to leave something to a cause at death, naming it in your will or trust, or as a retirement account beneficiary, costs little and keeps all your assets available during life.

## What state questions come up?

Most states follow the federal treatment of CRTs, but some tax trust income differently, and a few have rules about who may serve as trustee or how a trust is registered. If you own property in more than one state, the trust may have filing duties in each. Check the rules for your state through our [state guides](/estate-planning/california), and read [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies) for the lifetime giving options that sit alongside a CRT.
