You are the executor, and someone told you the estate may need a Form 706. You have found the form on irs.gov, counted the pages, and wondered whether paying a professional is worth it. The honest answer is that it depends on what is in the estate and why the return is being filed.

This page gives you a way to decide, and a list of the records you need either way.

## Can an executor prepare Form 706 without a professional?

Yes. The law lets the executor, or anyone appointed to act for the estate, prepare and sign the return. Nothing requires an attorney or a CPA. The executor signs under penalty of perjury, which is the part to take seriously, because the numbers on the form become the estate's official record.

The form itself is not hard to read. What makes it hard is that it asks for opinions of value. Real estate, a business, a collection, a partnership interest, and any property that is not a publicly traded stock needs a qualified appraisal. The executor cannot supply those from memory or from an online estimate.

For the basics of who must file and by when, see the [Form 706 explainer](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return). The deadline is nine months after death, with a six-month extension to file available on Form 4768.

## When is doing it yourself reasonable?

A do-it-yourself return is a fair choice when the stakes are small and the facts are tidy. These conditions point that way:

- No tax is due and the only reason to file is to elect [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples).
- Nearly everything passes to the surviving spouse.
- The assets are mostly bank accounts, brokerage accounts and one house.
- There are no lifetime gifts large enough to need a Form 709.
- The executor is comfortable keeping records and reading instructions.

In a portability-only filing, the rules allow you to estimate some values for property that passes to a spouse or charity, because those assets do not change the tax. That relief makes a simple estate much more manageable than a full return.

Even then, get the numbers checked. Nobody but the executor sees the return until the survivor dies years later, which means a mistake can sit unnoticed for decades.

## When should you hire help?

Call in a professional if any of these is true:

1. **The estate is near or over $15,000,000** when you add the gross estate and lifetime taxable gifts.
2. **There is a closely held business, farm or land.** Valuation drives the tax. See [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).
3. **There are trusts**, especially irrevocable ones, or the person made large lifetime gifts.
4. **The estate may owe state tax** as well as federal. Illinois has its own return, and the interplay with federal deductions needs care.
5. **The family disagrees** about values, since a professional's report is easier to defend.
6. **The estate wants special elections**, such as the alternate valuation date or deferred payment. These are covered in our page on [estate tax elections](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-elections-alternate-valuation-deferral-and-farm-valuation).

A good division of labor looks like this: the estate planning attorney coordinates and reviews, a CPA prepares the numbers, and an appraiser values the unusual assets. The executor signs. Our page on [hiring help as an executor](/learn/after-a-death/executor-hiring-help-and-staying-protected) describes how to do it without becoming personally exposed.

## What will it cost?

Fees vary too much to quote a number here. The drivers are predictable: how many schedules apply, how many appraisals are needed, whether there is a business, and how organized the records are. Ask three things before you hire:

- Is the fee flat, hourly, or capped?
- Are appraiser fees included or separate?
- Who will answer the IRS if it writes back?

The cost of the return is also a deductible administration expense in many estates, covered in [what the estate can deduct](/learn/estate-tax/what-the-estate-can-deduct-before-estate-tax). A paid preparer's fee for a return that locks in a spouse's unused exclusion can be small next to the tax saved later.

## Worked example: Ellen's portability return

Ellen's husband Gary died in February at 78. Their combined estate is about $3.4 million: a house, two brokerage accounts, and a pension. It all passes to Ellen. No tax is due, and no return is required.

Ellen is the executor. She decides to file Form 706 for portability. She gathers the date-of-death statements for each account, orders a market appraisal of the house, and reports the pension using the plan administrator's letter. Because everything passes to her, she uses estimated values for items that qualify for the marital deduction and a full appraisal only for the house. She pays an estate planning attorney for a flat-fee review before filing. The return goes in by the nine-month deadline.

Years later, Ellen's own estate has grown. Gary's unused exclusion is on the record, and no one has to reconstruct anything.

## What mistakes do first-time filers make?

Three come up again and again. The first is valuing the house from a listing site instead of a dated appraisal, which the IRS can reject. The second is leaving out assets that pass by beneficiary form, such as life insurance and retirement accounts, because they never touch the will. The third is assuming a filing extension also extends the time to pay, which it does not. Reading the instructions on irs.gov line by line before you start takes an hour and prevents most of these. The estate-tax pillar page, [estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax), links to the valuation and deduction articles that go with each schedule.

## What records should you keep?

Keep these together in one folder, paper or digital, labeled with the date of death.

- [ ] Death certificate and a copy of the will or trust.
- [ ] Date-of-death statements for every account.
- [ ] Deeds and appraisals, with the appraiser's credentials.
- [ ] Business valuation reports and tax returns for the three years before death.
- [ ] Insurance statements, including Form 712 from each insurer.
- [ ] Records of debts, funeral costs and administration expenses.
- [ ] Prior gift tax returns and a list of lifetime gifts.
- [ ] The filed Form 706, extension forms, and proof of mailing.
- [ ] IRS letters and any closing correspondence.

Keep the return itself and the supporting papers for as long as the surviving spouse lives and then for the period after the survivor's own estate return. That can be a very long time, which is why a clear folder matters.
