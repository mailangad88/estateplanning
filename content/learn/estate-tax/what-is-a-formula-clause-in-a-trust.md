Somewhere in the middle of many couples' living trusts is a paragraph about "the applicable exclusion amount." It is easy to skip. It also controls who gets what when the first spouse dies. That paragraph is a formula clause.

If your trust was signed more than a dozen years ago, the formula may be doing something you never intended. This page explains what it is, how it can go wrong, and what to ask your attorney.

## What does a formula clause do?

A formula clause divides the first spouse's estate into two parts without naming a dollar figure. One part, in older documents, goes into a bypass trust (also called a credit shelter or family trust), which uses the first spouse's exemption and keeps the money out of the survivor's taxable estate. The rest goes to the survivor outright or in a marital trust, where the [marital deduction](/learn/estate-tax/marital-deduction-explained) defers the tax.

The point of using a formula is flexibility. Nobody knows what the exemption will be when the first spouse dies, or what the estate will be worth. Instead of writing "$1 million to the bypass trust," the lawyer wrote a rule that adjusts itself to the law and the facts at that time.

For the comparison with leaving everything to the spouse, see our explanation of [bypass trust vs portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability).

## Why can an old formula cause trouble?

Because the federal exemption grew and the state exemption did not. Many couples' trusts were written when the federal exemption was $1 million, then $5 million. The formula tied the bypass trust to the federal number, which at the time was a limit of a few million dollars. In 2026 the federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person.

Take Walter and Joan, a married couple in suburban Chicago who signed a trust in 2009. Their estate is $8 million: house, brokerage accounts and a retirement account. Their trust says the bypass trust gets "the largest amount that can pass free of federal estate tax." Walter dies in 2026.

The federal exemption is $15,000,000, which is more than the entire estate. So by the plain words of the formula, everything goes into the bypass trust and nothing goes to Joan directly. Two things follow:

1. **Joan loses control.** The money sits in a trust she may use only for support under the trust's terms. She may not be able to sell the house freely or change the investments.
2. **Illinois may tax it now.** Illinois lets a surviving spouse's marital share pass tax-free, but a bypass trust is not marital property. Illinois's exclusion is far below $8 million, so the amount above it is taxed at Walter's death. No federal tax is due, and yet the family writes a check to the state a generation earlier than needed.

That is the trap. A formula written to save federal tax produces a state tax bill that nobody planned for.

## What are the common ways to fix it?

There are several, and they are not equal. Your attorney will choose based on your estate and your goals.

| Approach | What it does | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| State-exemption formula | Funds the bypass trust only up to the state exemption | Uses the first spouse's state exemption and defers the rest |
| Disclaimer-based plan | Everything goes to the survivor, who may disclaim into a bypass trust within nine months | Flexible, but the survivor must act in time and in the right way |
| Separate state marital election | Where the state allows it, the executor elects marital treatment for state purposes only | Not every state permits it, and the rules are technical |
| Outright to spouse plus portability | No bypass trust at all | Federal exemption saved, state exemption lost in Illinois |
| Reviewed and amended formula | A new trust or amendment replaces the old wording | Requires both spouses alive and competent |

A disclaimer plan is attractive for couples whose future is uncertain, since the surviving spouse decides after seeing the numbers. The survivor can also file a federal return electing [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) as a backup.

## How do you find the formula in your trust?

1. **Find the section on the first death.** It is usually titled something like "Division of Trust at the Death of the First Spouse" or "Creation of Family Trust and Survivor's Trust."
2. **Mark the phrases.** "Applicable exclusion amount," "unified credit," "largest amount that can pass free of federal estate tax," or "reduce the federal estate tax to zero."
3. **Look for state language.** Does the clause mention any state tax or a state exemption?
4. **Check for a disclaimer.** Does the survivor have the option to refuse some assets?
5. **Compare to today.** Add up your combined estate and compare it to the federal $15,000,000 and your state's threshold.

If the formula refers only to the federal number, ask your attorney to run the first-death numbers for your actual estate and tell you what would happen. Do not rely on a software estimate.

## Does this matter in New Jersey?

Less so. New Jersey has no estate tax, and its inheritance tax exempts spouses and children (see [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax)). A New Jersey couple with a federal formula may still end up with the survivor locked out of money. For couples with children from a prior marriage, a bypass or marital trust can be a deliberate choice. Our page on [joint living trusts for married couples](/learn/trusts/joint-living-trust-for-married-couples) covers how those documents are built.

## Pecuniary or fractional: what is the difference?

Formula clauses come in two shapes, and the difference matters at funding time. A **pecuniary** formula gives the bypass trust a dollar amount that the formula calculates. A **fractional** formula gives it a share, say 40%, of the estate. With a pecuniary gift, if the assets used to fund it have risen in value since the death, the estate can owe income tax on the gain. A fractional share avoids that, because each asset is divided in kind. Neither is wrong. Your attorney picks one depending on how the assets are held, and you should know which yours is. Our page on [credit shelter and other trust names](/learn/trusts/trust-names-explained-dynasty-credit-shelter-family-and-more) describes the trust that receives the money.

## What should you do now?

If the surviving spouse is already alone, the formula has probably done its work, and the question is whether the trust was funded the right way. If both spouses are alive, a trust review is worth a meeting, especially when:

- the trust was signed before 2013,
- the combined estate is over your state's exemption, even if far under $15,000,000, or
- there are children from earlier marriages.
