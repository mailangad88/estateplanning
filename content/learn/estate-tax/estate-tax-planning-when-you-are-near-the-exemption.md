Very few estates owe federal estate tax. That is true, and it also leaves out a group of readers: people whose house has doubled, whose life insurance is larger than they remember, or who live in a state with a much lower threshold. If your total is somewhere in the neighborhood of the exemption, a short list of choices can matter a great deal.

This article helps you place yourself. It covers how to total your estate, the question of whether a lower state limit applies, and what planning looks like at each distance from the line. For definitions, see our [guide to estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## What are the numbers in 2026?

The federal estate and gift tax exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. A married couple can shelter $30 million if the survivor elects portability. Amounts above the exclusion are taxed at a top rate of 40%. The annual gift exclusion is $19,000 per recipient. After 2026 the exclusion is adjusted for inflation and has no scheduled sunset.

The [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) article covers the mechanics. This page is about how close you are.

## How far from the line are you?

Add up what you own and what you control at death, not only what is in your name.

1. Bank, brokerage, and retirement accounts at their full balance.
2. Real estate, at market value, less the mortgage.
3. Business interests, valued as a buyer would value them.
4. Life insurance on your own life, if you own the policy or hold any control.
5. Property in trusts you can revoke or control.
6. Taxable gifts you made earlier, which are counted against the exclusion.

Many people are surprised by item four. A $2 million policy that is yours counts at face value. Our article on [irrevocable life insurance trusts](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) explains how the same policy can be held outside the estate.

| Estate size vs. exclusion | Typical posture |
|---|---|
| Under about half the exemption | Wills, beneficiary forms, powers of attorney; no tax strategy |
| Half to three quarters | Watch growth; use annual exclusion gifts; review every few years |
| Three quarters to the limit | Model the future; consider insurance trusts, charitable plans |
| At or over the limit | Active planning with an attorney and a tax adviser |

## Why does growth matter so much?

Because the number you have today is not the number your estate has at death. A couple in their sixties with $9 million of investments and a house can see that figure pass $15 million in twenty years, even before inflation indexing. A business can jump in value in a single sale offer.

### Example: Raj and Meera

Raj, 61, and Meera, 59, own a home worth $1.4 million, retirement accounts of $3.1 million, and a consulting company that a buyer has just valued at $4 million. Their total is above $8 million, comfortably under the combined $30 million. Planning for the federal tax looks unnecessary. But they live in a state with a much lower threshold, and a sale would add proceeds. They kept the review in their calendar, bought no unusual products, and updated their beneficiary forms. When the sale closed, they met their attorney again, and the state numbers became the real question.

The lesson is that the question is not binary. It is a distance, and the distance moves.

## Does a state tax change the answer?

Often. A number of states levy their own estate tax, and a few levy an inheritance tax paid by the heirs. Thresholds vary and many are far below $15 million. Because state tax is not covered by the federal exemption, an estate can owe state tax and no federal tax at all.

Two practical points. First, check your state's rules directly, starting with our article on [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes), and the [estate tax versus inheritance tax](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-vs-inheritance-tax) comparison. Second, if you own real estate in another state, that state can tax the property even if you do not live there.

## Does moving to another state help?

It can, if the move is real. Changing your domicile to a state with no estate or inheritance tax can remove a state tax bill, but only if you actually change where you live, vote, hold a license, and spend your time, and not merely own a second home. States examine these facts closely when a large estate is involved. Moving at the end of life, for tax reasons alone, tends to draw questions. Treat the move as a life decision first.

## What are the main moves for a close estate?

Families near the line generally look at a few tools in about this order.

1. **Use the annual exclusion every year.** $19,000 per recipient, more with a spouse. It adds up quietly over decades.
2. **Pay tuition and medical bills directly.** Direct payments to the school or provider do not count as gifts.
3. **Move life insurance out of the estate.** An irrevocable trust owning the policy keeps proceeds out of the taxable total.
4. **Plan charitable giving.** A bequest to charity is deductible in the estate, and tools like [donor-advised funds](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds) or a [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust) can shape the timing.
5. **Make larger lifetime gifts of assets expected to grow.** The gain after the gift stays outside the estate. This costs the step-up in basis, so use it for the right assets.
6. **Consider portability or a bypass trust.** For married couples, see [bypass trust vs. portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability) for how they differ and when each makes sense.

The full menu is in [how to reduce estate taxes](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-reduce-estate-taxes). Retirees weighing these choices alongside income needs will find more in our guide to [estate planning for retirees](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-retirees).

## What should you avoid?

- **Giving away too much too soon.** Gifts are hard to undo, can leave you short in later life, and can affect Medicaid eligibility if care is ever needed.
- **Giving up the step-up unnecessarily.** For a family well under the exemption, the capital gains cost of a gift can exceed any estate tax saved. Our article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) shows how.
- **Relying on a number that may change.** The exemption could be raised or cut. A plan that only works at one number is brittle.
- **Skipping the basics.** A tax plan with outdated beneficiary forms is not a plan.

## When can you relax?

If your total estate, with every item counted, is far below the federal exemption and your state has no estate or inheritance tax, estate tax is not your problem. Your job is simpler: a will or trust, powers of attorney, healthcare directives, and current beneficiary designations. Check back when something big changes, such as an inheritance, a sale, or a move.

## How state law changes this

The state layer decides how seriously you should take all of this. The thresholds, rates, and filing dates differ, and some states tax lifetime gifts differently. Start with your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois) as a model and confirm the current rules with an attorney in your state.
