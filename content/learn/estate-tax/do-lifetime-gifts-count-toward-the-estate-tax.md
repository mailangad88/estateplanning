Gifts made while you are alive feel like they leave the estate for good. For most gifts that is roughly true, but the full answer has several moving parts. Some gifts are tracked, some are added back, and some never really leave.

This article explains how the federal system counts gifts when you die, how the annual exclusion and the lifetime exclusion fit together, and which gifts the IRS pulls back into your estate. For the basics on giving, start with our [gift tax annual exclusion article](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion), and for the whole picture see our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## The short version

- Small gifts disappear from the tax system.
- Large gifts are remembered and subtracted from your $15,000,000 exclusion (2026).
- The estate tax uses a single combined exclusion for lifetime and death transfers.
- Growth after a gift is the real prize.
- Gifts where you keep control do not escape.

## How does the system count lifetime gifts?

Federal estate and gift tax are one system with one exclusion. In 2026 each person has $15,000,000, which can be used on gifts during life, on the estate at death, or on both. Think of it as a bank account. Every taxable gift draws it down, and what is left shelters the estate.

Two kinds of gift sit on either side of a line.

- **Within the annual exclusion.** You may give $19,000 to any number of people in 2026 without filing a return and without touching the account. These gifts are not counted at death.
- **Above the annual exclusion.** The excess is a taxable gift, reported on Form 709. No tax is owed now, but the amount is subtracted from your $15,000,000.

At death, the executor adds your lifetime taxable gifts to the estate when computing the tax, then subtracts the exclusion you already used. The result is that the same dollars are not taxed twice.

### Example: Henry's two children

Henry, 72, gives each of his two sons $119,000 in 2026. Each gift is $100,000 above the annual exclusion, and that $100,000 is the taxable part. He files Form 709 and the $200,000 total reduces his exclusion to $14,800,000. At his death years later, the $200,000 is added back to his estate figure, but the remaining exclusion is already smaller, so the net effect is the same as if he had never counted it at all.

## So why give at all?

Because of what happens after the gift. The estate tax counts the gift at its value on the day you made it, not at its value on the day you die. If Henry gave $100,000 of stock that grew to $400,000, only the original $100,000 is counted. The $300,000 of growth escaped.

That is why people with assets expected to rise sharply, such as a business, a rental house, or a growth stock position, look at lifetime gifts. Note the trade: property given away keeps your original tax basis, so the recipient loses the step-up. For appreciated assets, weigh estate tax saved against capital gains created. Our article on [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies) lays the choices side by side.

## What is a clawback, and is it a real risk?

The fear is this. Suppose you use $12 million of exclusion on gifts in a year when the exclusion is high, and a later law lowers it to a smaller number. Could your estate be taxed as though some of the gifts had never been sheltered?

Federal regulations answer that question: gifts that were sheltered when made are not retaxed because the exclusion later dropped. That is the "no clawback" rule. It is a regulation, which Congress could in theory override, and the 2025 tax law left the exclusion with no scheduled sunset anyway. Nobody can promise how a future law will treat old gifts. Families who gift to use the exemption should keep records and ask their attorney about the current rule.

## Which gifts get pulled back into the estate?

Some transfers do not really leave, so the law brings them back.

1. **Gifts where you keep the use or income.** A house you give to your children but keep living in for free is pulled back. A [qualified personal residence trust](/learn/estate-tax/qualified-personal-residence-trust-qprt) is the structured way to do something similar.
2. **Gifts you can revoke or control.** An incomplete gift, or a revocable trust, stays in your estate.
3. **Life insurance transferred within three years of death.** If you give away a policy and die within three years, the proceeds are generally included. An [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) formed early avoids this.
4. **Gift tax paid within three years of death.** Any gift tax paid on gifts in that window is added back.
5. **529 front-loading.** If you elect to spread a large 529 contribution over five years and die early, the unspread part comes back.

## What about deathbed gifts?

A gift made shortly before death is legal, and a completed gift still leaves the estate. Two cautions apply. First, gifts of appreciated assets forfeit the step-up in basis, which would have been free at death. A parent who gives a house with huge built-in gain just before dying has usually made the heirs' tax situation worse. Second, if the parent is receiving Medicaid or may apply, the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) in most states is 60 months, and a transfer can cause a penalty.

A better use of a late-life moment may be to give within the annual exclusion, or to pay tuition and medical bills directly to the school or provider, which never count as gifts.

## Does the order of gifts and bequests matter?

Slightly. Your executor applies the exclusion to the estate after subtracting gifts already counted, so the gifts you made in life are the ones that used it first. If you gave away $3 million of taxable gifts and then died with an estate of $13 million, the combined $16 million is over the $15,000,000 line and the excess is taxed at up to 40%. A married couple can often shelter more, as our article on [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) explains.

That example is not meant to alarm anyone. Most households are nowhere near these numbers. The point is that a large lifetime gift is not free just because no check went to the IRS in the year you made it. It shows up in the final calculation, and the person who finishes the return, usually your executor, needs the records to prove what you used.

## What should you do next?

1. List the gifts you have made over $19,000 per person in any year.
2. Confirm you filed Form 709 for each one.
3. Keep a running total of exclusion used.
4. Before another large gift, ask whether the asset will grow, and whether the step-up matters.
5. Tell your executor where the records are.

## How state law changes this

Some states tax estates at much lower levels than the federal system, and a few add back lifetime gifts made shortly before death. Others have no gift tax of their own but do count gifts in the estate calculation. Our article on [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) explains the variation, and your state guide, such as [New York](/estate-planning/new-york), has the details.
