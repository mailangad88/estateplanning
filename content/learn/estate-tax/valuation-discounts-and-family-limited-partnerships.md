You may have heard that wealthy families put assets into a family limited partnership and then give away pieces at a steep markdown. A $1,000,000 portfolio goes in. A $200,000 share comes out at a lower number. The tax bill shrinks. That story has some truth in it, and a lot of exaggeration.

This page explains how valuation discounts actually work, when they survive an audit, when they do not, and why many families no longer need them. For the wider subject of how the IRS values property at death, see [how assets are valued for estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/how-assets-are-valued-for-estate-tax). The rest of the tax is in our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## What is a valuation discount?

It is a reduction in the appraised value of an ownership interest compared with a simple share of the underlying assets. The tax law values property at what a willing buyer would pay a willing seller. A small piece of a private entity is worth less than its slice of the total, for two reasons.

- **Lack of control.** A minority owner cannot decide when to sell, borrow or distribute.
- **Lack of marketability.** A share in a closely held entity has no ready buyer. Selling takes time and costs money.

Appraisers combine these into a percentage reduction. The percentage depends on the facts of the entity and the appraiser's analysis, not on a published table, so be skeptical of anyone who promises a number in advance.

## How does a family limited partnership work?

A family limited partnership, or FLP, is a partnership owned by relatives. A typical setup runs like this:

1. A parent and sometimes the children form the entity and sign a partnership agreement.
2. The parent transfers assets in, such as rental property, a business interest or an investment portfolio, and receives general and limited partner interests in return.
3. The parent keeps a small general partner interest, which controls management, and gives limited partner interests to children or trusts over time.
4. Each gift is reported at the appraised, discounted value.

The point is that a gift of limited interests uses less of the parent's lifetime exclusion than a gift of the same assets directly. Our article on [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies) compares this with simpler gifts, and the guide to the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) explains why partial interests help here as well.

| Direct gift of $100,000 of stock | Gift of a limited partnership interest holding $100,000 of stock |
| --- | --- |
| Reported at $100,000 | Reported at the appraised value, which can be lower |
| Simple to value | Needs a qualified appraisal |
| No entity upkeep | Needs tax returns, an account and records |
| Recipient owns the shares | Recipient owns a restricted interest |

## Where do the IRS and the courts push back?

Not every discount is allowed. The main problems are these.

**Retained control and benefit.** If you form the entity, transfer nearly everything you own, and keep running it as if it were your personal account, the IRS can argue that you kept the enjoyment of the assets. In that case the whole value is included in your estate, with no discount. The classic bad facts: a parent puts the family home in the partnership and keeps living there for free, or the partnership pays the parent's bills.

**No business purpose.** A partnership with only a brokerage account in it and no reason to exist besides tax savings invites challenge. Real reasons include centralized management of rental property, keeping a family farm together, or protecting assets from a child's divorce.

**Deathbed formation.** Setting one up when death is near looks like a tax move and not a plan.

**Weak records.** Partners who never meet, mix partnership money with personal money or ignore the agreement make the entity look like a sham.

**Rule changes.** Treasury proposed regulations in 2016 that would have limited discounts for family-controlled entities. They were withdrawn in 2017, so the older rules still apply. Congress and the Treasury have shown interest in this area before, which is one more reason to plan on facts that would hold up under a stricter rule.

## What about the step-up in basis?

This is the part that surprises people. A discount lowers the value for tax purposes at your death too. Your heirs' basis in inherited property equals the estate tax value. If the interest is valued at $700,000 on the estate tax return but the underlying property is worth $1,000,000, the heirs may start with a lower basis than a plain inheritance would have given them. Our article on the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains the rule.

For a family far below the exemption, that tradeoff is a bad deal. A discount saves no estate tax when none would be owed, and it can raise income tax on a later sale. This is one reason the strategy has lost ground since the exclusion reached $15,000,000 in 2026.

## Who should still consider it?

An FLP or LLC with discounts still fits a few situations. Look for these signs.

- The estate is large enough to approach or pass the exclusion.
- The main asset is real estate, farmland or a closely held business that needs central management.
- The owner plans to give interests over many years.
- The family will actually run the entity as a business.

For business owners the gifts often work alongside a buy-sell agreement or a sale. See [gifting business interests to children](/learn/business-owners/gifting-business-interests-to-children) and [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).

## An example that holds up, and one that does not

Marcus owns six rental houses in Aurora, Illinois, worth $4,200,000 together. He forms an LLC that owns and manages them. The LLC has its own bank account, a written operating agreement, annual meetings noted in writing and a property manager. Over six years he gives members' interests to his two daughters and files a gift tax return each time, with a qualified appraisal attached. Marcus pays market rent on the one unit where he keeps an office.

Contrast that with Linda, who moves her home and her savings account into a partnership two months before surgery, keeps living in the house rent-free, and pays her dentist from the partnership account. If she dies, an examiner will likely say she never gave up the assets at all.

## How much does this cost to set up?

More than most people expect, and the cost does not stop at formation. There are legal fees to draft the agreement, an appraisal for each gift, a partnership tax return every year, and usually an accountant. For a small estate the yearly cost can exceed any tax saved. Ask for a written estimate of both the setup and the annual upkeep before you commit, and compare it with what a simpler gift would save. Our article on [use your exemption now or wait](/learn/estate-tax/use-your-estate-tax-exemption-now-or-wait) frames the larger question of whether to give at all.

## What steps protect the discount?

1. Have a business reason and write it down.
2. Keep out of the entity anything you will need for living expenses.
3. Run the entity separately: its own account, its own books and tax return, its own deposits.
4. Hire a credentialed appraiser and obtain a written report for each gift.
5. File Form 709 with enough detail to count as adequate disclosure. See [Form 709 and gift splitting](/learn/estate-tax/form-709-gift-tax-return-gift-splitting-and-net-gifts).
6. Review the plan when the law, the family or the assets change.
