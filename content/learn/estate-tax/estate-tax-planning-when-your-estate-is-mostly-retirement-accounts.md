Some people reach retirement with a modest house and a very large 401(k). Their wealth is real, but it sits in one kind of account, and that account behaves differently from everything else they own. At death it is counted for the estate tax. Then the same dollars are taxed again as income to the person who inherits them.

This page is for families whose estate is mostly pre-tax retirement money. It explains how the two taxes stack, who pays what, and the planning moves that make sense. For the broader tax, see our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## Do retirement accounts count toward the estate tax?

Yes, at full value. The gross estate includes every account you own at death, whether or not a will controls it. A beneficiary form moves the money outside of probate, not outside of the estate tax calculation. Our article on [what counts toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-counts-toward-the-estate-tax) lists the categories, and the guide to [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains why the two lists differ.

Most families will not owe federal estate tax at all. The basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. But a retired executive with a $9 million 401(k), a $4 million house and $3 million in other savings has a $16 million estate. The excess is taxed at up to 40 percent, even though a big part of that wealth has never been taxed as income.

## Why is this the "double tax" problem?

Pre-tax accounts hold money no one has paid income tax on yet. When you die, three things happen at once:

- The account counts in your taxable estate.
- There is no step-up in basis, as there is for a house or stock. The heir does not get a reset to date-of-death value.
- The heir pays ordinary income tax on each withdrawal.

Take Walter, who leaves a $3 million IRA to his daughter Priya. If his estate is under the exclusion, there is no estate tax, and Priya pays income tax only. If his estate is over, both taxes apply to the same money. Priya may be able to claim a deduction for the estate tax attributable to the IRA, which softens the second hit, but she still pays income tax on top.

The 10-year rule makes timing harder. Most non-spouse heirs, including adult children, must empty an inherited account within 10 years of the owner's death. Pull it out evenly and the heir may sit in a high bracket for a decade. Some heirs, called eligible designated beneficiaries, have other options. These include a surviving spouse, a minor child of the owner until majority, a disabled or chronically ill person, and someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner. Our article on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) covers each, and the guide to [taxes on inherited IRAs and 401(k)s](/learn/after-a-death/tax-on-inherited-ira-401k-annuity-and-savings-bonds) shows how withdrawals are taxed.

## Which planning moves help most?

| Move | What it does | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name your spouse | Marital deduction removes the account from the first estate; spouse can roll over | Tax returns at the second death |
| Leave the account to charity | No income tax for the charity, and an estate tax charitable deduction | The family must receive other assets in its place |
| Convert part to a Roth | You pay income tax now, from outside money, and shrink the estate | Conversion raises current taxes; bracket planning matters |
| Spend from the IRA first | Drawing the account down reduces both taxes | Higher income taxes while you live |
| Give other assets, keep the IRA for charity | Uses the exclusion on assets that get no extra income tax cost | Lifetime gifts have no step-up |
| Buy life insurance in a trust | Creates tax-free cash to pay estate tax or equalize shares | Premium cost; see the three-year rule |

### Leave the IRA to charity, leave other assets to family

If you plan to leave anything to charity, this is the most efficient way. A charity does not pay income tax on an IRA it receives, but your children would. They would also get a step-up on a house or stocks that you leave them, so the swap helps both ways. A qualified charitable distribution during life, available from age 70 and a half, also moves money from an IRA to charity without income. See [donor-advised funds and QCDs](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds) and [charitable gifts and bequests](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-gifts-and-bequests-gift-tax-estate-tax-and-income-tax).

### Convert to a Roth in the years you control

A Roth conversion means paying income tax now so the account can grow and be inherited income tax free. For a taxpayer whose estate will exceed the exclusion, there is a second benefit. The income tax paid from other savings leaves the estate, and that payment is not a taxable gift. The cost is a bigger tax bill this year, so it works best in low-income years such as the gap between retiring and starting Social Security.

### Plan around the beneficiary list

Review each form at least every few years. A beneficiary designation overrides your will, so a stale form can undo a careful plan. Use the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) to check every account. If you consider naming a trust, read about [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) first, since the drafting has to match the tax rules.

## What if the estate cannot pay the tax in cash?

An estate made mostly of retirement accounts is often short on cash. The executor may need to withdraw from the IRA to pay estate tax, which adds income tax on the withdrawal. Life insurance owned by an irrevocable trust can solve this, as described in our article on the [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust). The IRS can also allow payment over time in some cases. See [what if the estate cannot pay the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-if-the-estate-cannot-pay-the-estate-tax).

## How do state taxes change this?

State rules add another layer. Illinois taxes estates above $4 million, so a $5 million estate that is mostly retirement money can owe Illinois estate tax with no federal tax at all. See the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax). New Jersey has no estate tax but has an inheritance tax. Retirement accounts left to a sibling or a nephew can be taxed there, while a spouse, child or grandchild is exempt. The article on the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) shows how the classes work. The [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) gives a quick read.

## A simple way to think about it

Ask three questions in order.

1. **Will the estate tax apply to me?** Add up all assets, including the accounts. If the total is well under $15 million, set the estate tax aside.
2. **Who pays income tax on the account?** If the heir is in a high bracket, think about a Roth conversion, a charity or splitting the account across several heirs.
3. **Who should get which asset?** Match the account to the person who will pay the least tax on it. Charity first, children's brackets second, spouse at the end.
