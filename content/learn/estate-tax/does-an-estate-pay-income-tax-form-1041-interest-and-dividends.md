Tom died in April. His daughter Lena is the executor, and the estate bank account has been earning interest while she sorts out the house. A brokerage account is paying dividends. Her cousin told her, "Estates don't pay tax unless they're rich." That is true of the federal estate tax. It is not true of income tax, which is the one most estates actually owe.

This page explains the estate's income tax return, Form 1041, for people settling an estate. It is separate from the [federal estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption), which applies above $15,000,000 per person in 2026. It is part of our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## Does an estate pay income tax?

Yes, when the estate earns income. At death, the person's own tax life ends and the estate begins as a new taxpayer with its own identification number. Anything the estate earns after that date, such as interest, dividends, rent, business profit or gain on a sale, is the estate's income.

There are three tax filings people mix up:

| Return | Whose income | Covers | Due |
|---|---|---|---|
| Final Form 1040 | The person who died | January 1 to the date of death | April of the year after death |
| Form 1041 | The estate | Income earned after death | 15th day of the fourth month after the estate's tax year ends |
| Form 706 | The estate | Estate tax on the value of everything owned | Nine months after death |

Our page on [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) walks through the first one. This page is about the second.

## When must an estate file Form 1041?

The executor files Form 1041 when the estate has gross income of $600 or more in a tax year, or when any beneficiary is a nonresident alien, whatever the income. Gross income means the income before deductions. If Tom's estate earns $450 of interest and nothing else, no return is required, though some executors file anyway to claim a refund or close the books.

You need an employer identification number first. The executor requests one from the IRS and files Form 56 to tell the IRS who is acting. The steps are in our article on [IRS paperwork for an executor](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810). Banks will not retitle an account in the estate's name without the number, so most executors get it in the first weeks.

## Which year-end should the estate pick?

An estate can choose a calendar year or a fiscal year ending on the last day of any month within 12 months of death. Trusts that are not estates generally cannot, though a trust that elects to be treated as part of the estate can.

Here is why it matters. Tom died April 12. A fiscal year ending March 31 gives Lena a first return for 11.5 months, due July 15 of the next year. A calendar-year choice gives a short first return due the following April, and then a full second year. The fiscal year can push the first tax bill back and often reduces how many returns are filed. It also gives Lena a longer period to make distributions that shift income to heirs.

The choice is made on the first Form 1041, so it should be settled with a tax preparer before the first filing, not after.

## How does the K-1 move the tax to the heirs?

This is the core idea, and it is why most estates pay less than they fear. Income the estate distributes to beneficiaries for the year is deducted by the estate and reported by the beneficiary instead, on Schedule K-1. The estate pays tax only on income it keeps.

Example. In its first year, Tom's estate earns $9,000: $6,200 of interest and $2,800 of dividends. The estate pays $4,000 in legal and accounting fees. Lena distributes $3,000 of cash to her brother Mike in December.

1. Start with the $9,000 of income.
2. Subtract the $4,000 of deductible administration costs, leaving $5,000.
3. The estate takes a distribution deduction of up to the $3,000 it paid out, limited by the income that was available.
4. The estate owes tax on the rest, about $2,000, subject to a small exemption.
5. Mike receives a K-1 reporting his $3,000 share by type of income and reports it on his own return.

The estate's income tax brackets reach the top rate at a much lower income level than an individual's brackets. This is why executors are often told to distribute income rather than let it pile up. It does not change if the will says to hold the money, because the tax follows the actual payments in the year. Your tax preparer applies the exact rates and the small estate exemption.

## Is a gift or inheritance itself income to the heirs?

No. Cash, a house, stocks and other property you inherit are generally not income to you. The estate tax, if any, was the tax on the transfer. Our article on [taxes on an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) covers the exceptions, and the inherited retirement account case is covered in the page on [inherited IRAs, 401(k)s and annuities](/learn/after-a-death/tax-on-inherited-ira-401k-annuity-and-savings-bonds).

What heirs do pay tax on is income from the inherited asset after they get it, and income the estate passes to them through the K-1. If Lena sells Tom's stock for more than its value on the date of death, only the increase after death is a gain, because of the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). The estate usually reports that gain on Form 1041 if it sells before distributing.

## What can the estate deduct?

Ordinary and necessary costs of running the estate: attorney, accountant and appraisal fees, executor compensation, bond premiums, and some property taxes and insurance on estate property. If there is also an estate tax return, administration costs can go on either return, but not both. Funeral costs are not an income tax deduction. See [what the estate can deduct before estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-the-estate-can-deduct-before-estate-tax) for the estate tax side.

When the estate closes, any unused deductions and losses can pass out to the beneficiaries on the final year's K-1. That is a reason to plan the last year of the estate carefully rather than closing the account on the first day it can be done.

## Do Illinois and New Jersey require their own return?

Yes, when the estate has income that is taxable in the state. Illinois estates use Form IL-1041 and New Jersey estates use Form NJ-1041. Both follow the federal return closely, but the filing thresholds and the treatment of out-of-state beneficiaries differ, so check the current instructions or ask your preparer. The final individual returns are different forms. We cover them in [filing a final Illinois return](/learn/illinois/final-illinois-income-tax-return-for-someone-who-died) and [a final New Jersey return](/learn/new-jersey/final-new-jersey-income-tax-return-for-someone-who-died). New Jersey also has its own inheritance tax, which is unrelated to income; see the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

## Common mistakes with the estate's income tax

- Using Tom's Social Security number on the estate's bank account after death. Interest is then reported to the wrong taxpayer, and the estate's filing is a mess.
- Missing the first return because the estate "has no value." Income counts, not value.
- Paying heirs right after the year ends and expecting the K-1 to follow the check. An election lets distributions in the first 65 days of the next year count for the prior year, but it has to be made on the return, so ask first.
- Letting income pile up in the estate account because the heirs "do not need it."
- Closing the estate in December and losing the chance to pass out deductions.
