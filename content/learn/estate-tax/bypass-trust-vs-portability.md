Married couples with a decent amount of money get two pieces of advice that sound opposed. One adviser says to set up a bypass trust. Another says portability makes that old technique unnecessary. Both are partly right, and the answer for your family depends on where you live, how much you own, and what you want to happen after the first of you dies.

This comparison explains how each works and when each is the better choice. The basic rules are in our [guide to estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax), and portability has its own article, [estate tax portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples).

## What is the problem both tools solve?

Leaving everything to a spouse who is a U.S. citizen is free of estate tax because of the unlimited marital deduction. The cost is that the first spouse's own $15,000,000 exclusion (2026) goes unused. At the survivor's death everything is stacked into one estate, which may exceed what one exclusion covers.

Portability and a bypass trust are two ways to rescue that wasted exclusion.

## How does a bypass trust work?

A bypass trust is created under the first spouse's will or revocable living trust. When that spouse dies, assets up to a chosen amount, often the full exclusion, go into the trust rather than to the survivor. The survivor can usually receive income and use principal for health, education, support and maintenance, and can serve as a trustee in a limited role. The rest of the estate goes to the survivor, tax free.

When the survivor dies, the trust assets "bypass" the survivor's estate and pass to the children or other beneficiaries. They were never owned by the survivor for tax purposes, so any growth on them, including a house that appreciates or a stock portfolio that doubles, escapes estate tax too.

The trust is irrevocable once the first spouse dies. That means the surviving spouse cannot reclaim it or rewrite who gets it.

## How does portability work?

Under portability, the survivor receives everything outright, and the executor files Form 706 to elect to carry over the unused exclusion, called the DSUE. The survivor then has up to $30 million in 2026. There is no trust, no trustee and no separate tax account. Our article on [Form 706](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) covers the filing rules and the deadline.

## How do they compare?

| Question | Bypass trust | Portability |
|---|---|---|
| Set up before death? | Yes, in a will or living trust | No documents needed, only the election after death |
| Survivor's control over assets | Limited by trust terms | Full ownership |
| Growth after the first death | Outside survivor's estate | Counts in survivor's estate; DSUE does not grow |
| Basis step-up at second death | No, for trust assets | Yes, for everything the survivor owns |
| State estate tax exemption | Can be used | Usually lost |
| GST exemption of first spouse | Can be preserved | Not portable |
| Protects first spouse's children | Yes | No |
| Costs | Drafting plus trust administration, tax returns for the trust | Cost of the Form 706 |
| Flexibility if laws change | Can be built with discretion | Depends on rule at the time |

The basis row deserves a second look. Assets in a bypass trust miss the second step-up in basis, so a house worth $1 million that cost $300,000 may carry a taxable gain when the children sell. In a couple that is under the exclusion, that cost can be bigger than the estate tax saved, which is why simple plans often lean on portability. Read [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) if you want the arithmetic.

## When does a bypass trust make sense?

- **You live in a state with its own estate tax.** Most such states do not honor portability, so the first spouse's state exemption disappears without a trust. If your state's threshold is $2 million and your estate is $4 million, that matters. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).
- **Your estate is well above $15,000,000.** A frozen DSUE leaves less room for growth. A trust holds its growth outside the survivor's estate.
- **It is a second marriage.** A trust can make sure that the first spouse's children inherit, which portability cannot do. A QTIP trust is a close relative; see [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts).
- **You worry about the survivor's choices.** Remarriage, creditors, a lawsuit or early dementia can all redirect outright inheritance. A trust adds a guard rail.
- **You want to use the generation-skipping exemption.** The first spouse's GST exemption is lost unless allocated to a trust.

## When is portability the better choice?

- **Your combined estate is comfortably under $15,000,000 and your state has no estate tax.** Neither spouse's estate is likely to owe federal tax, and the trust would only add complexity.
- **You have mostly retirement accounts and a house.** Retirement accounts do not mix easily with trusts, and the house gets a clean step-up if the survivor owns it.
- **Both spouses want the survivor to have complete freedom.** A trust that restricts the survivor creates friction.
- **The estate is mostly the survivor's own.** There may be little in the first spouse's name to fund a trust.

## Can we keep both options open?

Yes, and this is how many modern plans are written. The usual design leaves everything to the survivor, but gives the survivor the right to disclaim, which means decline, part of the inheritance within nine months. The disclaimed share falls into a trust. The survivor looks at actual values, the current law and the state tax position, then decides. A portability election goes in as a backup in any case.

A plan with a mandatory trust, by contrast, locks in the choice in advance and can be a poor fit if the law has changed by the time of death. Mandatory trusts can also be set up with a formula tied to the exclusion, which sometimes led to unwanted results in the past, such as an enormous share of a small estate going into trust when the exclusion rose. A good drafter will address that.

## An example

Hannah and Craig, both 64, live in a state that taxes estates above $2 million. They have $5.5 million, mostly the house and investments. Under a simple plan with everything going to the survivor and a portability election, there would be no federal tax at either death, but the state tax could take a bite out of the survivor's estate of $5.5 million.

With a bypass trust funded to the state exemption amount, the first death uses Craig's state exemption, so about $2 million sits outside Hannah's estate. If Hannah lives 20 more years and that portion grows to $4 million, the growth is also outside. The trade is that Hannah has limited control over the trust and the children may face capital gains tax on a sale of low-basis trust assets. Hannah and Craig decide it is worth it, and they use a disclaimer design so Hannah can decide at the time.

## What should we do next?

1. Add up your assets together and separately, including life insurance and retirement accounts.
2. Find out whether your state has an estate tax and whether it allows portability.
3. Decide how much control you want the survivor to have.
4. Look at your trust and will to see whether they contain a bypass trust or a disclaimer.
5. Review after major changes, such as a move, a sale of a business or a remarriage. Our guide to [estate planning after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies) covers the steps for a survivor.
