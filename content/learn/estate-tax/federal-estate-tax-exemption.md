If you have heard that "the estate tax is coming for everyone," here is the reassuring version: it almost never is. As of 2026 the federal government taxes only estates worth more than $15,000,000 per person. For a couple, that is $30 million.

This article explains how the number works, what is counted, how the tax is calculated, and why planning can still make sense below the threshold. For the whole topic, start with our guide to [estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## What is the federal estate tax exemption?

The federal estate and gift tax basic exclusion amount is the total value you can give away, during life and at death, before federal transfer tax applies. For 2026 it is $15,000,000 per person. The 2025 tax law set that figure, and it will be adjusted for inflation each year after 2026. There is no scheduled sunset under current law. Congress can still amend it, so confirm the latest figure at irs.gov.

Estate tax and gift tax share the same exclusion. If you make large taxable gifts during life and report them, that amount is used up and reduces what is left at death. Our article on the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) covers which gifts count.

## Who actually owes federal estate tax?

Only people whose combined taxable gifts and estate exceed the exclusion. Here is how that looks.

| Estate value at death | Federal estate tax (2026, single person) |
|---|---|
| $2 million | None |
| $8 million | None |
| $15 million | None |
| $17 million | Tax applies to the $2 million over the exclusion, at up to 40% |

A $17 million estate would owe federal tax on the $2 million above the exclusion, so roughly $800,000 at the 40% marginal rate (subject to deductions and the exact rate schedule). The tax is paid by the estate, usually within nine months of death, and not by the individual heirs.

## What counts as part of my estate for tax purposes?

The "gross estate" is broader than the probate estate. It generally includes:

- Real estate, including your home and property in other states
- Bank and brokerage accounts, including POD and TOD accounts
- Retirement accounts such as IRAs and 401(k)s
- Life insurance on your life if you owned the policy or could change the beneficiary
- Business interests and partnership shares
- Assets in a revocable trust
- Your share of jointly owned property (for spouses, generally half)
- Certain gifts where you kept control or the right to the income

Assets you gave away with no retained control, and amounts held in a properly structured [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts), are generally not part of the gross estate, though large gifts use up part of the exclusion.

A common surprise is that a life insurance policy you own is part of the estate. Someone with a $1 million policy, a $3 million house, and retirement accounts can feel closer to a taxable estate than they are. Our article on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) covers ownership issues.

## How is the tax calculated?

1. **Add up the gross estate.** The executor values every asset as of the date of death. In some cases the executor can choose an alternate valuation date six months later.
2. **Subtract deductions.** These include funeral and administration expenses, debts, charitable gifts, and the unlimited marital deduction for assets passing to a U.S. citizen spouse.
3. **Add adjusted taxable gifts.** Taxable gifts made during life are added back to the calculation.
4. **Apply the tax rates.** The rates are graduated and reach 40% on the portion above the top bracket.
5. **Subtract the unified credit.** The exclusion amount is applied as a credit, which wipes out the tax on the first $15 million.
6. **Pay or file.** The executor files Form 706 and pays any tax due within nine months of death, with extensions available for filing.

### Marital deduction

Assets left to a surviving spouse who is a U.S. citizen generally qualify for the unlimited [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction), so no estate tax is due when the first spouse dies. The tax question then shifts to the surviving spouse's estate. That is why [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) matters: it lets the survivor use the unused part of the first spouse's exclusion.

### Charitable deduction

Gifts to qualified charities at death are deductible with no cap, which is one reason very large estates use charitable giving as a planning tool.

## Do I need to plan if I am under $15 million?

Probably yes, though not for the federal tax. Here are reasons.

**State taxes.** Roughly a dozen states plus D.C. levy their own estate tax at far lower thresholds, and a handful have inheritance taxes. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

**Rules may change.** The exclusion has changed many times, including in 2010, 2013, 2018, and 2025. Families with estates in the $10 to $15 million range often keep an eye on possible legislation.

**Growth.** A $9 million estate invested for 20 years can easily exceed $15 million even with inflation indexing.

**Income tax.** For most families, income tax issues matter more than estate tax. Whether an heir gets a step-up in basis on appreciated property can be worth more than any estate tax savings. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

**Non-tax goals.** Probate avoidance, incapacity planning, protection for children, and care for a family member with special needs are often the real reasons for a plan.

## What planning options exist for larger estates?

- Using the full exclusion at the first spouse's death, either with portability or with a credit shelter trust
- Annual-exclusion gifts and lifetime gifts, which move growth out of the estate
- Irrevocable life insurance trusts to keep policy proceeds out of the taxable estate
- Charitable trusts and donor-advised funds
- Family limited partnerships and valuation discounts for business owners, which need careful professional advice
- Planning for the generation-skipping transfer tax if assets will go to grandchildren; the [GST tax](/glossary/generation-skipping-transfer-tax) has its own exemption

None of these removes tax for free, and several give up control or require tax reporting. Our article on whether a [trust avoids taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes) explains why a basic living trust does not change the estate tax result.

## Example: Ana and Luis

Ana and Luis, both 66, own a home worth $1.2 million, investments of $4 million, and retirement accounts of $3 million, for a combined $8.2 million. That is well under $15 million each. They owe no federal estate tax today. If they live in a state with a $3 million estate tax threshold, though, a state tax could apply. They also care about a clean plan for their son, who has a disability. Their needs are about state tax and protecting their son, not federal tax.

## When is Form 706 required?

An executor must file Form 706 if the gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts is above the exclusion for the year of death, which is $15,000,000 for deaths in 2026. The return is due nine months after the date of death, and a six-month extension to file is available, though any tax is still due at nine months unless a payment extension is granted. Estates owed tax can often pay over time if they hold illiquid assets such as a family business or farm. Even where no filing is required, a return can be useful to elect portability, and it creates an IRS record of date-of-death values that heirs can rely on for basis. Most families who file do so with a CPA and an attorney working together.
