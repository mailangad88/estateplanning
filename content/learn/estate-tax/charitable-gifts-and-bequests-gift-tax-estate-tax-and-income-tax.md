People who want to leave money to a church, a school or a hospital often assume the tax treatment is obvious. It is not, because three different taxes touch a charitable gift: the gift tax, the estate tax and the income tax. Each treats the same dollars differently depending on when and how you give.

This article sorts those out in plain terms, as part of our wider [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax). For the specific vehicles, see our separate guides to [donor-advised funds and QCDs](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds) and the [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust).

## The short version

| Gift | Gift tax | Estate tax | Income tax |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash to a charity while alive | None | Money leaves your estate | Possible deduction |
| Bequest in a will or trust | Not applicable | Deductible, no cap | No personal deduction |
| IRA left to a charity | Not applicable | Deductible | Charity pays no tax on withdrawal |
| Gift to a political organization | Generally none | Leaves estate | Not deductible |
| Donor-advised fund | None | Leaves estate | Possible deduction |

Always verify that the recipient is a qualified charity. Churches, schools and most nonprofits with a determination letter are. A GoFundMe for a friend, or a gift to a family member's trust, is not charity.

## Are gifts to charity subject to gift tax?

No. A completed gift to a qualified charity is exempt, with no dollar limit. It does not use your annual exclusion, which is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, and it does not touch the lifetime exclusion of $15,000,000. Some gifts still call for Form 709, mainly gifts of a partial interest in property, where the charity gets only part of it.

Gifts that look charitable but are not are different. Paying a friend's medical bill directly to the hospital is excluded from the gift tax, but not because of charity. A gift to a relative's trust is a gift to a person, and our article on [taxable gifts](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies) explains when it counts.

### What about political gifts?

Gifts to a political organization are generally exempt from gift tax if the money is for the group's own use. Gifts to a candidate's personal account can be treated like gifts to any person. Neither kind is an income tax deduction. Because the rules are different from charity, ask before giving a large amount.

## How is a bequest treated for estate tax?

A bequest to a qualified charity is deductible from the gross estate with no cap. For most families this does not matter, because the estate is under $15,000,000 and owes nothing. For an estate over the line, a bequest reduces the taxable amount dollar for dollar. At 40%, a $1 million gift saves up to $400,000, so the family receives less but the charity receives more than the family loses.

Example: Dolores, 80, single, has an estate of $17 million. Without charitable gifts, about $2 million of federal tax. She leaves $2 million to her university. The taxable estate drops to $15 million and the tax goes to zero. The family gets $15 million; the university gets $2 million.

Compare the same bequest in an estate under the exclusion. There is no estate tax to save, so the benefit is purely the gift. See [what the estate can deduct](/learn/estate-tax/what-the-estate-can-deduct-before-estate-tax) for how it fits with other deductions.

## Is a bequest deductible on my own tax return?

No. This surprises people. Income tax deductions are for gifts you make while alive. A bequest happens after death, so the deduction is on the estate's side. An estate can sometimes take a deduction on its own income tax return, Form 1041, for income it pays to charity under the will or trust terms. That is a technical point for the executor, covered in [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## Which is better, giving through a will or while alive?

Neither is right for everyone. Think in terms of what each does.

1. **Giving now.** You get the income tax deduction if you itemize, and you see the result. The money is gone. Appreciated stock given directly avoids the capital gain.
2. **Giving at death.** You keep the use of the asset for life. The estate gets the deduction. There is no income tax benefit to you.
3. **Mixing the two.** A donor-advised fund lets you take the deduction this year and spread grants later. A retirement account left to charity avoids income tax that individual heirs would pay.

The IRA is often the best asset to leave to charity, because family members who inherit an IRA pay income tax on withdrawals, while a charity pays none. Leave the house or the brokerage account, which get a step-up, to the family. Our article on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) explains how to name a charity.

## How do I make a bequest properly?

1. Use the charity's exact legal name and its tax ID number.
2. Decide whether the gift is a specific amount, a percentage or an asset.
3. For retirement accounts and life insurance, name the charity on the beneficiary form. A will does not control them.
4. Name a backup in case the charity no longer exists.
5. Tell the charity. Some will help with wording.

## What about state tax?

Most states with their own estate tax allow a charitable deduction, but the rules differ. A state with a low threshold may tax an estate that the federal government does not, and a charitable bequest can reduce it. In a state with an inheritance tax, charities are usually exempt. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes), and check your own state's guide such as [New York](/estate-planning/new-york).

## What mistakes do people make with charitable bequests?

The most common is a vague gift. "To my church" fails when two congregations share a name or the church merged. Use the legal name and tax ID.

The second is putting a charity in the will for an account that passes by beneficiary form. The form wins, so the gift never happens. Our article on [the beneficiary form that overrides your will](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) explains why.

The third is a percentage gift that clashes with the family's expectations. If the will gives 10% to charity and 90% to children, and the estate holds a large IRA, the children may end up paying the income tax on their share while the charity's share is untouched. Decide who receives which assets, and not only how much.

The fourth is forgetting the executor. Someone has to file the estate return, claim the deduction and send the charity its check. Pick an executor who can do that; our article on the [executor's workload](/tools/executor-workload) shows how much it can be.

## When does a charitable trust make sense?

A charitable remainder trust pays income to you or a family member for a time, then leaves the rest to charity, with a partial income tax deduction at funding. It suits someone with a highly appreciated asset who wants income and a gift. It is not for a small estate; the setup and yearly returns have a cost. If you want to keep it simple, a donor-advised fund is usually easier. Compare them in [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust) and [donor-advised funds and QCDs](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds).
