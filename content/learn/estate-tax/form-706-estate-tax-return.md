A few months after a death, the executor often learns there is another return besides the final income tax return. It is called Form 706, the United States Estate (and Generation-Skipping Transfer) Tax Return. For many families it is not required. For a few, missing it is expensive.

This article, part of our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax), explains who has to file, who should file even when they do not have to, what the form asks for, and the dates that matter. The tax itself is covered in our [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) article; here the focus is the paperwork.

## What is Form 706?

Form 706 is the federal return on which an estate reports everything the person owned or controlled at death, subtracts allowable deductions, and calculates whether any estate tax is due. It also is the vehicle for two elections: portability of a deceased spouse's unused exclusion, and, for larger estates, allocation of the generation-skipping transfer tax exemption.

It is filed by the estate, not by the heirs, and it is separate from the decedent's final Form 1040 and from any fiduciary income tax return. For those returns see [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## Who has to file Form 706?

The executor must file when the gross estate, plus adjusted taxable gifts made during life, is larger than the basic exclusion for the year of death. In 2026 that amount is $15,000,000. The filing requirement looks at the total, not just what is left after deductions, so an estate that owes nothing because of the marital deduction can still be required to file if the numbers are large enough.

| Situation | Must file? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Gross estate plus taxable gifts under the exclusion, no portability wanted | No | No federal tax and no election needed |
| Same, but a spouse survives and portability is wanted | Yes, to elect | Portability is made only on a timely, complete return |
| Gross estate plus taxable gifts above the exclusion | Yes | Required even if all passes to a spouse or charity |
| Estate wants to allocate GST exemption to a trust | Yes | Allocation is made on the return |
| Estate is under the federal level but in a state with a lower estate tax | Maybe a state return | State returns have their own thresholds |

Gifts matter here. A person who made $4 million of taxable gifts during life and dies with $12 million has $16 million for filing purposes, which is above $15,000,000 in 2026. Lifetime gifts are reported on Form 709; see the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) article for how those add up.

## Why file when you do not have to?

The main reason is portability. If Elena's husband dies and leaves her the whole estate, no tax is due. But his unused exclusion disappears unless the executor files a timely and complete Form 706 and elects portability. For Elena that can mean the difference between sheltering $15,000,000 and $30 million at her own death. The details, including the remarriage rules, are in our article on [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples).

There are other reasons. Some families file to put a formal value on property such as a business or farm, which can support the heirs' basis later. An executor who is unsure whether the estate is over the limit may file to make the position clear. Filing also starts the clock for the IRS to examine the return, although the clock for portability numbers can stay open longer.

The cost is real. A return with appraisals can run to several thousand dollars in professional fees, and for a modest estate that is worth it only when portability or a large-asset valuation is the reason.

## When is Form 706 due?

Nine months after the date of death. That is the date on which the tax, if any, is also due.

If the executor cannot finish, Form 4768 requests an automatic six-month extension to file. Request it before the nine-month date. The extension covers filing only. Tax unpaid after nine months generally carries interest, and a separate showing is needed for an extension to pay. If the estate owes tax, pay an estimate with Form 4768.

If the nine-month deadline passes and the estate was not required to file, an IRS revenue procedure allows a late portability-only return up to five years after death under certain conditions. It is a safety net, not a plan.

The alternate valuation date is a related choice. The executor may value assets six months after death instead of on the date of death, but only if that lowers both the gross estate and the tax. This option is not available to estates that owe no tax.

## What goes into the return?

Form 706 asks for a lot. The executor gathers:

1. The death certificate and the will or trust documents.
2. Date-of-death statements for bank, brokerage and retirement accounts.
3. Deeds and appraisals for real estate.
4. Valuations for closely held businesses. For help understanding these, see [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).
5. Life insurance statements for policies the person owned or controlled (Form 712 from each insurer).
6. Records of debts, funeral costs and administration expenses, which are deductions.
7. Records of lifetime taxable gifts and prior gift tax returns.
8. Details of property passing to a spouse or charity, which are deductible.
9. For a portability election, the information needed to compute the unused amount.

The gross estate is broader than what passes under the will. It includes assets in a revocable trust, accounts with a beneficiary designation, jointly held property in part, and life insurance the person owned. Our article on [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) sorts these out.

Estates below the filing threshold that file only for portability can often report some assets passing to a spouse or charity by category and good-faith estimate, rather than full appraisals. This relief is limited, and it does not apply when the value is needed for other reasons.

## What are the common mistakes?

Missing the deadline for portability is the most frequent. The second is assuming that because everything goes to the spouse, nothing needs to be filed. The third is valuing a house or business from memory.

Some others:

- Forgetting that retirement accounts and life insurance belong in the gross estate.
- Paying a state tax and assuming the federal return is not needed, or the reverse.
- Treating an extension to file as an extension to pay.
- Filing without checking prior lifetime gifts.
- Not keeping the return after it is filed.

## An example

Walter, 79, dies in October 2026 with a house, brokerage accounts and a pension that together are worth about $4.2 million. He made no large gifts. Everything goes to his wife, Joan, 76. No estate tax is due, and the return is not required. Joan's own assets are about $3 million, so their combined $7.2 million is far below $15,000,000.

Joan's attorney still recommends filing, because Joan's investments have grown strongly and she could live another twenty years. The executor files Form 706 by the nine-month date, elects portability, and keeps the return. Walter's executor also asks his CPA for date-of-death statements. If Joan's estate ever tops $15,000,000, Walter's unused exclusion is available to her. The filing fee is modest next to the possible tax saved.
