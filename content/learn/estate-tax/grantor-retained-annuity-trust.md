If your estate is comfortably above the federal exclusion and much of it sits in assets you expect to rise in value, you have probably heard the term GRAT. It is one of the few planning tools that lets a person pass growth to the next generation while keeping an income stream for years.

This article explains what a grantor retained annuity trust is, how the payments and the leftover amount are calculated, what can go wrong, and who should and should not consider one. For the bigger picture on thresholds and rates, start with our [guide to estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax).

## What is a GRAT?

A GRAT is an [irrevocable trust](/glossary/irrevocable-trust) in which the [grantor](/glossary/grantor) transfers assets and keeps the right to receive a fixed annuity for a stated number of years. When the term ends, whatever remains in the trust goes to the beneficiaries you named, usually your children or a trust for them.

The gift to your beneficiaries is measured at the start. The IRS treats the annuity you keep as having a value, calculated using a government interest rate (the Section 7520 rate, published monthly). Subtract that value from what you put in, and the difference is the taxable gift. If the annuity is large enough to equal the full amount contributed plus the assumed interest, the gift is nearly zero.

The idea is simple. The IRS assumes the trust earns the interest rate. If the assets do better than that, the extra growth is left over after your payments and goes to your family without further gift tax. If they do no better, the annuity payments simply return your own money to you, and nothing has been lost except the legal fees.

## How does a GRAT work, step by step?

1. **You and your attorney choose the assets and the term.** Terms commonly run from two to ten years. Cash, marketable securities, and closely held business interests are the usual candidates.
2. **The trust is drafted and signed.** A trustee is named. You can serve as trustee, and many grantors do, but a successor trustee must be named for the end of the term.
3. **You fund the trust.** Assets are retitled in the name of the trust. Hard-to-value assets need an appraisal.
4. **You receive an annuity each year.** The trustee pays you a fixed dollar amount or percentage, often paid from the trust assets themselves.
5. **A gift tax return is filed.** The transfer is reported on Form 709 in the year the trust is funded, even when the taxable gift is close to zero.
6. **The term ends.** The trustee distributes what is left to the remainder beneficiaries, or holds it for them in a continuing trust.

Because you remain the tax owner of the trust while it runs, you pay the income tax on its earnings. That is a hidden benefit. The tax you pay is not treated as another gift, so the trust can grow without being reduced by income tax.

## What does a GRAT look like in numbers?

Take Marcus, 62, a widower whose estate is about $32 million, most of it a block of company stock he expects to rise sharply. He puts $5 million of stock into a five-year GRAT. Suppose, for simple math, the IRS rate is lower than the stock's actual return and the stock grows faster than that rate for the whole term. After five years of annuity payments back to Marcus, the trust still holds a meaningful sum, and that leftover passes to his daughters. The gift tax cost on creating the trust was about zero.

Now change the story. The stock falls in year two and never recovers. The annuity payments eat through the trust, nothing is left at the end, and the daughters receive nothing. Marcus is back where he began, minus the costs of the setup. That asymmetry is the main reason advisers like the tool: the downside is limited, and the upside is real.

| Outcome during the term | What happens |
|---|---|
| Assets grow faster than the IRS rate | Leftover growth passes to beneficiaries at little or no gift tax |
| Assets grow at or below the IRS rate | Annuity returns the principal to you; beneficiaries receive little or nothing |
| You die during the term | Much of the trust comes back into your taxable estate |
| Assets fall sharply | The trust may run out; no tax benefit, modest setup cost |

## What are the risks and limits of a GRAT?

### You have to outlive the term

This is the central trade-off. If you die before the last annuity payment, the assets needed to produce the remaining payments are generally counted in your gross estate. So grantors with serious health problems usually should not use a long-term GRAT. Many people choose a series of short terms, two years each, so one early death costs less.

### The generation-skipping tax is awkward

If the remainder is meant for grandchildren, the generation-skipping transfer tax rules apply, and a GRAT is a poor way to use that exemption. Most GRATs pass the remainder to children or to trusts for children instead.

### Basis does not step up

Property that passes to your family from a GRAT keeps your original tax basis. A child who sells inherited stock would normally owe tax only on growth after your death, but a child who receives stock from a GRAT may owe tax on all of the growth since you bought it. Read our explanation of the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) before funding a trust with low-basis assets. In some designs, the grantor can swap high-basis assets in later, which is a conversation for your attorney.

### You cannot add more later

A GRAT is funded once. If you want to add assets, you create another trust. This is why people often "roll" a series of short GRATs, each funded with the annuity payments from the last.

### The rate, the rules, and Congress

The benefit depends on the IRS interest rate in the month you create the trust. Lower rates help. Proposals to restrict GRATs, such as minimum terms, have been discussed in Congress over the years, and rules could change. As of 2026, a short-term GRAT remains a recognized planning tool.

## Who should consider a GRAT?

A GRAT is not for most families. With the federal exclusion at $15,000,000 per person in 2026 and $30 million for a married couple using portability, the vast majority of estates will never owe federal tax. Our guide to the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) explains why.

It tends to fit someone who:

- has a taxable estate well above the exclusion, or expects to
- owns liquid assets or business interests expected to rise in value
- can live without the assets for the trust term
- is in reasonable health
- has already made use of simpler tools, such as annual gifts and an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust)

It tends to be a poor fit for someone who needs the assets for living expenses, has a short life expectancy, or holds mostly a home and retirement accounts. A retirement account cannot be put into a GRAT at all.

Ravi and Neha, both 55, own a regional business worth about $40 million and are preparing for a sale in a few years. A short GRAT holding a minority share of the company's stock before an expected sale is the kind of situation where an attorney would model a GRAT. If their estate were $6 million, the same trust would only add cost and paperwork. For business owners, see also [gifting business interests to children](/learn/business-owners/gifting-business-interests-to-children) and [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).

## How does a GRAT compare with other tools?

| Tool | What it does | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Annual exclusion gifts ($19,000 per recipient in 2026) | Moves small amounts out with no filing | Slow for large estates |
| Using the lifetime exclusion by gift | Moves assets and all their growth out | Uses up exclusion; no basis step-up |
| GRAT | Moves only growth above the IRS rate | Needs survival; no basis step-up |
| Irrevocable life insurance trust | Keeps policy proceeds outside the estate | Gives up ownership of the policy |
| Charitable remainder trust | Income to you, remainder to charity | Remainder goes to charity, not family |

If your goal is a gift to charity with an income for life, a [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust) is a close cousin. For the wider menu of lifetime giving, see [gifting strategies and the gift tax](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies), and for how trust types compare, [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts).

## What should I do before talking to an attorney?

1. List your assets with their current values and what you paid for them.
2. Note which assets you expect to rise and which you need for income.
3. Check whether your state has its own estate tax, because a GRAT affects state and federal calculations differently.
4. Gather any business valuation or appraisal reports.
5. Think about who should receive the remainder and who would serve as trustee.

A GRAT is built for a narrow problem, and it is worth modeling several scenarios, including a poor market, before committing. A good attorney will also tell you when the answer is a simpler plan.
