Every year around March, a few clients call with the same worry. They gave a daughter help with a down payment, or put money into a grandchild's trust, and now someone has told them they need to file something with the IRS. Usually that something is Form 709. The name sounds worse than the form. It sits under the [estate tax](/learn/estate-tax) umbrella, and for almost everyone the result is a return that costs time and no tax.

This article covers who has to file, how gift splitting and net gifts work, and the mistakes that make a simple return harder than it needs to be. If you want the broader picture first, start with the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) and the question of whether [lifetime gifts count toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax).

## What is Form 709?

Form 709 is the United States Gift (and Generation-Skipping Transfer) Tax Return. It is filed by the person who makes the gift, never by the person who receives it. It tracks how much of your lifetime exclusion you have used, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. The IRS keeps the running total, and the total is compared with the estate tax exclusion at death.

Gift tax is owed only after cumulative taxable gifts pass that lifetime figure. Because the number is so high, the return is mostly a record-keeping exercise. The tax rate on gifts above the exclusion is the top rate of 40 percent, which is the same as the estate tax.

## Who has to file a gift tax return?

You file when you made a reportable gift in the calendar year. The table separates the common cases.

| What you did | File Form 709? |
|---|---|
| Gave one person $19,000 or less in 2026 | No |
| Gave one person more than $19,000 | Yes, for the amount over |
| Gave a U.S. citizen spouse any amount | No, in most cases |
| Gave a spouse who is not a U.S. citizen more than that special annual limit | Yes |
| Split gifts with your spouse | Yes, even if no tax results |
| Paid tuition or medical bills directly to the school or provider | No |
| Gave to a trust where the beneficiary cannot withdraw the money | Yes, since the gift is not eligible for the annual exclusion |
| Gave to a qualified charity in full | Generally no |
| Chose the five-year election for a large 529 plan contribution | Yes |

Gifts of "future interests" are the trap in that list. If the recipient cannot use or enjoy the money now, as with many trusts, the gift does not qualify for the annual exclusion at all and has to be reported even if it is small. That is why trustees of an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) send out withdrawal notices to the beneficiaries. The notice is often called a Crummey letter, and it gives the beneficiary a short window to withdraw the contribution. That window turns the gift into a present interest, so it qualifies for the exclusion.

## What counts as a taxable gift?

A taxable gift is the part of a gift that is more than the annual exclusion. The exclusion applies per recipient, per year.

Say Dolores gives her niece Carla $24,000 in 2026. The first $19,000 is excluded. The other $5,000 is a taxable gift, which she reports on Form 709. She owes nothing. Her $15,000,000 lifetime exclusion is reduced by $5,000, to $14,995,000.

Gifts are measured at fair market value on the day they are made. Cash is easy. A house, a stake in a family business, or stock in a private company needs an appraisal. Loans at no interest, a co-signed mortgage that you end up paying, and forgiven debts can all be gifts, and our article on [cars, loans and down payments as taxable gifts](/learn/estate-tax/is-it-a-taxable-gift-cars-loans-and-down-payments) walks through those cases.

## How does gift splitting work?

Gift splitting lets spouses treat any gift to a third person as if each of them gave half. It is the way a couple reaches $38,000 per recipient in 2026 when only one of them has the money or owns the asset.

Here is how it works in practice.

1. Both spouses must be U.S. citizens or residents at the time of the gift.
2. The gift must go to someone other than the spouses, such as a child.
3. Both agree to split on Form 709. The spouse who made the gift files, and the other spouse signs the consent. If the non-giving spouse made no gifts, they may not need to file their own return.
4. The election applies to all gifts either spouse made to anyone during the year. A couple cannot split only some of them.

Suppose Ahmed has $60,000 in an account and gives $38,000 to his son for a car. Ahmed's wife, Rana, owns nothing in her own name. If they split, the gift is treated as $19,000 from each. No lifetime exclusion is used, and neither of them owes tax. Without the election, Ahmed's gift would exceed his $19,000 exclusion by $19,000, and he would report it.

Gift splitting does not change who owns the money, only how the IRS counts the gift. It also has a long reach, because the spouses become jointly liable for tax on the split gifts.

## What is a net gift?

A net gift is a gift where the receiver agrees to pay the gift tax that the gift triggers. Because the receiver takes on that debt, the value of the gift is reduced by the amount of tax paid, and the tax is calculated on that smaller amount. The giver in effect pays tax on the money the recipient keeps, not the money the giver started with.

The idea is simple, and the arithmetic is not. It requires a circular calculation, since the tax depends on the net value and the net value depends on the tax. At a 40 percent rate, the tax on a net gift works out to about 29 percent of the total the giver parts with (the gift plus the tax), instead of 40 percent of the amount the recipient keeps. Planners use it for large transfers of illiquid property, like a family company, where the recipient has the means to pay the tax.

With a $15,000,000 exclusion, net gifts are unusual. They apply only after someone has used the whole exclusion, which most people never will. If it comes up, the recipient's agreement should be in writing before the transfer, and the return must disclose it.

## How are the deadline and the extension handled?

Form 709 is due April 15 of the year after the gift. If you file for an extension of your income tax return, that automatically extends the time to file Form 709 as well. If you owe any gift tax, the payment is still due on April 15.

If you have already missed it and no tax was owed, there is generally no penalty for filing late. Filing late is much better than never filing. A late return still starts the three-year clock for the IRS to question the values you reported.

## Why does reporting the gift properly matter?

The IRS generally has three years from the date a return is filed to challenge the value of a gift, but only if the gift was adequately disclosed. Disclosure means describing the property, who received it, how you valued it, and attaching an appraisal when the asset is not publicly traded.

An undisclosed gift can be questioned many years later, often at death, when the executor is trying to prepare [Form 706](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return). If the family has been through a gift of a house or business shares, the extra hour of preparing a clean 709 is cheap protection.

A related point for married couples. If your spouse died and you received their unused exclusion through [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples), that deceased spousal unused exclusion is used first on your lifetime gifts, before your own. Form 709 is where that gets tracked. The rule that applies is based on your last deceased spouse, so a remarriage and another death can change which spouse's exclusion you hold.

## What about basis, income tax and the recipient?

The recipient never owes gift tax. They also do not report the gift as income. What they do receive is the giver's cost basis. If Dolores bought stock for $10,000 and gives it to Carla when it is worth $60,000, Carla's basis is $10,000. If she sells it for $60,000, she reports a $50,000 gain. If she had inherited it instead, the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) would have wiped out that gain. This is the main reason gifts of appreciated assets are not always smart, even for estates that are well under the exemption.

Gifting also affects benefits. A gift made within the look-back window can create a penalty period for Medicaid, so gifts by an older person who may need care deserve a conversation with an attorney first. The article on [gifting to children before Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid) covers that.
