Few topics in estate planning cause more worry than taxes, and few are more misunderstood. People hear about a 40% rate and picture their family losing nearly half of what they leave. For nearly every household, that picture is wrong at the federal level. It is not always wrong at the state level, and taxes of other kinds can matter a lot.

This guide explains the federal estate and gift tax, state estate and inheritance taxes, the gift tax annual exclusion, the step-up in basis, and portability for married couples. Each section links to an article with the details.

## What taxes can apply when someone dies?

Several different taxes can be involved, and they are often confused:

| Tax | Who pays | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Federal estate tax | The estate | Estates above the exclusion ($15,000,000 per person in 2026) |
| Federal gift tax | The giver | Lifetime gifts above the annual exclusion, once the lifetime exclusion is used up |
| State estate tax | The estate | In about a dozen states and D.C. |
| State inheritance tax | The heir | In five states, based on relationship |
| Income tax | The heir | On inherited traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, and deferred income |
| Capital gains tax | The heir | On growth after death when inherited property is sold |
| Final income tax return | The estate or surviving spouse | For income in the year of death |

Most families owe none of the first four. Most have income tax and capital gains questions. For what heirs themselves must handle, see [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) and [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## What is the federal estate tax exemption in 2026?

The federal estate and gift tax basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. It was set by the 2025 tax law, will be indexed for inflation after 2026, and has no scheduled sunset. Only the value above it is taxed, at rates that reach 40%.

The tax is calculated on the gross estate, which includes real estate, bank and brokerage accounts, retirement accounts, business interests, assets in a revocable trust, and life insurance you own. Deductions for debts, expenses, charitable gifts, and assets left to a U.S. citizen spouse are subtracted. The balance, together with adjusted taxable gifts, is compared against the exclusion.

For the full picture, including how the tax is figured, what counts, and when planning still matters under the threshold, read [the federal estate tax exemption in 2026](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

A quick example. Priya, a widow, has an estate of $9 million. She owes no federal estate tax. Her friend Frank has $18 million and is single. The $3 million above his exclusion would be taxed at up to 40%, which is roughly $1.2 million of tax before deductions. That gap is why planning concentrates on the small number of larger estates, and why the Frank-type family should talk with an attorney.

## How do states tax estates and inheritances?

Some states tax at much lower levels than the federal government. As of 2026, Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington, and the District of Columbia levy an estate tax. Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania levy an inheritance tax. Maryland has both.

An estate tax is paid by the estate on its total value. An inheritance tax is paid by each heir on what they receive, usually at a rate based on how closely they are related to the person who died. Spouses are generally exempt.

Exemption levels vary widely, from about $1 million in Oregon to levels tied to the federal amount in Connecticut, and they change with legislation. Real estate in a taxing state can be taxed even if you live elsewhere. Most states do not offer portability. Our article on [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) covers each state list, the "cliff" some states use, and what to do if you own out-of-state property. You can also look up your state in our [state guides](/estate-planning/massachusetts).

## How does the gift tax work?

The gift tax limits how much you can give away during life without using your lifetime exclusion. In 2026 you may give up to $19,000 per recipient with no reporting and no effect on your exclusion. A married couple can combine to give $38,000 per recipient. Payments of tuition and medical bills made directly to the school or provider, gifts to a U.S. citizen spouse, and gifts to charity do not count.

Gifts above the annual exclusion are reported on Form 709 and reduce your $15,000,000 lifetime exclusion. No gift tax is due until the exclusion is exhausted.

Before giving significant assets, weigh the other costs. Gifted property keeps your tax basis, so the recipient may owe more capital gains tax than if they had inherited it. Gifts may also be treated as transfers during the Medicaid look-back period, which in most states is 60 months. Our article on the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) covers 529 plans, gift splitting, and a checklist for deciding whether to give.

For a wider look at lifetime giving, including 529 plans, direct tuition and medical payments, and the Medicaid and basis traps, read [gifting strategies and the gift tax](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies).

## What is the step-up in basis?

For many families, this rule is worth more than the estate tax exclusion. Basis is the tax starting value of an asset. When someone inherits property, its basis is generally reset to fair market value on the date of death. If the heir sells soon after, little or no capital gain is taxed.

Consider Ruth's house, bought for $90,000 and worth $360,000 when she died. Her son inherits it with a $360,000 basis. If Ruth had given it to him earlier, his basis would have been $90,000, and a sale at $370,000 would produce a $280,000 gain.

The step-up generally does not apply to lifetime gifts or to traditional retirement accounts, and in community property states both halves of a couple's property may be stepped up at the first death. Read [step-up in basis: why inheriting can beat gifting](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) for examples and a checklist for heirs. If a transfer-on-death deed or a joint ownership arrangement is under consideration, our article on [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) explains how to keep the step-up.

## What is portability for married couples?

Assets left to a U.S. citizen spouse qualify for the unlimited marital deduction, so no estate tax is due at the first death. But that also means the first spouse's own exclusion goes unused. Portability lets the survivor claim the unused amount, so a couple can shelter $30 million in 2026.

Portability is not automatic. The executor must file Form 706 and elect it, usually within nine months of death. There is a simplified late-election path in some cases, available up to five years after death. Portability does not cover the generation-skipping transfer tax, is not indexed for inflation, and is usually not recognized for state estate tax.

We cover the election steps, remarriage rules, and a comparison with credit shelter trusts in [estate tax portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples). Couples in second marriages may also want to read about [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts).

## Can a trust avoid estate tax?

A revocable living trust is a probate tool, not a tax tool. Because you keep control, assets in it are part of your taxable estate and the step-up still applies. Certain irrevocable trusts can take assets out of your taxable estate, such as irrevocable life insurance trusts, but they require giving up control and have their own costs. See [does a trust avoid taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes) and [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) for how the choices compare.

For married couples where one spouse is not a U.S. citizen, the unlimited marital deduction works differently, and a qualified domestic trust may be needed. See [estate planning with a non-citizen spouse and QDOTs](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot).

## What planning options exist?

The right tools depend on the size of your estate and your state:

| Situation | Typical tools |
|---|---|
| Under $15 million, no state tax | Usually no tax planning; focus on forms, wills or trusts, and incapacity documents |
| Under $15 million, in a state with an estate tax | Credit shelter trust, charitable gifts, careful titling, review of state exemption |
| Married couple, one estate tax return possible | Portability election, review of disclaimer options |
| Estate over $15 million | Lifetime gifts, irrevocable trusts, charitable trusts, business planning, GST planning |
| Heirs inherit retirement accounts | Choice of beneficiary, trust design, 10-year payout strategy |
| Appreciated property | Hold until death for step-up, or sell if it makes sense |

If your estate is large, the generation-skipping transfer tax may also apply to gifts that skip a generation. It has its own exemption, and, as described above, it is not portable between spouses.

Larger estates sometimes add trusts built for a single purpose. An [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) keeps policy proceeds out of the taxable estate, and a [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust) pays you income now and gives the remainder to charity. For simpler giving, see [donor-advised funds and qualified charitable distributions](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds).

Beneficiary forms also feed into the tax picture. Life insurance you own is part of your gross estate, and retirement accounts carry income tax for heirs. Our [beneficiary designation guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations) explains how forms interact with your plan, and [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) covers the 10-year rule for inherited IRAs.

## Does estate tax law change?

Yes, often. The exclusion has changed in 2001, 2010, 2013, 2018, and 2025. The 2025 law removed the scheduled drop and indexed the $15,000,000 amount. Congress could change it again, and states change their own thresholds regularly. This is one reason to review your plan every few years, and why this site says "as of 2026" when giving numbers.

## What should I do now? A short checklist

1. Add up your assets, including life insurance you own, retirement accounts, real estate, and business interests.
2. Find out whether your state, or a state where you own property, has an estate or inheritance tax.
3. If you are married, decide whether portability or a credit shelter trust fits.
4. Before gifting appreciated property, compare the step-up in basis to any estate tax saved.
5. Check your beneficiary forms and your will or trust together.
6. If your estate is near or above the exclusion, talk with an attorney and a tax advisor.
7. Review your plan every few years and whenever tax law changes.
