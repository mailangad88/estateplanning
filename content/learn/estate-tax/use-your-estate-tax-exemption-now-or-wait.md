For years the standard advice was to hurry. The exclusion was set to be cut roughly in half, so people with large estates made big gifts before the clock ran out. That rush has quieted. The 2025 tax law set the basic exclusion at $15,000,000 per person in 2026, adjusted for inflation after that, with no scheduled sunset.

So the question has changed. It is no longer "will I lose it?" It is "does a large gift help my family, or cost it more than it saves?" The honest answer turns on three things: how big your estate is, what you would give, and what your heirs would owe in capital gains tax.

## The short version

- Under $15 million single, as covered in our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax), or $30 million married, with no state tax problem, waiting is usually better.
- Over that line, or close to it, gifts of assets that will grow can pay off.
- Appreciated property is a poor gift unless the estate tax is the bigger risk.
- Cash, or assets with little built-in gain, are the cheapest things to give.
- Do not impoverish yourself to save tax. Keep what you need.

## What does using the exemption actually mean?

The exclusion is a single pool covering gifts during life and the estate at death. A taxable gift is one above the annual exclusion, $19,000 per recipient in 2026. If you give your daughter $1,019,000, the first $19,000 is covered by the annual exclusion and $1,000,000 is a taxable gift. You file Form 709. No tax is due, but your remaining exclusion falls by $1,000,000, from $15,000,000 to $14,000,000.

Our article on [do lifetime gifts count toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax) walks through the reporting. The key idea here: using it is not a cost in itself. The cost is what the recipient gives up.

## What do you lose by giving instead of leaving?

The step-up. Property given during life keeps your cost basis. Property left at death gets the value at death.

| | Give now | Leave at death |
|---|---|---|
| Recipient's basis | Your original cost | Value at death |
| Growth after the gift | Outside your estate | Inside your estate |
| Estate tax on the asset | Removed, if gift is complete | Counted |
| Gift tax return | Form 709 | Not needed |

Take Lena, 72, with stock she bought for $200,000 that is now worth $1 million. She can give it to her son or leave it to him. If she gives it and he sells at $1 million, he reports an $800,000 gain. If she leaves it, he reports roughly none. At a federal long-term gain rate of 15% to 20%, plus any state tax, that is real money. For Lena, with a $6 million estate, there is no estate tax saved to offset it, so keeping is clearly better. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

Now Hal, 66, single, with a $22 million estate and a private company stake he expects to double. A gift moves the stake and its future growth out of the estate. At 40% the estate tax saved on that growth could exceed the capital gains cost the son carries. That is the situation where giving wins.

## Is the "clawback" a concern?

Not for gifts covered by the exclusion when you made them. The IRS issued rules saying that if Congress later lowers the exclusion, a donor who used the higher amount is not taxed retroactively on those gifts. Said another way, you keep the benefit of the larger amount for what you actually gave. That is why gifts make sense for those who expect a cut, and why holding back until the last minute only has value if you are certain you will do it.

The rule applies to gifts that are complete. A trust that keeps your control or gives you back the right to the property can be pulled into the estate, which is why design matters. A [spousal lifetime access trust](/learn/estate-tax/spousal-lifetime-access-trust-slat) is one way married couples keep indirect access.

## How do you decide? A short checklist

1. Estimate your estate today and in 10 years at a reasonable growth rate.
2. Compare it to $15,000,000 (single) or $30 million (married), and also your state's threshold.
3. List the assets with the lowest built-in gain and those with the fastest expected growth.
4. Decide how much you can give and still be comfortable if costs, taxes or health change.
5. Choose the vehicle: outright gift, a trust, a SLAT, a grantor retained annuity trust.
6. Report on Form 709 and keep the valuation support.

## What if I am single?

Single people feel this most, because no spouse brings a second exclusion. A single person at $13 million is not taxed today, but with growth and inflation-indexing that gap can close. We usually suggest testing the plan under two scenarios: the law as it is now, and a lower exclusion. If a gift of a modest slice makes sense under both, it is low regret.

If you live in a state with its own tax and a low threshold, gifts can cut both state and federal exposure, although a few states add gifts back to the estate for a number of years before death. Check [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## What about Medicaid?

Large gifts also trigger a look-back, 60 months in most states, if you might need Medicaid long-term care. Gifts that save estate tax can cause a penalty period. If you are in your 80s and health is uncertain, this can matter more than the tax. See [how to reduce estate taxes](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-reduce-estate-taxes) for how the options fit together.

## What kinds of gifts work best?

Cash and assets with little built-in gain, because the recipient takes your basis and there is little to tax. Shares of a company expected to grow quickly, because all the growth lands outside your estate. A minority interest in a family business or partnership, where valuation rules may reduce the gift's reported value; the IRS looks closely at those discounts, and the appraisal has to hold up. A life insurance policy placed in a trust, which keeps a future death benefit out of the estate if the transfer is made properly and the three-year rule is respected. See [irrevocable life insurance trusts](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust).

## What kinds of gifts work badly?

Your home, if you still live in it and might sell. A highly appreciated stock that you could instead leave at death. Anything you may need to pay for care. And gifts made out of fear rather than numbers. We have seen families give away a house for a tax saving they never needed, then lose the step-up and the control.

## What does a sensible middle path look like?

For many people it is modest and repeatable: use the $19,000 annual exclusion each year for children and grandchildren, fund education directly, and keep the large exclusion for the end. For couples, a married couple can give $38,000 to each recipient in 2026 by splitting gifts. For those over the line, a trust designed for growth assets, reviewed every few years, usually beats a single big transfer. The right amount is the one you can still live comfortably after giving.
