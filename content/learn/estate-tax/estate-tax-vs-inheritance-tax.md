The words sound alike and people use them interchangeably, but they describe two different taxes with two different taxpayers. If your parent dies and you are told that "the estate owes tax," that is one situation. If you are told you owe tax on your share, that is another, and which one you face depends mostly on the state.

This article explains the difference, who is exempt, and how to tell which tax, if either, applies. It is part of our [guide to estate and gift taxes](/learn/estate-tax), and the state lists are in [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## What is the difference between estate tax and inheritance tax?

An estate tax is a tax on the privilege of passing property at death. The estate pays it. The executor adds up everything the person owned, subtracts deductions and the exemption, and pays tax on the rest before heirs get their shares.

An inheritance tax is a tax on the privilege of receiving property. Each heir pays on what he or she receives. The rate and exemption often depend on the relationship: a child may pay nothing or a low rate, a sibling a middle rate, and a friend or distant relative the highest rate.

| | Estate tax | Inheritance tax |
|---|---|---|
| Who pays | The estate, through the executor | Each heir, on that heir's share |
| What is taxed | The total estate above an exemption | The amount each heir receives |
| Rate depends on | The size of the estate | The size of the share and the heir's relationship |
| Federal version | Yes, above $15,000,000 per person in 2026 | None |
| State versions | About a dozen states and D.C. | Five states |
| Spouse | Marital deduction for a U.S. citizen spouse | Generally exempt |

See the glossary entries on [estate tax](/glossary/estate-tax) and [inheritance tax](/glossary/inheritance-tax).

## Which taxes does the federal government charge?

Only the estate tax. The federal estate tax basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and a couple can shelter $30 million with portability. The rate on the excess runs up to 40%. That is why most families owe nothing: only a tiny share of estates reach that level.

There is no federal inheritance tax. When a child receives money from a parent's estate, the federal government does not tax the child on it. The child can owe income tax on retirement accounts and capital gains tax later on growth, but those are different taxes. Our article on [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) lists them.

## Which states have an estate tax and which have an inheritance tax?

As of 2026, these states and the District of Columbia charge an estate tax: Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington and D.C. These states charge an inheritance tax: Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey and Pennsylvania.

Maryland appears on both lists. The thresholds and rates change often, and this page does not give them, because they differ so much. Washington and Oregon, for example, start taxing estates at far lower levels than the federal government. If you are in Illinois, see our article on [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).

Two points to remember. First, the tax follows the deceased person, not the heir. If your aunt lived in a state with an inheritance tax, her heirs may owe it even if they live somewhere else. Second, real estate located in a taxing state can be taxed there even if the owner lived elsewhere, which is one reason out-of-state land complicates planning. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) and [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

## Who is usually exempt from inheritance tax?

The state rules differ, but there are patterns.

- **Surviving spouses** are generally exempt in every state that has the tax.
- **Children and grandchildren** are exempt or taxed at low rates in many of them, though not all.
- **Siblings, nieces and nephews** often pay a middle rate, sometimes after a small exemption.
- **Friends, unmarried partners and more distant relatives** usually pay the highest rate.
- **Charities** are typically exempt.

The friends-and-partners group matters for unmarried couples. A partner of twenty years may be treated as a stranger to the family and taxed accordingly. If you are unmarried, see [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples).

## An example

Dolores, a widow, lives in Pennsylvania. She dies leaving $900,000 split three ways: her son Tom, her sister Ruth and a longtime friend, Aaron. There is no federal estate tax, because the estate is far below $15,000,000 per person. Under an inheritance tax, each of the three beneficiaries could be taxed at a different rate according to relationship, and the executor typically files the return and pays from the share involved, depending on what the will says. Her son might owe a low rate, her sister more, and her friend more still.

Now suppose Dolores had lived in a state with an estate tax instead and left the same amounts. The estate itself would be tested against the state exemption, and the three beneficiaries would not individually pay. The same family, the same money, a different tax and a different person writing the check. This is why planning starts with the question "which state, and which tax?"

## How can I tell which tax applies to me?

1. Identify the state where the person lived at death, and any state where they owned real estate.
2. Check whether that state has an estate tax, an inheritance tax, both, or neither.
3. Add up the gross estate. Compare it with the federal exclusion and the state threshold.
4. For an inheritance tax, find your relationship class and your share.
5. Ask the executor or the estate's attorney who will file and who will pay, and read the will's tax clause. Some wills direct that taxes be paid from the residue before division, which spreads the cost.
6. Mark the state filing deadline, which is often shorter than the federal nine months.

## Can planning reduce either tax?

For an estate tax, yes, through spousal and charitable deductions, lifetime gifts and certain trusts; see [how to reduce estate taxes legally](/learn/estate-tax/how-to-reduce-estate-taxes). Married couples may add a trust that preserves each spouse's state exemption; see [bypass trust vs portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability).

For an inheritance tax, the rules are more rigid because the rate is tied to the heir's relationship. Planning options include the order of gifts, choice of beneficiaries, life insurance and retirement account designations, which are often treated differently by the states. Some states do not apply inheritance tax to certain life insurance proceeds, but that varies. Many states also add back gifts made shortly before death. Ask an attorney in the relevant state.

## What do people commonly get wrong?

The first mistake is assuming the heir's home state controls. It is the decedent's state, plus the location of any real estate. The second is thinking a 40% federal rate applies to the whole estate; it applies only to the amount over the exclusion. The third is mixing up inheritance tax with income tax on a retirement account, which can be the larger bill for an heir. A son who inherits a $400,000 traditional IRA owes income tax as he withdraws it, usually within 10 years, even where no inheritance tax exists. Our article on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) covers those payout rules.

Finally, people forget that a will can say who bears the tax. If it is silent, state law decides, and the result may not be what the person intended. A short tax clause costs nothing to include.
