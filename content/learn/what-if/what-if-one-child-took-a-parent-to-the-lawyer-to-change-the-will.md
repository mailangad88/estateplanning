Carol, 81, has three children. Her son Greg lives nearby, drives her to appointments and manages her checkbook under a power of attorney. In September he booked a lawyer, took her to the office, waited in the lobby, then came in for the signing. Her will had left everything equally. The new one leaves the house to Greg. Her daughters learn about it only after Carol is in a nursing home.

If a child took a parent to a lawyer to change the will, you probably want to know three things: is it valid, what counts as a red flag, and what you can do. This page answers each for Illinois and New Jersey. It builds on our broader guide to [undue influence](/learn/basics/what-is-undue-influence-in-estate-planning) and sits in our [what-if library](/learn/what-if).

## Is a will valid if one child arranged the lawyer visit?

Often yes. A parent can change a will at any time, for any reason, and can leave more to one child than the others. Driving a parent to an appointment, helping with scheduling, even paying the bill, are normal things that adult children do. Courts know this.

The will becomes vulnerable when several facts line up:

- The child had a position of trust, such as a power of attorney agent, caregiver or joint account holder.
- The parent was dependent on that child.
- The child chose the lawyer, arranged the meeting or gave the instructions.
- The child receives much more than before.
- The change departs sharply from the parent's earlier plan and from what the parent told others.

No single item decides it. A court looks at the whole story. In Illinois, a presumption of undue influence generally arises when a fiduciary relationship exists, the parent is dependent, the child benefits and the child procured the document. In New Jersey, a confidential relationship combined with suspicious circumstances shifts the burden to the person who benefits. Under both approaches the child may need to show the will reflected the parent's own wishes.

## What are the red flags worth writing down?

Write facts, not conclusions. A list that says "Greg chose the lawyer" is useful. A list that says "Greg manipulated Mom" is not.

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The child picked the lawyer, and the parent had no prior relationship with the firm | The lawyer may not know the parent's real wishes |
| The child paid the lawyer from the parent's account or their own | Shows who engaged the work |
| The child was in the meeting or waited at the door | The lawyer should see the parent alone |
| Other family members were told nothing, or kept away | Secrecy supports a pressure theory |
| The parent had a recent hospital stay, new medication or memory decline | Raises both capacity and influence questions |
| The new will differs sharply from earlier ones | Departures need an explanation |
| The child held a power of attorney while it happened | Triggers the trusted-position rule |

Add dates, names, and where you got each fact. Your attorney will sort it out.

## Does it matter who paid the lawyer?

It matters for appearance, not for validity. A third party can pay a lawyer's bill, but the lawyer's duty runs to the parent. Professional conduct rules in both Illinois and New Jersey bar a lawyer from letting someone who pays interfere with the lawyer's judgment, and require the client's consent. A careful lawyer meets the parent alone, asks open questions about family, assets and wishes, and notes the answers. If that was not done, it helps a contest. If it was, the file protects the will.

If you are the child who arranged the visit, take the practical lesson. Stay out of the room, let the parent pay, and keep your hands off the draft. Our page on [signing a will with illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure) sets out the same protections from the planning side.

## What can siblings do if they think the change was improper?

Steps depend on whether the parent is alive. Most of the time the answer is to gather facts quietly and wait.

**While the parent is alive**

1. **Talk to the parent first.** Ask what they want, without accusing anyone. Many parents have a reason that the others never heard.
2. **Keep records of what you see.** Dates of visits, who attended appointments, any decline.
3. **Raise capacity concerns with the doctor.** Ask the parent's physician to document cognitive status.
4. **Do not take, copy or hide documents.** That can backfire and expose you.
5. **Speak to an attorney** if there are signs of financial abuse. A will itself cannot be challenged until death.

**After the parent has died**

1. **Find out where the will is.** It must be filed with the court. See our page on [finding a will after a death](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) for Illinois, or [how to find a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/how-to-find-a-will-after-a-death-in-new-jersey).
2. **Mark the deadline today.** Illinois gives six months after admission ([Illinois deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline)). New Jersey is generally four months after probate, with a caveat option described in [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats).
3. **Request the drafting lawyer's file.** In a contest it can be reached by subpoena and often tells the story.
4. **Collect the earlier wills.** The shift from one to the next is the evidence.
5. **Talk to a probate attorney before the family talks to each other.** Early emails and texts often decide how the case reads.

Our page on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) covers standing and cost. Note also that a [no-contest clause](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) can put a challenger's own share at risk, though many courts enforce them narrowly.

## What if the parent has dementia?

Then two questions apply at once: capacity and influence. A parent with a diagnosis can still sign a valid will on a good day if they understand their family and property. Our page on [contesting a will because of dementia](/learn/what-if/can-a-will-be-contested-because-of-dementia) walks through that test. The child's role then matters more, because a person with memory loss is easier to steer.

## How can a parent protect the plan from a challenge?

This is the part many families skip. If you want to favor a caregiver child or change things in a way that will surprise others, put the reasoning on the record.

- Choose a lawyer with no tie to the child who benefits, and go alone.
- Ask the doctor for a short note on capacity near the signing date.
- Write a brief signed letter saying why, in your own words.
- Tell the other children, or at least the executor, what you did.
- Keep the earlier will and note that it was replaced on purpose.

A signed plan with a visible reason rarely ends up in court. One without a reason often does. Our [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) helps you check what a challenger could point to.
