Priya and her husband paid for a living trust eight years ago. The lawyer handed them a binder, they filed it in a closet, and they felt done. Then Priya's husband died and the bank asked for a court order before it would release his savings account. The trust was real. It just did not own anything.

This is one of the most common gaps in an estate plan. This article explains what happens to an unfunded trust, what your family would face, and how to fix it. For the how-to steps in full, see our guide on [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## What happens by default

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is a container. It only controls what is inside. Signing the trust document creates the container, and funding puts assets into it by changing the title or the payee.

If you never funded the trust, three things follow.

- **The assets stay in your name.** The house, bank accounts and brokerage accounts are still yours personally, so the trust has no control over them.
- **A pour-over will does the sorting.** Most trust plans include a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) that sends leftover assets into the trust at death. The will is a court document, so those assets usually go through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate), the very thing the trust was meant to avoid.
- **Your successor trustee has no authority outside the trust.** The person you chose cannot sell the house or close the account until a court names an executor, unless a small-estate procedure applies.

Some states allow a small estate to skip full probate. The limits and forms differ by state. See [small estate affidavits](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

### What if you are incapacitated?

This is the less obvious risk. If you cannot manage your money and your assets are not in the trust, your successor trustee cannot step in for them. Your family would need an agent under a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney). Without one, they may have to ask a court for a conservatorship or guardianship, which is public, slow and costly.

### Signs your trust may be unfunded

You can often spot the problem without a lawyer. Watch for these signs.

- The deed to your home still shows only your name, or you and a spouse as individuals.
- Your bank and brokerage statements are not in the name of the trust.
- You bought a home, opened an account or sold a business after you signed the trust, and nobody updated the title.
- You cannot find a funding list or deed from the attorney who made the trust.
- Your attorney's file shows a trust, but your own records show no assignment of personal property.

If two or more of these sound familiar, spend an hour checking titles. Even a partly funded trust can leave the biggest asset outside.

## A realistic example

Priya's husband, Anil, owned the house in his name only, kept $90,000 in a savings account and had a brokerage account in his name. The trust owned a few household items and a small account the lawyer helped open.

When Anil died, the successor trustee, Priya, found she could not use the trust to handle the house or the brokerage account. His pour-over will had to be filed with the probate court. The court appointed Priya as executor, the creditor notice period ran, and only then could the assets move into the trust and on to the children.

The drivers of time and cost were court filing fees, attorney time, the waiting period for creditors and how quickly the banks cooperated. The result matched Anil's wishes, but the trust did not save the family the step it was built to avoid.

### Why the trust still has value

An unfunded trust is not worthless. The trust document may still name guardians for children through the pour-over will, set rules for how money is held, and give your family a clear plan once assets arrive. But it only delivers its main benefit, a private and faster transfer, when the assets are actually in it. A partly funded trust gives partial benefit, and a fully funded one gives the most.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are alive

Use this checklist. You can also download the [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist).

1. **Real estate.** Sign and record a deed moving each property to the trustee of your trust. Check your mortgage, title insurance and property tax rules before you sign.
2. **Bank and brokerage accounts.** Ask each institution to retitle the account in the trust's name, or open a new one. Many require their own forms.
3. **Retirement accounts.** Do not retitle a 401(k), IRA or similar account to the trust. Update the beneficiary form instead, and ask an attorney before naming the trust. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).
4. **Life insurance and annuities.** Review the beneficiary forms. Some people name the trust, and others name a person.
5. **Vehicles.** Check your state's rules. Many people leave them out and use a simple transfer process.
6. **Business interests.** Review the operating agreement or shareholder documents, then sign an assignment. Some agreements require consent.
7. **Personal property.** Sign a general assignment that moves items such as furniture, art and jewelry into the trust.
8. **Keep a funding log.** List each asset, the date, and who confirmed the change.

### If it is now too late

1. **Look for a small-estate route.** If the assets fit under your state's limit, a simplified process may work.
2. **Open probate.** Use the pour-over will to move assets into the trust.
3. **Ask about a court confirmation.** In California, a petition commonly called a Heggstad petition, after *Estate of Heggstad* (1993), can ask the court to confirm that an asset belongs in the trust. That is a California-specific tool. Other states have their own procedures, and some have none.
4. **Use the power of attorney.** If the owner is incapacitated and the document allows it, the agent may be able to fund the trust.

## How state law changes this

State law decides the probate limits, whether a small-estate affidavit is available, whether a court can confirm assets into a trust without full probate, and how deeds and transfer taxes work. Real estate rules are local, and recording a deed in the wrong form can create problems. Look at the guide for your state at [estate planning by state](/estate-planning/california), and ask an attorney before relying on a shortcut.

## How to prevent it

Many unfunded trusts happen because funding sounds like paperwork for later. A better habit is to treat the signing meeting as the start of a short project, with a date for each deed and form.

Funding is the step most people skip. The easy fix is to fund the trust when you sign it, or within a few weeks. Ask the attorney who drafted it for a written list of what to retitle, and then check your work.

Review the list each time you buy or sell a house, open an account or change jobs. The [annual estate plan review](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review) gives you a regular reminder. If you are still deciding whether you need a trust at all, our article on [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) can help.
