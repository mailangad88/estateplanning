Walter's will says, "I leave my lake cabin to my son Dev." Two years later he sells the cabin for $310,000 and puts the money in a brokerage account. He does not touch the will. When Walter dies, Dev expects something. Does he get it?

Usually not. A will can only give away what the person owns when they die, and a named item that is gone usually takes the gift with it. This article explains how that rule works, what happens to the value, which states soften it and how to word a will so a sale or trade-in does not undo your plan.

## What is ademption?

Ademption is the legal term for a gift in a will that fails because the property is no longer part of the estate. Lawyers call the older version "ademption by extinction." The gift is not cancelled by a court. It simply has nothing to attach to.

Only certain gifts can be adeemed. It helps to know the four kinds.

| Type of gift | Example | Can it fail if you sell something? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specific | "My lake cabin to Dev" | Yes |
| General | "$50,000 to my niece Anna" | No, paid from whatever the estate holds |
| Demonstrative | "$20,000 to Anna, from my savings account" | Usually paid from another source if the account is gone |
| Residuary | "The rest of my estate to my children equally" | No, it takes whatever is left |

The rule bites hardest on specific gifts: the house, the car, the ring, a named brokerage account. Everything else survives a change in your assets.

## What happens to the money if the gift fails?

It moves to the next gift in the will, which is normally the residuary clause. In Walter's case, suppose his will leaves the rest of his estate equally to Dev and his daughter Priya. The cabin gift is adeemed. The $310,000 sale proceeds, now sitting in a brokerage account, are part of the residue. After costs, Dev and Priya each receive half. These numbers are only an illustration.

Now change one fact. If Walter's will leaves the residue to Priya alone, Dev receives nothing for the cabin, and Priya takes the proceeds. That is the version families do not see coming, and it is how a will made to be fair ends up unfair.

When there is no residuary clause, the property that is left passes by intestacy to the closest relatives. A short will with no residuary clause can therefore redirect property by accident.

## Do the proceeds ever follow the gift?

Sometimes. The traditional rule is strict: if the item is gone, the beneficiary gets nothing in its place. Some states have softened it by statute, and the exceptions are narrow. They tend to cover situations where the person did not choose to give up the item, such as:

- Insurance money owed because the property burned or was stolen.
- A sale made by a guardian or agent under a power of attorney, rather than by the owner.
- Proceeds still unpaid at death, where the buyer owes the estate part of the price.
- A condemnation award, if the government took the property.

Illinois and New Jersey both have case law and statutes addressing parts of this area, and the details change which exception applies. The practical lesson does not depend on those details. A buyer who has paid in full and a sale made by you personally are the situation where the beneficiary is most likely to lose out.

An agent acting under your [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) selling the house to pay for your care is the common painful version. The child who was promised the house may later argue that the gift should convert to the proceeds. Courts in many states are more willing to say so when the sale was out of the owner's hands, but the outcome is uncertain.

## What other events work like a sale?

A sale is only one way an item leaves your estate. People often forget the quieter ones.

1. **Giving it away during life.** A gift of the car to your daughter before death cancels the car gift in the will.
2. **Moving it into a trust.** If you re-title the house into a living trust, the will can no longer give it. A good attorney will check whether the trust document picks the gift up. Our guide to [pour-over wills](/glossary/pour-over-will) explains how will and trust fit together.
3. **Adding a joint owner.** If you add a child to the deed with survivorship rights, the house passes outside the will at death.
4. **Foreclosure or a deed in lieu.** The house is no longer yours.
5. **Loss or destruction without insurance.** There is nothing left to give.
6. **Trading in a car.** A will that says "my 2019 Honda Accord" may have nothing to give after the next purchase.

Notice that the beneficiary never did anything wrong in any of these cases. The will simply stopped matching the facts.

## What if the beneficiary dies before you instead?

That is a different rule, called lapse. If the person named dies first, the gift usually lapses, unless a state's anti-lapse law passes it to that person's children or other descendants. In New Jersey, the rules for a beneficiary who dies first are covered in [when a beneficiary dies first in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/when-a-beneficiary-dies-first-in-new-jersey). Illinois has its own rule, explained in [children born after an Illinois will and lapsed gifts](/learn/illinois/children-born-after-an-illinois-will-and-lapsed-gifts).

The two ideas tend to arrive together. A will that names a person who has died and an item that has been sold can fail twice over. Check both when you review.

## How do you word a will so the plan survives a sale?

The cleanest fix is to avoid naming things that will change. A few approaches work well.

- **Leave shares, not items.** "One-half of my estate to Dev, one-half to Priya" survives any sale, trade-in or refinance. If you want Dev to get the cabin in particular, say so but let the shares equalize.
- **Use a replacement clause.** "My lake cabin to Dev. If I no longer own it at my death, Dev receives the net sale proceeds, held in my brokerage account, instead." Wording like this tells a court what you wanted.
- **Describe by category.** "Any vehicle I own at my death" beats a make and model.
- **Say what happens if the gift fails.** A fallback gift to a named person is better than letting the residue decide.
- **Leave a cash amount.** If the real goal is equal treatment, a dollar sum paid from the estate has no property to disappear.

Our article on [leaving a house in a will](/learn/wills/leaving-a-house-in-a-will) goes through a full set of drafting options.

## How often should you check the will?

Each time you sell, buy, refinance, retitle or gift a significant asset. Sort your major assets into a list, mark which ones your will names specifically and read each line against that list. If a named asset is gone, either amend the will or sign a new one. The [outdated will](/learn/what-if/outdated-will) page, part of our [what-if guide](/learn/what-if), lists other life events that call for the same review, and the guidance on [whether old wills stay valid](/learn/wills/do-wills-expire-is-an-old-will-still-valid) covers age.

A simple check takes ten minutes:

1. List every asset your will names.
2. Mark which ones you still own, in your own name.
3. For each one that is gone, write what you now want.
4. Check any beneficiary named in the will is alive and still the person you mean.
5. Date the review and store the note with the will.

If you also have life insurance or retirement accounts, remember that those pass by beneficiary form and are not affected by the will at all. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) shows where each one stands.
