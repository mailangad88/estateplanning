Ellen had her will and trust prepared eleven years ago by a lawyer in a one-person office. She signed in his conference room and he told her he would keep the originals in his vault. Last month a letter from the state bar arrived. Her lawyer had passed away, and a court had appointed another attorney to take over his files. Ellen does not know who that attorney is, whether her papers are safe, or whether anything in them is wrong.

Most estate planning is done once, then filed. Years later, the person who knows where things are may be gone. This article covers four related situations: the lawyer dies, the lawyer retires, a mistake turns up, or the lawyer will not hand over your papers.

## What happens by default

Your file belongs to you. Lawyers hold it on your behalf, and their professional rules generally require them to give you your original documents and key papers when you ask. They may keep a copy for themselves, and some are allowed to keep working notes.

When a solo lawyer dies or becomes unable to practice, many states have a way to protect clients. A successor attorney named in advance may take over. In other cases the state supreme court or the state bar appoints a custodian to inventory the files, notify clients and return documents. The custodian is a licensed attorney with duties to keep the files confidential.

When a lawyer in a firm retires, the firm usually keeps the files. A good firm sends a letter telling clients where they are going and giving them the choice to take the file or leave it in storage.

### Why originals matter

A will is generally proved in court by filing the signed original. A copy can often be accepted if the family shows that the original was lost without any intent to cancel it, but that takes extra work and an extra hearing. Our article on [what happens if the original will is lost](/learn/wills/lost-original-will) explains that process. For a [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), the trust document is needed for banks and title companies, so the trustee needs the original or a certified copy.

## A realistic example

Ellen calls the state bar and learns that the custodian is a lawyer two counties away. She writes to the custodian, asks for her originals, and encloses a copy of her photo ID. A paralegal replies in three weeks with a package. The will and trust are there, but while reading them, Ellen sees something troubling. Her late husband is named as successor trustee, and so is her sister, who now lives in a care facility.

Nothing in her plan has actually failed. The problem is that her plan reads like the year it was signed. If she had died unnoticed, her children would have faced a trust with no usable trustee, and might have needed a court to appoint one. Because she checked, a new attorney could prepare an amendment naming her daughter and a backup within a few weeks.

The time and cost drivers in a case like Ellen's are small when the problem is found early. They grow quickly when it surfaces after death: court fees, hearings, an attorney for each side and months of waiting.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are alive

1. **Request your file in writing.** Send a letter to the firm or custodian asking for all originals and a copy of the full file. Keep proof that you sent it.
2. **Check the state bar's website.** Many bars list how to find a deceased or retired lawyer's custodian.
3. **Have another attorney review the documents.** Ask the reviewer to look at the named agents, trustees and beneficiaries, the signing formalities, and whether anything has changed since. Our article on [how to choose an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) explains what to look for.
4. **Correct what is wrong.** Depending on the problem, that means a new will, a [codicil or amendment](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils), a corrected beneficiary form, a new deed or a trust restatement.
5. **Store originals where you control them.** See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) and [how to organize your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents).
6. **Tell your executor and agents** where the originals are and who holds them.

### If a death has already happened

1. **Locate the originals.** Ask the lawyer's successor, search the home and any safe deposit box, and check with the probate court clerk.
2. **File what you have.** If only a copy exists, speak with a probate attorney about proving a lost will.
3. **Check for defects.** A missing witness, a missing signature page or a date problem may be correctable in some states. See [will signing defects](/learn/wills/will-signing-defects-witnesses-dates-and-missing-pages).
4. **Ask about a court correction.** Some states let a court reform a document to match the clear intent of the person who signed it, if the evidence is strong.
5. **Consider a claim against the lawyer.** Whether anyone can sue depends on state law and on deadlines that may run from discovery of the problem. Get advice promptly.

## How to spot a mistake before it costs anything

Most drafting errors are quiet. Nobody notices until the plan is tested. A short read-through catches most of them, and you do not need a law degree to do it.

- **Names and roles.** Check that every executor, trustee, agent and guardian is alive, current and spelled correctly, and that each has a backup.
- **Shares.** Add up percentages and make sure the numbers equal 100. Confirm that gifts of specific items or dollar amounts still make sense with what you own.
- **Signatures.** Look for a signature, a date, and witness and notary blocks on every document that needs them. A missing page is more common than people think.
- **Assets.** Compare the deed, account titles and beneficiary forms with what the plan assumes. A trust that was never funded is the usual finding.
- **Marital status and family.** If you married, divorced or had a child after signing, the plan may be silent about it.

If something looks off, do not mark up the original. Write down the question and take it to an attorney.

## How state law changes this

States set the rules on file retention, what must be returned and when, how a deceased lawyer's practice is closed, whether a will can be deposited with the court, whether a court can fix a drafting error, and whether non-clients can sue a lawyer over a will. These rules also differ on whether a copy of a will can be admitted without extra proof. Look at your [state's guide](/estate-planning/florida) for local rules, and check with the state bar for the custodian process.

## How to prevent it

Treat your estate plan as something you hold, not something your lawyer holds. Keep a complete signed copy at home. Keep a note of where the originals are. Tell your executor, your trustee and your agents. Every few years, read the documents again and ask whether the named people are still alive, still willing and still the right choices, as covered in [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

If you are choosing a new lawyer, ask where originals are stored, who has access, and what happens to your file if the lawyer retires. For the broader set of situations like this, see [what happens if you put off estate planning](/learn/what-if).
