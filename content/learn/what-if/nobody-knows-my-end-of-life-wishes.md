Walter, 71, is a widower with three adult children. He has always said he would never want to live on a machine. He has said it at Thanksgiving, in the car, to his neighbor. He has never written it down. Then a stroke leaves him unable to speak, and a ventilator is keeping him alive in the intensive care unit.

His children each remember the conversations differently. One says Dad meant never, ever. One says he meant only if there was no hope. One says he would have wanted every chance. The doctor is waiting for someone to decide. This is what happens next.

## What happens by default

When a patient lacks capacity and has no directive, the hospital works through a ladder.

1. **Look for a named agent.** A healthcare power of attorney or proxy settles who speaks. If none exists, go on.
2. **Look for written instructions.** A living will or a medical order, such as a POLST, tells the team what the patient decided.
3. **Use the state's surrogate list.** Many states have a statute that lists who may consent, often a guardian, then spouse or partner, adult children, parents and siblings. The order varies.
4. **Ask the surrogate to apply the right standard.** The surrogate is supposed to decide as the patient would have, a "substituted judgment," and if that cannot be known, in the patient's best interest.
5. **Escalate if needed.** An ethics committee, a mediator or a court guardianship can resolve a deadlock.

The default in medicine is to treat. A team that is not given clear permission to stop will continue life-sustaining care, and that includes feeding tubes, ventilators and dialysis. That is not callousness. It is the safe course, because the opposite error cannot be undone.

Where there are several people of the same rank, such as three adult children, some states require agreement. Others let a majority decide. A few leave it to the hospital. The result is that your family's disagreements become part of your medical record.

## A realistic example

Walter's three children, Elise, Tom and Grace, meet the doctors twice a day. Elise says their father told her he did not want a machine. Tom says he told him to fight. Grace is somewhere in the middle and cries every time the question is raised.

The attending physician explains the options plainly: continue the ventilator for another week and reassess, or begin a conversation about comfort care. Without a majority, the team continues. Two weeks pass. Elise asks for the ethics committee, which meets within a day and recommends a family meeting with palliative care. At the meeting, Grace says she cannot bear to be the deciding vote. The children eventually agree on a time-limited trial, then on comfort care.

Nobody acted badly. But the family spent weeks in conflict they will remember, and Walter's wish was reconstructed from three memories. The time drivers were the number of decision-makers and whether the state requires unanimity. If they had been unable to agree, a guardianship petition would have added legal fees and weeks more.

## What can still be fixed

### If you can still speak for yourself

1. **Pick an agent.** Choose one person, plus a backup, who will follow your wishes rather than their own. See [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent).
2. **Sign a healthcare power of attorney.** Use your state's form, with the witnesses or notary it requires. Read about the [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
3. **Write a living will.** Describe the situations you fear and the treatments you would refuse. Be concrete about ventilators, feeding tubes and dialysis. See [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).
4. **Have the conversation.** Tell your agent and your family what matters to you: independence, being at home, not being in pain. Values help more than lists.
5. **Ask your doctor about a medical order.** If you are seriously ill, a POLST or DNR turns your wishes into orders. See [DNR and POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).
6. **Spread the copies.** Give them to your agent, your doctor, your family and your hospital portal.

### If the person can no longer speak

1. **Ask the hospital for the chart.** Check whether a directive, POLST or earlier statement exists.
2. **Look around the house.** Papers in a desk, a lawyer's file, a note to the family, a church or clinic record.
3. **Identify who the law treats as the surrogate.** The social worker can tell you.
4. **Gather what the person said.** Write down what they said, when and to whom. It helps the surrogate use the right standard.
5. **Ask for palliative care.** This team helps with decisions and symptoms, and it does not mean giving up.
6. **If the family cannot agree,** ask the hospital about the ethics process before going to court.

### What to put in a conversation with your agent

A conversation works best when it is specific. Describe what a good day looks like for you and what would make life not worth prolonging: not recognizing your children, being unable to eat, living permanently in a care facility. Say how much risk you would accept for a chance at recovery. Tell your agent they have permission to decide, even if the family is unhappy. Many agents say that permission was the most useful thing they were given.

## How state law changes this

States differ on the surrogate list and its order, on whether unmarried partners are included, on what happens when the surrogates are equal in rank and disagree, on witness and notary rules for directives, and on how strong the evidence of a patient's wishes must be before life support is withdrawn. A few states require clear and convincing evidence of the patient's wishes. Check your [state guide](/estate-planning/new-york) or ask an attorney in your state. Our article on [who can overrule your healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive) covers what happens when a team or relative disagrees with a signed document.

## How to prevent it

The prevention is plain and takes about an hour. Sign a healthcare power of attorney and a living will, and talk. Our guide on [how to document your end-of-life wishes](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-document-your-end-of-life-wishes) walks through what to write, and the [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) tells you what you have in place.

## What do people miss?

They miss that a wish is not an order. A living will speaks to doctors in general terms, but when a patient is already in a hospital with a serious illness, a medical order may be the thing that actually changes treatment.

They miss unmarried partners. If you live with a partner but have not married, your state's surrogate list may not mention them. Your parents or siblings could outrank the person who knows you best.

They also miss that the people you love may not agree with you. A document is a gift to them as well, because it lets them follow your lead rather than argue.
