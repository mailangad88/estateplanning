Most people do not skip estate planning on purpose. They mean to get to it after the move, after the baby, after things calm down at work. This page is about what happens in the meantime. Each scenario below is something families run into when a plan is missing, out of date or half finished. For each one we explain what the law does by default, what can still be fixed and which document would have prevented it.

None of this is meant to frighten you. Most of these outcomes are slow and frustrating rather than catastrophic, and almost all of them are avoidable. The point is to show what "waiting" actually means in practice, so you can decide what to do first.

Everything here is general education. The details depend on your state and your accounts, so treat each page as a starting point for a conversation with an attorney licensed where you live.

## Why delay matters: default rules fill every gap

There is no such thing as having no estate plan. If you do not write one, your state has written one for you. That default plan has three parts.

1. **State intestacy law** decides who inherits property in your name alone. It follows a fixed order of relatives and ignores friends, partners you are not married to and charities.
2. **A court** decides who manages your money if you cannot, who settles your estate after you die and who raises your minor children.
3. **Account paperwork** decides who receives retirement accounts, life insurance and accounts with a payable-on-death name. Whatever form is on file wins, even if it is twenty years old.

The default plan works for some families. It works poorly for blended families, unmarried couples, parents of young children, people with a child who has a disability and anyone whose life has changed since they last signed a form.

## If you die without a will

When someone dies without a will, they die "intestate." Their state's intestacy statute decides who inherits. A spouse and children usually come first, then parents, siblings and more distant relatives. An unmarried partner and stepchildren usually receive nothing. A court appoints an administrator, often the spouse or an adult child, who must be approved and sometimes bonded before acting.

The rules differ a lot between states. In some states a surviving spouse takes everything when all the children are also the spouse's. In others the spouse shares with the children from the start. Our [state guides](/estate-planning) each have a section on what happens without a will in that state. The full walk-through is in [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will), and the [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers how to make one.

## If your beneficiary forms are wrong or missing

Retirement accounts and life insurance pass by beneficiary designation, not by your will. That makes them fast and private, but it also means an old form overrides everything else.

The most painful version is an ex-spouse who is still named. Many states revoke an ex-spouse's designation automatically at divorce. Employer plans governed by federal law, such as most 401(k) plans, follow the form on file instead, and the U.S. Supreme Court has upheld that rule. See [what happens if your ex-spouse is still your beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).

The opposite problem is a blank form, or a form whose only beneficiary has died. Then the plan's default order applies. That often means the spouse, and failing that the estate, which drags the money into probate. Inherited retirement accounts paid to an estate can also lose some tax-deferral options. See [what happens if you never named a beneficiary](/learn/what-if/no-beneficiary-named) and the [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations). The [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) walks through each account in a few minutes.

## If you become unable to make decisions

A will only works after death. If an accident, stroke or dementia leaves you unable to manage your own affairs, someone needs legal authority to pay your bills, talk to your bank and sign on your behalf. Being your spouse or your child does not give them that authority over accounts in your name.

Without a durable power of attorney, the usual route is a court guardianship or conservatorship. A judge appoints someone, who may have to file reports and accountings for as long as you live. It is slower, more expensive and more public than the document it replaces. See [what happens if you are incapacitated without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney).

Medical decisions follow a separate path. Many states have a default order of relatives who can decide for you, but disagreements and unmarried partners fall through the gaps. A healthcare power of attorney and a HIPAA release prevent most of that. The [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney) and the [healthcare directives guide](/learn/healthcare-directives) cover both.

If a parent is already showing signs of memory loss, timing matters. A person with early dementia may still have enough capacity to sign. Read [my parent has dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) before assuming it is too late.

## If your plan is out of date

An old plan can be almost as risky as no plan. A will from before your divorce, before your second child or before you moved states will still be read as written, with a few automatic adjustments by state law.

- Many states revoke gifts to an ex-spouse in a will after a divorce, but not every state, and not always for every role the ex was given.
- Children born after a will was signed are often protected by "pretermitted child" rules. Those rules give them a share, but not necessarily the share you would have chosen.
- An executor or guardian you named may have died, moved away or fallen out of touch.

See [what happens if you die with an outdated will](/learn/what-if/outdated-will). A [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) is an easy way to make sure you look again after big life events.

## If you set up a trust but never finished

A revocable living trust only controls what is titled in the trust's name. Signing the trust is the first half of the job. Retitling the house, accounts and other assets into it is the second half, and it is often never done.

An unfunded trust usually still works for what is in it, and a pour-over will can catch the rest. That catch usually goes through probate, which is the very thing the trust was meant to avoid. See [what happens if you never funded your living trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) and the [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist). The [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) explains how funding works.

## If you think it may be too late

People often put off planning until a diagnosis, surgery or a parent's decline makes it urgent. It is usually not too late. The legal test is capacity at the moment of signing, not good health. A person in hospice can sign a valid will if they understand what they own, who their family is and what the document does.

Last-minute documents do attract more challenges, so the details of how they are signed matter more. Witnesses, a short note of the person's understanding and keeping family members who benefit out of the room all help. See [is it too late to make a will?](/learn/what-if/too-late-for-a-will).

## How state law changes these outcomes

Every scenario on this page runs through state law. The parts that vary the most are these:

- **Who inherits without a will.** The spouse's share, the treatment of children from another relationship and the order of more distant relatives.
- **Spousal protections.** Elective share rights, community property and homestead rules can give a spouse a share no matter what the documents say.
- **Divorce revocation.** Whether a divorce automatically cancels gifts and appointments to an ex in a will, trust or non-federal account.
- **Default medical decision-makers.** Which relatives may decide for you without a healthcare power of attorney, and in what order.
- **Small estate shortcuts.** How much property can pass without full probate.

Look up your state in our [laws by state](/estate-planning) section. Each guide lists these rules and links to the statute where we have it.

## Where to start if you have done nothing yet

You do not need to fix everything at once. For most families, this order covers the biggest risks first.

1. **Check your beneficiary forms.** It is free, it takes an afternoon and it controls the largest accounts most people own.
2. **Sign a durable power of attorney and a healthcare power of attorney.** These protect you while you are alive, which is when most people actually need help.
3. **Write a will that names a guardian** if you have children under 18.
4. **Decide whether a trust makes sense** for your house, a blended family or a child who will need help managing money.
5. **Put a review on the calendar** after any major life change.
