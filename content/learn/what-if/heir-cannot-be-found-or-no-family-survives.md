Walter was 81, a widower, and he had never had children. His wife had died eleven years earlier, his only brother before that. When Walter died in a care home, the staff found a bank statement and a house deed but no will and no phone number for any relative. His neighbor, who had mowed his lawn for years, asked the home who would get the house. Nobody knew.

This happens more often than you would think, and it has two forms. In one, the family exists but cannot be located. In the other, there seems to be no family at all. Here is what the court does in both, what it costs the estate in time, and the short document that avoids it.

## What happens by default

Every state has an order of inheritance for people who die without a will, called [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession). It starts with a spouse and children, then moves outward to parents, brothers and sisters and their children, grandparents, and aunts, uncles and cousins. How far it goes depends on the state. Many states keep going through descendants of grandparents. Some keep going further back.

Because the law reaches that far, a truly "no heirs" death is rare. Far more often someone is out there, just unknown to the people at the funeral.

The sequence usually looks like this.

1. **A person asks the probate court to open the estate.** This can be a friend, a creditor, the care home, a funeral director or the public administrator that many counties maintain for estates with nobody to step up.
2. **The court appoints an [administrator](/glossary/administrator).** With no will there is no named [executor](/glossary/executor), so the court chooses, usually the closest relative who asks, or a public official or professional if no relative does.
3. **The administrator secures the property.** That means locking the house, collecting mail, and stopping the bills that can be stopped. It also means insuring the property, which a vacant house often needs.
4. **Notice goes out.** The court requires a published notice, and creditors get a deadline to make claims.
5. **A search for heirs begins.** The administrator looks through papers, address books, old tax returns, obituaries of his parents and siblings, and family trees.
6. **The court decides who the heirs are.** After a hearing, it issues an order called a determination of heirship. If it finds none, the property goes to the state.

### What happens to a missing heir's share

If the court knows an heir exists but cannot locate them, it usually does not give their share to the others. The money is held, in a blocked account or with the court clerk, for a period set by state law. In many states, the share then moves to an unclaimed property office, and the heir or their descendants can claim it later with proof of kinship.

That protects the missing person. It also means the other heirs may wait. An estate with an unresolved heir often cannot make a final distribution or close.

## A realistic example

Back to Walter. The public administrator in his county was appointed because no relative came forward. She found a Christmas card from 2009 signed by a cousin, Denise, in another state. A search of public records and an obituary for Denise's mother led to Denise's daughter, who was found by letter. It took about a year to confirm that the only surviving relatives were Denise and two other cousins who had never met Walter.

The time and cost drivers were the same as in many estates: the court calendar, the creditor claim period, the cost of insuring and maintaining a vacant house, the administrator's fee and attorney's fees, and the fees of a professional who traced the family. The house sold, and the money was split three ways among cousins who had never visited him.

Walter had once said he wanted his neighbor and a local animal shelter to have something. Without a will, none of that could happen.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are the person with no close family

1. **Write a will.** A short will can name any person or organization. It does not need a relative anywhere.
2. **Name an executor and a backup.** A friend, a professional fiduciary or a bank trust department can serve. Our article on [estate planning with no heirs or family nearby](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-with-no-heirs-or-family-nearby) covers professional options.
3. **Add a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) and a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).** The same gap affects you before death, if you cannot make decisions.
4. **Tell someone where the papers are.** A will nobody can find does little good. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

### If you are looking for a relative or an unknown heir

1. **Ask the probate court clerk** whether an estate has been opened, and who the administrator or attorney is. This is a public record and free.
2. **Check state unclaimed property databases.** Every state runs one, and you search by name at no charge.
3. **Gather proof of kinship.** Birth certificates, marriage records and a simple family tree are what the court wants. Our article on [how to prove you are an heir](/learn/probate/how-to-prove-you-are-an-heir) lists them.
4. **Be wary of unsolicited offers.** A firm that contacts you about an inheritance may be legitimate, and may also charge a large share. You can usually verify the estate with the court directly.

### If you are the administrator and cannot find anyone

1. **Document each step of the search.** Courts want to see what you tried, when, and what you found.
2. **Ask the court about a guardian ad litem or an heir search.** Some courts appoint someone to represent unknown heirs.
3. **Do not distribute early.** Giving the known heirs everything while another may exist can make you personally responsible later.

## How state law changes this

The order of heirs, how far out it extends and whether half-siblings or stepchildren count all depend on the state. So does the time the court holds a missing heir's share, whether the property goes to the state's general fund or a school fund, and whether a public administrator exists in your county. Some states allow a later claim by an heir, others close the door after a deadline. If you want the rules for your state, start with our [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) and the broader article on [who inherits without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## How to prevent it

One document does the job. A [valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) can leave everything to a friend, a caregiver, a charity or a mix. For people with a house and few relatives, a [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can also avoid probate and name a trustee who is not family. Either one removes the search, the publication and the wait.

If you already have a will, check that the people in it are alive and the addresses are current. A will that names only a person who died before you can leave you back in intestacy.
