Alicia, 38, has a nine-year-old daughter, Mina. Alicia has a will that leaves everything to her husband, and if he dies first, to Mina. She also has a $400,000 term life insurance policy naming her husband. She has never thought about what happens if both of them die together.

If a child inherits and no trust is in place, the law fills the gap. It does so with a court process that was designed for protection, not for flexibility. This article covers what that looks like and how to avoid it.

## What happens by default

A minor cannot sign a contract, open most accounts or sell property. So whoever holds the inheritance has to pay it to someone else for the child's benefit. The common routes are these.

| Route | Who manages the money | Court involved | When the child gets it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Court-appointed guardian of the estate (called conservator in some states) | Person the judge picks | Yes, with reports | Usually 18 |
| Custodian under UTMA | Person named in the will or form, or picked by the executor | Usually no | 18 to 21, set by state |
| Trust | Trustee you pick | No, unless problems arise | Ages you choose |
| Parent receives informally | The parent, with no authority | Not at first | Not a legal option |

The last row is a trap. Banks and insurers will not release large sums to a parent just because the parent asks, and a parent who spends the child's money without authority can face a claim later.

The fallback in a will is to leave money outright to the child. The executor then has to find a legal way to hold it. If the will does not name a custodian, the court usually appoints a guardian of the estate, which is a separate role from the guardian who raises the child. See [guardian versus trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee).

## A realistic example

Alicia and her husband Kofi die in the same car accident. Alicia's will leaves her estate to Mina and names her sister Dana as guardian of the person. It says nothing about who manages money. Kofi's life insurance is payable to Alicia, who died, so the proceeds go to Mina or the estate.

Dana files in probate court. The judge appoints a guardian of the estate. Dana is chosen, but must post a bond, file an inventory and submit an annual accounting. Withdrawals for Mina's care need approval above a set amount. If Dana wants to use funds to move into a larger house for Mina, she has to ask. Each step uses attorney time and filing fees, which come out of the account.

Eleven years later, at 18, Mina receives the entire balance in one transfer. No one can stop her from spending it as she likes. Dana loved her and managed the money well. The system still gave Mina the money at the age the statute said, not at the age Alicia would have chosen. The time and cost drivers were the number of accountings, the size of the bond and whether any dispute arose.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are a parent or grandparent

1. **Pick the manager.** Choose someone steady with money, who may not be the same person as the guardian who raises the child.
2. **Choose the structure.** A trust named in your will, or a living trust, gives you the most control. A UTMA custodian is simpler for smaller amounts. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).
3. **Set the ages.** Many parents split distributions, such as part at 25, part at 30, and let the trustee pay for school and health care in between.
4. **Update beneficiary forms.** Name the trust or a custodian on life insurance and retirement accounts. The form beats the will.
5. **Name a backup.** If the first manager cannot serve, name a second.

### If the minor has already inherited

1. **Find out who holds the money now.** Ask the executor or insurer.
2. **Ask about a court-approved structure.** A judge can sometimes approve a restricted account or move funds into a trust for the child.
3. **Keep records.** Receipts for every expense that comes from the child's funds.
4. **Talk to the guardian about the plan for age 18.** A frank talk with the young person, before the date, helps.
5. **Speak with an attorney** if the sum is large or the family is in conflict.

## How state law changes this

States differ on the age of transfer under their UTMA statutes, on the dollar limit for informal payments to a parent, on whether a court-supervised guardianship of the estate is required for amounts above a threshold, and on bond rules. Some states call the role a conservator. Look at your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois) for specifics, or ask us.

## How to prevent it

A will that creates a [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust) for children, or a funded [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), is the most complete answer. Pair it with beneficiary forms that name the trust. Our article on [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) shows how to set the ages, and the [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) helps estimate how much money a guardian might need.

Parents often leave this task for last, because it begins with a question no one wants to answer. The documents take a few weeks to complete. See [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) for the companion question.

## What do parents usually get wrong?

The most common mistake is assuming the guardian and the money manager are one job. They are not. The person who raises the child decides about school, doctors and bedtime. The person who manages the money decides about the account. If one person fills both roles, that can work, but it means the caregiver also has to justify every expense to a court or a trustee.

The second mistake is relying on a simple will that says "everything to my spouse, then to my children." That works while the spouse is alive. It says nothing about the case where the children are minors and the spouse is gone.

The third is the life insurance form. Parents often carry the largest asset they will ever leave as a policy, and the beneficiary line is the most important sentence in the plan. Many name a spouse and leave the contingent line blank. If the spouse dies first, or at the same time, the money can end up with a minor and no one authorized to hold it.

Think about the ages too. Few eighteen-year-olds are ready for six figures. A trust can pay for tuition, rent and medical bills directly and hold the rest until the child is older, without ever reaching into a court file.
