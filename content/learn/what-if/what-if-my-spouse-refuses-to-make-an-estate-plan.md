If your spouse refuses to make an estate plan, you are not stuck waiting. Dan has asked Rita to see a lawyer three times in four years, and each time she says, "We're fine, it all goes to each other anyway." Dan doubts that is true and is tired of raising it.

One spouse cannot be forced to plan, and the other can still act. This article covers what you can sign alone, what that leaves uncovered, and how to get a reluctant spouse to at least a partial yes. It sits in our [what-if library](/learn/what-if) because the gaps only show up on the worst day.

## What can I do on my own if my spouse won't plan?

You can sign every personal document yourself. Each one controls your own property and your own decisions, so none of them needs your spouse's signature. The table shows where each one stops.

| Document or step | Can you do it alone? | Where it stops |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Yes | Does not reach joint accounts, a jointly titled house, or accounts with a beneficiary form. Spouse keeps a statutory share |
| Financial power of attorney | Yes | Names who handles your money if you cannot. Does nothing for your spouse's accounts |
| Healthcare directive | Yes | Covers your medical choices only |
| Beneficiary forms | Yes, with exceptions | A 401(k) usually needs your spouse's written consent to name someone else |
| Living trust | Yes | Covers only what you retitle into it |
| Guardian for your children | Named in your will | A surviving parent generally keeps custody, so it matters mainly if you both die |

If you are a married person with children and an IRA, a house and a life insurance policy, you can fix more than you think in one afternoon. Our guide on [whether you need a will](/learn/wills/do-i-need-a-will) explains the baseline.

## Why do spouses refuse, and what actually helps?

Refusal is rarely about the paperwork. It is usually one of four things, and each has a different answer.

- **"It feels like planning to die."** Offer a narrower task. A power of attorney and a healthcare directive are about being sick, not dying, and most people can accept that.
- **"We have nothing worth planning."** A house and a retirement account are enough to need a plan. Show the specifics, such as the Illinois split below.
- **"I don't trust lawyers or the cost."** Ask for one consultation with no obligation to sign. Read what happens at an appointment first, in our page on [what an estate planning appointment involves](/learn/basics/what-happens-at-an-estate-planning-appointment-and-what-the-attorney-will-ask).
- **"We disagree about who gets what."** This is the hardest. A blended family, a child from a prior relationship or a parent to support can make one spouse avoid the conversation. Our page on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) helps with the wording.

One approach that works is a short written list: three decisions you need from each other, not twenty. Who would you each want as guardian? Who handles money if one of us is in the hospital? Does everything go to the survivor first? Ask for those three answers, then make the call.

## What does a spouse's refusal leave exposed?

Mostly the refusing spouse's side, and the family's side if you both die. Your own documents are in place, so your wishes are covered. Theirs are not.

Here is an example with invented numbers. Rita owns a $90,000 savings account in her name alone and has two children from an earlier marriage. The house, worth $360,000, is in joint names with Dan. Rita has no will. She dies first.

- The house goes to Dan by title, so no will is needed.
- The savings account passes by intestacy. In Illinois a spouse and descendants share the estate half and half, so Dan takes $45,000 and her two children split $45,000. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) has the full order. New Jersey uses a different formula, set out in [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey).
- If Rita wanted Dan to have everything, she needed a will. If she wanted her children to have more, she needed one too. Either way, the default is a guess.

The same thing happens on incapacity. If Rita has a stroke and has no power of attorney, Dan cannot simply sign for her on her accounts. The usual route is a guardianship petition in court, which is slow and public. Our page on [incapacity without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney) walks through it.

## Can I leave my spouse out of my own plan?

Not completely, and this is where a reluctant spouse's rights matter. Both states give a surviving spouse a floor.

In Illinois, a spouse who is left less than the law allows can renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if there are descendants, or one-half if not, filing within seven months after the will is admitted. The details are in [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death). In New Jersey the elective share is one-third of the augmented estate, claimed by a complaint filed within six months after a personal representative is appointed. See the [New Jersey elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse).

A signed marital agreement can change these rights, but a will alone cannot. If you are planning around a spouse you expect to resist, talk to an attorney before you sign. The mistake we see is a will that "disinherits" a spouse and then falls apart in court.

## What are the steps if my spouse still won't engage?

Take them in order. Each one stands on its own.

1. **Sign your own power of attorney and healthcare directive.** These protect you if you are hurt, no matter what your spouse does.
2. **Review every beneficiary form.** Retirement accounts and life insurance often name an ex, a parent or no one. This takes an hour and costs nothing. Our page on [whether your spouse must approve your choices](/learn/beneficiary-designations/does-your-spouse-have-to-approve-your-beneficiary-choices) covers which accounts need consent.
3. **Make your will.** Name an executor and a guardian for any minor children, and a backup for each.
4. **Ask for one meeting.** Suggest your spouse hear the options and sign nothing. Many people say yes to listening.
5. **Re-ask at a natural moment.** A new baby, a surgery, a parent's death, a move or a new job reopens the conversation without nagging.
6. **Consider a separate attorney for your spouse.** If your spouse distrusts the plan because you arranged it, their own lawyer may help.

Check where you stand first with the [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment).

## What if my spouse signs nothing and we both die?

Then a court picks the guardian for minor children, and the estate follows the default rules. If your will names a guardian and your spouse's side has no will, the court usually looks at both. Disagreement between two sets of grandparents is what turns this into a fight. Even a spouse who refuses everything else may agree to name a guardian, and that is worth a direct request. See [what if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) for how courts decide.
