Walter and Helen signed their wills the year their youngest started school. Each left everything to the other, with the three children as backup. Each named the other as executor and as agent under the power of attorney. It was a tidy, sensible plan for a couple who expected to be around for a long time.

Helen died when she was 71. Walter, 74, filed her death certificate, retitled a few accounts and went on with life. Nobody suggested he look at his own documents, and he never did. This article is about what that leaves behind, and what a widow or widower can do about it.

## What happens by default

When your spouse dies, your plan loses its center. In most two-person plans, the spouse is first in nearly every position: sole beneficiary of the will, executor, trustee, financial agent, healthcare agent and guardian of any minor children. The documents are still valid. The people in them are not all still living.

Each document handles that differently.

| Document | What it says about your late spouse | What happens now |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Leaves everything to spouse, then to children | Spouse's gift lapses; children usually take if named as backup |
| Will, executor clause | Names spouse, maybe nobody second | Court appoints someone if no backup is named |
| Revocable trust | Spouse as co-trustee and successor | Surviving trustee continues; if no successor is named, a court may need to appoint |
| Financial power of attorney | Spouse is the only agent | The document has no one to act |
| Healthcare directive | Spouse is the healthcare agent | Hospital looks to default state order of relatives |
| Beneficiary forms | Spouse is primary beneficiary | Contingent beneficiary, if any, is paid; otherwise the plan default |

If your will does name backups, the plan quietly holds together. If it does not, you are relying on state defaults, which is the exact situation the plan was meant to avoid. Our article on [what happens when you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) shows what those defaults look like.

### The lapsed gift

When a will says "everything to my spouse" and the spouse is already dead, the gift fails. Many states have an anti-lapse law (see [lapsed gifts](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death)) that redirects a gift to a dead relative toward that person's descendants, but these statutes are narrow and do not always apply to a spouse. If they do not, the property falls into the residue of the estate or, if there is no residuary clause, into intestacy. Either way, a court has to sort it out, and the result may not be what you would have picked.

## A realistic example

Back to Walter. At 79 he has a fall and cannot manage his own affairs. His power of attorney names Helen. Helen cannot serve. Nobody else is named. His daughter Joanne goes to the bank, which says it needs legal authority. The hospital will talk to her about his care under its default rules, because adult children are usually next in line for medical decisions, but the bank will not budge.

That leaves a court case. Joanne petitions to be named guardian or conservator of her father's finances. The court appoints a lawyer for Walter, holds a hearing and sets a bond, and each step costs money from his accounts. It also puts his medical and financial details in a public file. In the meantime, his mortgage-free house needs property taxes paid and a roof repaired, and no one has the authority to sign.

If Walter had gone to an attorney six months after Helen's death and signed a new power of attorney naming Joanne and, as a backup, her brother, none of that would have happened. The same visit would have fixed the will, the healthcare directive and the beneficiary forms. For a deeper look at the court route, read about [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## What can still be fixed

### If you are the surviving spouse and have capacity

1. **Do an inventory of every role your spouse held.** List each document and each account. Write down who is named second, or note that nobody is.
2. **Sign new lifetime documents.** A durable financial power of attorney with at least one successor agent, a healthcare directive with a backup agent, and a HIPAA release. These are the ones you cannot fix after you lose capacity.
3. **Rewrite or amend the will or trust.** Choose a new executor and successor trustee, then spell out who inherits if a beneficiary dies first. Our guides on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) go through the questions.
4. **Update beneficiary forms.** Every retirement account, annuity, life insurance policy and payable-on-death account needs a fresh primary and contingent name. Use the [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) to keep track.
5. **Retitle assets.** Joint bank accounts, vehicles and the house may still show your spouse. Recording the death and updating the title keeps the property from landing in probate later. Our piece on [a house still in a late spouse's name](/learn/what-if/house-still-in-late-spouses-name) explains why.
6. **Decide on portability.** If your spouse's estate is under the filing threshold, no return may be required, but filing can preserve the unused exemption. The federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and portability can carry a late spouse's unused amount to you. See the page on [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples).

### If you are the family and the surviving spouse has already lost capacity

Then the route is a court. Find out whether any older document survives, check for joint accounts and beneficiary forms, and talk to an attorney about guardianship or conservatorship in the person's state. Ask about the least restrictive route, since some states allow supported decision-making or a limited order. For the wider picture, see our [guardianship guides](/learn/guardianship).

## How state law changes this

States differ on four things that matter here: whether an anti-lapse statute applies to a gift for a spouse, who ranks next to make medical decisions when there is no named agent, what happens to a trust with no successor trustee named, and the process and cost of a guardianship. In community property states such as Texas and California, a surviving spouse's share of the marital property and the step-up in basis can work differently than in common-law states. Review the [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) or [California](/estate-planning/california) guide if that is your state.

## What about the guardian of minor children?

If you are a widowed parent of children under 18, this is the gap that matters most. Most couples name each other as the parent who raises the children, with a guardian chosen only for the rare case where both die. After one death, you are that case. If you die, the court looks at who the children can live with, and a guardian named in your old will, if there is one, may be the only guidance the judge has. Read about [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) and then check whether the person in your will is still willing, still alive and still the right choice.

## How to prevent it

Prevention here means two habits. When you sign documents as a couple, name at least one real backup in every role, so the spouse's death does not empty it. And when a spouse dies, put your own plan on the list of things to finish within the year. Our guide on [updating your plan after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies) goes through the whole sequence, including which jobs can wait and which cannot.
