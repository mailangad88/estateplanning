Three years after his wife died, Walter, 74, married Lorraine. His son and daughter like her. They also noticed that the house now has both names on it, that Lorraine is on his checking account, and that Walter has mentioned "taking care of her." Nobody has said anything out loud.

This page is written from the adult child's side of that table: what usually happens, what you can and cannot do, and what your parent can do if they want to look after a new spouse and the children both. The parent's version of the same problem is covered in [remarried without updating your will](/learn/blended-families/remarried-without-updating-your-will). For the full set of topics, see the [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## What happens by default

A remarriage reorders the family in the eyes of the law. The new spouse becomes the first person the system protects, and the children from the first marriage move down a rung. Four mechanisms do most of the work.

1. **State protection for a surviving spouse.** Most states give a surviving spouse a minimum share, often called an elective share, and some also provide a homestead right and an allowance. A will that ignores the spouse can be overridden to that extent.
2. **Omitted spouse rules.** If the will pre-dates the marriage, many states treat the spouse as though they were left out by accident and give them a share.
3. **Intestacy.** With no will at all, a surviving spouse often takes the whole estate or a large share. Children get what is left, or nothing.
4. **Title and forms.** A joint account, a retitled house or a retirement account naming the spouse passes outright. The will never gets a vote.

## A realistic example

Walter dies at 79 with a will from 2009 that leaves everything to his two children. Since then he retitled the house to himself and Lorraine as joint owners, added her to the bank account and named her on his IRA. The will governs very little. The house and account pass to Lorraine by survivorship. The IRA goes to her by the beneficiary form. What is left in his name alone is small.

Lorraine, 76, now owns it all. She is kind and means to be fair, but she has a daughter of her own. Her will, signed years ago, leaves her estate to that daughter. When Lorraine dies, the house goes there, not to Walter's children. No one did anything wrong. The result still surprises a lot of families.

Time and cost drivers: whether anyone contests, whether the surviving spouse needs probate at all, and how many accounts need to be sorted. Most of this ends up quiet, because nothing is in dispute. The damage happens at the signing table years earlier.

## What can still be fixed?

**If the parent is alive and has capacity:** only the parent can change anything. Children can open the conversation, but pressing hard can backfire.

1. Ask a gentle question: "If something happens to you, what do you want to happen to the house?" Let them answer.
2. Suggest a visit to an estate planning attorney, and offer to drive, but do not sit in the meeting. A lawyer will want to speak with the parent alone.
3. Ask the parent to list assets with the title and beneficiary on each. The [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) is a quick way to do that.
4. The parent can use tools that serve both goals. A [QTIP trust](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) pays income to the spouse for life and passes the principal to the children afterward. A [life estate](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse) lets the spouse live in the house while the remainder goes to the children. Separate trusts keep each spouse's property apart.
5. A signed marital agreement, either before or after the wedding, can limit what the spouse claims.

**If the parent has died and it is already done:**

1. Read the documents before assuming the worst. Some wills include a trust for the spouse.
2. Ask for copies of the will, trust and beneficiary forms through the executor.
3. If you believe a document was signed under pressure or without capacity, see an attorney quickly, since contest deadlines are strict. Our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains the grounds and the limits.
4. The surviving spouse can choose to give some property to the children. A gift or a disclaimer may have tax effects, so get advice first.

## Is this always unfair to the children?

Not always, and it helps to be honest about that. A parent in their seventies who remarries may be looking for companionship and care, and may reasonably want the new spouse to be secure. If the house is the only major asset, leaving the spouse with nowhere to live is not a kind outcome either. Many parents want exactly what the children want, a fair split, and just never got around to the paperwork.

The real danger is not generosity. It is accidental outcomes. Property goes to the new spouse because a form said so, not because the parent chose it. And a surviving spouse who inherits outright can later change their own plan, remarry again or be pressured by someone else. A trust or a life estate keeps the parent's decision intact after they are gone.

If you suspect something is wrong beyond the ordinary, such as a parent who is confused, isolated from you or suddenly changing every document, that is a different conversation. Read [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) and speak to an attorney about options.

## How state law changes this

Two big groupings matter. Community property states, including [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), treat marital property as owned equally, which means a surviving spouse already owns half of what was earned during the marriage. Common law states use elective share formulas, and those formulas differ in size and in what they count. Some states let the spouse waive these rights in writing. Others set rules for prenups. Our overview of [the spouse's right to a share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) explains the idea.

Not every estate is affected the same way. A long first marriage with a wealthy parent and a short second marriage can look very different from a modest estate where the spouse needs the house. A fair plan often has to account for the new spouse's real needs.

## How to prevent it

For the parent, three documents do the work. A revised will or trust that says what happens on remarriage. Beneficiary forms that match it. A written agreement with the new spouse, ideally before the wedding. Our guide to [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) and the article on the [family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) go into the options.

Adult children can help by being the ones who ask early, kindly and without a hidden motive. They can also point the parent to the [plan finder](/plan-finder), then step back. More scenarios like this one are in the [what-if library](/learn/what-if).
