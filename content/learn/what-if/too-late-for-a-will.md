Maya's father has weeks, maybe less. He never made a will, and the family just realized it. She asks the question many people type into a search box: can you make a will in the hospital, and does it count if he has memory problems?

The honest answer is that it is often still possible, but it takes care. This page covers the legal test, the practical hurdles in a hospital, how contests arise and what else can help if a full plan cannot be done. For the basics of a valid will, see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will). For a related situation, see [estate planning after a serious diagnosis](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-a-serious-diagnosis).

## What happens by default

If a person dies with no valid will, state intestacy law decides who inherits, usually a spouse and children, then other relatives. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) walks through that. If the person has a will that was signed when they lacked capacity, a court can set it aside. In that case, an earlier will applies if one exists, and otherwise intestacy does.

That is why a last-minute will needs two things: a person who can understand it, and a signing that holds up later.

## What is the legal test for making a will?

In most states, the test for testamentary capacity asks whether the person:

- understands they are making a will,
- has a general idea of what they own,
- knows who their close family is, and
- understands how the will passes property.

It is usually a lower bar than the one for contracts. A person does not need to list every account or manage their money. The person must understand the decision in front of them. This is often called testamentary capacity.

Capacity is judged at the time of signing. A person with dementia, medication effects or a serious illness may have it on one afternoon and not the next. A signing during a clear stretch, called a lucid interval, can be valid. A diagnosis alone does not decide the question, and neither does age.

## What goes wrong in a hospital or hospice?

The legal rules are the same, but the setting adds friction.

1. **Witnesses.** Most states require two adult witnesses who do not benefit from the will. A relative who inherits should not be a witness. Hospital policy often keeps staff from acting as witnesses, so the attorney usually brings people.
2. **Notary.** In states that use a self-proving affidavit, a notary makes the will easier to prove in probate. Check ahead, because a notary may need to be at the bedside.
3. **Timing.** Medication, pain and tiredness vary through the day. Pick the clearest hour, and confirm with the care team.
4. **Privacy.** Some hospitals limit visitors or recording. Ask about visiting rules early.
5. **Signing ability.** If the person cannot hold a pen, some states allow a mark or another person signing at their direction and in their presence. The rules vary, so ask an attorney.

## What about undue influence and contests?

A will can be challenged if it was the product of undue influence, meaning someone's pressure replaced the person's own wishes. The risk rises when a beneficiary finds the lawyer, attends the meeting, or speaks for the person. In some situations, many states scrutinize the will more closely or shift the burden to the person who benefits. See our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

Attorneys lower the risk with ordinary steps:

1. Meeting the person alone, without the family in the room.
2. Asking open questions about family, property and why they chose each gift.
3. Getting a short note from the treating doctor close to the signing date.
4. Keeping notes, and sometimes having a witness confirm the person's clarity.
5. Making sure the will fits what the person has long said, or recording the reason it does not.

None of this prevents a contest. It does give a court strong evidence of what the person wanted, and it can discourage a weak claim.

Families sometimes worry that a short will signed near the end of life will always be attacked. That is not the usual result. Most wills signed under careful conditions are never challenged. A contest is more likely when the plan is a surprise, when one child is left out without explanation, or when a new friend or caregiver receives a large share. A clear reason written in the file or a short, calm letter can help a court see the person's own thinking.

## A realistic example

Back to Maya. Her father, Rahul, has early dementia and a hospital stay for pneumonia. Maya contacts an attorney, who asks her to leave the room. Rahul is clearer in the late morning, so the attorney comes at 11. He asks Rahul to describe his family, his house and savings, and who he wants to inherit. Rahul gives a clear answer, including why he is leaving his younger son a smaller share.

Two people from the attorney's office witness the will, a notary signs a self-proving affidavit, and the doctor writes a short note the same day. The attorney keeps her notes. The time and cost drivers are how fast an attorney can get there, how complex the property is and whether anyone is likely to object.

## What can still be fixed?

**While the person can still act:**

1. Call an attorney today and say capacity and timing are concerns.
2. Gather a list of accounts, property and family members.
3. Sign a simple will rather than a complex plan, if time is short.
4. Sign a financial power of attorney and healthcare directive while capacity remains. See [planning for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).
5. Update beneficiary forms and payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designations. These can often be done with a form and a bank visit. See [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).

**If the person can no longer sign anything:**

1. Intestacy decides who inherits, so the family follows probate rules.
2. If someone must manage money or care now, a guardianship or conservatorship may be needed. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).
3. Families can still agree among themselves after a death to share things differently, though taxes and court approval may apply.

## How state law changes this

States differ on witness numbers, whether handwritten wills count and whether a notary is needed. They also differ on the burden of proof in an undue influence claim and on how a person who cannot sign may make a will. We have not named a state here on purpose. Check our state guides, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), and ask an attorney about the local rule.

## How to prevent it

The best fix is a plan made while things are calm. A will, a power of attorney and a healthcare directive take little time when no one is rushed.
