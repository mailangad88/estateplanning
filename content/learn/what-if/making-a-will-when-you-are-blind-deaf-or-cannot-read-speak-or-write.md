Gloria is 74 and lost most of her sight to macular degeneration two years ago. Her husband died last spring, and she wants to leave her house to her daughter and her savings to her two grandsons. A neighbor told her she "can't sign a legal paper if you can't see it." She stopped calling lawyers.

The neighbor was wrong, and Gloria is far from alone in believing something like it. This page covers what the law asks of a will-maker with a physical limit, how the signing changes in practice, and what to avoid. For the general rules, see [how to make a legally valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

## What the law actually asks

A will needs a person with testamentary capacity, a writing, a signature, and witnesses in most states. Nothing in that list requires sight, hearing, a steady hand or a voice. The capacity test looks at your mind. Do you know you are making a will, roughly what you own, who your family is, and who gets what? If yes, the body is a logistics problem, not a legal one.

That said, a disability changes what a court will want to see later. The main worry in a contest is not "she was blind." It is "did she know what the paper said?" Someone who cannot read the will herself, or who communicated through a relative, invites that question. A good signing answers it on the day.

## How does each limitation change the signing?

| Limit | What usually works | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blind or low vision | Read the whole will aloud; offer braille or large print; you confirm it matches your wishes in front of the witnesses | Having a child who benefits read it to you |
| Cannot read (illiteracy, dyslexia, stroke) | Same as above, plus a plain-language summary and questions in your own words | Signing a document you only had summarized by a beneficiary |
| Deaf or hard of hearing | Written questions and answers, a certified interpreter, a hearing loop; keep the notes | An interpreter who is a beneficiary or close relative |
| Cannot speak | Writing, a letter board, a speech device, reliable nods to yes and no questions | Anything where one family member "translates" for you |
| Cannot write or hold a pen | A mark, a stamp if your state allows it, or another person signing for you at your direction and in your presence | Letting someone sign when you are not in the room |

The last row has a small catch. When another person signs your name, most states want that person to do it in your conscious presence and at your direction, and they often want the witnesses to watch too. Illinois and New Jersey both allow a will to be signed that way, but the details are worth confirming with a lawyer rather than guessing. Do not rely on a form downloaded from a website for this.

## Does the will have to be read aloud?

There is no rule that says every will must be read aloud. The practice exists because it protects you. If you cannot read the will yourself, reading it aloud is the cleanest proof that you heard each paragraph. Courts sometimes presume that a person who signs a will knew its contents. A person who could not read can rebut that presumption, and a challenger will try.

A reasonable routine looks like this:

1. The attorney meets you alone first and asks what you want, in your words.
2. The draft is prepared from those notes, not from a relative's instructions.
3. At signing, the attorney reads each section aloud and pauses for questions.
4. You say, or signal, that this is your will and that it matches what you want.
5. The witnesses watch you do so, then sign in your presence.
6. A [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) is signed in front of a notary, and the attorney keeps a short file note.

None of this is complicated. It takes about an hour. The point is that someone other than the family can later describe what happened.

## What if you cannot speak or sign at all?

This is the hard end of the range, and it usually comes after a stroke, ALS or another neurological condition. The question is not whether the body works but whether the mind can reliably express a choice.

Take Tomas, 66, who had a stroke and cannot speak. He can understand questions and answers with a thumbs up or down, and he can type slowly on a tablet with one hand. His wife wants to leave things as they are, but he has an adult son from a first marriage and has told his attorney, by typing, that he wants the son to receive his workshop tools and half the savings.

The attorney asks the same question two ways, on two different days, to see if his answers stay stable. She has him type the key gifts himself. A speech therapist writes a short letter saying that Tomas understands complex questions and answers consistently. At signing, Tomas makes his mark with his good hand while two witnesses watch. His wife is not in the room.

That is the kind of record that holds up. If Tomas's answers had shifted from day to day, or if he could only respond through his wife, the attorney would stop and say so. A will is the wrong tool then, and a different plan is needed.

## What if no will can be made?

If someone truly cannot show capacity or intent, you cannot fix that with paperwork. Three things then matter.

- **State default rules.** Without a will, your state's intestacy law decides who inherits. For a married person with children, that can mean a split between spouse and children that you would not have chosen. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).
- **Assets that skip the will.** Beneficiary designations, payable-on-death accounts and joint ownership pass outside the will. They may be changeable on a form that is easier to sign than a will, if the bank or plan accepts a mark. See [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).
- **Decisions while you are alive.** A will does nothing until death. For money and health decisions today, you need a power of attorney and a healthcare directive. The article on [getting a power of attorney when someone cannot sign](/learn/power-of-attorney/getting-power-of-attorney-when-someone-cannot-sign) covers that situation in detail.

## Can a family member help set this up?

They can help with logistics: driving, scheduling, bringing a list of accounts. They should not be in the room for the conversation about who gets what, and they should not pick the lawyer, interpret for you or read the will aloud. The reasoning is the same as in any case of possible [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence). A child who helps often also benefits, and a disability makes a challenger's argument easier to tell.

If your family insists on being there, that is worth discussing with the attorney in private, before the signing day. It is a common situation, and there are ways to handle it.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how many witnesses are needed, whether a mark counts, whether another person may sign for you and how they must do it, and whether a notarized affidavit is needed to prove the will. They also differ on when a will can be validly signed by video.
