Dolores, 79, took out a reverse mortgage on her Joliet bungalow six years ago to cover property taxes and home repairs after her husband died. She never told her son Raymond the details, only that "the bank can't make me leave." When Dolores died in March, a letter from the loan servicer arrived at the house two weeks later. It was polite and it had a date on it.

That date is what this page is about. A reverse mortgage works differently from the mortgage most people carry, and heirs get less time than they expect. Here is what happens by default, the numbers on a realistic example, and what your family can still do. It is one of many [what-if scenarios](/learn/what-if) where default rules decide for you.

## What happens by default?

A reverse mortgage lets an older homeowner borrow against the house and pay nothing back while living there. The balance grows because interest and fees are added each month. The loan comes due, in the language of the paperwork "due and payable," when the last borrower dies, sells or permanently moves out.

When that happens, five things follow.

1. **The servicer sends a notice.** It is addressed to the estate or to the borrower's address, and it states the amount owed and the date by which someone must act.
2. **Heirs get a short window to respond.** For the common federally insured type, called a home equity conversion mortgage or HECM, the heirs generally have about 30 days to tell the servicer what they plan to do.
3. **There is a period to resolve it.** The usual period is about six months to sell, pay off or sign the house over. Servicers can often grant extensions in steps, up to about a year in total, when the family is making real progress. The loan documents and the servicer's own notice are the final word.
4. **Interest keeps running.** Every month of delay adds to the balance and reduces what is left for the family.
5. **If nothing happens, the lender forecloses.** Both Illinois and New Jersey handle foreclosure through the courts, which gives some time, but fighting a foreclosure is expensive and rarely worth it when the real issue is a missed deadline.

The ownership of the house itself is not changed by the loan. Whoever inherits under the will, the trust or the intestacy rules receives the house subject to the lender's lien. ## What are the heirs' choices?

There are four, and they are not equal.

| Choice | How it works | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Sell the house | List it, pay the loan from the sale, and the family keeps the leftover equity | The home is worth more than the balance |
| Keep it and pay the lesser amount | Pay the lesser of the full balance or about 95 percent of the current appraised value, with cash or a new loan | Heirs want the house and the balance is higher than the value |
| Deed in lieu | Sign the house over to the lender with no sale | The house is worth less than the balance and no one wants it |
| Do nothing | Foreclosure follows | Only if the house is truly a burden |

The 95 percent option is the one most families do not hear about. Because the loan is non-recourse, the borrower and the heirs cannot be made to pay more than the house is worth. Federal insurance covers any shortfall to the lender. The servicer is expected to order an appraisal, and heirs can generally ask for a copy.

## A realistic example with invented numbers

Take Dolores's house. A real estate agent estimates it at $320,000. Six years of draws, interest and mortgage insurance have pushed the balance to $340,000.

- **Sell:** The sale price of about $320,000 pays most of the loan. The servicer accepts the sale proceeds for the full payoff, which the non-recourse rule allows, and the estate receives nothing. No one owes the other $20,000 or the sale costs.
- **Keep the house:** Raymond wants it. The 95 percent figure is $304,000 ($320,000 times 0.95), which is less than the $340,000 balance. He can pay $304,000, perhaps with a new mortgage, and keep the house. That is a $36,000 saving over the balance.

Now change the numbers. The house appraises at $320,000 and the balance is $150,000. The loan is well covered, so the family sells, pays $150,000 and the estate receives roughly $170,000 before agent fees and closing costs.

The lesson is the same either way: find the balance and the appraised value in the first two weeks, because every other decision depends on those two numbers.

## What if my spouse is still in the house?

A spouse who stays in the home is the situation families worry about most. The answer depends on what the loan paperwork said at the start.

- **Spouse was a co-borrower:** The loan does not come due when the first borrower dies. It stays in place until the second one dies or leaves.
- **Spouse was named at closing as a non-borrowing spouse:** For HECM loans made in recent years, this spouse can generally stay after the borrower dies, with repayment deferred, provided they meet the conditions in the loan documents. Typically that means keeping up taxes, insurance and the home itself, and proving they have a legal right to remain, such as being on the deed or having a right to occupy it. The servicer will give a deadline for providing proof.
- **Spouse was not on the loan and not listed:** There is no automatic protection. An older couple who put only one name on the loan to qualify for a larger payment sometimes discover this late.

If you are in this position, call the servicer immediately and ask in writing which category applies and what the deadline is. Do not assume a verbal answer will hold.

## What can still be fixed?

Whether you are the borrower or the family, there are concrete steps.

### If the borrower is still alive

1. **Give the family the loan details.** Name of the servicer, loan number, balance and phone number, plus a copy of the closing papers.
2. **Confirm who counts as a borrower.** If a spouse is not on the loan, ask the servicer in writing what protections they have and whether the status can be documented now.
3. **Keep the house in good repair and the taxes paid.** Falling behind on property tax, insurance or maintenance can trigger a default while you are living.
4. **Review the plan together.** The reverse mortgage may change which assets are equal. If one child inherits a house with little equity and another receives cash, the shares in your will may no longer be fair. Our page on [reverse mortgages and Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/reverse-mortgage-vs-medicaid-planning) shows how the loan affects other decisions.

### If the borrower has died

1. **Locate the notice and write down the dates.** Put the deadline on a calendar.
2. **Call the servicer.** Give your name and relationship and ask for the payoff amount, the appraisal process and the extension rules. Servicers often will not discuss details with someone who lacks authority, but they usually must tell you the deadlines.
3. **Open the estate if you need to.** Selling a house held in the decedent's name generally requires an executor with court authority. Our article on whether [heirs can sell the house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) explains how to start.
4. **Get an appraisal and a market opinion.** The two numbers tell you whether to sell, keep or sign over.
5. **Ask for extensions in writing** the moment a sale is under contract or an estate petition is filed.

## How does state law change this?

The federal rules for insured reverse mortgages are the same in every state. What differs is the process around them: who can sell the house, how long probate takes and how foreclosure works.

In Illinois, heirs usually need letters of office from the circuit court before an executor can sign a deed. See [inheriting a house in Illinois](/learn/illinois/inheriting-a-house-in-illinois) and the state guide for [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois). In New Jersey, the county Surrogate issues letters, and tax waivers can add time to a sale. See [inheriting a house in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/inheriting-a-house-in-new-jersey) and the state guide for [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey).

Privately issued reverse mortgages, sometimes called proprietary loans, follow their own contracts. Read the document rather than relying on the HECM rules above.

## How to prevent a rushed sale

Three habits keep the clock manageable:

- A **list of loans and the servicer's contact** kept with your will, so the executor finds the loan in the first week.
- A **durable power of attorney** that lets an agent deal with the servicer if you cannot, covered in our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney).
- A **will or trust that anticipates the loan**, so the person who inherits the house has the cash or the authority to pay it off.
