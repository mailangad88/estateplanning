Margaret was 84 and had been living with Alzheimer's for about three years. In March she signed a new will that left her house to the niece who drove her to appointments, and cut her two sons from half of it to nothing. Margaret died that autumn. Her sons want to know whether they can undo it.

They may be able to, but a diagnosis is not the test. This article explains what a court actually asks about dementia and a will, how the deadlines work in Illinois and New Jersey, and what evidence tends to win or lose these cases. If you are the one making a will while living with memory loss, our article on [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure) covers the planning side.

## Does a dementia diagnosis make a will invalid?

No. The law presumes a signed, witnessed will is valid, and a diagnosis does not overturn that presumption. What matters is testamentary capacity: whether the person, at the time of signing, had enough understanding to make the will. Courts generally ask four things.

1. Did the person know they were making a will?
2. Did they have a general idea of what they owned?
3. Did they know who their closest relatives were, the people a will would normally provide for?
4. Could they connect those pieces and decide who gets what?

The standard is lower than most families expect. A person can forget the date, repeat a story three times and still pass it. A person who cannot say whether they have children, or who believes they sold the house years ago, does not.

Dementia also comes and goes. Judges and lawyers talk about lucid intervals, hours or days when a person is clearer. A will signed in one of those can stand even if the medical file shows severe decline a month either side.

## Capacity and undue influence are different claims

Families often mix these up, and the difference decides how a case is built.

| | Lack of capacity | Undue influence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it says | The person could not understand the will | The person understood but someone else overrode their free choice |
| Focus | The person's mind on the signing day | The relationship and what the helper did |
| Typical proof | Doctor notes, cognitive tests, witnesses | Who arranged the lawyer, who was in the room, sudden changes, isolation |
| Common fact pattern | Advanced dementia, a hospital stay | A caregiver or one child managing everything |

Dementia cases usually plead both. A frail person is easier to influence, so evidence of confusion supports each claim. Our [undue influence glossary entry](/glossary/undue-influence) gives the short definition.

Both states treat a gift to a helper with extra care. In New Jersey, a confidential relationship plus suspicious circumstances can shift the burden to the person who benefits, as described in our guide to [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats). In Illinois, a will that benefits someone who held a fiduciary role, such as a power of attorney agent, and who helped procure it can draw the same kind of presumption. That is a reason to be careful about who sits in the lawyer's office.

## What if a caregiver or one child was added late in life?

A late change is a red flag, not a verdict. Courts look at the whole picture. A parent who always planned to reward the child who moved home to care for her is different from a parent who signed a first-ever will, drafted by a lawyer the caregiver found, one week after a hospital discharge.

Facts that tend to raise concern:

- The beneficiary chose the lawyer, made the appointment or stayed in the room.
- The new will departs sharply from earlier wills, with no explanation.
- The person had been cut off from other family or friends.
- The beneficiary also held the power of attorney or the person's bank cards.
- The signing happened close to a hospitalization, a fall, a medication change or a documented decline.

Facts that tend to defend the will: a lawyer who met the person alone, a note explaining the reason for the change, earlier drafts leaning the same way, and two independent witnesses.

## How do you prove capacity or lack of it?

With records created around the signing, not opinions formed later.

For a family challenging the will, the useful sources are:

- Primary care, neurology and memory clinic notes within a few weeks of signing.
- Cognitive screening scores and the dates they were taken.
- Hospital, rehab and memory-care charts, including medication lists.
- The drafting lawyer's file and the witnesses' accounts.
- Emails, texts and voicemails that show how the person was thinking.

For a family defending a will, the same records matter. One practical point: if you hold a parent's power of attorney, you can usually see these records while the parent is alive, but after death you generally need the executor's authority or a court order. A family that expects a fight should ask an attorney how to preserve medical and bank records before they are lost.

A medical review of the chart after death is common, and it is more persuasive when it rests on contemporaneous notes. A physician who never met the person is arguing from paper. A physician who examined her a week before signing is giving evidence.

## What are the deadlines in Illinois and New Jersey?

Short, and strict.

| | Illinois | New Jersey |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deadline to contest | 6 months after the will is admitted to probate (755 ILCS 5/8-1) | Generally 4 months after probate (R. 4:85-1) |
| Where | The probate court handling the estate | Superior Court, Probate Part |
| Early warning step | Watch for the notice of the will's admission | File a caveat with the county Surrogate before probate |
| Who can usually file | Heirs and people named in an earlier will | Heirs and people named in an earlier will |

Missing the deadline usually ends the dispute, even when the facts are good. Our pages on the [Illinois six-month contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) and the [probate objection process](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate) walk through filing. If you only just learned about the will, count from the date it was admitted, not from the date you found out.

Margaret's sons, in an Illinois example, would count six months from the day the court admitted the will. If they wait for the funeral, the holidays and a "cooling-off" period, a good part of the window can pass.

## What happens if the will is thrown out?

The court looks to the next valid will. If the new will falls but an earlier one survives, the earlier will controls. If there is no earlier will, the estate passes by intestacy to the closest relatives. In Margaret's case, the earlier will leaving everything to her sons would probably come back.

One thing to keep in mind: contests cost money and time, and they split families. The strongest claims are the ones with specific, dated evidence. If the main complaint is that the will seems unfair, that alone is not a legal ground. A parent may leave unequal shares. Our guide to [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains who has standing and what to expect.

## What can you do now if you are worried about a parent?

If a parent is still alive and showing memory loss, the most protective step is to make or update documents early, while capacity is clear.

1. Book the will signing for the time of day the parent is sharpest.
2. Have the attorney meet the parent alone, and write down the questions asked and the answers given.
3. Ask the treating doctor for a short letter dated the same week.
4. Use two witnesses with no stake in the will, and a self-proving affidavit where the state allows one.
5. Write a brief note, kept with the will, explaining an unequal gift. Courts weigh it heavily.
6. Sign the power of attorney and the healthcare directive in the same visit. See [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

If the parent can no longer meet the capacity test, a new will is not possible, and the family should look at other options with a lawyer. Our [what-if guide](/learn/what-if) covers the related situations.
