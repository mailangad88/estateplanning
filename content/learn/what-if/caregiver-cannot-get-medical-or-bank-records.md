Tomas drives his mother, Elena, to every appointment. He picks up her prescriptions and pays her bills when she hands him a pile of envelopes. When her cardiologist's office changes a medication, the nurse will not tell him why. When the electric company threatens to shut off service, the bank will not let him look at her account.

Tomas is doing the work of an agent without the paper of one. This page explains what record-keepers can and cannot share, what that looks like in practice and which documents change it. For the broader picture of helping a parent plan, see [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan), and for the full set of questions see the [what-if pillar](/learn/what-if).

## What happens by default

Two separate systems say no, for two separate reasons.

Medical records are protected by privacy law. Health providers are allowed to share information with a family member who is involved in care when the patient agrees or does not object, and may use judgment when the patient is unable to answer. In practice, front desks and portals are cautious. Many staff will only talk to someone with a signed release on file. They are also free to refuse, since the rule permits sharing but rarely requires it.

Banks have no equivalent flexibility. The bank's customer is the account owner. It will talk to the owner, a joint owner or someone with proper legal authority such as an agent under a power of attorney, a trustee or a court-appointed conservator. A caregiver who is none of those can be turned away even if the owner is in the next room, and even if the bills are overdue.

| What you need | Who can open it | Document that gives a caregiver access |
|---|---|---|
| Doctor's notes, test results, billing | The patient | HIPAA authorization |
| A say in treatment when the patient cannot decide | A named agent | Healthcare power of attorney |
| Bank statements, paying bills | Owner, joint owner, agent | Durable financial power of attorney |
| Social Security or VA payments | The beneficiary or an appointed representative | Representative payee or fiduciary appointment |
| Anything, if the person cannot sign | A court | Guardianship or conservatorship |

## A realistic example

Tomas calls the bank with his mother in the room. The representative will not discuss the account without the owner on the line. Elena, who is 83 and hard of hearing, gets frustrated and hangs up. The bill goes unpaid, a late fee accrues, and the pharmacy's insurance letter goes unanswered.

Tomas does the sensible thing. He takes a day, and a lawyer's appointment is made. Elena still understands what she is signing, so the attorney prepares a durable power of attorney for finances, a healthcare power of attorney, a HIPAA authorization and a simple will. The documents are signed that week, and the bank reviews its power of attorney form within days. The time and cost drivers are the person's capacity, how fast the bank reviews the document and whether the family needs a court at all.

If Elena had already lost the ability to understand the documents, the same day would have led somewhere else: a petition for guardianship and conservatorship, months of paperwork and court fees. The difference is whether the person can sign.

## What can still be fixed?

**While the person can still sign:**

1. Book an appointment with an estate planning attorney and tell them the person's capacity may be changing. Earlier in the day often works better.
2. Sign a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) naming the caregiver, plus any others who should be kept informed. Ask each provider to put a copy in the chart.
3. Sign a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) so someone can make decisions if the person cannot.
4. Sign a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney). Ask the bank in advance whether it accepts the form, and see [when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).
5. Ask the bank about limited options, such as a third-party view-only access or a bill-pay arrangement, that do not add a joint owner.
6. Keep copies in a shared folder, and carry a card with the agent's name and phone number.

**If the person cannot sign anything:**

1. Ask the provider whether they will share limited information under the family-involved-in-care rule, and ask for the hospital's social worker.
2. For benefits, apply to be representative payee. The agency has its own process, described on ssa.gov.
3. For bank accounts and property, a conservatorship or guardianship may be the only route. See [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) and the page on a [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).
4. Meanwhile, pay urgent bills in ways that leave a record, and do not move money into your own name.

## Why adding the caregiver to the account is not a clean fix

When the bank says no, the quickest idea is to add the child as a joint owner. It works in the narrow sense that the child can then see and move the money. The side effects are what bother lawyers.

A joint owner's creditors, a divorce or a lawsuit can reach the account. At death, a joint account with survivorship usually passes to the surviving owner, not under the will, so siblings who were meant to share equally may find the account went to one. For a parent who may need Medicaid, adding a child can be treated as a transfer. And the child may owe gift tax reporting in some cases.

A better pattern for day-to-day help is a power of attorney, which gives authority without ownership. Some banks also offer convenience or view-only access. For other options, see [can a power of attorney or guardian change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary) for what an agent can and cannot do, and our guide to [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do).

## How state law changes this

The federal privacy rule is the same everywhere, but the provider's own policies vary. State law controls the power of attorney statute, whether witnesses or a notary are needed, whether a bank must accept a form and the process for guardianship. Some states have statutory forms and penalties for banks that refuse them without reason. Check your state guide, such as [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) or [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), and confirm the local rule.

## How to prevent it

The prevention is a one-hour meeting, ideally before there is a crisis. Three signed documents cover most of it: a HIPAA release, a healthcare power of attorney and a financial power of attorney. Add a short list of accounts and providers so the agent can start on day one. A caregiver who is paid or who gives up work should also have a written agreement, as discussed in [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

The [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) will show what a parent is missing. The scenario for a young adult who turns 18 is covered in [medical emergency without a HIPAA release](/learn/what-if/adult-child-medical-emergency-no-hipaa).
