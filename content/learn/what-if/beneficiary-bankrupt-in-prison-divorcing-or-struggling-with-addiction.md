Tom, 62, named his son Eli as the sole beneficiary of his $200,000 life insurance policy and his IRA when Eli was in college. Eli is now 35, going through a divorce and carrying a heavy credit card debt, and Tom has started to worry. He has been told that a trust might help, but he has never changed a beneficiary form and does not know whether he can.

Parents often write the form in one year and live with it for thirty. People's lives change faster than forms do. This article explains what actually happens when the person on the form is in trouble, and what the owner can do while there is time.

## What happens by default

A beneficiary designation is a contract between you and the company. It says: when I die, pay this person. The company does exactly that. It does not ask whether the person has debts, a pending divorce or a drinking problem. It does not hold the money back or pay it out in installments, unless you chose a payout option when you filled out the form.

So the money lands in the beneficiary's name, as an adult's own funds. What happens next depends on the trouble.

<!-- visual: whatif id=beneficiary-form-overrides-will -->

| Situation | What usually happens to the money |
|---|---|
| Beneficiary has creditors, not in bankruptcy | Ordinary creditors can pursue it once the beneficiary holds it, with limits that vary by state and by account type |
| Beneficiary files bankruptcy | An inheritance received within 180 days after the filing date generally becomes part of the bankruptcy estate |
| Beneficiary in prison | The money is theirs, but courts may reach it for restitution, fines or fees, and rules differ by state |
| Beneficiary divorcing | Inheritance is usually separate property, unless mixed into joint accounts or used for the home |
| Beneficiary with addiction | Nothing stops a lump sum from being spent fast, and the owner has no say after death |

### Bankruptcy

Federal bankruptcy law pulls certain property into the estate if the debtor becomes entitled to it within 180 days after filing, including an inheritance or life insurance payout. A beneficiary who files and then receives a death benefit can lose it to the trustee. A beneficiary who receives the money long before filing can spend it freely, or lose it to creditors who sue. For the rules on who can reach what, see [can creditors take what a beneficiary inherits](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits).

### Divorce

In most states an inheritance is the heir's separate property, which a divorce court does not divide. The protection weakens when it is deposited into a joint account, used to pay down a shared mortgage or otherwise mixed with marital money. Our article on [whether an inheritance is marital property](/learn/after-a-death/is-an-inheritance-marital-property) explains the mixing problem.

### Prison

An inmate can inherit. The practical problems are access, who manages the property while the person is away, and claims for restitution or costs. Some states let the government claim part of an inmate's funds. A trustee can manage the money and pay for the person's needs where the rules allow.

## A realistic example

Tom dies with the forms unchanged. The insurer pays Eli the full $200,000 within weeks. The IRA, because Eli is not a spouse, must be emptied within ten years under the [10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals), and each withdrawal is taxable income to Eli.

Eli's divorce is not final. He deposits the insurance money in the joint account he shares with his wife to cover a mortgage payment. Months later, his wife's lawyer argues the funds were mixed. A credit card company, holding a judgment against him, serves the bank with a levy. Within a year, little of the $200,000 remains, and the IRA withdrawals arrive on top.

Nothing here was illegal. Tom's plan simply gave Eli control of a large sum at the worst time, and gave no one else the right to slow it down.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are the owner and can still sign

1. **Decide what you are protecting.** Is it a creditor problem, a spouse risk, a health problem or just caution? The answer shapes the trust.
2. **Name a trust as the beneficiary.** A [trust for the beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) lets a trustee decide when and how to pay out. The trust can include a [spendthrift clause](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts), which in many states keeps creditors away from money still inside the trust.
3. **Pick the trustee with care.** A sibling, a friend, a bank or a professional can serve. Avoid someone the beneficiary can easily pressure.
4. **Write clear payout terms.** Options include paying housing and medical bills directly, giving small monthly amounts, or releasing larger sums at set ages.
5. **Treat retirement accounts separately.** Naming a trust on an IRA has tax consequences, so get advice on the trust's wording before you sign.
6. **Update the contingent beneficiary.** If the first person cannot take the money, [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) decide where it goes next.
7. **Tell your family.** Explain the reasoning in a letter so no one is surprised.

### If the owner has died or cannot sign

1. **The beneficiary may disclaim.** A [disclaimer](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) passes the money to the next person in line, if it is done properly and before any benefit is taken. It does not always work against creditors or in bankruptcy, so get advice first.
2. **Keep inherited money separate.** Open a new account in only the heir's name and do not mix it with joint funds.
3. **Ask a lawyer before the claim is filed.** Bankruptcy timing and divorce timing can turn a good outcome into a bad one.

## Talking to the beneficiary

Many owners avoid changing the form because they dread the conversation. A trust does not have to read as a verdict. You can tell your child that the trust protects the money from a lawsuit, a divorce or a bad year, and that it is the same structure you would use for anyone you love.

Some families go further and let the beneficiary have a role. The trust might name the beneficiary as co-trustee at an age you pick, or allow them to ask the trustee for a purchase such as a car or a down payment. That keeps dignity intact while the trustee holds the brake. If the problem is addiction, you can also allow the trustee to pay a treatment program directly, which is hard to do with an outright payment.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how much a beneficiary's creditors can reach, whether life insurance proceeds get any protection, how inheritances are treated in a divorce, and whether the prison system can take funds. Spendthrift trust rules also vary. Check the [state guides](/estate-planning/new-york) and ask an attorney before relying on a rule you read online.
