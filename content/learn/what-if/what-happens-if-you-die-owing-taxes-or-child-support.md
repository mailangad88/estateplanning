Walter, 67, a self-employed electrician in Trenton, put off his 2025 tax payment because business was slow. He also fell behind on child support for a teenage daughter after a layoff. When he died in February, his son from his first marriage asked the question that most families ask: "Do we owe this now?"

Short answer, no. The longer answer covers who gets paid first, what an executor must watch for, and the few ways family members can end up on the hook anyway. This page covers taxes and child support, the debts owed to government offices, and fits among the other [what-if scenarios](/learn/what-if) families run into. For consumer debts such as cards and loans, our guide to [what happens when an estate owes more than it has](/learn/probate/what-happens-when-an-estate-owes-more-than-it-has) is the better starting point.

## What happens by default?

A debt is a claim against the estate, which is the property the person owned at death. The executor, or an administrator if there was no will, collects the assets, sorts the claims and pays them in the order state law sets. What is left goes to the heirs. If nothing is left, the heirs receive nothing and the unpaid portion usually goes away. See our overview of [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

Three features make government debts different.

- **Taxes do not stop at death.** The person's final income tax return is still due on the normal date, filed by the executor. Any tax owed for earlier years that was never paid also remains. Our page on [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) lists the returns an executor files.
- **Federal claims have priority.** Under a federal priority rule, when an estate cannot pay everyone, claims of the United States come ahead of many others. An executor who knew or should have known about a federal claim and paid other creditors or distributed assets first can be personally liable up to the amount paid out. This is the single biggest risk in this situation, and it is why the executor should confirm the tax position early.
- **Liens attach to property.** A recorded tax lien on a house stays with the house. The tax is paid at a sale or refinance, usually from the proceeds.

The family's own money is not in the pile. Children, siblings and parents are not responsible for a relative's tax bill simply because they are related.

## Who ends up responsible anyway?

A handful of people can be liable, and each one has a reason that does not depend on the death.

| Person | Why they might owe | How to check |
|---|---|---|
| Surviving spouse who filed a joint return | Each spouse is generally liable for the whole tax on a joint return | Look at the signed return and any IRS notice |
| Executor | Paid other creditors or heirs before a known government claim | Keep a written ledger of what was paid and when |
| Heir who received estate property early | The estate had unpaid taxes and the property can be pursued | Ask the executor for a written confirmation before spending |
| Co-owner or co-signer | Signed for a business loan or a lien-bearing obligation | Read what you signed |
| Parent who owes its own support | Child support the survivor owes remains theirs | Ask the court or child support agency for a balance |

An adult child who is only an heir, and who took nothing, owes nothing.

## A realistic example with invented numbers

Walter's estate looked like this when the executor, his sister Gail, finished the inventory.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Checking account in his name | $14,000 |
| Truck | $9,000 |
| Tools and equipment | $6,500 |
| Life insurance payable to his daughter (outside the estate) | $100,000 |
| Unpaid 2025 federal balance, with interest and penalty | $11,200 |
| Final-year return balance | $1,900 |
| Child support arrears | $3,600 |
| Funeral bill | $7,800 |
| Court fees and attorney fees | $5,500 |

The probate assets add up to $29,500. Costs of administration and a reasonable funeral usually come first, which takes $13,300, leaving $16,200. The federal balance is $11,200 and the final return is $1,900, together $13,100. After those, $3,100 remains, which falls short of the $3,600 in arrears. Whether child support arrears rank before or after taxes depends on the state, so Gail should have a local attorney confirm the order before paying. The key point for the family is that nothing came from Gail's pocket. The daughter's $100,000 insurance went to her directly and was not touched by the tax claim, and the arrears were settled as far as the estate could cover them.

Had Gail given the truck to Walter's son and paid the funeral home first, she might have paid out money that belonged to the tax claim.

## What can still be fixed?

If a person with tax or support debts is still alive, the steps are practical.

1. **Get a transcript.** The IRS and the state tax office will show what they think is owed. Many debts are smaller than the person fears.
2. **Set up a payment plan or ask about relief.** A plan sometimes beats a lump sum, and a lump sum from the estate is not the same as a plan now.
3. **Check how child support is secured.** If a court order requires life insurance, keep the policy in force and the beneficiary current.
4. **Name beneficiaries deliberately.** Accounts that pass by beneficiary form generally avoid probate and ordinary creditors, but not every claim, so do not rely on them to hide a debt.

If the person has died and you are the executor or a family member:

1. **Do not pay anyone yet.** Make a list of claims and assets first.
2. **Notify the IRS with Form 56** and request transcripts. Our guide to [IRS paperwork for an executor](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810) shows how, including the request that speeds up the IRS's determination of the tax.
3. **File the final return** and any missing prior-year returns.
4. **Pay in the legal order** and keep a ledger.
5. **Tell the child's other parent or the support agency.** An open order may need to be modified or settled, and the child may be eligible for Social Security survivor payments. See [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death).
6. **Hold back distributions** until the tax position is clear. Most executors wait for confirmation that the final return is processed.

## What about student loans and other government debts?

Federal student loans are discharged when the borrower dies, and a Parent PLUS loan is discharged if either the parent or the student dies. Private loans follow the contract, and a co-signer may owe. See [mortgages, car loans and student loans after death](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-car-loan-and-student-loans-after-death) for the full picture.

Medicaid recovery is a government claim too, and it is its own subject. Illinois and New Jersey both have estate recovery programs; see [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) and [New Jersey estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery).

## How does state law change this?

States differ on the order of payment, how long creditors have, and whether child support arrears can be claimed. Illinois and New Jersey both set a deadline for creditor claims. In Illinois, claims are barred two years after death at the latest, and in New Jersey, creditors generally must present claims within nine months. Federal tax claims have their own timing and are not cut off by a state claims window in the same way.

State death taxes are not the person's own debts, but they are paid from the estate too. New Jersey has an inheritance tax, with a return and payment due eight months after death. Illinois has an estate tax on larger estates. See [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey), and the pages on [New Jersey's inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) and the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).
