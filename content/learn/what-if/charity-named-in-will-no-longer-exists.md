Dolores left $25,000 in her will to "the Riverside Animal Shelter." She wrote the will in 2012 and filed it away. By the time she died in 2026, Riverside had merged with a county humane society, closed its building, and moved its adoption work to another town. The executor, her nephew Paul, was not sure who to write the check to.

This kind of problem is easy to miss because charities change slowly and wills sit untouched for years. Here is what happens to a charitable gift when the recipient has changed, closed or never quite existed in the form you described.

## What happens by default

Start with the executor. Before paying a charitable gift, the executor tries to identify the organization the will meant. Three situations come up again and again, and each has a different default result.

| What happened to the charity | What usually happens to the gift |
|---|---|
| Renamed | The gift goes to the same organization under its new name |
| Merged into another charity | The gift generally goes to the surviving organization, if it carries on the work |
| Closed or dissolved before you died | The court may redirect it to a similar charity (cy pres), or the gift lapses |
| Closed after you died, but before payment | Depends on the state and the timing, so the executor should ask a court before paying anyone |
| Never existed as described (a wrong or vague name) | The court tries to work out which organization you meant, which can take a hearing |
| Refuses the gift | The gift fails unless the will has a backup |

A renamed organization is the easy case. Paul can call the shelter's successor, confirm its tax ID and legal name in writing, and send the money with a letter. A merged charity is nearly as simple if the successor still carries out the same mission.

The hard cases are the closed charity and the vague description. Then a judge, often with the state attorney general's office in the room, decides what to do. Many states treat the attorney general as the protector of charitable gifts, and the office is commonly notified when a court is asked to redirect one.

### What is cy pres?

Cy pres is a French-derived phrase meaning "as near as possible." When a charitable gift cannot be carried out as written, a court may direct it to a charity that does work close to what the donor wanted. The key requirement is that the will must show a general charitable purpose. A gift "to help stray animals in Riverside" suggests one. A gift to "Riverside Animal Shelter, for its building fund, and for no other purpose" suggests the opposite, and a court might let it fail.

If a court will not apply cy pres, the gift lapses. The money then drops into the [residuary clause](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts), which names who gets everything left over. If the residue itself was the charity's gift, it goes to your heirs under state law. The ordinary lapse rules are covered in our article on [lapsed gifts](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death). The usual anti-lapse statutes protect close relatives, not organizations.

## A realistic example

Paul reads the will and finds the sentence: "I give $25,000 to the Riverside Animal Shelter." It does not say why, and it has no backup.

He calls the county humane society, which confirms it took over Riverside's assets and programs in 2021. Its lawyer writes a letter stating that it is the legal successor. Paul gives the letter to the probate attorney, and the court approves a distribution to the humane society. The whole thing adds a few weeks to the estate's closing and a modest amount of attorney time.

Now change one fact. Suppose Riverside had dissolved with no successor and given its remaining cash to a bankruptcy trustee. The attorney would then petition the court for cy pres. The judge would ask whether Dolores meant to support animal welfare in general. A line in her will such as "I love animals" might be enough. Without it, her estate might go to her residuary beneficiary, her niece, and the animals would see nothing.

The time and cost drivers in a case like this are the court hearing, notice to the attorney general, and how fast the successor organization produces its papers.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are alive and can still sign

1. **Check each charity named in your will and trust.** Look it up on the organization's own website and in the IRS tax-exempt search at irs.gov. Confirm the legal name, address and tax ID number.
2. **Update the wording.** Use the full legal name and the city. A bequest to "the cancer society" has been fought over by more than one organization.
3. **Add a backup.** For example: to the named charity, or if it no longer exists, to a charity chosen by my executor that carries on similar work.
4. **Say what the gift is for.** A sentence about purpose gives a court the evidence it needs for cy pres.
5. **Check beneficiary forms.** Charities are often named on IRAs, life insurance or donor-advised funds. Those forms ignore your will. See [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).
6. **Think about timing.** If you give regularly during life, a gift in the will may be small next to what you do each year. Some people use a [donor-advised fund or a qualified charitable distribution](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds) instead.

### If it is now too late

1. **Executor: identify the charity.** Search state charity registries, the IRS tax-exempt search and local news for merger announcements.
2. **Ask the charity or its successor for written confirmation** of its legal name, tax ID and acceptance of the gift.
3. **Read the whole will.** Look for a backup, a stated purpose, or a statement of general charitable intent.
4. **Talk to a probate attorney** if the charity is gone. A cy pres petition is a court filing, and the attorney general is usually given notice.
5. **Do not pay the money to a similar charity on your own.** If the court has not approved it, the executor could be personally responsible.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how readily courts use cy pres, whether the attorney general must be notified, and whether a gift to a dissolved organization lapses or is redirected. Some states presume general charitable intent unless the will says otherwise. Others require proof. Rules for gifts to churches, to unincorporated groups and to organizations that cease to exist before the will is signed also vary. See our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides, and ask an attorney about local practice.

## How to prevent it

Pull out your will every few years and read only the charitable gifts. Ask three questions: does the organization still exist, does it still do the work I care about, and is my backup still the right one? Our [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) is a quick way to schedule that check.

The better drafting habits are small. Use the legal name. Add a backup. Add a sentence about purpose. If your gift is large, call the charity's planned giving office before you sign, since some will tell you how they prefer to be named and whether they can honor a restriction. Plan for the gift to be a percentage of the estate, rather than a fixed sum, if your assets may change a lot.
