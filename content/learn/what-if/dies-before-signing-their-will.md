Daniel met with a lawyer in March and approved the draft of his will. The signing appointment was set for the following Thursday. He died on Tuesday. His daughters now hold a finished-looking document with their father's name on it and no signature at the bottom.

This page explains what the law does with a will that was never signed, when a court might still accept it, and what a family can do from here. If you are the one holding the draft and the person is alive, skip to the fix section, because the answer is simple. For how a signing is supposed to work, see [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

## What happens by default

A will is a formal document, and the formality is the point. State law lists what makes one valid: usually a signature by the person, and two witnesses who watched or heard them acknowledge it. Until that happens, what exists is a draft. Courts treat a draft as evidence of what someone was thinking, not as an instruction.

So the estate is handled as if there were no will. Under [intestacy law](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will), the state's formula decides who inherits, starting with a spouse and children and moving outward to parents, siblings and more distant relatives. A court appoints an administrator rather than the executor the draft named. The draft may suggest who to pick, and judges often honor that, but they do not have to.

Assets that never pass through the will are unaffected. A life insurance policy pays its named beneficiary. A house held jointly with a right of survivorship goes to the surviving owner. A [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) that was signed and funded continues under its own terms. Only the property that would have been governed by the will falls into intestacy.

## When can a court accept an unsigned will?

A growing number of states have adopted what lawyers call a harmless error or dispensing power rule. It lets a judge treat a document as a will, even though a formality was missed, if the family proves by clear and convincing evidence that the person intended it to be their will. That is a high standard. Many other states still insist on strict compliance and will not make exceptions.

Courts that do use the rule tend to look at the same questions:

1. Was the document finished, or still being edited?
2. Did the person approve the final text, in writing or out loud?
3. Was a signing scheduled, and did something outside their control stop it?
4. Did the person tell anyone it was their will?
5. Does the draft match what they said for years?

Daniel's case helps on several of these. The lawyer's file shows an approved final draft and a booked appointment. A court could be asked to accept it. Even so, the daughters would be asking a judge for an exception, and a sibling who would inherit more under intestacy has every reason to object.

## A realistic example

Daniel was widowed and had two daughters, Priya and Anjali. His draft left the house to Priya, who lived nearby and had been caring for him, and split the savings equally. Intestacy law in his state would divide everything equally between the two daughters.

Priya's lawyer explains her options. She can ask Anjali to sign a family settlement that follows the draft. Anjali is fond of her sister and agrees after reading the lawyer's notes. The agreement is put in writing and filed with the probate court. If Anjali had said no, Priya could have petitioned the court to accept the draft and brought the lawyer's file as evidence. That would add hearings and legal fees, and the result would be uncertain.

Time and cost drivers here are the number of heirs, whether any of them disagree, whether the court needs to appoint an administrator first, and how much the lawyer's evidence can show about Daniel's final intentions.

## What can still be fixed?

**If the person is alive and can still act:**

1. Sign now. If the appointment was weeks away, ask whether the lawyer can come to the person's home or hospital room sooner.
2. Bring the right people. Two adults who will not inherit should witness. A relative who benefits should not be one of them.
3. Ask about a notarized self-proving affidavit so the will is easy to prove later.
4. Do not change the text at the signing table unless the person insists. Edits made by hand are a common reason for challenges.
5. Fill in the beneficiary forms in the meantime. Those take effect when the institution receives them and do not wait for a will.

**If the person has already died:**

1. Ask the drafting lawyer for the complete file, including notes, emails and the appointment record.
2. Talk to a probate attorney about whether your state accepts an unsigned document and what proof it needs.
3. If the heirs are adults and all agree, consider a written family settlement that follows the draft. Have each heir get their own advice if the shares are unequal.
4. If the heirs cannot agree, ask the court to appoint an administrator and let intestacy proceed. See [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate).
5. Consider disclaimers. An heir who would receive more under intestacy can sometimes disclaim part of it so it passes the way the draft intended, subject to rules about when and how.

## What about the other things the person left behind?

An unsigned will does not wipe out the rest of a plan. Think about each piece separately. A power of attorney ends at death anyway, so it plays no role now. A trust that was signed and funded keeps working, and a pour-over will that never got signed only matters for assets that were never moved into the trust. Those leftover assets fall into intestacy, then pour into the trust only if the heirs choose to carry out the plan.

Another common surprise is an older will. If Daniel had signed a will ten years earlier and the new one was meant to replace it, the old one is still valid, because only a properly signed document can revoke it. That can help or hurt. An old will that names a former spouse or leaves out a grandchild born since may be worse than intestacy. Look for it before assuming there is nothing.

Ask also about funeral wishes. A letter of instruction can guide the family, though it does not bind anyone. The [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) article explains what it can and cannot do.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how many witnesses are needed, whether a notary can substitute, whether electronic wills are allowed, and whether a harmless error rule exists at all. Some states also accept handwritten wills without witnesses if the key parts are in the person's own writing, which can matter if the person scribbled a note about their wishes. Look at your state's page, such as [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) or [Ohio](/estate-planning/ohio), and ask a local attorney how courts there treat near misses.

## How to prevent it

Treat the signing as the finish line, not the lawyer's meeting. If someone is ill or older, ask for the earliest possible date, and ask what happens if the date slips. Many firms can sign a short will first and replace it with a fuller plan later. A signed short will beats a perfect unsigned one every time.

Pair the will with a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) and current beneficiary forms so no single document carries everything. The [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) shows what is still missing. More situations like this one are listed in the [what-if library](/learn/what-if), and the [too late for a will](/learn/what-if/too-late-for-a-will) article covers last-minute signings in hospitals.
