Walter, 79, has three children, Dana, Paul and Kim. His will says everything goes to the three of them "in equal shares." That sounds simple. Then you look at what he owns: a house worth about $420,000, a checking account with $30,000, and an IRA worth $200,000 that names Dana alone. Years ago he also paid $60,000 toward Paul's business.

Nobody did anything wrong. The will is clear. But when Walter dies, the three children will not end up with the same thing. This page explains why, and how to write or read a will so "equal" has a firm meaning.

## What does "equal shares" actually divide?

It divides the probate estate, meaning what is in Walter's name alone and has no beneficiary named. Here that is the house and the checking account, about $450,000. Each child is owed one third of that value, about $150,000. The will does not reach the IRA, which goes to Dana under the form. For the sorting rule, see [which document controls: will, trust, joint account or beneficiary form](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form).

| Asset | Value | How it passes | Who gets it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| House | $420,000 | Will, in equal shares | All three, one third each |
| Checking account | $30,000 | Will, in equal shares | All three, one third each |
| IRA | $200,000 | Beneficiary form | Dana alone |
| Business loan years ago | $60,000 | Lifetime gift | Paul, not counted |

Add it up and Dana receives about $350,000, Paul about $210,000 and Kim about $150,000. The will said equal. The result is far from it.

## Three different problems hide in this

Families often lump them together, but each has a different fix.

**1. Non-probate assets.** Beneficiary forms, joint accounts, payable-on-death accounts and trust assets ignore the will. Fix: change the forms to match what you intend, or add an equalizing provision.

**2. Lifetime gifts.** In many states, a gift is a gift. It does not reduce the child's share unless the will, a signed note, or the child's own acknowledgment says it was an advance on the inheritance. Fix: decide whether you want it counted, and say so in writing.

**3. Assets that cannot be split.** A house cannot be cut in thirds. Fix: choose in advance how the house will be handled.

## How does the executor divide a house and a bank account?

An executor has four basic routes when a will says equal shares and the main asset is real estate.

1. **Sell and split.** The house is sold, expenses are paid and the money is divided. This is the cleanest route and the one a judge will order if the children cannot agree.
2. **One child buys out the others.** The house is appraised, and the child who wants it pays the others their shares in cash, often with a mortgage. It works only if that child can qualify for the loan or has the money.
3. **Divide in kind.** If there are several properties or items, each child takes assets of matching value. This needs a will that allows it, or everyone's consent, and an appraisal.
4. **Keep it together.** The children inherit as co-owners. This avoids a sale but can lead to disagreements about repairs and taxes. Our article on [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) covers that.

In Walter's case, Kim might want to keep the house and agree to pay Dana and Paul one third each of its value. Her share of the estate is the house plus the account, about $150,000. To keep a $420,000 house she would owe the other two about $280,000. Unless she has savings, that means a mortgage.

## Are equal dollars really equal?

Not always, and a careful will looks at that. The same $200,000 can be worth different amounts after tax.

- **Pre-tax retirement accounts.** An inherited traditional IRA is taxed as income when withdrawn, and most non-spouse heirs must empty it within 10 years. The heir who gets it pays income tax on each withdrawal.
- **A house.** Most inherited property gets a step-up in basis to its value at death, so a quick sale usually produces little or no capital gain. See [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis).
- **Cash.** No tax at all.

So $200,000 of IRA is worth meaningfully less than $200,000 of cash to the person receiving it, depending on their tax bracket. A parent who wants true fairness can weight the shares, name children in different percentages on the forms, or leave the IRA to the child in the lowest bracket. This is a judgment call, and it belongs in the planning conversation, not left to the executor.

## How to make "equal" mean what you intend

1. List everything you own and mark how each asset passes: will, form, joint title or trust. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) does this in a few minutes.
2. Add up what each child will receive under the current setup.
3. Decide which gifts you want counted. Many parents want a $60,000 business loan to be an advance. Others want it forgiven.
4. Write a signed list of advances, or a sentence in the will that names them, and update it when you make more gifts.
5. Pick an approach for the house: sell, buy-out with a stated price method, or a trust that holds it. See [leaving a house in a will](/learn/wills/leaving-a-house-in-a-will).
6. Change beneficiary forms so they fit, or add an equalizing clause that gives the shorted children more from the probate estate. That only works if the probate estate is large enough.
7. Give the executor power to divide in kind and to use appraisals, so a dispute does not need a judge.

### Do loans count?

A loan you expect repaid is a debt owed to the estate, and the executor can collect it or reduce that child's share. A loan you never intend to collect is a gift. If you say nothing, the family will argue about which it was. A short note signed by both of you, or a line in the will, ends that argument.

## What if the will is already written and you are the executor?

You cannot rewrite it. Follow its words, then look for what it allows.

- Read whether the will says anything about advances, loans or beneficiary-form assets.
- Get appraisals before anyone talks about who wants what.
- Share the whole picture with all the beneficiaries. Hidden numbers cause more fights than unequal ones.
- If a beneficiary wants to keep the house, give a reasonable deadline for financing.
- If the children want to trade items of equal value, get that in writing.

Siblings can always agree to something different from the will, as long as they all consent. Court approval is needed if a minor or incapacitated beneficiary is involved. For wider conflict, see [when siblings fight over a parent's estate](/learn/what-if/siblings-fight-over-a-parents-estate).

## How state law changes this

States differ on advancements, on what a vague phrase like "equal" covers, and on whether a child can force a sale. Illinois and New Jersey both let courts order a partition, a sale of jointly inherited property, when co-owners cannot agree. Probate is the first step in either state, and the [Illinois guide](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey guide](/estate-planning/new-jersey) cover the process.
