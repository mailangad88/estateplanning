Ravi, 61, has a will his attorney drafted fifteen years ago. His wife, Anita, is the executor, and they own a house together. Last spring Ravi had a stroke. He is alive and in rehab, and he cannot sign his name. The rent from a small duplex he owns alone is going into an account Anita is not on. His IRA statements are coming in his name. The will in the drawer is, for now, a piece of paper with no authority at all.

People assume the documents they already have will cover them in a crisis. Often they will not, because a will and a trust do different jobs from the one a crisis calls for. This page explains what each one does when you cannot act, and what is still missing.

## What happens by default

The answer depends on which document you have. Here is the comparison.

| Question | Will only | Funded living trust only | Trust plus power of attorney and healthcare documents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does it work while you are alive? | No | Yes, for trust assets | Yes |
| Who pays bills from accounts in your own name? | Nobody, without a court order or a joint owner | Nobody, unless the trust owns the account | Your agent |
| Who manages trust property? | Not applicable | Your successor trustee | Your successor trustee |
| Who makes medical decisions? | Hospital's default rules | Hospital's default rules | Your healthcare agent |
| Is court involved? | Often (conservatorship or guardianship) | For assets outside the trust | Usually not |

### If you have only a will

A [will](/learn/wills/do-i-need-a-will) is a set of instructions for the court to follow after you die. It gives no one power to act for you now. If you cannot manage your affairs and no one else has legal authority, a family member has to ask a court to appoint a conservator for your money and sometimes a guardian for your person. Our article on the [conservator versus guardian roles](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate) explains the split.

That process is public and supervised. It generally involves a petition, notice to relatives, a medical report, a hearing, and a bond or periodic accounting. Judges and clerks do their best, but it takes time, and the family spends money on it that a short document would have saved.

### If you have a funded living trust

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is built for this. When you can no longer serve as trustee, the successor trustee you named takes over, usually after a doctor's letter or two. The trustee then pays your bills, handles the house and invests the money according to the trust, without a court. For most families this is the main advantage over a will alone.

The catch is the word funded. A trust controls only what it owns or what is payable to it. If your house is deeded to the trust but your checking account, brokerage account and car are still in your name, your successor trustee has no power over those. Our article on [the unfunded trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) covers that gap in detail.

### What neither document does

Neither a will nor a trust makes medical decisions, speaks to the hospital, or tells your doctors what you want. That takes a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and a HIPAA authorization. Without them, the hospital follows its own state's default list of who may decide, which may or may not match your wishes.

## A realistic example

Return to Ravi. Anita goes to the bank with the will. The teller is polite and says she needs a court order, since the checking account holding his rent is in his name alone. The IRA custodian says the same. The duplex tenant keeps paying rent into the account, and the mortgage on the duplex comes due on the same day each month.

Anita can still pay the household bills from their joint account. She files a petition for conservatorship. The court sets a hearing, appoints a lawyer to represent Ravi, asks for a doctor's report, and issues letters after several weeks. Until then, the duplex mortgage is paid from household savings, and a late fee or two builds up.

Time and cost drivers are the court's calendar, the doctor's availability, any objection from relatives, and the ongoing reporting once the conservator is in place. Now picture the same case with a signed [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney). Anita would have shown the document to the bank in the first week.

## What can still be fixed

### If you can still sign

1. **Sign a durable financial power of attorney** and a healthcare power of attorney. Pick agents you trust and name a backup for each.
2. **Sign a HIPAA authorization** so your agent and a few named people can talk to your doctors.
3. **If you have a trust, check the incapacity clause.** Make sure it says who decides, and that the person named is likely to be reachable.
4. **Fund the trust** by retitling accounts and deeding real estate. The [funding guide](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) walks through each asset type.
5. **Add pay-on-death or transfer-on-death designations** where they make sense, so accounts need not wait on a court.
6. **Tell your agents where the documents are.** A power of attorney in a safe deposit box nobody can open does little good.

### If it is already too late to sign

1. **Check for joint accounts and beneficiary forms.** Joint owners can use their accounts without a court.
2. **Ask each institution what it requires.** Some banks have a small-account process for family members.
3. **See whether your state has a surrogate decision law** for medical care. Many states list a default order of relatives.
4. **Consult an attorney about conservatorship or guardianship.** Ask for the narrowest order that covers the need, and ask about a temporary order if bills are urgent.
5. **Check whether a trust exists.** If the person signed a trust before losing capacity, the successor trustee may be able to step in for the trust assets at once.

## How state law changes this

States set the rules for whether a spouse or adult child can make medical decisions without paperwork, how incapacity under a trust is determined if the document is silent, and how conservatorship and guardianship proceedings work. The names differ too: some states say guardian for both roles, and some use a different term for the financial role. See our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides. A lawyer in your state can tell you which default rules apply.

## How to prevent it

Treat incapacity planning as a package of four items that work together.

1. A durable financial power of attorney for anything outside the trust.
2. A healthcare power of attorney and living will for medical choices.
3. A HIPAA authorization.
4. A funded trust, if your situation calls for one. Our [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) helps you decide.

Even a simple will plan should include the first three. They cost less than a will and trust together, and they do the job a will cannot.
