Your mother is 79, owns a house in Joliet, and changes the subject every time you say the word "will." Or your father in Bergen County signed nothing, and he told you last month that he is "not dead yet." You are not trying to take anything. You are trying to avoid a scramble in a hospital hallway.

This page explains what the law does when a parent refuses to make a will or sign a power of attorney, what you can still do, and how to raise it so the answer changes from no to yes. For a parent who is already forgetting things, read [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) first, because the rules are different once capacity is in doubt.

## Can I make my parent sign a will or power of attorney?

No. Both documents depend on the signer's own choice and capacity. A will must be signed by the person and witnessed, and a power of attorney names an agent chosen by the principal. Our page on [making an estate plan for someone else](/learn/basics/can-you-make-an-estate-plan-for-someone-else) explains why even a family member holding a power of attorney cannot sign a will on the parent's behalf.

Pressure also backfires legally. A document signed after a child badgered, cornered or arranged everything is the textbook setup for an [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) claim by a sibling. A parent who says no is, in a narrow legal sense, making a valid decision. What you can change is the reason behind the no.

## What happens if a parent never signs?

Each missing document leaves a gap that the state fills with its own default.

| Missing document | What fills the gap | What it costs the family |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Will | Intestacy statute decides heirs; court appoints an administrator | Equal shares whether or not they match what your parent said out loud |
| Financial power of attorney | Guardianship or conservatorship petition if they lose capacity | Hearings, a lawyer, court reports, loss of privacy |
| Healthcare directive | Default surrogate rules, if the state has them | A hospital working down a list of relatives |
| HIPAA release | Providers say little to you | Delay when you need to speak with a doctor |
| Beneficiary forms | The account passes by contract or into the estate | A retirement account may land in probate |

Here is what that looks like in numbers. Say Ruth, a widow in Illinois, owns a $240,000 house and $120,000 in savings in her own name and never signs a will. She has three children. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-1 the three children share equally, so each receives $120,000. That might be exactly what Ruth wanted. It does not match what she told you if she meant the house to go to Kevin, who moved back in to care for her. See [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) or the [New Jersey version](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) for the actual order.

If Ruth later has a stroke with no power of attorney, the money and the house are both frozen for practical purposes. Kevin would have to ask a judge to appoint a guardian of her estate. Illinois requires clear and convincing evidence that a person has a disability before a court does that (755 ILCS 5/11a-3), and New Jersey requires physician certifications and a hearing. The ordinary work of [paying her bills and selling the house](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney) turns into a case file.

## Why do parents say no?

Reasons matter more than arguments. Most refusals fit one of these:

1. **Dying talk feels like dying.** Signing a will is admitting a limit. Reframing it as protecting the surviving spouse or the dog sometimes lands better.
2. **Fear of losing control.** A parent hears "power of attorney" and pictures being declared incompetent. A springing or limited power addresses that fear; see [springing vs. immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).
3. **Choosing among children.** Naming one child as agent feels like ranking them. The parent avoids the decision by avoiding the paper.
4. **Distrust of lawyers or cost.** Some parents had a bad experience with a relative's estate. Others assume the fee is thousands. A fixed quote can end the objection.
5. **They think they already have one.** An old will from 1998 or a joint account feels like a plan. Ask to see it. It may help, or it may name someone who has died.
6. **A quiet disagreement with you.** Sometimes the parent is saying they do not want the child who raised the topic involved. Listen for it.

## What should I try instead of arguing?

Work from small and low-stakes toward large. A sequence our attorneys see work:

1. **Ask a question, not a favor.** "If you were in the hospital tomorrow, who should the doctors call?" invites an answer. "You need a power of attorney" invites a fight.
2. **Start with the healthcare side.** A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and a [HIPAA release](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) are about being sick, not dying, and they can be signed in one visit.
3. **Use a neutral voice.** A family doctor, a pastor, or a friend who has been through it often says the same words more effectively than a child does.
4. **Show them your own.** Telling a parent you signed yours last week and what it cost removes the sense that you are accusing them.
5. **Offer to book and drive, then step back.** Take them to the appointment and leave the room. Parents say more to an attorney alone, and the later record is cleaner.
6. **Put the cost of no on paper.** A one-page list of what a guardianship case involves in your state is more persuasive than a lecture. Our [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) page has wording to borrow.
7. **Accept a partial yes.** Beneficiary forms on the IRA and life insurance, a transfer on death designation, or a limited power of attorney for one bank account can each be signed without a full plan.

Do not move your parent's money into your own name to "make it easier." A bank convenience account or joint title can solve one problem and create a gift-tax, creditor, Medicaid and sibling-fairness problem at the same time.

## What if my parent says everything goes to one child?

They are allowed to, and an unequal plan is not a defect. A written, explained choice is far less likely to be overturned than a silent one. What makes a plan suspicious is the circumstances: a parent who changed documents right after a diagnosis, a beneficiary child who found and took the parent to the lawyer, secrecy, isolation from other relatives, or a parent who cannot say what the plan does. In Illinois a will can be contested within six months after admission to probate (755 ILCS 5/8-1), and New Jersey has its own caveat procedure; both are covered in our pages on [Illinois will contests](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) and [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats).

If you are the favored child, the best protection for your parent and for you is to stay out of the lawyer's office, let the parent explain the reasoning in a short signed statement, and consider asking the parent's doctor for a capacity note near the signing date.

## What if my parent already lacks capacity?

Then the question changes from "will they sign" to "can they." A person with early memory loss can often still sign a power of attorney on a good day if they understand who the agent is and what the agent may do. An attorney will meet with them alone and decide. If the answer is no, the remaining route is guardianship, which in Illinois is governed by 755 ILCS 5/11a and in New Jersey by N.J.S.A. 3B:12 and Court Rule 4:86. The [guardianship alternatives](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) page lists options short of a full order. Moving quickly matters more than moving perfectly.

## How state law changes this

Every state sets its own intestacy shares, its own witness rules, and its own guardianship process. Illinois and New Jersey both give a court the power to appoint a guardian for an adult only after a hearing with notice to the person. Illinois also has a statutory short form for property powers of attorney, and New Jersey requires specific signing formalities. Your state guide, such as [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) or [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey), summarizes both.
