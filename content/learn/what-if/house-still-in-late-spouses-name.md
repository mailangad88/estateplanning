Margaret's husband, Ray, died in the spring. She has lived in the house for thirty-one years. A month later she opens the property tax bill and sees his name alone on it, and she wonders whether the house is hers, whether the bank can take it, and who she is supposed to call.

If you are in her seat, the first answer is calm: you are probably not about to lose the home. The second is more practical. The next steps depend on how the deed was written, and on that point the paperwork matters more than what everyone assumed. For the wider picture of what to do after a spouse dies, see [estate planning after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies).

## What happens by default

Start with the deed, which you can get from the county recorder or clerk, often online. Three common versions lead to three different outcomes.

| How the deed reads | What happens at death | What you need to do |
|---|---|---|
| Both spouses, with right of survivorship or as tenants by the entirety | The survivor owns it automatically | Record a death certificate or survivorship affidavit |
| Late spouse alone, with a will | Goes through probate to the person named in the will | Probate court, then a deed from the executor |
| Late spouse alone, no will | Goes through probate to heirs under state intestacy law | Court appoints an administrator, then a deed |
| Late spouse alone, with a transfer-on-death deed or living trust | Passes outside probate to the named person or the trust | Record the death and follow the deed or trust terms |

Many couples assume the house is "ours" and never check. Sometimes one spouse bought before the marriage. Sometimes a refinance quietly removed a name. Sometimes a title company recorded the deed as "tenants in common," which does not carry survivorship. The difference is large, so read the actual document, not a closing summary.

## Do I have to leave the house?

Generally no. A surviving spouse has the right to stay in the family home while the estate is open. Most states add specific protections, such as a homestead right, a spousal allowance or a right to take a share even if the will left the house to someone else. Community property states treat much of what was earned during the marriage as jointly owned, which changes the math. The rules vary, so have someone read your state's version.

The mortgage is a separate worry. Federal law generally bars lenders from demanding full payment just because the home passes to a surviving spouse. Keep making payments, send the servicer a copy of the death certificate and ask what they need to treat you as the successor. If the loan was in both names, you were already on it.

## A realistic example

Back to Margaret. The deed shows only Ray. He left a will naming her as his sole heir and executor. She files the will with the county probate court, receives letters testamentary a few weeks later and gets a certified copy. The court appoints her, and she pays the taxes and insurance in the meantime. The house is listed in the estate inventory and, after the creditor period ends, she signs an executor's deed to herself.

What drives time and cost: the court's calendar, whether a lawyer helps, whether a creditor claim is filed, and whether any child contests the will. If Ray had owned the house with her as joint tenants, she could have skipped all of that and recorded one affidavit. Our article on [selling a house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) explains the limits on acting before the court appoints someone.

## What can still be fixed?

**If you are the surviving spouse:**

1. Get several certified copies of the death certificate.
2. Pull the current deed and any mortgage documents. Read the vesting language, the part that says how title is held.
3. Call the insurer, tell them about the death and confirm the policy stays in force. Vacant or changed-ownership clauses can matter.
4. Keep paying the mortgage, taxes and utilities. Do not skip them while waiting on the court.
5. If the deed shows survivorship, record the affidavit and have the deed updated.
6. If the house is in his or her name alone, find the will and speak to a probate attorney about the filing route. Small estates in some states can use a simpler procedure for real estate. See the [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker).
7. After title is clear, update your own will, deed and beneficiary forms.

**If you are a child or other heir:** you cannot sell or borrow against the house until an executor or administrator has authority. Do not move in, list it or change locks without checking with the family and the court first.

## What about taxes, insurance and bills while the deed is stuck?

The house does not stop costing money because the paperwork is unfinished. Property taxes come due on schedule, and a missed payment can lead to penalties and eventually a lien. Homeowner's insurance is the one people forget. Many policies name only the deceased as the insured, and some insurers limit coverage if a home sits empty for a stretch. Call the agent, say that the owner has died and ask for the policy to be updated to the surviving spouse or the estate.

Utilities and the mortgage are easier to handle if you are already on the account. If the loan is only in your spouse's name, ask the servicer for its successor-in-interest process. You may need to send the death certificate, a proof of your claim to the house and, later, the court papers. Keep a folder with every call date and name. That habit saves time when a different representative picks up the file.

Finally, do not make large repairs or a sale decision yet unless the court has given you authority. If the roof leaks, fix it and keep receipts. A buyer's title company will want to see who had authority to sign.

## How state law changes this

States differ on homestead rights, how much a spouse can claim outside the will, whether small estates can skip court for real estate, and whether a simple affidavit can transfer survivorship property. Community property states, such as [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), treat marital property differently from common-law states. A few states also let a house go to a spouse with a short form when no other assets need probate. Check your state guide and the clerk's website, then confirm with an attorney.

Tax is mostly good news for spouses. Property passing to a surviving spouse is generally free of federal estate tax under the marital deduction, and part or all of the house usually gets a new tax basis at death. The details depend on how title was held and whether your state is community property. Ask a tax professional before you sell.

## How to prevent it

Pick one of three clean fixes while both spouses are alive. Hold the home jointly with survivorship, where your state permits it. Sign a [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) naming the next owner. Or put the house in a living trust, as explained in [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust). Each has trade-offs for creditors, Medicaid and taxes, and joint ownership can create problems of its own, discussed in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

Whichever route you choose, repeat the exercise after the first death, because the survivor now has the same problem with no spouse as backup.
