Gordon and his sister inherited their parents' lake cabin in Michigan years ago, and Gordon later bought out her half. He lives in Ohio, where he raised his family. He kept meaning to write a will. When Gordon died at 67, his wife and two sons were relieved to learn that Ohio law would handle things without much fuss. Then the title company asked who had authority to deal with the cabin.

The cabin is the problem. This article explains how two states' laws apply to one person's death, why a second case is often required, and the few documents that prevent it.

## What happens by default

The basic rule is simple even if the details are not. Property is divided into two groups.

- **Real property**, meaning land, houses and buildings, is controlled by the state where it is located.
- **Personal property**, meaning bank accounts, vehicles, investments and household goods, is generally controlled by the state where the person was living at death, called the domicile.

With no will, each state applies its own [intestacy](/glossary/intestate-succession) formula to the property it controls. In both states, a surviving spouse and children are first in line, but the shares can differ. One state may give a spouse the first block of money plus a portion of the rest, and another may give the spouse half.

The home state opens the main probate case and appoints an [administrator](/glossary/administrator). The other state usually requires its own court case, called [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate), before the land can be sold or retitled. The administrator from the home state typically has no automatic power in the second state.

### What ancillary probate involves

Ancillary means supporting. The second case is smaller, but it has most of the same steps.

1. The home-state administrator or an heir sends certified copies of the court order and, if there is one, the will.
2. The second state's court accepts those papers and appoints a local representative, sometimes the same person.
3. Notice goes to creditors and heirs under that state's rules.
4. The representative sells the property or transfers it to the heirs by court order.
5. The court closes the case.

Our article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) lists the steps in more detail.

## A realistic example

Gordon's Ohio case went smoothly. His wife, Ruth, was appointed administrator. In Ohio, his spouse and sons shared what he left under the state's formula. In Michigan, the cabin was a separate matter, and Michigan law applied.

Ruth hired a Michigan attorney and filed the Ohio order with the county probate court. A Michigan court appointed her as personal representative with a bond, and the case ran through the creditor period before the cabin could be sold. Ruth and the boys then disagreed about whether to keep the cabin or sell it, which is easy to argue about when the shares are fixed by law and nobody planned who gets what.

The cost drivers were two sets of court fees, a second lawyer, publication of notice, the bond premium, the travel to a distant county, and the months of carrying costs on the cabin, including taxes and insurance for a house nobody was using.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are still alive

1. **List every property you own in every state.** Include timeshares, farmland, mineral rights and small lots people forget.
2. **Make a will or trust.** A [valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) names who gets the property and who handles it. It does not avoid probate, but it avoids the intestacy formulas and can name a person in both states.
3. **Move the out-of-state property into a [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).** A deed from you to the trustee means the property is not in your name at death. No ancillary case is needed. See [living trusts and owning property in another state](/learn/trusts/living-trusts-and-moving-or-owning-property-in-another-state).
4. **Ask about a transfer-on-death deed.** A number of states permit one for real estate, which lets the property pass to a named person with a short paperwork step. Whether you can record one in the other state depends on that state's law. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).
5. **Update the deeds.** A joint tenancy with right of survivorship or a spouse co-owner may also pass the property without probate, depending on the state.

### If the death has already happened

1. **Collect certified copies** of the death certificate and the home-state court order.
2. **Hire a lawyer in the second state** or ask the county court about a simplified procedure. Some states have an affidavit for small out-of-state real estate or for property under a value limit.
3. **Do not sell or list the property yet.** A title company will want the court order first.
4. **Keep it insured and maintained** while the case runs. A vacant out-of-state house is a common target for problems, as covered in [vacant inherited house insurance, repairs and squatters](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters).

## Does a will fix the two-state problem?

Partly. A will answers the question of who inherits, so the intestacy formulas in both states stop mattering. It also lets you name an executor who is willing to handle two cases, and you can pick a backup who lives near the second property.

What a will does not do is skip the court. Land held in your name still needs a probate case where it sits, and a will usually has to be admitted in each state. Some states accept a will already approved elsewhere with minimal review. Others make the family prove it again. That is why people who own a vacation home, a rental or inherited farmland often pair a will with a trust or a transfer-on-death deed, so the out-of-state property never enters probate.

One more quiet risk is co-ownership. If you and a sibling still share the inherited family place, your half passes by your plan or by intestacy, and your sibling may suddenly co-own with your children. Our scenario on [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) shows how that tends to go.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how large an estate must be to need ancillary probate, whether a short affidavit works for real estate, whether a nonresident can serve as administrator, and whether a bond is required. Community property states treat spousal shares differently from common law states, which matters when the two are paired. Some states allow a foreign will to be recorded with little fuss. Others require a full court case. The guides for [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) and [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) are good places to see how local rules read.

## How to prevent it

Prevention is simple, and the cost is usually lower than one ancillary case. A funded revocable trust that holds both properties is the most direct answer. A will plus a transfer-on-death deed on the out-of-state land is another. For a small piece of land, a deed to a family LLC can also work, though it adds its own paperwork and fees.

If your documents were written before you bought the second property, they likely do not mention it. Review them as part of the [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder).
