Gerald signed a will in 2011 that split his estate between his two daughters. After his second marriage in 2019 he went back to the same lawyer and signed a new one that left the house to his wife and the rest to the daughters. Nobody told the daughters. When Gerald died, one of them found the 2011 will in his desk and the other found a copy of the 2019 will in the lawyer's file. Each assumed hers was the real one.

A will is the last word only if it is the last valid word, and sometimes nobody can say with confidence which one that is. This page covers how courts sort it out, and what you can do before your family has to.

## What happens by default

The probate court decides, not the family and not the executor. Someone files a will. Anyone with a stake, such as an heir or a person named in either document, can bring the other one forward. The judge then asks three questions in order.

1. **Was each will validly signed?** That means the right number of witnesses, a signature in the right place, and a testator who understood what he was signing. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) for the basics.
2. **Which one came later?** The date helps, and so do the notary stamp, the witnesses and the attorney's file.
3. **Does the later will cancel the earlier one?** It does if it says so, or if the two cannot both be carried out.

An express revocation clause settles the third question fast. A line such as "I revoke all wills and codicils I have made before" cancels the older document in full. If that clause is missing, courts in most states treat the later will as replacing the earlier one only where they conflict. A gift in the old will that the new one never mentions may survive.

That is the messy case. Say the old will leaves the lake cabin to a nephew and the new will leaves "my real estate" to a daughter. The cabin is real estate, so the newer will wins on the cabin. But the old will's $10,000 gift to a church might stand next to the new will, because the new one never touched it.

## A realistic example

Back to Gerald. His widow, Carol, files the 2019 will, since the lawyer's office has the signed original. The older daughter, Jess, objects. She says her father was ill in 2019 and Carol arranged the appointment. Now the court has a contest on its hands, with a hearing, testimony from the lawyer and the witnesses, and medical records.

Three outcomes are possible.

- **The 2019 will is valid.** It opens with a revocation clause, so the 2011 will has no effect. The house goes to Carol.
- **The 2019 will fails** for lack of capacity or undue influence. It is treated as if it never existed, and the 2011 will, which splits everything between the daughters, is probated instead. Carol then looks to her state's spousal protections, which can be substantial. See [what happens when a will is contested](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).
- **The parties settle.** Many cases end here, with a negotiated split that matches neither document.

Time and cost depend on how hard the contest is fought, how many witnesses there are, and whether the court has to rule on capacity. Even a short fight usually delays the whole estate, because the executor cannot distribute with a will in dispute.

## Can two wills both be valid on purpose?

Yes, and for a narrow group of people it is good planning. Someone who owns a home in Ohio and a flat abroad may sign one will for each country, each stating that it covers only that country's property and does not revoke the other. A local lawyer in each place drafts its will so the two read as one plan. Our article on [wills when you move or live abroad](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) explains why.

The problem is the accidental pair. It usually comes from one of three habits.

- Signing a new will after a divorce or a move without a revocation clause.
- Keeping an old will "just in case" in the same drawer as the new one.
- Having a lawyer prepare a will and then, years later, using an online form without telling the lawyer.

None of these is rare. Each can leave two documents that both look official.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are alive and can still sign

1. **Gather every will you have signed.** Check your desk, a safe deposit box, your old lawyer's file and any online account where you made a will.
2. **Read them side by side.** Note every gift and every person named as executor or guardian that differs.
3. **Sign one new will** that begins by revoking all earlier wills and codicils. If you want to keep a separate will on purpose, have your attorney say so in both documents.
4. **Deal with the old originals** the way your attorney advises. In most states, tearing, burning or writing "void" on a will with the intent to revoke it is effective, but do it only after the new will is signed.
5. **Tell your executor where the current will is kept.** See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
6. **Check your beneficiary forms and trust** so they match the new will. A will does not control accounts with their own beneficiary.

### If it is now too late

1. **Ask the executor named in the latest will to file it** with the probate court, along with a statement that you know of an earlier will.
2. **Do not hide or discard the older will.** Concealing a will is a serious problem in every state. The court needs to see both.
3. **Collect the evidence of dates**: the attorney's file, the witnesses' contact details, the receipts or emails around signing.
4. **Talk to an attorney before the hearing** if you stand to lose under one will and gain under the other. Deadlines to object are short in many states. Our article on [how to object to probate](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate) explains the usual process.
5. **Consider mediation** if the dispute is between siblings who will have to see each other for decades.

## How state law changes this

States agree on the broad rule that a later valid will beats an earlier one. They differ on details that matter in a fight: whether a will can be revoked by a later inconsistent writing, how a destroyed original is treated, whether a handwritten will counts, whether an old will can be revived, and how long heirs have to object. Some states let the court admit a document that missed a formality if there is strong proof the person meant it to be a will, and others do not. Check our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides and ask an attorney about local rules.

## How to prevent it

The first step is one current will at a time. Treat a new will like a new passport: once it is issued, the old one is retired. When you sign, make sure the first paragraph revokes all prior wills, as laid out in [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils). If a small change is all you need, a codicil signed with the same formalities can be cleaner than a full rewrite, though it should refer to the date of the will it amends.

The second step is a review habit. Pick a date each year, perhaps the same one you use for insurance renewal, and use the [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) to be prompted. Life events that call for a fresh look include marriage, divorce, a new child, a move, and the death of a named executor.

For other situations where a plan falls out of step with life, see the [what-if library](/learn/what-if).
