Dale married Renee when her son Marcus was nine. He packed lunches, drove to practices and paid for the community college tuition. Dale had a daughter of his own, Priya, from his first marriage, and no will. When Dale dies at 67, Marcus is 31 and assumes that he and Priya will be treated alike. They will not.

Intestacy law is a list of relatives, and a stepchild is not on it in almost every state. This page walks through what happens to stepchildren when a stepparent dies without a will, and what the family can still do.

## What happens by default

When someone dies without a valid will, the probate court appoints an administrator and state law decides who inherits. The statute works down a fixed list: the surviving spouse, the decedent's children, then parents, siblings and more distant blood relatives. Adopted children are on the list. Stepchildren are not. Our overview of [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) covers the general rules.

How the estate splits depends on who survives. The table shows the common patterns, though each state sets its own fractions.

| Who survives the stepparent | Typical result for the estate | Where stepchildren stand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spouse and the stepparent's own children | Spouse and children share, often in fractions set by state law | Nothing from the stepparent |
| Spouse, no children of their own | Spouse often takes most or all | Nothing directly; depends on the spouse's later plan |
| Own children, no spouse (the marriage ended) | Children share equally | Nothing |
| No spouse, children, parents or siblings | Distant relatives, then the state in some cases | A few states list stepchildren late, ahead of the state |

The last row is the only place stepchildren appear, and it rarely comes into play because most people have some blood relative.

### What passes outside the will anyway

Intestacy only governs the probate estate. Anything with a beneficiary or a co-owner skips it. A life insurance policy naming Marcus pays Marcus. A bank account owned jointly with right of survivorship goes to the surviving owner. A house held as joint tenants with Renee becomes hers. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for the sorting. Many blended families only find out which assets go where after the funeral.

## A realistic example

Dale's estate is a house worth the most of what he owned, a checking account in his name alone, a pension with Renee as survivor, and a small life insurance policy from work. He never updated the policy's beneficiary form, and it still lists his first wife.

Renee files for probate and is appointed administrator. The house was titled in Dale's name only, so it is a probate asset. Under the state's formula, Renee takes part and Priya takes part, as Dale's only child. Marcus, who lived in that house through high school and has helped with its repairs, takes nothing. Renee can sell her share, or buy Priya out, but if the two cannot agree, a court-ordered sale is possible. If it comes to that, our article on [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) explains how those sales work.

The pension goes to Renee under its own rules. The life insurance goes to the ex-wife, because the form controls, which we cover in [what happens if an ex-spouse is still the beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).

Time and cost depend on the court and on whether anyone disputes the filing. A contested sale of the house adds attorney fees and delay. In a smooth case, the estate closes in months. Marcus is not a party to any of it.

## What can still be fixed

### If the stepparent is alive

1. **Decide who you want to include.** Write down each stepchild by full legal name and the share you intend.
2. **Sign a will or trust that names them.** Do not rely on the words "my children." Our article on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) explains drafting choices.
3. **Update beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts, life insurance and bank accounts with payable-on-death designations ignore the will.
4. **Consider adoption.** An adult adoption makes a stepchild a legal child in many states. It is a serious step with its own consequences, so talk to an attorney first.
5. **Talk to your spouse.** What the spouse inherits becomes the spouse's to give, so coordinate the two plans.

### If the stepparent has already died

1. **Find out what passes outside probate.** Ask the surviving spouse about beneficiary forms, joint accounts and deeds.
2. **Ask whether a will turns up.** A search of the desk, the safe deposit box and the attorney's office is worth doing. Read [what to do if the original will cannot be found](/learn/wills/lost-original-will).
3. **Talk to the surviving parent.** Heirs can sometimes redirect assets through a qualified disclaimer, and a surviving spouse can then plan their own estate to include stepchildren.
4. **Do not assume a promise counts.** A spoken promise to leave the house to a stepchild is not enforceable in most states. A signed writing is stronger.
5. **Consult a probate attorney** if a written promise, a long caregiving history or an equitable adoption argument exists.

## How state law changes this

States agree on the main point, but differ on the details. Intestacy fractions for a spouse and children vary widely. Some states give the spouse the first slice of the estate plus a share of the rest, others split by headcount. Community property states handle the spouse's half separately. A small number of states allow stepchildren to inherit when no closer relative exists. Equitable adoption exists in some courts and not in others. Read your state's rules in our [state guides](/estate-planning/illinois) or use the [who inherits tool](/tools/who-inherits) to see a first-pass result.

## How to prevent it

Three documents solve most of this problem. A [will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) naming each stepchild, or a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) that does the same, is the base. Beneficiary forms should match. And if a stepparent wants a spouse to live in the house for life while stepchildren inherit afterward, [a marital trust](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) can hold the home that way.

The conversation matters as much as the paper. Couples who explain the plan to the children, even briefly, tend to avoid the surprise that turns grief into a lawsuit. See [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan).

For more scenarios like this, see [what happens if you put off estate planning](/learn/what-if).

## What does this feel like for the stepchild?

The legal answer is short, and the human one is not. A stepchild who helped care for the stepparent in the last years often does more of the unglamorous work than the heirs do, and then watches a stranger to the household sign the papers. Nobody did anything wrong. The statute simply never asked who showed up.

Two habits make the experience easier. Stepchildren should ask early, and politely, what the surviving parent knows about the paperwork, because many blended families have never said the plan aloud. And the heirs should remember that the stepchild may be the one with the keys, the password and the history with the house. A fair settlement made between family members, even though the law does not require it, is often cheaper than a hearing.

If the stepparent had a plan in mind and never signed it, the family can honor it by agreement. Heirs are free to give a share to a stepchild, and a written family settlement agreement, reviewed by a lawyer, makes that clean. Gifts of that size can have tax consequences for the giver, so the paperwork should be looked at first.
