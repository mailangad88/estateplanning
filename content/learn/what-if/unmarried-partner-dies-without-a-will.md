Nora and Teo have lived together for sixteen years. They split the bills, adopted a dog and talk about "when we get old." The house is in Teo's name because he bought it before they met. Teo, 54, has a sister and a nephew in another state, and no will.

If Teo dies tomorrow, the law does not see Nora as family. This page explains what happens to an unmarried partner when the other person dies without a will, and what can be done about it.

## What happens by default

Without a will, state intestacy law decides who inherits. The list runs through a spouse, children, parents, siblings and other relatives, and an unmarried partner is not on it. The partner may be the person who knew the decedent best, and that does not matter to the statute. See our overview of [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

In Teo's case, with no spouse or children, his parents come first if living, and then his siblings. If a sibling has died, that sibling's children step in. The court appoints an administrator, often a relative, and that person controls the probate estate.

Which assets are at risk depends on how each one is held.

| Asset | How it passes with no will | Does Nora get it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| House titled to Teo alone | Probate, then to his heirs | No |
| House titled to both with survivorship | Directly to the survivor | Yes |
| Joint bank account | To the surviving owner | Yes |
| 401(k) or IRA naming Nora | To Nora under the beneficiary form | Yes |
| 401(k) or IRA with no beneficiary | Plan default, often the estate | Probably not |
| Car, furniture, personal items titled to Teo | Probate, then to heirs | No |

For more on sorting assets this way, see [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## A realistic example

Teo dies of a sudden illness. The sister, Lucia, learns there is no will and files for appointment as administrator. A few weeks later she is appointed. She has authority over the house, the car, and the checking account in Teo's name alone.

Nora lives in the house, but she is not an heir. Lucia is polite. Still, the estate has to settle debts and divide property among the heirs, who are Lucia and her son. That usually means selling the house. Nora has a limited period to move, determined by the estate and sometimes the court. She can bid on the house if she has money, but she cannot assume a right to it.

Nora keeps the joint savings account and the life insurance that named her. She has no claim to the furniture they bought together unless she can show receipts. The cost drivers are the court's pace, whether the heirs agree, and how long the house takes to sell. A contested case takes much longer than a friendly one.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are alive

1. **Sign a will.** Name your partner by full legal name and say what they receive. Include a contingent beneficiary and an executor, who can be the partner.
2. **Check the title to your home.** Joint tenancy with right of survivorship or a [transfer on death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) can carry the house to the partner. Understand the tradeoffs described in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).
3. **Update every beneficiary form.** Retirement accounts and life insurance follow the form, not the will.
4. **Sign incapacity documents.** An unmarried partner has no automatic say in a hospital or at a bank. Choose each other as agent under a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and a financial power of attorney.
5. **Consider a trust.** A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) avoids probate and keeps the details private.

### If it is now too late

1. **Gather proof of your contributions.** Mortgage payments, repair receipts and joint account records matter.
2. **Locate non-probate assets.** Ask the plan administrators and banks which ones name you.
3. **Speak to the administrator early.** A calm conversation about a purchase or a lease can protect your housing.
4. **Look at any written agreement.** A signed cohabitation agreement or promise can support a claim.
5. **Talk to a probate attorney** before you sign anything or move out.

## How state law changes this

Almost every state ignores unmarried partners in intestacy. The exceptions are states that recognize registered domestic partnerships or civil unions, and those that still allow common-law marriage. Some states also tax inheritances by relationship, so a partner may pay a higher rate than a spouse. See your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois) or our [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker). The article on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) goes further.

## How to prevent it

A will, a current set of beneficiary forms and a pair of incapacity documents is the basic kit. If you own a home together, review how the deed is held. If one person owns it alone, decide whether the partner should receive it outright, receive a right to live there, or have a trust hold it. Run the [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) if you are unsure which fits.

Marriage changes the legal default, but it is not the only answer. Plenty of couples prefer not to marry, and they can reach most of the same results with documents.

For more scenarios like this, see [what happens if you put off estate planning](/learn/what-if).

## Why do couples end up here?

Most unmarried couples do not choose to be unprotected. They assume the law will notice them. People say "we are basically married" and mean it, and a bank, a hospital or a probate clerk has no such category. The gap shows up at the worst moment, when one partner is in intensive care or has just died.

A few other points surprise people. A partner who contributed to the mortgage has no automatic ownership interest if only one name is on the deed. Bills for the funeral usually fall to whoever signs the contract, and the estate may reimburse them only after it opens. And a partner who is not a legal heir may have no standing to ask a court for anything at all in the estate, even to be told what is happening.

None of that requires a dramatic fix. Ask yourselves three plain questions. If I die first, where should the house go? Who do I want making medical choices? Who do I want holding my retirement account? Write the answers down, sign them properly, and the law will follow your answers instead of its own list.

One last point is about timing. A cohabitation agreement, signed while both partners are well, can record who owns what and who pays for what. It does not replace a will, but it gives a surviving partner something in writing if the heirs dispute who contributed to the house.
