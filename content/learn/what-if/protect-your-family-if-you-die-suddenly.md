Marcus, 41, was a project manager in Naperville with a wife, two kids under ten, and a heart that nobody knew was weak. He died on a Tuesday. His wife Elena had a joint checking account and a house in both names, so the mortgage kept getting paid. What she did not have was the password to his laptop, the name of the broker who held a small investment account, or any idea whether the life insurance he mentioned once came through work.

Nobody plans to die suddenly, which is exactly why the plan has to be finished before it is needed. This page shows what happens to a family in the first weeks when a person dies without warning, and the short list of things that change the outcome. For the wider set of gaps people leave open, see our [what-if guide](/learn/what-if).

## What happens in the first two weeks?

The first days are practical and fast. Someone has to identify the person, a funeral home takes over, and the family starts making calls. If the death was unexpected, a medical examiner or coroner may be involved, which can delay release of the body by a day or two. None of that needs a lawyer, and none of it depends on your estate plan.

The money side moves on a different clock. Think of your property in two groups.

| Property | Who can reach it, and when |
|---|---|
| Joint bank account | The surviving owner, immediately |
| House or account with a right of survivorship | The survivor, after recording a death certificate or sending one to the bank |
| Life insurance and retirement accounts with a named beneficiary | The beneficiary, usually within weeks of filing a claim |
| Assets in a funded living trust | The successor trustee, after showing the death certificate and trust pages |
| Anything in your name alone | No one, until a court appoints your executor or a small estate affidavit is accepted |

The last row is where families get stuck. An account in your name alone is frozen at the bank the day it learns of the death. A spouse can wait months for access, even with a will that leaves everything to them. Our guide to [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) explains the freeze and the ways around it.

## Who takes care of the children?

If one parent survives, the surviving parent is generally the legal guardian and nothing special is needed. The risk is the rare case in which both parents die, or one parent dies and the other cannot parent. Without a named guardian, a court appoints one. Family members can ask, and the judge decides what is best for the children, which may or may not be the aunt you would have chosen.

Naming a guardian in a will is the one place the law gives you a direct vote. Pick a first choice and a backup, and tell both. Our article on [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) works through the hard part, which is deciding between a loving sibling with a small house and a grandparent with a large one. If you will be away or ill, [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians) cover the gap before a court acts.

A guardian raises the children. Someone also has to manage whatever money they inherit, and an 18-year-old with a life insurance payout is a problem. For that, see [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Where do families actually get stuck?

In our experience, not at the will. Families get stuck on information. An executor with a perfect will still has to find every account, and the search can take months. These are the ones that go missing.

1. **The employer benefits.** Group life insurance, a 401(k) and a final paycheck all sit with the employer, and the family often does not know the HR contact.
2. **The phone and email.** Many accounts send a code to the phone. If it is locked, resetting passwords turns slow. Our page on [digital assets after death](/learn/digital-assets) covers what is possible without the password.
3. **The paper trail.** The deed, the car title, the insurance policies and the tax returns that name the accounts.
4. **The professionals.** Whether there is an attorney, an accountant or a financial advisor who already knows the picture.
5. **The wishes.** Burial or cremation, who should be called, what to do with the dog.

A single page can answer most of this. Our [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) article lists what to put on it, and [how to organize your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents) shows where to keep the originals.

## What is an eight-step plan that works this month?

You do not need a complete estate plan to improve the outcome. Do these in order and stop when you run out of time. Each one closes a different hole.

1. **Check every beneficiary form.** Retirement accounts and life insurance pay whoever is on the form, even if the will says otherwise. Look for an ex, a parent who has died, or a blank. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) walks through each account type.
2. **Name a guardian for your children** in a signed will, with a backup.
3. **Sign a will.** Even a simple will names an executor and a guardian and sets the shares. See [do I need a will](/learn/wills/do-i-need-a-will).
4. **Sign a durable power of attorney and a health care directive.** These work while you are alive. A sudden collapse that leaves you in a coma is as likely as sudden death.
5. **Make sure one account can be reached.** A joint account or a payable-on-death account with enough for three months of bills keeps a household running while the rest waits.
6. **Review life insurance.** If your family depends on your income, term coverage is often the cheapest protection you can buy. Use the [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) to size it.
7. **Write the one-page letter** described above and put a copy where your spouse and your executor can find it.
8. **Tell two people where it is.** A document in a safe nobody can open does not help.

## A worked example with invented numbers

Back to Marcus. Suppose he had done steps 1, 2 and 3 only.

His 401(k) of $210,000 named Elena, so she rolls it over without a court. His work life insurance of $300,000 named Elena, and she receives it after filing a claim. The house was in both names. The only asset in his name alone was a $58,000 brokerage account. In Illinois, a small estate affidavit can collect personal property up to $150,000 without a full probate case, so Elena uses one for the brokerage account and never opens an estate. The will's main job was the guardian clause.

Now change one fact: the 401(k) form still names Marcus's mother, who is 76. The money goes to her, not to Elena, and the will cannot change it. A form signed at his first job, and never looked at again, redirects $210,000. This is the most common version of the sudden-death surprise. Our article on [how often to review beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-often-to-review-beneficiary-designations) gives a simple schedule.

## How does state law change this?

State law decides the shortcut for a small estate and the default rules if there is no will.

In Illinois, a small estate affidavit works for personal property up to $150,000 as of 2026, and does not reach real estate. See the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) page and the state guide for [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois).

In New Jersey, the affidavit routes are $50,000 for a surviving spouse or partner and $20,000 for next of kin, filed with the county Surrogate. See [New Jersey's small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) and the state guide for [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey).

Without a will, each state's intestacy statute decides who inherits and who raises minor children, usually spouse and children first. The details differ enough to matter, so read the rules for [Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) or [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) rather than guess.
