Teresa's father, Luis, died in April. He owned a small house outright and left a will naming Teresa's brother Eddie as executor. It is now the following March. The house is empty, the grass is waist-high, and a notice from the county says the property tax is overdue. Eddie lives in another state and says the lawyer is "working on it."

A house is the most common reason a family feels trapped in probate. You cannot sell it, you cannot easily rent it, and you cannot walk away from it. This article explains why homes get stuck and what you can do.

## What happens by default

Title to a house owned by one person passes through the probate court. Until the court appoints a personal representative and issues letters, nobody has authority to sign a deed. After that, the executor holds the house for the estate, and the heirs do not own it yet in a practical sense.

That produces a set of limits:

| Task | Who can do it | Typical limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sell the house | Executor | Letters required; some states need court approval |
| Refinance or borrow | Executor | Rarely allowed; lenders resist |
| Rent it out | Executor | Needs authority and proper insurance |
| Move in | Heir | Needs the executor's consent |
| Retitle to heirs | Executor | Only after debts and court steps are done |

Probate steps include filing the will, giving notice to heirs and creditors, inventorying assets, waiting out the creditor claim period, paying debts and taxes, and then asking to distribute. The creditor period alone often runs several months, and it is set by state law. See [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) for the typical stages.

While all this runs, the house costs money. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, utilities and repairs continue. Many insurers restrict coverage on a vacant house after a set period, so a claim on an empty home can be denied. Mortgage servicers expect payments to continue as well.

## A realistic example

Back to Luis's house. Eddie files for probate in May. A cousin, Paula, who was not named in the will, files an objection in July and says Luis had been confused. The court sets a hearing for October, then continues it to January. The creditor period cannot close until the dispute resolves.

Meanwhile the roof leaks in a storm. The insurer, informed late that the house was vacant, disputes the claim. Teresa pays the property tax herself to stop penalties. The executor has no estate cash because Luis's bank account is also frozen under the same process. By the time the will is upheld, the house has lost value and the estate owes attorney fees for two hearings. Nobody can sell until the court signs the order.

The cost drivers here were not the house. They were the dispute, the vacancy and an executor who did not communicate. A routine estate with a cooperative family often finishes in under a year, though the timeline depends on the state and the court.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are an executor or an heir

1. **Secure the house today.** Change the locks if needed, collect mail, and have the water and heat set to prevent damage.
2. **Call the insurance company.** Tell them the owner died and the house may be vacant. Ask what coverage is available and what it costs. A vacancy endorsement may be needed.
3. **Call the mortgage servicer.** Find out the balance, the payment status and whether the loan is assumable by an heir.
4. **Pay or track carrying costs.** Keep receipts. The estate repays them at the end.
5. **Ask for a sale authority.** If you want to sell, ask the lawyer whether the court issues letters with power to sell, or whether a confirmation hearing is needed. Our article on [heirs selling during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) explains the options.
6. **Check for a faster path.** Some states let a smaller or simpler procedure move a house sooner. Ask whether yours does.

### If the executor is the problem

1. **Ask in writing for a status report.** Name the date and the questions.
2. **Request a court status conference.** Heirs are usually allowed to ask the court to set one.
3. **Ask the court to compel an accounting.** This puts the executor's work on the record.
4. **Petition to remove the executor** when there is neglect or conflict. See [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).
5. **Get an attorney involved.** Our article on [whether you need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer) lays out when it helps.

### A simple monthly routine

Stuck estates move faster when someone keeps a rhythm. Once a month, the executor can drive by or visit the house, confirm the insurance and tax payments, email the heirs a three-line update, and note what the court is waiting on. If the wait is on the court, ask the clerk for the next available date instead of assuming. Small habits like these also protect the executor, since a clear record answers most later complaints about how the house was handled.

## How state law changes this

Probate is run by state law and often by county courts. States differ on how long creditors have to file claims, on whether the executor can sell without a hearing, on whether a will contest suspends the sale, on simplified procedures for small estates or for estates where the house is the only asset, and on how fast courts move. Some states treat a primary residence specially for exemptions and homestead rights for a surviving spouse or children. See the [Florida guide](/estate-planning/florida) for how one state handles homestead property, or ask an attorney in your state.

## How to prevent it

The house should not be in probate in the first place. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) funded with the deed, a transfer-on-death deed where the state allows one, or joint ownership with the right type of title can each move the house outside the court. Each has tradeoffs, including property tax, Medicaid and tax basis, which our article on [ways to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) compares. The [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) will give you a sense of the cost for your own situation.

## What do families miss?

They miss that the clock starts at death, not at filing. Delays in opening the case are delays in the creditor period, and so in every step after.

They miss vacancy rules in insurance policies. A phone call in week one is cheap. A denied roof claim in month eight is not.

And they miss that a stuck probate is often a communication problem. An executor who sends a short update every month rarely faces a complaint. One who goes silent invites a petition. If you are the executor, say what you are doing, even when the answer is waiting.
