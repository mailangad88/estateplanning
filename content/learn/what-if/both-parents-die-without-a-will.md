Dan and Priya, both 36, have a six-year-old son, Arjun, and a baby, Leela. They own a house with a mortgage, each has an employer life insurance policy, and between them they have two retirement accounts. They have been meaning to see a lawyer since Leela was born. Neither has a will.

The chance of both parents dying together is small. It is not zero, and the consequences are lopsided. A court will make the biggest decisions in these children's lives, and it will make them from a file. This article follows that process and shows what a short set of documents would change.

## What happens by default

Two things happen, and they run on separate tracks.

**Who raises the children.** The probate or family court appoints a guardian of the person. No one is chosen in advance, so relatives petition. The judge looks at the children's best interests: where they live, who they already know, how the home looks, whether siblings stay together. The result may be reasonable. It may also be a contest between two sets of grandparents. Read our companion article on [what happens to children when no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) for how that proceeding runs.

**Who gets and manages the property.** Each parent's probate estate passes under the state's [intestacy rules](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will). When a married parent dies, the surviving spouse generally takes all or most. When both are gone, the children are the heirs. They inherit in equal shares, and because they are minors, a court appoints someone to hold the money until each reaches the age set by state law. See [what happens if a minor inherits money with no trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust).

Some assets skip all of this. Life insurance, retirement accounts and payable-on-death accounts go to the named beneficiary. Property held in joint tenancy or as tenants by the entirety passes to the surviving owner, which does not help when both owners are gone.

## A realistic example

Dan and Priya die in a highway accident on a Saturday. The children are with Priya's brother, Anil, that weekend. Anil keeps them, which is the easy part. On Monday Dan's mother, Carol, arrives from three states away and asks to take them home. Both are loving, and both are qualified. Neither has any legal document.

Anil files for emergency guardianship. Carol files her own petition. A judge appoints Anil as temporary guardian and sets a hearing in six weeks. A guardian ad litem, a person who investigates for the court, interviews both households. The final order comes after four months. Nobody did anything wrong, and the children lived through months of uncertainty.

Meanwhile the estates open. The house has a mortgage, and nobody is living in it. The employer policies named each other, so the proceeds go to the estates and then to the children. A separate court officer is appointed to hold that money. It will sit in a restricted account, with bond costs and annual reports. Attorney time, filing fees and the number of hearings drive the cost. At 18, Arjun receives his share outright.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are a parent today

1. **Name a guardian in a will.** Both parents should sign matching wills. Name a first choice and a backup. See [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian).
2. **Put the money in a trust.** A trust in the will, or a living trust, lets you pick the manager and the ages. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).
3. **Fix beneficiary forms.** Name each other first, then the trust as contingent beneficiary. Do not leave the contingent line empty.
4. **Estimate what the guardian will need.** The [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) turns years of childcare, school and housing into a number, which helps you size term life insurance.
5. **Tell the people you picked.** A guardian who first learns of the role at a funeral is a poor plan.
6. **Write a letter.** See the [letter of intent for your children's guardian](/learn/guardianship/letter-of-intent-for-your-childrens-guardian).

### If it has already happened

1. **Get an emergency order quickly.** A relative caring for the children should consult an attorney about temporary guardianship right away.
2. **Look for any documents.** A will may be in a drawer or a safe deposit box, a lawyer's file or a digital account.
3. **Find the beneficiary forms.** Employer plans, insurance and retirement accounts usually have these.
4. **Ask the family to agree on a single petitioner.** Joint support before a judge speeds a hearing.
5. **Open the estates.** Someone has to apply to be administrator of each estate.

### Who pays for the interim

Until the court acts, a relative caring for the children often pays for food, clothing and school out of pocket. Keep every receipt. Once a guardian of the estate is appointed, that person can usually ask to be repaid from the children's funds, but only with records. Some families also find that the children qualify for Social Security survivor benefits through a deceased parent's work record, which can be applied for while the estates are still open. Ask the Social Security Administration at ssa.gov or by phone about survivor benefits for children.

## How state law changes this

Intestacy shares, the age at which minors receive property, the rules on emergency guardianship, who may petition and whether the court names a separate manager of the money all vary by state. States also differ on the standard of proof for choosing among relatives. Community property states treat the couple's assets differently from common-law states. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/california) lays out the specifics for one example, and an attorney in your state can answer the rest.

## How to prevent it

For parents of minor children, the first documents should be a will with a guardian nomination and a trust for the children's money. Many families add a durable power of attorney and a healthcare directive for each parent at the same visit. ## What do families overlook?

They overlook that being the best choice is not the same as being the named choice. A judge cannot read your mind, and a relative who is entirely right for the children still has to be proved right.

They overlook the mortgage and the house. A home with a loan and no occupant has to be insured, maintained and eventually sold or kept, and someone needs legal authority to do any of it. That is the estate administrator's job, and the court has to appoint one first.

They also overlook the gap between a will and a plan. A will without a trust still hands the children their money outright at a statutory age. Naming the guardian answers the first question and leaves the second one open.
