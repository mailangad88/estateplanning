Helen, 81, had a fall last winter. Her son Greg lives nearby and offered to help with her bills, so her bank put his name on her checking and savings accounts. It took five minutes. Greg meant well. Two years later he lost his job, borrowed from the accounts "until things pick up," and has not paid it back. Her other daughter, Joan, found out when a statement showed a balance that had dropped by $38,000.

Helen asked the bank to put the money back. The bank said it could not, since Greg was an owner and the withdrawals were his right. This scenario is among the most common. This page explains why, what can go wrong beyond spending, and what to do instead.

## What does a joint account actually do?

It makes each person an owner of the whole balance. The bank does not track whose money it was, and either owner can withdraw all of it. Banks use the same form for a joint account whether you are married, a parent with an adult child, or two friends. See [joint ownership versus a beneficiary designation](/learn/beneficiary-designations/joint-ownership-vs-beneficiary-designation).

Four things follow, and most people only think of the first.

| Risk | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spending | The child can withdraw everything and the bank will allow it | Greg takes $38,000 |
| Child's creditors | A judgment creditor or a tax lien against the child can reach the account | A court judgment against Greg freezes Helen's savings |
| Child's divorce or bankruptcy | The account may be argued to be marital property or part of a bankruptcy estate | Greg's wife claims half of what is in the account |
| Your death | The account usually goes to the surviving owner outside the will | Greg gets it all, Joan gets nothing |

The second and third rows catch people out. Your child's financial troubles become your money's troubles. Courts often look at whose money it was and whether the child had any real claim, but you may spend years proving it.

## What if the child spent the money?

First, separate two situations.

**You agreed to it, or at least went along.** The law mostly treats a joint owner's withdrawals as theirs to make. Unless there was a written understanding that the money was for you only, a court is unlikely to order it repaid. A moral claim is not the same as a legal one.

**You did not agree and did not know.** Then you may have a claim, because a joint owner who takes money for personal use without consent can be liable in many states, especially if you were relying on them as a helper. If the account holder is older or has dementia, the conduct may be financial exploitation. In that case, adult protective services can investigate, and so can the police. See [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) for how those complaints work.

### What should Helen do this week?

1. **Stop further withdrawals.** Ask the bank to remove Greg or to close the account and open a new one in her name only.
2. **Pull 12 months of statements** and mark the transactions she did not know about.
3. **Move to a safer setup,** such as a power of attorney for bill-paying that she can revoke.
4. **Talk to Greg,** preferably with a third person present, and put any repayment plan in writing.
5. **Speak to an attorney** if the amount is large or if she suspects exploitation.
6. **Tell Joan,** so there are no secrets that later become a will contest.

## What if the child dies first, or sues, or divorces?

If the child dies first, you usually remain the owner of the entire account, and their name is simply removed. Banks often ask for a death certificate. The child's estate generally has no right to your money, though some states and banks apply a presumption about each owner's share, so ask the bank.

If a creditor sues the child, a bank can freeze a joint account while it sorts out whose money it is. The freeze can last weeks, and your bills may bounce in the meantime. A divorce or bankruptcy filing raises the same question in a different court.

## What happens to the account when you die?

For a joint account with a right of survivorship, the surviving owner takes it automatically. Your will does not control it. If Helen's will leaves everything equally to Greg and Joan, but the $200,000 account is joint with Greg, Greg gets all of it and Joan gets half of everything else. That is the surprise in [which document controls](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form).

Some states let other heirs argue that the account was only a convenience, opened so the child could pay bills. The estate must usually show this with evidence such as the bank form, the history of deposits and what the owner said. A clear written statement helps. In Illinois, the rules on joint and payable-on-death accounts are covered in [our Illinois article](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts), and New Jersey has its own rules.

## Does it affect taxes or Medicaid?

Adding a child to an account is generally not a completed gift on that day, because you can still take the money back. A gift can occur when the child withdraws funds for themselves. Gifts above the annual exclusion, $19,000 per recipient currently, need a gift tax return, though most families owe no tax. Medicaid looks at transfers more strictly. Money moved to a child within the 60-month look-back period in most states can cause a penalty. See [gifting to children before Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid).

## What would a better conversation sound like?

Most of these cases start with a kind offer and no discussion of the rules. Before adding anyone, say out loud what the account is for: "This is my money, you pay my bills from it, and what is left at my death goes equally to you and your sister." Then write that sentence down, sign it, and keep a copy with the bank form. It will not stop a determined person, but it changes what a court sees later, and it often stops an honest child from drifting into using the money for themselves.

## What are safer ways to get help?

| Goal | Better tool | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Child pays your bills | Financial power of attorney | Lets them act for you, stays your money, ends at your death |
| Child helps but you want a bank record | Agency or convenience account | Names them as your agent with a duty to use funds for you |
| Child inherits the account | Payable-on-death designation | They own nothing until you die, and you can change it |
| Several children share equally | POD with percentages, or a trust | Divides the account as you choose |
| Large or complex assets | Revocable living trust | A trustee you pick manages the money |

A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) handles the helper role cleanly. A [payable-on-death account](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) handles the inheritance. Doing them separately keeps the two jobs from getting tangled.
