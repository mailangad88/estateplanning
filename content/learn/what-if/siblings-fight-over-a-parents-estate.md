Carol and her brother Dennis did not speak for eleven months after their mother died. The will left the house to Carol, who had lived with their mother, and split the savings in half. Dennis believed the will had been changed while their mother was ill. Carol believed Dennis wanted money he had not earned. Neither of them wanted to be the sibling who sued.

If you are in a version of this, you are not unusual. This page follows how such disputes proceed, what they cost and which doors are still open. For one common flashpoint, the shared house, see [siblings inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree). The wider topic of will disputes sits in the [wills guide](/learn/wills).

## What happens by default

The probate court supervises the estate, and a judge has the last word. A dispute generally takes one of four forms.

| Type of dispute | What the sibling argues | What the court looks at |
|---|---|---|
| Will contest | The will is invalid because of incapacity, undue influence, fraud or improper signing | Evidence of the person's state of mind and who had influence |
| Executor dispute | The executor is hiding records, favoring themselves or moving too slowly | The executor's duties, an accounting, possible removal |
| Asset dispute | An item was a gift, a loan or already given away | Receipts, records, testimony |
| Personal property | Who gets the jewelry, tools or photos | Whatever the will says, then agreement or a court order |

Challenges have deadlines. In many states, a person who wants to contest a will must file within a short window after receiving notice, sometimes just a few months. Missing it can end the claim.

## What do estate fights actually cost?

Mostly money, time and family. Specific numbers vary too much by state and case to quote, but the pattern is steady. Attorneys charge by the hour. Each filing invites a response. Medical reviewers such as doctors who read the records add cost. The estate may be frozen, so the siblings who need money cannot get it, and the house may sit empty and need insurance and repairs.

Courts and judges also push parties toward settlement. Mediation is often required before trial, and the large majority of estate disputes settle before a judge decides. The relationships rarely recover as quickly as the legal case does.

## A realistic example

Back to Carol and Dennis. Dennis's lawyer sends a letter asking for the medical records, the lawyer's file for the will and an accounting of the estate. Carol, as executor, hires her own attorney. The court sets a schedule. The estate cannot distribute until it ends. The house remains in Carol's hands, but she cannot sell it, and carrying costs run from the estate.

The mother's doctor says she was clear when she signed. The drafting lawyer testifies that she met with their mother alone. That evidence makes the contest weak. At mediation, Dennis agrees to drop it in exchange for the family piano, a share of the photographs and Carol's promise to provide a full accounting. The whole thing took most of a year. The drivers were the court's calendar, how many documents had to be exchanged and how firm each sibling felt.

## What can still be fixed?

**If you are in a dispute now:**

1. Ask for the documents in writing: the will, any trust, the inventory and the accounting. Keep a log of who said what.
2. Note any deadline in the notice you received. Talk to a probate attorney before it passes.
3. Separate the money question from the grievance. A contest needs a legal ground, such as lack of capacity or undue influence. See [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).
4. Try mediation early. A neutral person can price an offer, divide the belongings and reduce legal fees.
5. If the executor is the problem, read about [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).
6. Watch for a no-contest clause. If the will includes one, an unsuccessful challenge may cost the challenger their gift. See [no-contest clauses](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will).

**If you are the executor:** keep every record, communicate in writing, avoid favoring yourself and consider hiring a lawyer sooner rather than later. An executor owes duties to every beneficiary, including the one who is angry.

## What are the most common triggers?

After watching many families go through this, certain patterns repeat. The first is surprise. A child learns at the reading of the will that a sibling got the house, or that a charity or a caregiver was added late. Silence in life turns into suspicion after death.

The second is the executor who is also a beneficiary, often the child who lived closest. They make decisions about the house, sell items and get paid or reimbursed, and a distant sibling reads every move as self-dealing. Keeping receipts and sending short updates matters more than people expect.

The third is personal belongings. A set of dishes or a watch costs little, but carries a parent's memory, and arguments over such things can cost more than the house. The fourth is money given during life, such as a down payment or a loan. Was it a gift, an advance on the inheritance or a debt? If the parent never wrote it down, each child remembers differently.

Naming the trigger helps pick the fix. A document dispute needs a lawyer. A belongings dispute usually needs a turn-taking system. A gift dispute needs the records.

## How state law changes this

States differ on contest deadlines, on who has standing to sue, on whether a jury is allowed, on what gets presumed when a caregiver benefits and on whether a no-contest clause is enforced. In some states, a clause is enforced unless the challenger had good reason, and in others it is weak. Mediation rules differ too. Check your state guide, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [New York](/estate-planning/new-york), and talk to a local attorney about deadlines.

## How to prevent it

Parents can do more than most realize. Explain unequal shares in writing or in a family conversation, because surprise is what lights the fuse. Name a neutral executor or a professional if the family is tense. Use a [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) when privacy and speed matter, and keep a signed list for heirlooms, as described in [dividing personal property](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms). A short note of why each choice was made is cheap and useful. The article on [unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) covers how to do that without inviting a fight.
