Their mother, Doreen, died in March. The house on Alder Street was the only real asset. Her three children, Paul, Renata and Chris, each own a third of it now, and they have not spoken civilly since the funeral. Paul lives two towns away and wants the money. Renata wants to keep the house for sentimental reasons. Chris has been living there rent-free for six years.

This is one of the most common ways a good plan goes bad. Nobody did anything wrong. The house was simply left to several people with no instructions. This article explains what the law does then, and what the family can still do.

## What happens by default

When a house passes to more than one person, they usually hold it together as co-owners. Each owns a share of the entire property, not a room or a floor. Whether that is through a will, a trust that says "equally to my children," or [intestate succession](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) when there was no will, the result is the same: everyone has a say, and no one has the final one.

That means:

- No sibling can sell the house alone. A buyer and a title company will ask for every owner's signature.
- No sibling can force the others to keep it. Any owner can ask a court to end the co-ownership.
- Every owner is responsible for the carrying costs: property tax, insurance, utilities and repairs.
- Each owner may use the house, but none may lock the others out.

The court tool for ending co-ownership is called a partition action. The judge can divide the property, which almost never works for a single house, or order a sale and split the money by shares. Many states also let the owners who want to stay buy out the owner who wants to leave at a court-set value. Partition is slow, public and costly, since attorneys, an appraiser and sometimes a court-appointed referee all get paid from the property.

Before any of this, the estate itself usually has to be settled. If the house is in a probate estate, the executor holds legal control until the court transfers title. See [can heirs sell the house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) for what the executor can and cannot do in the meantime.

## A realistic example

Back to Doreen's children. Probate closes after about nine months, and a deed puts the house in all three names. Paul sends an email proposing a sale. Renata replies that she will not sell her mother's home. Chris says nothing and keeps living there, but stops forwarding the tax bills.

Property tax goes unpaid for a year. Renata pays it, then asks for reimbursement. Chris says he has maintained the house and owes nothing. Paul files a partition action in the second year. The court orders an appraisal, then a sale. The house sells for the appraised value, less the agent's commission, the legal fees and the back taxes. Each child gets a third of what is left, which is noticeably less than a third of the house was worth on the day Doreen died.

The cost drivers here were time, the number of lawyers and how long the house sat. A buyout in month three would have cost a fraction of that. A similar problem is common with land held by families across generations. Our article on [heirs property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) explains how that can end in forced sales.

## What can still be fixed

### If the siblings already own the house

1. **Get the facts on paper.** Agree on who owns what share, what the mortgage balance is, what taxes and insurance cost, and who has paid what since the death.
2. **Order an appraisal.** Hire one licensed appraiser and share the report. Do not argue about value until you have a number.
3. **Choose a path.** The usual ones are a sale, a buyout of one or more siblings, or a period of joint ownership with rules. Write the choice down.
4. **Sign a co-ownership agreement if you keep it.** It should say who pays what, who may live there and pay rent, how repairs above a set amount get approved, and what happens if someone wants out. Include a way to price a buyout.
5. **Use a neutral person.** A mediator costs far less than a lawsuit. A trusted relative or family friend can help, but a mediator is better if the conflict is personal.
6. **Understand the tax side.** The house usually received a step-up in basis at the parent's death, so a quick sale often has little taxable gain. See [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property).

### If the parent is still alive

1. **Decide the house's future, then say it.** Leave it to one child with an equalizing gift to the others, direct a sale, or put it in a trust.
2. **Name a decision-maker.** A trustee or executor with clear power to sell, keep or rent the house stops the vote-counting.
3. **Say why.** A short letter explaining the choice settles more arguments than a clause does.

## How state law changes this

Partition law is state law. States differ on whether a court must offer a buyout before a sale, how a sale is conducted, whether a co-owner in possession owes rent, and how reimbursement for taxes and repairs is handled. Many states have adopted a uniform partition statute that gives family co-owners extra protections against a forced sale, while others have not. Property that passes at death in a community property state also raises its own questions. Check your [state guide](/estate-planning/texas) or ask an attorney in your state before filing anything.

## How to prevent it

The cleanest fix is a decision made by the parent. Options include leaving the house to one child with other assets to the rest, directing the trustee of a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) to sell and divide the money, or putting the house in an LLC or trust with operating rules. Our article on [leaving your home to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) compares the options. The [who inherits tool](/tools/who-inherits) shows what the default rules would do in your family.

What does not work is the sentence "my children will work it out." Some do. The ones who do not pay for it in cash and years.

## What do families usually miss?

They miss the carrying costs. A house with no one in it still costs money every month, and a vacant house can lose its insurance coverage after a set period, since many policies exclude long vacancies. Check with the insurer in the first few weeks.

They also miss that the sibling living there has a real advantage. Free housing is worth something, and the others usually notice. Naming a fair rent early, even a modest one, removes a source of resentment.

Another miss is the mortgage. If Doreen had a reverse mortgage or a home equity loan, the lender may demand repayment within months of the death, which can force the siblings' hand before they have talked at all. Ask for a payoff statement early.

Finally, they treat the house as a symbol. A house is also a pile of money split among people who may need it differently. Sorting out the money question first, with the appraisal and a plain statement of what each person needs, often makes the emotional question easier.
