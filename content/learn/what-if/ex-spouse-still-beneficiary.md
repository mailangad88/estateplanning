Dana and Rob divorced six years ago. Rob updated his will right away, leaving everything to their two kids. But he never touched the beneficiary form on his old life insurance policy from work, and Dana's name is still on it. If Rob died tomorrow, who would get the money?

The answer depends on the type of account and the state. This article explains how the rules work, what a family can do if the person has already died, and how to make sure it does not happen to you.

## What happens by default

A beneficiary form is a contract between you and the company that holds the account. When you die, the company pays the person named on the form. It does not read your will first, and it does not ask whether you are divorced. That is why a form can override a will.

What changes the result is state law, and sometimes federal law.

- **State revocation-on-divorce laws.** Many states have a law that treats an ex-spouse as if they had died before you. The model for these laws is Section 2-804 of the Uniform Probate Code. In states that follow it, a divorce usually cancels what you left the ex in a will, a trust, and many non-probate transfers such as life insurance and payable-on-death accounts.
- **States without the rule.** Not every state has this law, and the ones that do write it differently. Some cover only wills. Some cover insurance but not retirement accounts. In a state with no such rule, the ex may simply be paid.
- **Federal law for employer plans.** A 401(k), a pension and group life insurance through work are usually covered by a federal law called ERISA. In *Egelhoff v. Egelhoff* (2001), the U.S. Supreme Court held that ERISA overrides a state's revocation-on-divorce law for these plans. In *Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont* (2009), the Court said the plan must follow its documents and pay the named person, even when a divorce decree waived the ex's rights.

There is one more case. In *Sveen v. Melin* (2018), the Court upheld Minnesota's revocation law as applied to an individual life insurance policy. So for a policy you bought on your own, a state law can often remove the ex. Still, that depends on your state, and you should not count on it.

| Account type | Who usually decides | Does divorce remove an ex? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Will | State law | In most states, yes |
| Individual life insurance | State law and the policy | Often, but it varies by state |
| Bank account with a payable-on-death form | State law and the bank | Varies by state |
| IRA | State law and the custodian | Varies by state and by account |
| 401(k), pension, work life insurance | ERISA and the plan documents | Generally no |

### Why the divorce decree matters

A divorce decree can cut both ways. Some decrees say each side gives up all claims to the other's accounts, and that language can help a family later. Other decrees do the opposite and order one spouse to keep the other, or the children, as beneficiary of a life insurance policy until child support ends or until alimony is paid off. A court order like that is a real obligation. If you remove the ex anyway, the ex or the children may bring a claim against your estate for the value of the policy.

The *Kennedy* case shows the limit of a waiver. The ex-wife had waived her rights in the decree, but the plan documents still named her, and the plan paid her. The lesson is that a decree is not a substitute for changing the form.

## A realistic example

Back to Rob. He dies and the insurer pays the beneficiary on file, which is Dana. Rob lived in a state with a revocation law, so his children point to it and ask the insurer to hold the payment. The insurer may pause and ask for a court order, because it does not want to pay twice.

Meanwhile, Rob also had a 401(k) at work with Dana still named. That plan is covered by ERISA. The plan administrator pays Dana, and the state law does not change that.

Time and cost depend on how clear the form is and whether anyone disputes it. A simple claim can take weeks. A fight in court can take many months, and legal fees come out of someone's pocket. Rob's kids might have to prove which law applies to each account. All of it could have been avoided by a short form signed years earlier.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are alive

1. **Read your divorce decree.** Look for any clause that requires you to keep an ex as beneficiary, often to secure child support or alimony. If one exists, follow it or ask the court to change it.
2. **List every account.** Include life insurance, 401(k), pension, IRA, annuity, bank accounts with payable-on-death forms, brokerage accounts with transfer-on-death forms and health savings accounts. Our [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit) walks through them one by one.
3. **Request the current form.** Ask each company what is on file now. Do not rely on memory.
4. **Name new people.** Pick a primary and a contingent beneficiary for each account. See [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).
5. **Keep proof.** Save the confirmation page or letter. Store a list of accounts with your will.
6. **Update your will and any trust too.** The [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) article covers the other documents.

### If the person has already died

1. **Do not spend or move anything yet.** Contact the plan or insurer and tell them you are the family or the executor.
2. **Get the claim file.** Ask for the beneficiary form, the plan document and the date it was signed.
3. **Check the decree.** An ex may have a valid claim because the decree required it.
4. **Ask a lawyer about your state's law.** In some states, family members can bring a claim against an ex who was paid in error. The strength of that claim differs by state and by account.
5. **Consider negotiating.** Some exes agree to split or return a payment, particularly when the form was plainly outdated. A written agreement is safer than a handshake.

## How state law changes this

States differ on four points. First, whether a revocation-on-divorce law exists at all. Second, which assets it covers, such as wills only, or insurance and retirement accounts as well. Third, whether it applies when the divorce is pending but not final. Fourth, whether the family can recover money the ex was already paid.

Because of that, you should treat any state rule as a backup and not a plan. Our state guides, such as the one for [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), show how local rules shape a plan. The same general rule holds everywhere: the form on file is the safest thing to control.

## How to prevent it

Timing matters most. A divorce can take a year or more, and the forms often stay unchanged during that time. Some states and courts place automatic restraining orders on both spouses during a divorce, which can block changes to insurance and beneficiaries until the case ends. Ask your divorce lawyer what applies to you, then make your updates the week the decree is final.

The prevention is not complicated. After any divorce:

- Update each beneficiary form, one account at a time.
- Re-sign your will and, if you have one, your living trust.
- Review your plan with an attorney if you have children, a business or large accounts.
