Margaret, 78, signed a financial power of attorney naming her youngest son, Dennis, as agent. He lives ten minutes away and does her bills. Her older daughter, Louise, lives two states away and was not consulted. After Margaret's fall, Louise decides that Dennis is the wrong person and tells the bank she is "overriding" the document. The teller is not sure whether she can.

She cannot. But Louise is not without options, either. This page explains what the law allows when relatives disagree with the person a parent chose, and what a parent can do now to head it off.

## What happens by default

A signed, valid power of attorney is the principal's own decision, and it holds until something ends it. Four things can end it.

- **You revoke it**, in writing, while you still have capacity.
- **You die.** The agent's authority stops, and the executor named in your will takes over estate matters. See [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).
- **The document has an end date or condition** that has now occurred.
- **A court limits or ends it.**

An adult child cannot end it by objecting, by writing the bank a letter, or by calling the agent a bad choice. A bank that accepts such a letter without a court order is taking a risk. Banks are cautious in the other direction too, and may refuse to deal with a valid agent for their own reasons. Our piece on a [bank that refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) covers that problem.

What relatives can do is ask a court for help. Most states have a statute that lets certain people petition to review how an agent is acting. The court has several tools.

| Court order | What it does |
|---|---|
| Require an accounting | The agent must list receipts, payments and transfers |
| Limit the agent's powers | The agent can keep paying bills but cannot sell property or make gifts |
| Require a bond or a monitor | Adds oversight without changing who acts |
| Remove the agent | A named successor or a court-appointed substitute steps in |
| Appoint a conservator | Replaces the power of attorney with court supervision |

### What does a family have to show?

Courts begin from the principal's choice. A relative who says "I would have picked someone else" has not given a judge a reason to act. The facts that persuade a court are more concrete: money moving into the agent's own account without explanation, unpaid bills while the agent spends on himself, a parent isolated from other relatives, or a document signed when the principal was confused. Our article on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) lists the warning signs.

Agents also owe a duty to act in the principal's interest and keep their own money separate. A clean record is the strongest protection an agent has.

## A realistic example

Louise gets a lawyer and files a petition in the county where Margaret lives. She alleges that Dennis moved $12,000 to his own checking account the month after the fall.

Dennis answers with receipts. He used the money to pay a contractor for a wheelchair ramp and to cover Margaret's property tax, and he repaid the account from the sale of her car. The judge orders an accounting, looks at it, and finds the expenses reasonable. The petition is denied, but the court orders Dennis to send an annual summary to Louise.

That ending is common when an agent is honest. The cost to the family is real, though: legal fees on both sides, months of delay, and a relationship that does not heal quickly. Had Dennis kept no records, the result could easily have gone the other way. The time and cost drivers are the court calendar, the depth of the accounting, and whether anyone calls witnesses.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are the person who signed it

1. **Decide whether you want to keep your choice.** If you do, say so in a short signed letter, dated, stating why you picked this agent.
2. **If you want to change it, sign a new power of attorney** that revokes the old one. Tell the old agent in writing and notify banks and brokers.
3. **Name a backup agent** and consider naming a person to receive copies of statements. That gives a skeptical child a way to check in without going to court.
4. **Consider co-agents or a separate agent for medical decisions.** Splitting jobs between children can head off jealousy. Read [naming more than one agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-name-more-than-one-power-of-attorney-agent) first, since two agents can deadlock.
5. **Tell the family** what you chose and why, even if it is an awkward talk. Our guide on [talking to family about your plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) gives a script.

### If you are a relative and worried

1. **Ask to see the document** and read it. It may give you rights you did not know about, such as a reporting clause.
2. **Ask the agent for an accounting**, politely, in writing.
3. **Keep your own notes** on dates and amounts.
4. **If you suspect abuse, contact adult protective services** in your county. They can investigate without a lawsuit.
5. **See an attorney about a petition** if the agent will not cooperate. Say what you want, such as an accounting, a limit or a replacement, rather than only that you are unhappy.
6. **Consider mediation.** A neutral person can sometimes arrange a compromise, like adding a co-agent, faster than a courtroom.

## How state law changes this

States differ on who can petition, what the petitioner must plead, whether the agent must keep records, whether the court can order a transfer back, and how an agent's duties are described. Many states follow a uniform act, but with changes. A few have special rules for gifts or for real estate. Healthcare agents are governed by separate statutes. Start with our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides and ask an attorney about local practice.

## How to prevent it

The strongest protection is a clear choice and a clear explanation. Pick someone who is organized, honest and nearby, and read [how to choose a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) before you decide. Have the document signed with the formalities your state requires, with a notary even if your state does not insist. Add language that tells the agent to keep records and give accountings on request.

Healthcare choices need the same care. If you worry that relatives will clash with your healthcare agent, our article on [who can overrule a healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-can-overrule-your-healthcare-directive) explains how hospitals and courts treat those disputes.
