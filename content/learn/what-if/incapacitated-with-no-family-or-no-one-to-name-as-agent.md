Marguerite, 74, lives alone in an apartment she has rented for thirty years. She has no spouse, no children, and a sister in another country she talks to at Christmas. She does not know anyone she would trust with her bank account, so she never signed a power of attorney. One morning she falls and is taken to the hospital with a head injury. When she wakes, she is confused, and the doctors want to talk to someone about surgery.

This is the version of incapacity planning that people with strong families do not have to think about. This article explains who decides when there is no one obvious, what a court does, and how to name someone even if you feel you have no one.

## What happens by default

Three layers of rules apply, and they tend to move from informal to formal.

**1. The hospital's emergency authority.** When treatment cannot wait, doctors treat without consent to prevent death or serious harm. This covers the first hours. It does not cover choosing a rehabilitation facility, signing a nursing home contract, or agreeing to surgery that is not urgent.

**2. The default surrogate list.** Many states have a statute that lists, in order, who may make healthcare decisions for a patient who cannot. It typically starts with a spouse, then adult children, then parents, then adult siblings. A number of states add a close friend or an "adult who has shown special care and concern", but the wording and conditions vary. For Marguerite, a sister overseas may be on the list on paper and unreachable in practice.

**3. A court-appointed guardian.** For anything longer term, and for money, a hospital or social worker files a petition in the probate or family court. The judge appoints a [guardian](/glossary/guardian) for personal and medical decisions, a [conservator](/glossary/conservatorship) for finances, or one person for both. When no relative or friend steps up, the court often turns to a professional, a nonprofit or a county public guardian.

### What the money side looks like

Medical decisions are only half of it. Rent, the electric bill and the credit card statement keep arriving. A bank will not let a hospital social worker write checks. Without a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), nobody can pay bills, deal with Medicare or Social Security issues, or end a lease. That is the usual reason a guardianship petition is filed.

## A realistic example

Marguerite's surgeon cannot reach her sister. The hospital's ethics committee reviews the case, and two physicians agree on a limited procedure. For everything after that, the social worker finds there is nobody to sign her into a rehabilitation facility.

The hospital asks the court for a guardian. A court investigator interviews Marguerite and files a report. A hearing is set a few weeks out. Meanwhile she stays in the hospital longer than medically needed, because no one can authorize discharge. The court appoints a professional guardian from a county list.

The professional chooses where Marguerite lives, manages her income and files annual reports. The cost drivers are the court filing and the lawyer's fees, the investigator, the guardian's hourly rate and the ongoing bond and accounting. These come out of Marguerite's own money. Everything she would have decided for herself is now decided by a stranger, with court oversight.

## What can still be fixed

### If you can still sign

1. **Decide who, not whether.** People often say they have no one when they mean no obvious person. List the people who know you and would answer the phone: a friend from church, a former coworker, a neighbor, a cousin, the adult child of a friend.
2. **Sign a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney)** naming one agent and at least one backup. Our guide on [choosing a healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) lists what to look for.
3. **Sign a durable financial power of attorney** the same way. If you do not have a person for this, consider a professional fiduciary. The article on [geriatric care managers, money managers and fiduciaries](/learn/elder-care/geriatric-care-managers-money-managers-and-fiduciaries) explains what these roles cost and who supervises them.
4. **Write a [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will)** so your agent, or a doctor, knows what you want when the choice is hard.
5. **Name your preferred guardian** in the same documents, so a court has your choice on record.
6. **Give copies** to your doctor, your bank and the person you named. A document nobody knows about does not help.
7. **Consider a revocable trust.** A funded [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) lets a successor trustee, who can be a bank or a trust company, pay your bills if you cannot. It handles money only and does not replace a healthcare document.

### If you are already unable to sign

1. **Ask the hospital social worker** whether a surrogate can be identified under your state's statute.
2. **Tell the court of any friend or neighbor willing to serve.** Many courts prefer a person who knows the individual to a stranger.
3. **Ask about limited guardianship.** Some courts will grant authority only for the specific decisions that need it, which leaves the person more independence.

## Choosing between a friend and a professional

Each choice has a trade. A friend knows you and will often visit, but may not know how to deal with a bank or a nursing home, and may be reluctant to say no to a request that feels unkind. A professional knows the paperwork and will not get tired, but charges for time, has never met you, and may look after twenty other clients.

Many people end up with a mix: a friend as healthcare agent, who can sit beside the hospital bed and speak for your values, and a professional or trust company on the money side, who keeps records and reports. Whatever you choose, write down your reasons and wishes in a letter your agent can read. Our article on [how to document your end-of-life wishes](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-document-your-end-of-life-wishes) is a good model.

Ask any professional three questions before you name them: who supervises you, what do you charge for each task, and what happens to my file if your firm closes. The answers tell you more than a brochure.

## How state law changes this

The surrogate list differs sharply. Some states allow any close friend to act. Others stop at blood relatives and then direct the hospital to a court or an ethics process. Guardianship terms differ too, including who can petition, whether the person gets a court-appointed lawyer, how often reports are filed, and whether a public guardian office exists. Look at the [state guides](/estate-planning/california) for local detail, and see [the difference between power of attorney and guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## How to prevent it

For most people with no close family, two documents cover the problem. A durable power of attorney for finances and a healthcare power of attorney, each naming a person and a backup. If you have more than a modest estate, add a funded trust and a will that names a trustee and executor who are not relatives.

If your documents exist but name someone who has died or moved away, update them. Our scenario on [what happens if your agent cannot act](/learn/what-if/power-of-attorney-agent-unavailable) explains how successors work, and [estate planning for single people](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people) covers the larger plan.
