Carlos, 61, opened an IRA twenty years ago and rolled his old 401(k) into it. He remembers signing a pile of papers, but he cannot say whether he ever filled in the beneficiary box. His wife Elena assumes it goes to her. His son Mateo assumes it goes to him. Nobody has checked.

When no beneficiary is named, the account does not sit in limbo. A default rule applies, and that rule may not match what anyone expects. This article explains the defaults, what they cost, and how to fix the form while you still can.

## What happens by default

The first thing to know is that the document that governs the account sets the default. For a 401(k), that is the plan document. For an IRA, it is the custodian's account agreement. For life insurance, it is the policy contract. Each one lists who is paid when no one is named, and the order differs from company to company.

Here is how that usually looks.

| Account | Typical default order | What to confirm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 401(k) and other employer plans | Surviving spouse, then the estate or other named order in the plan | Whether the plan has its own list |
| IRA | Varies by custodian, often spouse, then estate | The default clause in the agreement |
| Individual life insurance | Policy default, often spouse, then children, then estate | The policy's "if no beneficiary" language |
| Annuity | Contract default, often the estate | The contract and any death benefit rules |

Employer plans have an extra rule. Under federal law, most 401(k) plans require the spouse to be the beneficiary unless the spouse signed a written waiver, witnessed by a notary or plan representative. So if Carlos were married and the account were a 401(k), Elena would generally be the beneficiary even if Carlos wanted someone else, unless she had signed away her right.

An IRA does not follow that rule. IRA owners can name anyone, though some states with community property laws give a spouse a claim.

### When the money goes to the estate

If the default order ends at your estate, the money is paid to the executor. That has four effects.

- **Probate.** The account goes through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate), which adds time and court costs. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for how that differs from a named beneficiary.
- **Creditors.** Money in an estate can be reached by the estate's creditors. Many accounts with a named beneficiary have more protection, depending on state law.
- **Who inherits.** Your will decides. With no will, state law picks the heirs.
- **Tax treatment.** Retirement accounts paid to an estate lose the most flexible payout options. Heirs generally have to empty the account faster than a named individual could, and the exact rule depends on the owner's age at death. For comparison, most named non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years. See [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

## A realistic example

Carlos dies at 66. His IRA agreement has no named beneficiary, and it says the default is the estate. Elena is the executor under his will, which leaves everything equally to her and to Mateo.

First, Elena opens the probate case so she has the authority to claim the account. That takes weeks to start and months to finish in many courts. Then the custodian pays the IRA to the estate. The estate does not have the same payout choices as Elena would have had as a named spouse, so the account must be emptied sooner, and income tax lands sooner too. A creditor of Carlos with a valid claim can also ask the estate for payment.

Finally Elena divides what is left with Mateo under the will. Everyone ends up in the right place on paper, but it took longer, cost more and left less tax flexibility than a one-page form would have.

### Other ways it goes wrong

A missing form is not the only problem. A form can also fail in these ways.

- **The beneficiary died first.** If you named only one person and that person died before you, the account usually falls back to the default order. The result can be the estate even though you once filled out a form.
- **The form is old.** A form that names a former spouse or a child who is now an adult may pay someone you no longer intend.
- **The name is unclear.** Entries such as "my children" or a nickname can slow a claim while the company asks for proof of who you meant.
- **The policy is forgotten.** A life insurance policy that nobody knows about may never be claimed. Families sometimes find these years later through the NAIC Life Insurance Policy Locator or a state unclaimed property search.

Each of these is easy to fix with a short call to the company, so it is worth checking even if you believe a form is on file.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are alive

1. **Find every account.** Use our [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) to list each IRA, 401(k), annuity and policy.
2. **Ask what is on file.** Call or log in and request the current beneficiary and the default order in writing.
3. **Name a primary beneficiary.** For a married person with a 401(k), talk about the spouse's rights first.
4. **Name a contingent beneficiary.** If your first choice dies before you, the second name keeps the money out of probate. See [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).
5. **Keep the confirmation.** Save it with your will and tell your executor where it is.

### If the person has already died

1. **Get the plan or policy documents.** Ask for the default order in writing.
2. **Look for lost policies.** Search the NAIC Life Insurance Policy Locator, old mail and unclaimed property records.
3. **Open the estate if needed.** If the estate is the beneficiary, the executor needs authority from the court to collect.
4. **Ask about disclaimers.** If an heir does not want an inherited asset, a qualified disclaimer may redirect it. Tax rules apply, so ask before acting.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how creditors can reach estate assets, on whether a small-estate procedure can skip full probate, and on spousal rights in community property states. Many states also protect life insurance and retirement accounts from some creditors, but only when a named person receives the money. Our [small estate affidavit guide](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains one way smaller amounts may avoid a full court case.

## How to prevent it

A beneficiary form takes a few minutes, but it controls more money than almost any other paper you sign. Many people have a few of these accounts, scattered across old jobs. Gather them in one list and fix them in a single sitting.

Prevention takes three steps and a few minutes per account.

- Name a primary and a contingent beneficiary on every retirement account and policy.
- Use full legal names and keep addresses current.
- Review the forms after a marriage, divorce, birth, death or move, and at least every few years. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) can help you find gaps.
