Nadia's father died in March. His life insurance policy, worth $250,000, was supposed to help pay for her brother's care. When the insurer sent the claim forms, it turned out the beneficiary was listed as "Rosalind Ames," his former wife, whom he had not spoken to in ten years. He had meant to name his daughter and never got around to filling in the replacement form.

Nadia is asking what almost everyone asks in this situation: can it be fixed now that he is gone? Sometimes. This page lays out which fixes exist, what each one needs and which problems cannot be repaired.

## Why doesn't the family just change it?

Because a beneficiary designation is a contract between the owner and the company. When the owner dies, the company owes the money to the person named on the form it holds. It does not have to look at a will, a text message, or a family promise, and it is protected from liability if it pays the named person in good faith. A power of attorney also ends at death, so nobody has authority to sign a new form. See [when a beneficiary designation can be challenged](/learn/beneficiary-designations/when-a-beneficiary-designation-can-be-challenged).

What remains are four routes, from easiest to hardest.

| Route | Who acts | What it needs | Works best when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Disclaimer | The named beneficiary | Written refusal before accepting any benefit, within the legal window | The named person agrees the money should go elsewhere |
| Family agreement | Everyone affected | Signed settlement; possible gift tax | All parties are adults and cooperative |
| Divorce or revocation statute | The family, through the administrator or court | Proof of the divorce and the right kind of account | The named person is an ex-spouse |
| Court correction | The person who was meant to be named | Strong proof of the owner's intent and of a mistake | There was an error, such as a typo, wrong name or missed filing |

## What mistakes are fixable?

**A name was misspelled or the wrong relative was named.** A court can sometimes reform the designation if there is clear and convincing evidence of what the owner intended. Useful proof includes an unsigned form found in the owner's files, an email to an agent, a will that uses the same words, and statements to friends. Courts are cautious here, because the other side may be a real person who relied on the form.

**The owner meant to name someone and never did.** This is usually not fixable. A wish to change a beneficiary that was never carried out is not a change. Courts rarely rewrite an incomplete form, though a few have done so where the owner did everything possible and only a clerk's step was missing.

**The form named an ex-spouse.** Many states have a law that cancels an ex-spouse's designation on a life insurance policy or retirement account after a divorce. Federal rules govern employer plans, and they can override the state law if the ex is still listed. So the result depends on whether the account is a workplace plan, an IRA or a policy. Our article on [what happens if your ex is still the beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) walks through it.

**The form was left blank or the beneficiary died first.** Money then falls to the plan's default order, often a spouse, children or the estate. See [no beneficiary named](/learn/what-if/no-beneficiary-named). The default may be fine, and may not match what the owner wanted.

**The estate was named by mistake.** The money lands in probate and becomes available to creditors. The family may be able to show that the owner meant a person, but the evidence has to be strong.

## What does a disclaimer do here?

A disclaimer lets a named beneficiary say "I refuse this." The money is then treated as if that person had died first, and it goes to the next beneficiary on the form, or to the estate. In Nadia's case, if Rosalind refuses, the insurer pays the contingent beneficiary, or the estate if there is none.

Three rules matter:

1. **Timing.** Federal tax law gives a qualified disclaimer a window of nine months from death. Many states have their own rules, and the beneficiary generally cannot have accepted the money or any benefit from it first.
2. **No direction.** The person disclaiming cannot choose who gets it. The form and the law decide.
3. **Form.** It must be written, signed and delivered correctly.

If Rosalind wants Nadia to have the money but is not the next in line, a disclaimer may not do it. A voluntary payment after she receives it can be treated as a gift. Read our guide to [disclaiming an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) before anyone signs.

## What should you do in the first month?

1. **Get a copy of the form.** Ask the insurer, bank or plan for the beneficiary designation on file and the date. Do not rely on memory.
2. **Notify the company in writing** that you dispute the designation, if you have a basis. A claim in dispute may be held while the company decides, and sometimes the insurer deposits the money with a court.
3. **Gather proof of intent.** Collect drafts, emails, letters to the agent, the will, and the names of people who heard the owner say it.
4. **Do not assume the named person will say no.** Write to them calmly, with no accusation, and ask whether they would consider a disclaimer.
5. **Ask an attorney about deadlines.** Court claims, insurer disputes and disclaimers each have their own clock.
6. **Do not spend money you might have to return.** If you are a beneficiary and a dispute is possible, keep funds where they can be traced.

## How did Nadia's case turn out?

Her lawyer first asked the insurer for the form and found it dated 2011, the year of the marriage. The divorce decree was from 2014. The policy was an individual one, not a workplace plan, so the state's divorce statute treated Rosalind as if she had died before him. The insurer paid the contingent beneficiary, which was Nadia, after reviewing the decree. Had the policy been through his employer, the plan documents and federal rules would have governed, and the answer could have gone the other way.

That is typical. The facts that decide these cases are small and dull: a date on a form, the kind of account, whether a contingent name was listed.

## What if the money has already been paid?

Then the claim is against the person who received it, not the insurer. A court can order repayment where it finds a mistake or that the recipient was not entitled to it, but it is slow, costly and uncertain, particularly if the funds were spent. This is why acting before payment matters.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether divorce cancels an ex-spouse's designation, on how strong the evidence must be to correct a form, and on the time for disclaimers and claims. Illinois and New Jersey both have rules on divorce and beneficiary forms, but federal law controls many workplace plans, so the plan type matters. See the [Illinois guide](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey guide](/estate-planning/new-jersey), or read about [whether a will can be changed after death](/learn/wills/can-a-will-be-changed-after-death), which raises related questions.

## How to avoid this in your own plan

Review every beneficiary form after a marriage, divorce, birth or death, and every few years. Name contingent beneficiaries. Keep copies.
