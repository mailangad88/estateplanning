Dale and Carol had been married for thirty-four years and had three grown children. They did what many couples do. Everything was in joint names, each was the other's beneficiary, and their wills left it all to each other. Dale died at 69. Carol, then 66, kept the house and the savings. Four years later she met someone, married him, and put his name on the house deed.

When Carol died, her new husband owned the house by survivorship. Her will, which she had never updated, left the rest to him. Dale's children heard about their father's estate from a letter written by the new spouse's lawyer.

Nobody did anything wrong. The plan simply worked as written. This article explains how that happens, what a remarriage changes, and how to build a plan that leaves your wishes in place.

## What happens by default

When you leave property outright to your spouse, you make a complete gift. After you die, it belongs to them. That is true whether it passes by will, by joint ownership with right of survivorship, or by beneficiary form. It is true of the house, the bank accounts, the brokerage account and the retirement accounts.

Once the property is theirs, your spouse can:

- spend it, give it away or sell it.
- retitle it into joint names with a new partner.
- leave it by their own will, trust or beneficiary form to anyone they choose.
- fail to update their documents, in which case state law decides.

Remarriage adds a new layer. A new spouse usually has rights that were not there before. In most states, a surviving spouse can claim an elective share of the other spouse's estate, even over a will that says otherwise. If the survivor dies without a will, the new spouse often shares at or near the top of the inheritance list. Our article on [the spouse's elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) explains how that right works.

### Which assets pass this way

| Asset | How it reaches your spouse | Can you control what happens next? |
|---|---|---|
| House held as joint tenants or tenants by the entirety | By survivorship, outside the will | No |
| Joint bank and brokerage accounts | By survivorship | No |
| Retirement accounts and life insurance naming spouse | By beneficiary form | No |
| Assets in your name only, left by will | By probate | No, unless left in trust |
| Assets left in a trust with terms | By the trustee | Yes, to the extent the trust says so |

The difference between the first four rows and the last is the whole point. Anything left outright is out of your hands. The article on [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) covers the house in more detail.

## A realistic example

Back to Dale and Carol. The children assumed what Dale said at the kitchen table: "Your mother will make sure you're taken care of." That was true when he said it.

After Dale's death, Carol did the sensible things. She paid off the mortgage with insurance money and retitled the accounts in her name. She saw no reason to see a lawyer. After her second marriage, her husband, Neil, asked to be added to the deed so that the house would be "ours." She agreed.

At Carol's death, Neil took the house by survivorship. He also had an elective share claim against the estate, and he was the beneficiary on her new retirement account paperwork. Dale's children received a few personal items. The money that had been Dale's for three decades went to a man he never met.

The costs here are not court fees. They are the loss of an intended inheritance and the strained relationships that follow, which are hard to repair. A related story appears in [what happens when a parent remarries](/learn/what-if/parent-remarries-and-new-spouse-inherits-everything).

## What can still be fixed

### If you are alive and married

1. **Decide what you want for your spouse and for your children.** For example: your spouse keeps the house for life and has income, and your children receive what is left.
2. **Use a trust for the survivor.** A [QTIP or marital trust](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) gives your spouse income for life, while you decide who gets the principal. A [life estate](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse) in the house does something similar for real estate.
3. **Retitle joint property.** A house held as joint tenants passes outright. If you want a trust to control it, the deed must change.
4. **Update beneficiary forms.** Naming a spouse directly sends the money outright. Naming a trust, or splitting among spouse and children, changes the outcome. See [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).
5. **Think about remarriage terms.** A trust may end the spouse's right to live in the house or reduce distributions if they remarry or move in with a partner. These terms should be written with care, because they can feel punishing, and a court looks at them closely.
6. **Name a trustee your spouse can work with.** A child can be a trustee, but that sets up a relationship you should think about in advance. An independent trustee is often better.

### If you are the surviving spouse

1. **Update your own plan now.** Your documents name your late spouse as agent, trustee and executor. See [estate planning after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies).
2. **Decide what you want for the children before any new relationship.** Dealing with it afterward is harder.
3. **Consider a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement** if you remarry, so that assets inherited from your first spouse stay with your family.
4. **Check Social Security.** Remarrying before age 60 generally ends survivor benefits on your late spouse's record, while remarrying at 60 or later does not. See [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) and the rules at ssa.gov.

## How state law changes this

The size of the elective share, how it is measured, and whether it reaches non-probate assets all vary. Community property states treat the property of the first marriage differently. Some states automatically revoke a will on marriage, and others do not. Some honor remarriage clauses and some limit them. The [state guides](/estate-planning/california) cover local rules for a surviving spouse.

## How to prevent it

For couples with children who need protection, the answer is usually a joint or separate trust that splits into a survivor's trust and a family trust at the first death. The survivor gets what they need. The children get what is left. Our article on [estate planning for blended families](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) walks through the options, even if your family is not blended in the usual sense.
