Walter, 84, told his daughter Beth years ago that "everything is taken care of." When he died, Beth found no will in his house. She called his bank, which said any box would require a court order. She called two lawyers whose names were on old business cards. One had retired and the other had no record. After three months she told her brother that they would have to go through the state's default rules.

Eight months later, while cleaning out a closet, she found a key taped inside a shoebox. It was for a safe deposit box at a small bank in another town. The will was in it.

This is more common than it should be. A will does its job only if the right person can find it at the right time. Below is what happens when they cannot, and how to fix it.

## What happens by default

For the first weeks after a death, the family looks for the will, and nothing legal happens until someone files something. Here is how the search usually plays out.

1. **The family searches the home.** Desks, closets, fireproof boxes, filing cabinets and the car glove box are common hiding places.
2. **They contact the likely lawyer.** Many attorneys keep originals in a vault. A lawyer can confirm whether he drafted a will, and may release the original to the named executor.
3. **They check the bank.** Statements may show an annual box rental fee. Banks follow strict rules on who may enter a box.
4. **They ask the court.** In some states, a person can lodge a will with the probate court during life. Clerks can check whether one is on file.
5. **If nothing turns up, the estate is opened as intestate.** A relative asks the court to name an administrator, and state law divides the property among heirs. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains the default order.

That last step is not final. If a will appears later, the executor named in it can usually ask the court to admit it. Courts take the dead person's wishes seriously. But the longer the delay, the more complicated the cleanup becomes, especially if the administrator has already sold property or paid out shares.

### How does a safe deposit box get opened after a death?

The bank, not the family, controls it. Most banks seal a box once they learn the renter has died, and open it only in one of three ways.

| Route | Who it fits | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| Co-renter | A spouse or child already listed on the box | Usually the key and ID, though some states or banks restrict access after a death |
| Executor or administrator | A person appointed by the court | Court letters, a death certificate and ID |
| Supervised search for a will | A relative with no court appointment | A bank employee or court-approved process, state-specific, often limited to taking out a will and a burial document |

Several states have statutes for that third route, allowing a limited opening to look for a will or burial instructions, with a bank officer present and a receipt for anything removed. Others require a petition. Rules vary enough that a call to the bank's legal department, and to an attorney, will save time. Our article on [what your will covers, including safe deposit boxes](/learn/property-and-assets/what-your-will-covers-timeshares-storage-units-safe-deposit-boxes) discusses the contents.

## A realistic example

Beth's brother, Marcus, wants to file for administration and be done. Beth finds the key and learns the bank's address from the tag. She takes the key and Walter's death certificate to the bank, where the manager explains that only a court-appointed representative can open the box, apart from a will search under state law.

Beth asks the probate court for a supervised search. A deputy clerk and a bank officer meet her in the vault. Inside the box is a signed will, a deed for a lot Beth had not known about and three savings bonds. The bank copies the will, hands the original to the clerk, and gives Beth a receipt. The will names Marcus as executor, and the estate opens under it.

Marcus had not yet been appointed administrator, so nothing had been sold or paid out. Had he been, he would have owed notice to creditors and heirs under the intestacy rules, and the cleanup would have been harder. The time cost here was the months of searching and a hearing. The money cost was filing fees and attorney time, which varied with the court.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are making your plan now

1. **Decide where the original will lives.** Options include your attorney's vault, a home fireproof safe, or the court's will repository, where your state has one. Whatever you pick, write it down.
2. **Tell your executor** where it is, who has the key or combination, and the name of your attorney.
3. **Keep a one-page document list.** Include the will, trust, power of attorney, beneficiary forms, insurance policies, account list and passwords hint. Our [letter of instruction guide](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) shows a layout.
4. **Add your executor to the safe deposit box** if you use one, or choose a home safe instead. Get a written receipt from any lawyer holding an original.
5. **Store copies separately** and mark them as copies. A signed duplicate original can create confusion, so ask your attorney first.
6. **Organize everything in one place.** See [how to organize your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents).

### If your loved one has already died

1. **Search methodically.** Home, desk, mail, digital accounts, and checkbook entries for box fees.
2. **Call the likely attorney and the court.** Ask the probate clerk whether a will has been deposited.
3. **Do not break open a box or destroy papers.** Both can create legal problems.
4. **Deliver any will you find** to the court or the named executor promptly. Most states require it.
5. **If nothing turns up**, talk to an attorney about opening an intestate estate, and ask what happens if a will appears later. Our guide on [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) explains the steps.
6. **If only a copy exists**, see [what happens if the original will is lost](/learn/wills/lost-original-will).

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether a person holding a will must deliver it and how soon, whether the court keeps wills in safekeeping, how a safe deposit box may be opened, how long a later-found will can be admitted, and whether a copy suffices. Some states set a deadline after which a will can no longer be admitted. Our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides are a good place to start, and a local attorney can say how the clerk's office works in practice.

## How to prevent it

The goal is to make your will easy to find without making it easy to tamper with. For most people that means one original in a safe place, one trusted person who knows where, and a list. Read [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) for the trade-offs between home, lawyer, bank and court.

Then keep the list current. Moves, new accounts and a change of lawyers all make last year's list wrong. A yearly check with the [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) is enough for most households. If you want to see how organized your plan is, the [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) asks the right questions.
