Marcus, 47, gets a call at 11 p.m. from a number in another state. His daughter Imani turned 18 in August and started college in September. She was hit by a car while crossing the street, and she is at a trauma center. Marcus drives through the night. At the desk, a clerk tells him she cannot confirm Imani is a patient there.

This happens to good, attentive parents every year. A birthday changes the law without changing anything else about the family. This article explains what the hospital can and cannot do, and which two documents prevent the problem.

## What happens by default

At 18, a person is a legal adult for medical purposes in nearly every state. Parents no longer sign consent forms. Doctors owe confidentiality to the patient, and the federal HIPAA privacy rule limits what they may share with anyone else. Parental access that felt automatic through the pediatrician's years simply ends.

In practice, the answer depends on the moment:

| Situation | What usually happens |
| --- | --- |
| Patient is awake and competent | Staff ask the patient who may be told. A patient who says no is respected. |
| Patient is unconscious in an emergency | Staff treat first. They may use judgment to tell family what is needed for care. |
| Patient is incapacitated for days | Staff look for a signed healthcare agent, then a state default list, then a guardian. |
| Parent asks for records later | The hospital needs a signed authorization from the patient or a legal representative. |

HIPAA allows providers to share limited information with family in some circumstances, but it does not require them to. Hospitals vary, and a cautious compliance office will often say no. A parent with no document in hand has no right to argue.

The harder problem is consent. If your child needs a surgery or a decision about a ventilator, someone must speak for them. Without a named agent, the hospital turns to your state's default rules. Some states give parents of an unmarried adult a clear place on that list. Others do not, and hospital counsel may ask for a court order. See [what happens if there is no power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney) for that road in the general case.

## A realistic example

Marcus shows the clerk his driver's license and a photo of Imani. She refers him to the social worker, who is kind but cautious. Imani is in surgery. Marcus cannot get a status update beyond "she is stable." The next morning the trauma team wants consent for a second operation. Imani is sedated.

The hospital checks for a healthcare directive and finds none. State law lets them accept consent from a parent in this case, and Marcus signs. But the records, the insurance calls and the university's questions are another matter. He cannot speak to Imani's roommate's family, her professor or her bank without a form. Her tuition account and the lease on her apartment are hers alone. Marcus spends three weeks doing paperwork he cannot finish.

Compare this with a family who signed two forms in the summer. Imani names her father as her healthcare agent and her mother as the backup. She signs a HIPAA release naming both parents. The hospital scans it at admission, and the calls start in minutes. The time and cost drivers here were not legal fees. They were hours at a desk, delayed records, and the stress of having to prove who you are.

## What can still be fixed

### If your child is healthy and can sign

1. **Sit down together.** Explain the purpose: this covers the day they cannot speak for themselves.
2. **Sign a HIPAA authorization.** It lets named people receive medical information. Review the [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) article to see what it covers and what it does not.
3. **Sign a healthcare power of attorney.** It names an agent to decide. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
4. **Consider a financial power of attorney.** Medical bills, tuition and a lease are money matters, and a medical form does not touch them.
5. **Share copies.** Give the agent a copy, upload one to a phone, and tell the campus health center and the primary care provider.
6. **Revisit at major moves.** A new school, a new state, a marriage or a first job are all good times to look again.

### If the emergency is happening now

1. **Call the hospital's patient advocate or social worker.** Ask what they need to see.
2. **Bring identification and proof of relationship.** A birth certificate can help.
3. **Ask whether your child can sign anything.** Even a brief, awake moment may allow a release.
4. **If your child will be incapacitated for a long time,** speak with an attorney about an emergency or temporary guardianship. A court process is possible, but it is slower and costs more than two forms would have. See [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## How state law changes this

States set their own rules on who can consent for an incapacitated adult who has no agent. Some have a statutory surrogate list, some leave it to hospital policy and some require court action if the family disagrees. States also differ on witness or notary requirements for a healthcare power of attorney, and on how a minor's rights shift at 18 versus 19 or 21 for certain decisions. The state guide for [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) gives one example of how the forms are structured. Ask us about your own state.

## How to prevent it

Neither form needs to be long, and neither requires your child to disclose anything today. They sit in a drawer until the day they are needed.

Two forms, signed before anything goes wrong. A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) names who decides. A [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) lets that person and others see records. Many families add a living will once the young adult is comfortable. Our article on [estate planning for young adults](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults) lists what else is worth doing at this age, and the [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) shows what is missing in your own plan.

## What do parents get wrong?

The first mistake is leaning on insurance. If your child is still on your plan, you may receive notices, but they tell you that a bill exists, not what happened or why. The second is believing that college health centers will call you. Most treat students as adults and will not, unless the student has signed a release.


A third is assuming the pediatrician's forms still apply. They do not. A school health form or a consent signed years ago has no force after 18.

A fourth is treating the paperwork as something for later. The week before a child leaves for college is the easiest time, because the family is already organizing documents, and the child is usually in the room. Waiting until a crisis means every hour counts.

The last is naming only one parent as the agent. If your family is divorced, or the parents disagree, a young adult may prefer a single person, and that choice is theirs. Naming a backup protects against the first choice being the one stuck in an airport.
