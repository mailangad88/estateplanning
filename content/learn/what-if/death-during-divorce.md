Gina and Mark had been separated for ten months. The petition was on file, the lawyers had traded drafts of a settlement, and a hearing was set for November. In October, Mark was killed in a car accident.

Gina had assumed that once a divorce was underway, she was already out of his life on paper. Mark's mother assumed the same, and expected the house and the life insurance to go to her and her grandchildren. Both were wrong in a way that surprises most families. This article explains what the law does when a divorce is cut short by a death, and what you can safely do while a case is open.

## What happens by default

The basic rule is simple. Divorce ends a marriage only when the judge signs the final decree. Until that moment, the two people are still spouses. If one of them dies first, the death generally ends the divorce case, because a court cannot dissolve a marriage that death has already ended. The judge dismisses the case as moot, and the surviving spouse is treated as a spouse.

That has four effects, and each of them catches families off guard.

1. **The will still names the spouse.** The laws that remove an ex-spouse from a will or trust normally apply only after a divorce is final. A will signed during the marriage stays in force with the spouse in it.
2. **A spouse's statutory share survives.** In most states a surviving spouse can claim a minimum share of the estate no matter what the will says. See our glossary entry on the [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) and the guide to [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).
3. **Without a will, the spouse inherits under state law.** The intestacy statute treats the survivor as a spouse, often with a large part of the estate and sometimes all of it. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) shows the common patterns.
4. **Beneficiary forms still name the spouse.** Retirement plans, life insurance and payable-on-death accounts pay whoever is on file. The "ex-spouse is treated as dead" statutes do not apply, because there is no ex. See the companion piece on an [ex-spouse who is still a beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).

### Does a court order change any of this?

Sometimes. Courts in many states issue automatic orders when a divorce is filed, and they can restrain both spouses from changing insurance beneficiaries, moving assets or borrowing against property. A judge can also enter a temporary support order. If a spouse dies, those orders usually end with the case, but a court that has already ruled on a specific point may leave a claim alive. In some places, a signed marital settlement agreement is enforceable as a contract even if the decree was never entered. This is the corner where families need a lawyer to read the case file.

## A realistic example

Mark's will, signed six years earlier, leaves everything to Gina, with his mother Joan named as backup. His life insurance through work names Gina. His house is in both of their names as joint tenants with survivorship.

After the accident, the divorce case is dismissed. Gina is the surviving spouse. The house passes to her automatically because of the survivorship clause, outside probate. The insurer pays her under the form. The will gives her the rest. Joan, who thought her son's children would be taken care of through her, has no claim unless the will says so.

The family then discovers that the children, who are minors, get little directly, since the assets went to Gina. Gina may well provide for them, but nothing in the law requires it. If Mark had signed a new will naming a trust for the kids, or a settlement agreement had been entered, the picture could have been different. Instead, the family spends months arguing, and some of them consider a court challenge that may or may not succeed.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are in a divorce and still alive

You cannot fix everything mid-case, but you can fix more than most people think.

1. **Ask your divorce attorney what the standing orders say.** Many courts bar changing beneficiaries or transferring property. If a restriction applies, do not change those forms without written permission or a court order.
2. **Sign a new healthcare directive and power of attorney.** These are not property transfers, so they are usually allowed. Name someone other than your spouse if that is what you want. Also sign a HIPAA release.
3. **Write a new will, even if you hold it until the decree.** A new will can name a guardian for minor children, a new executor and a trust for kids. It will not remove a spouse's statutory share, but it will cover the rest. Ask whether it is safe to sign now.
4. **Push for a prompt interim agreement.** If the settlement terms are close, ask your lawyer whether the parties can sign an agreement that addresses survivor rights and insurance now.
5. **Prepare the post-decree changes in advance.** Have beneficiary forms filled out and ready, so they are filed the day the decree is entered. See our guide to [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce).

### If a spouse has already died mid-case

The family should get the case file and every signed agreement, then talk to an estate attorney in the state where the divorce was filed. Do not assume the surviving spouse has no rights. Heirs who think a spouse should not inherit can ask a court whether the spouse's conduct, a separation agreement or a state statute limits the claim. Some states bar a spouse who abandoned the marriage from taking a share. Those cases turn on facts and take time.

## How state law changes this

Three things vary the most. First, whether a pending divorce changes a spouse's elective share or intestate share, since a few states have rules about long separations or abandonment. Second, which orders survive a death and which end with the case. Third, how soon a will or form is revoked, because some statutes trigger on the decree and others on a particular filing date. If your state has a long-separation rule or a rule for completed trials with decree pending, it can change the result completely. Check your [state guide](/estate-planning/new-york) or ask an attorney, and note that community property states handle the marital estate differently from common-law states.

## What about the children and the house?

Two practical questions come up in nearly every case. The first is the children. Minor children do not inherit control of anything. If a parent dies mid-divorce, the other parent usually keeps or takes over custody, and any money the children inherit is held by an adult or a court-supervised arrangement. If you do not want the surviving parent managing that money, a trust in your will can name a different trustee. The second is the house. Property held as joint tenants with survivorship, or as tenants by the entirety, goes to the surviving spouse by operation of law. If it is titled in one name only, it follows the will or intestacy law. Neither rule cares that a divorce was pending.

## How to prevent it

The most useful habit is to treat the gap between filing and decree as a risky period. Do not wait for the decree to deal with healthcare, guardianship and the will. Do ask your divorce lawyer about each change before making it. Then, as soon as the judge signs, replace the beneficiary forms and update the will and trust.
