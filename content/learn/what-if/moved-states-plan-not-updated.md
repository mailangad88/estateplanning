Priya and Dev lived in the same Ohio suburb for twenty-two years, and their estate plan was signed there. When Dev retired they moved to Arizona to be near their daughter. The movers packed the files with the kitchen things, and nobody opened them again. Six years later Dev has a stroke, and the first person to ask for his power of attorney is a bank teller who has never seen an Ohio form.

Moving does not blow up a plan. It leaves the plan in place and quietly changes everything around it. This article walks through what happens by default, which documents are most likely to cause trouble, and what a sensible after-the-move review covers.

## What happens by default

Nothing dramatic happens on the day you cross the state line. A will signed correctly in one state is generally honored in another, and a trust is a private contract that does not expire when you move. If you want the validity rules in detail, our guide on [whether a will survives a move](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) covers them.

What changes is who applies the rules. Once you become a resident of the new state, that state is where you are domiciled, and domicile is the main thing that decides which state's courts settle your estate. So when you die, the probate court in your new county opens the case, and the new state's law fills any gap your documents leave. Your old state's rules stop being the default.

Three kinds of problems follow from that.

1. **Acceptance problems.** A hospital, a bank or a title company has to be willing to rely on your document. Familiar forms get accepted. Unfamiliar ones get sent to the legal department.
2. **Fit problems.** The new state may treat a spouse, a house or an executor differently than the old one did, and your document was written around the old rules.
3. **Gap problems.** Some things the old plan never needed to say, because state law supplied the answer. Now the answer is different.

## Which documents are most at risk?

Not all of your documents are equally exposed. Roughly, the ones that someone must show to a stranger are the most fragile.

| Document | What can go wrong after a move | How urgent |
|---|---|---|
| Durable power of attorney | Bank refuses an out-of-state form, or the form lacks powers the new state's institutions expect | High |
| Healthcare directive and HIPAA release | Hospital questions witness or notary formalities | High |
| Will | Executor or witness rules differ; a spouse's share may work differently | Medium |
| Revocable trust | Usually fine, but the trustee, real estate and state tax picture may need a look | Medium |
| Beneficiary forms | Usually unaffected, though a spouse's consent rights can differ | Low to medium |

The powers of attorney lead the list because they are used while you are alive, often during a crisis, by a person with no time to argue. Many states have a statute telling institutions to honor a power of attorney validly signed elsewhere, and some of those statutes protect a bank that accepts one. But a statute does not stop a nervous employee from saying no. If you want to see how that plays out, read about what happens when a [bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).

## A realistic example

Back to Priya and Dev. After the stroke, their daughter Anika presents the Ohio power of attorney at Dev's Arizona bank so she can pay the rehab facility. The bank wants its own legal team to review it, and that takes nine days. The facility wants a deposit sooner. Anika ends up paying from her own credit card and waiting to be reimbursed.

Two years later Priya dies too, and the will names a friend in Ohio as executor. The friend is willing, but the Arizona court wants to know whether she must post a bond, and whether she has an in-state agent to accept legal papers. Their house in Arizona, which Dev and Priya own jointly, passes without trouble. Their Ohio rental condo does not. It needs a second court proceeding in Ohio, called [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate), because real estate is controlled by the state where it sits.

None of that was a disaster. Every piece was slower and more expensive than it had to be, and all of it came from a plan that was sound on the day it was signed and never looked at again.

## How state law changes this

This is the part where it matters most which two states are involved. A few rules that often shift on a move:

- **Marital property.** A handful of states, including Texas and California, use community property. Most use a common-law system with a spouse's elective share at death. Moving between the two kinds can change a surviving spouse's rights, and it can change how property bought earlier is classified. A couple should confirm how their savings and home are treated before assuming the old answer holds. Our glossary entry on [community property](/glossary/community-property) explains the basic split.
- **Executor and trustee rules.** Some states require an out-of-state executor to post a bond or name a local agent. Others do not.
- **Homestead and creditor protections.** The amount of a home that is shielded from creditors, and who may stay in it after a death, vary widely.
- **Estate and inheritance taxes.** The federal exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families owe no federal estate tax. A few states have their own estate or inheritance tax with a much lower threshold. Moving from a state with no such tax to one with it can matter. Try the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) to see where your new state stands.
- **Signing formalities.** Witness counts, notary requirements and the rules for a self-proving will differ, which is one reason an old form may get a second look.

For state-specific starting points, see our guides for [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) and [New York](/estate-planning/new-york), and the overview on [how estate planning differs by state](/learn/basics/how-estate-planning-differs-by-state).

## What can still be fixed

Almost all of it. The fixes are the same whether you moved last month or ten years ago, though sooner is cheaper.

1. **Locate every current document.** Gather the will, any trust, the powers of attorney, the healthcare directive and a list of beneficiary forms. Note the date and state of each.
2. **Redo the two lifetime documents first.** Sign a new durable power of attorney and healthcare directive under your new state's law, with the witnesses and notary that state expects. Add a HIPAA release.
3. **Have the will or trust reviewed by an attorney licensed where you now live.** Ask whether it needs a restatement, an amendment or nothing at all. A trust often needs only a short amendment, and the [trust-moving guide](/learn/trusts/living-trusts-and-moving-or-owning-property-in-another-state) explains why.
4. **Check how each asset is titled.** If you bought a home in the new state, confirm the deed matches your plan, and if you have a trust, confirm the house is in it.
5. **Deal with property left behind.** An out-of-state rental or vacation home can be placed in a trust or given a transfer-on-death deed where the state allows it, which avoids a second probate. See our overview of [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).
6. **Set a reminder.** Put the next review on the calendar with the [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder).

## How to prevent it

The prevention is a habit rather than a document. When you book the movers, add one more line to the list: estate plan review within twelve months. Update your driver's license, voter registration and tax residency at the same time, because those help establish domicile and make the paperwork consistent. If you are weighing whether you need a will or a trust in the new state, the [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) is a decent first pass.

Changes of address are also a good moment to swap in people who live closer. An agent a few miles away is easier to use than one in your old state, and the same goes for executors and trustees.
