Marcus is 41. Six years ago, during a severe manic episode, a court appointed his mother as his guardian over his finances. He is stable now, works part time and takes his medication, and he wants to leave his savings to his sister's two kids. When he called a lawyer's office, the receptionist told him that people under guardianship "can't make wills." He hung up.

That statement is wrong in many cases, and families get it wrong in both directions. Some people lose a right they still have. Others sign a will that falls apart later. This page explains how the law separates different kinds of capacity and what to do when a guardianship, a diagnosis or a medication is part of the picture.

## What is the test for making a will?

In most states you need to understand that you are making a will, roughly what you own, who your close family members are, and how the will divides your property. Lawyers call this testamentary capacity. It is one of the lowest legal thresholds. You do not need to read a balance sheet or manage a checkbook. For the full test, see our article on [signing a will during illness or under pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).

The reason a guardianship does not decide the question is that the law treats capacity as task-specific. Someone may be unable to manage a rental property and still know perfectly well who their children are and what they want them to have. A court that appointed a guardian looked at one set of abilities. A probate judge reading the will looks at a different set, on a different day.

## How does a guardianship or conservatorship affect a will?

It depends on the state and on what the order says. Three patterns come up.

- **The order is silent about wills.** The person may be able to make one, but the guardianship will be used as evidence if the will is challenged.
- **The order or state law limits it.** In some states a person under guardianship must get court permission, or the will carries a presumption that must be overcome.
- **The order has ended or been narrowed.** A restoration of rights, or a limited guardianship covering only money, leaves will-making alone.

Two points are consistent almost everywhere. A guardian or conservator cannot generally sign a will for the ward, and cannot change the ward's beneficiary forms. We cover the second point in [whether a power of attorney or guardian can change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary). A few states have a court-approved "statutory will" for people who never had capacity, but that is a narrow tool and the judge picks the terms.

If you are under a guardianship in Illinois or New Jersey, the first thing to read is the order itself. Our [Illinois guardianship article](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) and [New Jersey guardianship article](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) describe how those courts handle an adult's rights.

## Does a mental illness make a will invalid?

No diagnosis does. What can sink a will is one of two things:

1. **Lack of understanding at the moment of signing.** The person did not know what a will was, who their family was, or what they owned.
2. **An insane delusion.** This is a fixed, false belief with no basis in reality that drove a gift. The classic example is a father who believes, with no evidence, that his daughter poisoned him and leaves her out. A belief that is merely unfair or mistaken does not qualify. A court asks whether the person held it against all evidence.

Someone with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, severe depression or an anxiety disorder who understands the signing and holds no delusion about their family can make a valid will. Stable periods count. Courts accept that conditions come and go, which is why the timing of a signing matters so much.

## What about strong medication?

Medication is an evidence problem, not a legal bar. Opioids, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics and sleep aids can dull thinking, and challengers like to say "she was on morphine." The answer is a record showing that on the day of signing the person was alert and oriented.

A practical routine:

1. Tell the attorney what you take and when your clearest hours are.
2. Schedule the signing for that window, not late at night or just after a dose.
3. Ask the attorney to meet you alone first and to ask open questions that cannot be answered yes or no.
4. If you are being treated, ask your doctor or therapist for a brief note dated near the signing.
5. Have witnesses who are not family, and ask them to write down what they saw.

None of this is dramatic. It is the same record any careful lawyer builds for a will made during illness, and it is what stands between a signed document and a long contest.

## A realistic example

Back to Marcus. His mother, Elena, is his guardian over his finances. He calls a firm and says he is under a guardianship and has bipolar disorder. The attorney asks to see the court order. It covers money only, says nothing about a will, and is reviewed each year.

She meets Marcus alone twice. In the first meeting he names his sister Renata, her children Leo and Ines, and his bank account. He explains why he wants the children to have the money. He is also clear about why Elena should not be the executor: she would be uncomfortable doing it. His psychiatrist writes a short letter that he is stable and has good judgment. Two staff witnesses watch him sign. The attorney keeps her notes.

Elena is later upset she was left out of the plan and says her son was "not himself." The record shows otherwise. A contest would be hard to win. If Marcus had been in an acute episode, believed his sister was stealing from him with no evidence, or could not name his family, the lawyer would have said it was the wrong time to sign.

## What if the person cannot make a will?

If someone does not meet the standard on any day, a will is not the answer, and you should not try to find a lawyer who will "make it work." The risks are that the will is thrown out later, and that the family spends years fighting about it.

What helps then:

- Check what already passes by beneficiary form, joint title or a trust. The decisions have often been made.
- If assets need to be managed or protected, the guardian or conservator handles that during life. See [guardian of the person versus conservator of the estate](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate).
- If a will is truly needed, ask a lawyer about the court options in your state.

Without a will, intestacy decides, and that may be acceptable. A spouse and children often inherit by default.

## Can I make my own legal planning easier later?

Yes. Make the plan during a stable period. A will, a power of attorney and a healthcare directive can be signed years before they are needed. For anyone with an illness that comes and goes, early signing with good records is the best protection against the argument "he was not himself."
