Walt signed his will in 2009. It named his brother as executor, left his boat to his nephew, and split everything else between his two children. Since then his brother has died, he sold the boat, and he remarried. He has not looked at the will in years, and he assumes it still works.

If you are in a similar spot, your will probably still counts. The problem is how it reads against the life you have now. This page explains the specific things that go wrong and what usually fixes each one. For a checklist of when to revisit your plan, see [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan). This page focuses on the consequences.

## What happens by default

A will speaks on the day you die. Courts follow its words, and they generally do not rewrite it to match your current life. Where the will has a gap, state statutes fill it. Where it names someone who cannot serve, the court appoints a replacement. The process runs through [probate](/learn/probate), and it is slower and more expensive when the paperwork does not match reality.

## Which problems come from an old will?

They are separate problems, and each has its own rule.

### A named executor or guardian who cannot serve

If your executor died, moved, became ill or fell out with the family, and you named no backup, the court appoints someone. Many states follow a priority list that starts with a spouse, then adult children and so on. The result may differ from the person you would have chosen today. Our article on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) explains what the role involves. The same is true for a guardian for minor children, where the court steps in and picks the person it finds best for the child.

### Gifts of property you no longer own

When a will leaves a specific item and you no longer own it, the gift can fail. Lawyers call this ademption. In many states the beneficiary gets nothing, with limited state-specific exceptions. A gift of money or a percentage of the estate avoids this.

### A beneficiary who died first

If someone named in the will dies before you, their gift usually lapses. Many states have anti-lapse statutes that pass the gift to the descendants of a predeceased relative, often only if the person was a close relative. Without a statute that applies, the gift falls into the residue and goes to whoever shares in what is left. See [what happens if a beneficiary dies before you](/blog/what-happens-if-a-beneficiary-dies-before-you).

### Children born or adopted after the will

Many states have pretermitted or omitted child statutes that give a child born after the will a share unless the will shows intent to leave them out. The rules differ on which children qualify and how much they receive. A will that names children one by one, rather than "my children," is the most likely to miss someone.

### Marriage after the will

Some states give a spouse who married after the will was signed a share, unless the will shows an intent to leave them out. Other states give nothing for that reason alone, though spouses often have separate rights, such as an elective share. Your plan may need to change either way.

### Divorce after the will

Many states automatically revoke will gifts and roles for a former spouse. Not all do. Beneficiary forms on insurance and retirement accounts are separate, and an old form may still pay the ex. See our article on [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce).

### Moving to another state

A will valid where you signed it is generally accepted elsewhere. What changes are the rules around it: a surviving spouse's share, whether the state uses community property, and how out-of-state land is handled. Some states have different witness or self-proving rules, which can matter during probate.

### A will that no longer matches your accounts

The will does not control accounts with a named beneficiary, joint owner or payable-on-death designation. If those do not match the will, the accounts follow the forms. Our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) explains how the two work together.

## What changes cause what problems?

| Change since the will was signed | What can go wrong | What usually fixes it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executor or guardian died or is unsuitable | Court picks a replacement, perhaps not your choice | New will or codicil naming current and backup choices |
| Sold or gave away a specifically gifted item | Gift fails; beneficiary may get nothing | Restate gifts as amounts or percentages |
| Beneficiary died first | Gift lapses or passes to their descendants under state law | Name backups and say what happens if a person dies first |
| New child or adoption | Child may be omitted, or state statute decides their share | Update the will to name all children and any guardian |
| Marriage | New spouse may claim a statutory share | Decide on the intended plan, and put it in writing |
| Divorce | Former spouse may still benefit through forms or in some states the will | Revise will and every beneficiary form |
| Move to a new state | Different spouse and property rules | Review with an attorney in the new state |
| New accounts or a trust | Will and forms point different ways | Align the will with titles and designations |

## A realistic example

After Walt dies, his daughter Nora takes the will to the court. His brother cannot serve, so the court appoints Nora's stepmother, Linda, since she has priority as the surviving spouse. Nora expected to serve. The boat is gone, so Walt's nephew gets nothing from that line. Linda also asks for her spousal share, since the will predates the marriage.

The state's rules sort most of this out. Nobody did anything wrong, but it took more time, and the family argued about what Walt really wanted. The time and cost come from the court appointment, the number of people involved and any disputes.

## What can still be fixed?

If you are alive and have capacity:

1. Read your will and mark every name, gift and role.
2. Check each person is alive, willing and still a good fit.
3. List what you own now and compare it with the specific gifts.
4. Check beneficiary forms against the will.
5. Meet an attorney to sign a new will, or a codicil for a small change. A codicil must meet the same signing rules as a will.

If it is too late, the family works through probate under the will and state law. An attorney can explain what the statutes do and whether the family can agree on changes, such as a disclaimer. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for what happens when no valid will controls.

## How state law changes this

Anti-lapse rules, omitted child and spouse statutes, divorce revocation and priority lists for executors all differ by state. Check your state guide, such as [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), and have an attorney confirm the current rule.

## How to prevent it

Review your plan every three to five years, and sooner after a marriage, divorce, birth, death, move, sale of a home or business, or a large change in money. The [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) can send a nudge. Use the codicil for one small fix and a new will when several things changed. Name backups for every role. For more on the options, see our [guide to wills](/learn/wills).
