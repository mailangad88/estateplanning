Ray, 31, has a developmental disability and receives SSI and Medicaid. His mother Joyce dies, and her will, written years earlier, leaves her estate in equal shares to her three children. Ray's share is about $60,000.

On paper that sounds like good news. In practice it can cost Ray his monthly check and his health coverage. This article explains what happens when a person on means-tested benefits inherits directly, and what the family can do.

## What happens by default

SSI and most Medicaid programs are means-tested, which means they look at what a person has. An inheritance is counted twice in effect. In the month it is received it counts as income. After that it counts as a resource if the person still has it. For SSI, the resource limit is currently $2,000 for an individual. Medicaid limits vary by program and by state.

Once countable resources exceed the limit, benefits stop until the person is back under it. That can mean no SSI check and, in many cases, no Medicaid, which pays for home care, therapy or group home services that private insurance usually does not.

The following table compares the main ways the money can reach Ray.

| How Ray receives it | Effect on SSI and Medicaid | Payback to Medicaid at death |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outright, in his own name | Counted; benefits likely suspended until spent down | Not applicable |
| Third-party special needs trust created by Joyce | Not counted if drafted correctly | No |
| First-party special needs trust funded with Ray's own money | Not counted | Yes, for benefits paid |
| ABLE account | Not counted up to limits | Possible state claim after death |

The difference between the second and third rows is the heart of it. Money that was never Ray's can sit in a third-party trust and pass to other family members when he dies. Money that was Ray's must be paid back in part. See [first-party versus third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust).

## A realistic example

Joyce's executor, Ray's sister Tanya, distributes the estate. Ray's share arrives as a check in his own name. His case worker at the group home sees the deposit and tells Tanya. Ray is told his SSI is suspended and that his Medicaid redetermination is coming.

Tanya meets with an attorney. Because Ray is under 65, the firm explains that a first-party special needs trust can hold the funds. Ray's money is moved into it, a trustee pays for his phone, furniture and recreation, and his benefits are reinstated after the paperwork is reviewed. The trust includes a payback clause that returns remaining funds to the state Medicaid agency when Ray dies, up to what was spent on him.

It took months and cost attorney fees and trust set-up costs. Had Joyce's will named a third-party trust, none of that would have happened, and whatever remained at Ray's death could have gone to his niece. The time and cost drivers are the reporting deadlines, the agency's review and the trust drafting.

## What can still be fixed

### If you are the person leaving the money

1. **Change the will and beneficiary forms.** Leave the share for a person with a disability to a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust), not to the person.
2. **Pick a trustee.** Choose someone steady and give a successor.
3. **Write a letter of intent.** Describe the person's routine, doctors and preferences. See [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent).
4. **Tell the family.** Siblings and grandparents may want to leave gifts to the same trust.
5. **Check life insurance and retirement forms.** Each one needs the trust named.

### If the money has already arrived or is about to

1. **Do not spend or give it away yet.** Gifts can cause penalties.
2. **Report it to Social Security and the state Medicaid office.** Keep copies.
3. **Ask about a disclaimer.** The heir, or a guardian acting for them with court approval, may refuse the inheritance in some cases, which sends it to the next person. Timing and tax rules apply. This also may count as a transfer in some programs, so get advice first.
4. **Consider a first-party trust or an ABLE account.** See [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) for limits and eligibility.
5. **See an attorney familiar with benefits.** Rules differ by program and age.

## How state law changes this

SSI is federal, but Medicaid is run by states, so eligibility rules, asset limits, estate recovery and trust requirements differ. Some states treat a person's home differently, and some states allow pooled trusts run by nonprofits for people who are too old for a first-party trust. Read your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois), and review [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) for how the programs interact.

## How to prevent it

A third-party special needs trust, named in every parent's and grandparent's will, trust and beneficiary forms, is the safeguard. The [types of special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) explain the choices. Pair it with a plan for who will look after the person day to day, including [guardianship alternatives](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children).

Families often leave a disabled child equal shares out of love and fairness. A trust achieves fairness without risking care. The [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) can help you spot forms that still name the person directly.

For more scenarios like this, see [what happens if you put off estate planning](/learn/what-if).

## What counts as the heir's own money, and what does not?

Families sometimes assume only cash counts. For SSI, resources include bank accounts, stocks, a second property and the cash value of some life insurance. An inherited house can be a resource too, unless the person lives there as a home. Regular monthly payments from an inheritance, such as rent from a house, count as income.

Timing matters. Because an inheritance is income in the month received, the check may be reduced or cut for that month even if the money is gone by the next. Social Security looks at the first moment of the month for resources, so money received late in the month often is not counted as a resource until the following one. That is a reason to talk to an advisor before the money lands, not after.

Some spending is allowed. Buying a primary home, paying off debt, covering burial expenses in advance, and buying items the person needs for daily life are commonly treated as acceptable. Putting the money in a relative's name is not. Do not move funds around casually. A careful plan, with receipts, looks very different from a quick transfer to an agency reviewer.

A helpful rule of thumb is to do nothing until you have asked. The window between receiving money and reporting it is short, but it is long enough to make a phone call.
