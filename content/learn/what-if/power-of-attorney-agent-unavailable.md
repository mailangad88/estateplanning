Leon, 68, signed a financial power of attorney the year he retired. He named his brother Ray as agent and, on the same page, left the line for a backup blank because he could not think of anyone else he trusted. Ray died of a heart attack three years later. Leon was sad, and he was sure he would get around to a new document. Then Leon was diagnosed with early dementia.

His situation shows the quiet weakness in an otherwise sound document. A power of attorney is a grant of authority to one person, and it works only while that person is alive, willing and able. This article covers what happens when the agent cannot or will not act, and what you can do now.

## What happens by default

The document does not pass to someone else on its own. If the agent has died, is incapacitated, resigns or declines, and no successor is named, the power of attorney has nobody to exercise it. The authority is still written down, but there is no one to use it.

There are five common ways an agent becomes unavailable.

| Situation | What it means | Does the document survive? |
|---|---|---|
| Agent dies | Authority ends with the person | Only if a successor is named |
| Agent becomes incapacitated | Agent cannot lawfully act | Only if a successor is named |
| Agent declines or resigns | A person cannot be compelled to serve | Only if a successor is named |
| Agent divorces the principal | In many states, a spouse agent is removed by divorce unless the document says otherwise | Depends on state and document |
| Agent is hard to reach | Authority remains, but practical delay grows | Yes, but may need help |

For the divorce row, see the section on divorce in our guide on [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).

### What if there is a backup?

Then the system works as designed. Most forms let the principal list successor agents in order. When the first agent cannot serve, the next steps in, usually by signing a short statement that the first agent is unavailable. Some forms require the successor to attach proof, such as a death certificate or a doctor's letter. The difference between a successor and a co-agent is explained in our guide to [naming more than one agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-name-more-than-one-power-of-attorney-agent).

## A realistic example

Leon, now 71, can no longer follow his bills. His daughter Carmen finds the power of attorney and realizes it names only Ray. She calls Leon's bank, which tells her it needs a person with legal authority. She cannot use the old document, because Ray is dead and the form names no one else.

Leon's doctor writes a letter. Carmen then hires an attorney and files a petition for guardianship of his finances, called a conservatorship in some states. Notice is given to Leon and his other relatives. The court appoints an independent attorney or visitor to look into Leon's situation, holds a hearing, and may require Carmen to post a bond and file yearly accountings. The process takes weeks to months, and fees come out of Leon's funds.

During that time, the mortgage payment is late once, a small bill goes to collections, and Carmen pays a few things from her own account. None of it was avoidable once Leon lost capacity. What would have avoided it was one signature on the backup line years earlier. For how the two paths compare, read [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) and the story of an [incapacitated person without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney).

## What can still be fixed

### If you can still sign

1. **Check who is named, and whether each person can serve.** Read the agent and successor lines. Ask each person if they are still willing and able.
2. **Sign a new durable power of attorney.** It replaces the old one. Name a primary agent and at least one successor, and make the order explicit. Our page on [choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) helps with the decision.
3. **Do the healthcare side too.** A healthcare directive has its own agent, and the same problem applies. The agent should have a named backup and a HIPAA release.
4. **Give copies to the agents, the bank and your attorney.** A document no one can find fails the same way as one that names a dead person.
5. **Use the bank's own form if one exists.** Many banks offer a form that they accept without question. Having both the long-form document and the bank form on file avoids delays.

### If you have already lost capacity

Capacity decides what is possible. A person with early dementia may still understand what a power of attorney does, and an attorney can assess that on the day. Where capacity is clearly gone, the family needs the court route. The cost and process are described in our article on [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney). Ask the attorney for the least restrictive order available, such as a limited conservatorship for particular accounts.

### If the agent simply will not act

Treat it as a decision, not a mystery. Ask the agent in writing whether they will serve. If not, ask them to resign in writing, following the document's process, and move to the successor. If the agent is acting badly rather than not acting, the problem is different and is covered in [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

## What if the agent is willing but the institution says no?

Sometimes the agent is alive, well and eager, and the bank still will not deal with them. The effect on the family is the same as having no agent. Banks reject documents for being old, for lacking their own wording, for being signed out of state, or because a clerk has never seen one. The usual response is polite persistence. Ask for the bank's legal or fiduciary department, ask them to state the reason in writing and cite the state statute that requires acceptance. If that fails, a short letter from an attorney often works. Our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) covers the steps. Keeping a bank-specific form on file avoids the argument.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether a successor must sign anything to take over, how an agent resigns, whether divorce removes a spouse agent, what proof a financial institution may demand and how easy a conservatorship is to obtain. Many states have a statute requiring institutions to accept a properly signed power of attorney or face penalties, but enforcement is slow. If your agent and your assets sit in different states, the document should work in both, and the [moving-states scenario](/learn/what-if/moved-states-plan-not-updated) covers what that means. For state-specific starting points see [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [Florida](/estate-planning/florida).

## How to prevent it

Prevention is a one-page job. Name at least one successor in the financial power of attorney, and one in the healthcare directive. Pick people younger than you who live reasonably close. Talk to them before you sign. Then re-read the list whenever someone dies, moves, divorces or gets sick, and any time you review your plan with the [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder).
