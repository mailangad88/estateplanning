Marcus paid for a living trust in 2021 and signed it. His lawyer sent a funding letter listing the steps: a new deed for the house, a retitled brokerage account, a beneficiary change on the IRA. Marcus did the IRA and meant to do the rest. He died in March. His daughter, now successor trustee, asked the obvious question: can we finish the funding now?

The answer is almost always no. This page explains why you cannot fund a trust after the person who made it has died, what happens to the assets that never moved, what an unsigned trust means, and the narrow fixes that remain. For the broader picture of a trust with little in it, see our page on the [unfunded trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust), or browse the rest of the [what-if library](/learn/what-if).

## Can you fund a trust after the grantor has died?

No, because funding is an act the owner has to perform. A deed needs the owner's signature. A brokerage account needs the owner's instructions. A beneficiary form needs the owner's choice. Death ends the owner's ability to do any of them, and a power of attorney ends the same day.

What the family can do is work with what exists:

1. **Look at how each asset is titled.** Pull the deed, the account statements and the beneficiary forms. Our guide on [how to tell if your trust is funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded) gives the checks.
2. **Sort the assets into three groups.** Those in the trust, those with a named beneficiary, and those in the decedent's name alone.
3. **Handle the third group through the estate.** That means an executor, a court, and the will.

## What happens to assets that were never moved into the trust?

They are probate assets. The executor named in the will asks the court for authority, collects the assets, pays debts and then distributes what is left under the will. If the will is a pour-over will, the leftover goes to the trust, and the trustee then manages it under the trust terms. Our page on the [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) explains the mechanics.

Marcus's estate shows how the pieces split. These figures are an example.

| Asset | How it was titled | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| House, $410,000 | Marcus alone | Probate. The executor deeds it, or sells it, then the proceeds pour into the trust |
| Brokerage account, $120,000 | Marcus alone | Probate, then pours into the trust |
| IRA, $200,000 | Trust named as beneficiary | Passes to the trust outside probate |
| Checking account, $18,000 | Joint with his daughter | Goes to her by survivorship |

The trust still ends up with most of what Marcus wanted. The cost is time and court filings on the first two rows. In Illinois a small estate may skip court if the personal property is $150,000 or less, no real estate is involved and no letters are open, as described in our [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) page. Marcus's house is real estate, so that shortcut would not reach it.

A successor trustee cannot sell the house, even though she holds the trust documents. She has no authority over it until the court appoints an executor, and that is the same person only if the will says so. Many people in Marcus's position name the same daughter in both documents.

## What if the trust was drafted but never signed?

Then there is no trust. An unsigned trust has no legal effect, however polished the draft and however much the person intended it. This catches families who find a binder in a drawer with every page typed and the signature page blank.

Nothing in that draft controls anything. The person's last signed will governs, or state intestacy law if there is no will. Our page on [dying before signing a will](/learn/what-if/dies-before-signing-their-will) covers the same problem for wills. The reasoning is the same.

Two practical points:

- **Check for a signed copy elsewhere.** Lawyers, banks and safe-deposit boxes sometimes hold the executed original. Ask before concluding it was never signed.
- **Look at the notary and witness pages.** Some trusts need a notary or witnesses to be valid, depending on the assets they hold. Our guide on [trust signing, notary and witnesses](/learn/trusts/trust-signing-notary-witnesses-and-where-to-keep-the-documents) covers the requirement.

## What if my will leaves everything to a trust that was never created?

The answer turns on the wording and on whether the trust existed when the will was signed. A pour-over gift generally works when the trust was signed before the will or at the same time. If the trust never came into being, the gift can fail, and a court then looks at what the will says about a backup. Some wills include a fallback, such as leaving the property outright to named people. Others do not, and the estate may pass by intestacy.

This is a real risk for people who sign a will first and plan to finish the trust "next month." If you are in that position, the cleanest fix is to sign the trust now, or to add a fallback to the will until you do. We see this more often than families expect, and the will's text decides the outcome, so have an attorney read it.

## Are there any fixes after death?

A few, and they are narrower than people hope.

- **A court petition.** Some courts will treat an asset as trust property if the paperwork shows the owner clearly meant it to be in the trust. A signed list of assets, or an assignment of personal property, is the kind of evidence that helps. Whether a petition works depends on the state and the facts, so it is a fallback, not a plan.
- **An estate transfer to the trust.** The executor moves the asset in after the court process, as above.
- **A beneficiary form that already named the trust.** If the account named the trust as beneficiary before death, it is already outside the estate.
- **A disclaimer.** A beneficiary who does not want an asset can sometimes disclaim it so that it passes to the next person in line. Our page on [disclaiming an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) explains the limits.

## What if the owner is still alive but has dementia?

The question changes. The owner can fund the trust only while still able to understand and sign, and a power of attorney agent can sometimes do it, but only if the document grants that power. If you are in this situation, read [creating a trust with dementia or a power of attorney](/learn/trusts/creating-a-trust-with-dementia-or-a-power-of-attorney) and speak to an attorney soon, because capacity can be hard to prove later.

## How to prevent this

Funding is a checklist, and it belongs in the first month after the trust is signed. For each asset, write the date it was moved. Our guide on [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) lists the steps by asset type. Keep a signed [general assignment and schedule](/learn/trusts/schedule-a-and-general-assignment-for-a-living-trust) with the trust so that personal property is covered if anything is missed. Review the plan with the [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) once a year.
