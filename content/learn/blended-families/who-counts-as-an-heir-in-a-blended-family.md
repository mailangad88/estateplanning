Families do not fit a form. A man has a son with his first wife, adopts his second wife's daughter, and has a baby with her. His half-brother lives three states away. A woman raises a nephew as her own and never adopts him. When these people die without clear paperwork, a court has to decide who counts as family, and the courts use definitions that are narrower than many families expect.

This page lays out those definitions, which of your relatives are heirs by default and which are not, and where a will or trust is the only way to include someone you think of as family.

## Who inherits when someone dies without a will?

State law sets an order, called [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession). It starts with a spouse and children, then parents, then siblings, then more distant relatives. In a blended family the first question is which of the people in your life qualify as a spouse, a child or a sibling in the legal sense. The page on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) gives the general order. This page looks at the boundary cases.

| Relationship | Inherits by default? | What decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Biological child | Yes | Parentage, born inside or outside marriage |
| Legally adopted child | Yes | Final adoption order |
| Stepchild | No, in most states | Only a will, trust or adoption changes it |
| Foster child | No | Same, unless adopted |
| Half-sibling | Yes, if no closer relative | State rule on half-blood |
| Stepparent | No | Named in a will, or a few narrow state exceptions |
| Step-grandchild | No | A will or trust must name them |
| Child born after your death | Yes, in most states, if conceived in life and born within a set time | State statute |

## Do half-siblings inherit?

Usually yes, and usually on equal terms. Most states treat half-siblings like full siblings when siblings are the next in line. A minority of states, though, give a half-blood relative a smaller share, such as half of what a full sibling takes.

The real question is whether siblings are in line at all. A spouse, a child or a living parent comes first in nearly every state, so half-siblings tend to matter only for someone who died single, childless and with no parents. That is more common than it sounds, and it is where a blended family surprises people. Take Marcus, 61, divorced, no children, both parents dead. His half-sister Elena, whom he grew up with, and a full sister he has not spoken to in twenty years each take half of his estate. If Marcus wants his friend of thirty years to inherit, a will is the only way.

## Do adopted children inherit like biological children?

Yes, and that is one of the clearest rules in this area. After a final adoption, the child is the adoptive parents' child for nearly every legal purpose, including intestacy. Most modern trust and will wording that says "children" or "descendants" covers adopted children too, but old documents sometimes say "issue of the body" or "blood heirs." Reread your documents for those words. The stepchild-specific tools are covered in our guide to [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance).

### Does adoption cut off inheritance from birth parents?

Usually, yes. A child adopted by strangers generally loses inheritance rights from the birth family, and the birth family loses rights from the child. Stepparent adoption is the usual exception. In many states the child continues to inherit from and through the birth parent who is married to the stepparent, and the other birth parent's line may be cut off. State rules differ on this point, and a child who is adopted at 16 can end up with fewer relatives than the family assumes.

### Can an adult be adopted to inherit?

In many states, yes. An adult adoption requires the adult's consent and a court order, and some states require a minimum age gap or an existing relationship. People use it to make a stepchild an heir. It works, but it is a permanent change of legal family and it can alter inheritance from the adult's own birth parents. It also invites challenges if the timing looks tied to an estate. We recommend a will or trust first, and adoption only when it fits the relationship.

## What about children born outside marriage?

Every state lets a child inherit from the mother. From the father, the child must show paternity. That is automatic if the parents were married when the child was born. Otherwise, the usual paths are a signed acknowledgment of paternity, a court judgment, or genetic testing, sometimes after the father has died and sometimes within a deadline. Where a father quietly raised a child without paperwork, the child may need to prove parentage in court during probate, and other heirs can contest it.

For a parent, the practical advice is to avoid leaving it to chance. A will that names the child and a signed acknowledgment of paternity settle the question early.

## Are children you did not know about heirs?

If paternity is proven, they inherit under intestacy and in some states can claim a share even where a will leaves them out. A child born or adopted after you sign a will may be a "pretermitted" heir with a statutory share unless your will shows you meant to leave them out. The remedy is to review your will at each family change. Our page on [dying with an outdated will](/learn/what-if/outdated-will) shows what that looks like in practice.

## Who is left out by default?

The people left out are mostly those with deep emotional ties and no legal one. Common examples are these.

- Stepchildren and step-grandchildren.
- Foster children who were never adopted.
- A partner you did not marry.
- A cousin, godchild or friend who cared for you.
- A stepparent who raised you.

Picture Joanne, who married Pete when his daughter Kayla was six and raised her for thirty years. Pete dies without a will, with no other children. Joanne takes the estate as his spouse. When Joanne dies, also without a will, Kayla gets nothing, because she was never adopted and is not Joanne's child under the statute. Nobody did anything wrong. The paperwork just never caught up with the relationship.

Some states recognize a doctrine called equitable adoption for a child who was promised adoption and raised as a child of the family, but it is a narrow exception that requires a court fight. Do not plan around it.

## How do you make sure the right people are covered?

1. List everyone you think of as family: spouse, children, stepchildren, grandchildren, a foster child, a partner.
2. Note which ones are heirs by default using the table above.
3. For each person who is not, decide whether to name them in a will or trust, or by beneficiary form.
4. Use names, not labels. "My children" is risky. "My son Ben, my stepdaughter Aisha" is not.
5. Revisit the list whenever someone is born, adopted, married or divorced.
