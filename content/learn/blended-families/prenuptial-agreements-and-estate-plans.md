If either of you has children, property or an inheritance coming, a marital agreement can say in writing what stays separate and what does not. It is not romantic, but it is the clearest way to prevent a later dispute between your spouse and your children.

This page covers what prenuptial and postnuptial agreements can and cannot do for your estate plan, what makes them hold up, and how to keep your will or trust in step with them. It belongs to our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## What is the difference between a prenup and a postnup?

A prenuptial agreement is a contract signed before the wedding, and a postnuptial agreement is one signed after. Both can address the same subjects: which property is separate, how assets are divided on divorce, and what each spouse receives or gives up at death.

Prenups are recognized in every state, and many states have adopted a version of the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act. Postnups are recognized in a growing number of states but are less uniform. Some courts look harder at them because the spouses already owe each other duties and one of them may be agreeing out of pressure to keep the marriage together.

For estate planning, the death provisions matter most. You will see these terms:

- **Separate property.** Assets each person brought into the marriage, plus inheritances and gifts, stay separate.
- **Waiver of the elective share.** A spouse gives up the right to claim a minimum share of the other's estate. See [elective share](/glossary/elective-share).
- **Waiver of other rights.** These include homestead, allowances, and the right to serve as administrator.
- **Agreed provisions at death.** For example, a set sum, a right to live in the home for a period, or life insurance for the survivor.

## What can an agreement do for your children?

An agreement protects your children by limiting what your spouse can claim from your estate, which leaves more under your control. It does not, by itself, give your children anything.

Take Marcus, 58, a widower with two daughters who marries Lena, 55. He owns a house worth $600,000 and a rollover IRA worth $400,000, and he wants most of this to go to his daughters. They sign a prenup in which each waives any claim to the other's separate property at death. Without a will or trust, Marcus's intestate estate would still pass by state law. With the prenup and an estate plan leaving the IRA and house to a trust for the daughters, plus a lifetime right of residence for Lena, he gets the result he wants and Lena gets housing and income.

The agreement removed her claim, and the estate plan used that freedom. Both parts were needed. Read [how to make sure children from a first marriage inherit](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) for the plan side.

## What makes a marital agreement enforceable?

Courts enforce agreements that are fair in how they were made, even if the result looks one-sided. The common requirements are:

1. **A written agreement signed by both people.** Oral promises are rarely enough.
2. **Voluntary signing.** No pressure, threats or ultimatums. Signing days before the wedding, with guests booked and a cancelled wedding as the alternative, raises concern.
3. **Fair financial disclosure.** Each person lists assets, debts and income. Hiding something can void the agreement.
4. **Independent legal advice.** Each person should have their own lawyer. Some states require it, and it is always wise.
5. **Understandable terms.** A spouse who did not read English, or was not given time to review, can challenge it.
6. **Not unconscionable at signing or, in some states, at enforcement.** A clause that leaves one person destitute may be rejected.

Start months before the wedding if you can. Many people find the conversation easier with a lawyer present.

## What can a prenup not do?

There are real limits, and the first one catches many couples off guard.

**Employer retirement plans.** The federal ERISA law gives the spouse of a 401(k) participant survivor rights. A fiancé is not yet a spouse, so a prenup cannot waive them. After the wedding, the spouse must sign a consent on the plan's form, which often needs a notary or plan representative. IRAs are not covered by ERISA, so a waiver in the agreement may work, but the custodian will still follow the beneficiary form. See [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

**Child support and custody.** Courts can disregard terms that harm a child's interests.

**Illegal or unfair terms.** Provisions that encourage divorce, or that violate public policy, may be unenforceable.

**Property that changes character.** If you deposit separate funds in a joint account or retitle your house in both names, the agreement may no longer protect those funds unless it says how mixing is handled. Be disciplined about keeping separate assets separate, or write down the rule for each situation.

**Community property states.** In these states, such as Texas and California, a marital agreement can change the default rule that marital earnings belong equally to both spouses. Without one, [community property](/glossary/community-property) law applies automatically. See [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) and [California](/estate-planning/california) for state specifics.

## How does the agreement fit with your will or trust?

The agreement is a contract, and the will or trust is the plan. A contract can override a will where the two conflict, so they must agree.

| Subject | Marital agreement | Will or trust |
|---|---|---|
| Waiver of spouse's elective share | Yes, the best place for it | Cannot waive it |
| Gifts to children | Can promise them | Where they are actually given |
| Gifts to spouse | Can promise a set amount or right | Carries it out |
| Retirement accounts | Waiver may help, but forms control | Cannot override beneficiary forms |
| Home | Can set who owns it and when | Can create a right of residence |
| Updating over time | Both spouses must agree to change | You can change alone |

Sign the agreement first, then draft the plan to match, or update the plan the same month. Check beneficiary forms and titles at the same time. Our guide to [estate planning for newlyweds](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-newlyweds) lists the other updates after a wedding.

## Does marriage change an existing will?

In most states, marriage alone does not cancel a will signed earlier, but many states protect a spouse who married after the will was made, sometimes by giving them a share as though there were no will. Divorce, on the other hand, typically cancels gifts and appointments for the former spouse. If you did not plan for the marriage, do not assume your old will still matches your wishes. See [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) to see what happens if nothing is in place.

## Should you get one?

A marital agreement is worth serious thought when:

- Either of you has children from a prior relationship.
- One person has much more wealth, a business, or an expected inheritance.
- One of you owns a home that the other would otherwise have rights in.
- You are older and each have assets that took a lifetime to build.

It is less necessary when you have modest assets, no children from earlier relationships, and the same goals. But even modest couples are better off knowing what the default rules would do. A short consultation can show you.
