Parents in a second marriage often say the same thing: "I want my wife taken care of, and I want my kids to get something." Both statements are sincere and they pull in opposite directions, because the same dollars cannot be spent on one person and saved for the other.

This page is about the numbers. It does not tell you what percentage to pick, because nobody can. It gives you ways to frame the decision, three models that families actually use, and a test to run against your own figures. For the legal background see our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## Where do you start?

Start with what your spouse needs, then work outward. Most people start the other way, picking a split like 50/50 and hoping it works. That tends to produce a plan that is generous on paper and unworkable in life.

Ask these questions in order:

1. Where will your spouse live, and who owns that place?
2. What does your spouse already own, including pensions, retirement accounts and Social Security?
3. How old is your spouse compared to you, and to your children?
4. How long have you been married?
5. What do your children need now, and what will they need in ten years?
6. Which assets did you bring into the marriage, and which did you build together?

Two couples with the same estate can land in different places. Walter, 74, has been married to Joan, 71, for eleven years. Joan has her own pension and a paid-off condo. Walter's three children are in their forties and fine. Walter might leave Joan a modest amount and most of the rest to his children. Compare Hector, 70, married to Lena, 55, for four years. Lena has no retirement savings and left her career to care for Hector. Hector's duty, moral if not legal, runs further.

## What are the three common models?

Most plans fall into one of three shapes, sometimes combined. Each trades certainty for the spouse against certainty for the children.

| Model | How it works | Best when | Weak spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lifetime support, then remainder | A trust pays your spouse income, and sometimes principal, for life, then the rest goes to your children | The estate is large enough to support the spouse and still leave something | Spouse and children can resent each other during the trust |
| Fixed shares | Your spouse receives a set share or sum outright, and your children receive the rest now | The two sides want a clean break and distrust a shared trust | Spouse can spend or redirect their share, and it may pass to their own family |
| Split by asset type | Spouse gets the house or a pension, children get investments or the business | Values are close and everyone agrees on them | Values move, and the house carries costs the investments do not |

The first model is the one most attorneys draw for second marriages, since it protects both sides. It is also the one that makes the most demands, because it needs a trustee for decades. Our guides to [joint and separate trusts for remarriage](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage) and to [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) show how to build it.

## Should your spouse get the house or the money?

It depends on whether your spouse can afford to keep the house. A house feels like security, but it comes with property taxes, insurance and a new roof every twenty years. A spouse who inherits the house and nothing to maintain it can end up house-poor.

Giving the spouse the right to live there for life, with an agreed fund for upkeep, often fits better than giving title outright. Our page on the [family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) goes through the options, including what happens when the spouse wants to move.

## What does the law require you to leave?

In most states, the law sets a floor for a surviving spouse that your will cannot lower. This is the [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) or right of election. The percentage and the assets it reaches differ by state. Some states count only the probate estate, others reach trusts and accounts too, and some states give the spouse a smaller share when the marriage was short. Community property states take a different route altogether and treat much of what you earned during the marriage as half theirs.

A spouse can give up this right in a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement, but only if the agreement was signed knowingly and with disclosure. Without that waiver, a will that leaves the spouse nothing can be overridden. Read about [the elective share and disinheriting a spouse](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse), and [what a prenup can do for your estate plan](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).

## How do you test a plan with real numbers?

Write out three simple scenarios and add up the totals for each side.

1. You die first, next year, and your spouse lives another twenty years.
2. You die first in fifteen years, and your spouse dies soon after.
3. Your spouse dies first, and you need long-term care before you die.

For each scenario, list what your spouse receives, what your children receive, and what each side pays in taxes and costs. Do it on paper, with real balances. Many people find their first idea fails the second scenario. The fixed-share plan that looked fair gives the spouse a modest sum that runs out when they are 85, or leaves the children almost nothing because most of the estate went to the spouse for care.

A number to keep in mind: the federal estate tax exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families do not face federal tax. Some states tax estates or inheritances at lower levels. Leaving assets to a spouse usually qualifies for the [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction), which defers tax until the spouse's death, but a trust must be drafted properly to qualify. See [how the state estate and inheritance taxes work](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## How does life insurance help even things out?

Life insurance lets you give the spouse the house or the business and still leave your children a cash amount. Instead of selling assets, you name the children as beneficiaries of a policy equal to the gap. It works well when the gap is large and the policy is affordable for your age and health. Our guide to [life insurance in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/life-insurance-in-a-blended-family) explains the structure and the traps, such as an old beneficiary form that names an ex.

## What about unequal shares among your children?

If you are adding a spouse to the picture, also check whether your children are treated the way you want. Some parents give a stepchild a smaller share, or a biological child more because of need. That is allowed, but unexplained differences produce lawsuits. See [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) for the legal limits and the use of explanatory letters.

## How do you keep the peace?

Tell people what you decided, and why, while you can still answer questions. A short letter to each child and to your spouse explaining the reasoning removes the guessing. Keep it kind and brief. Choose a trustee both sides can live with, such as a bank, a trust company or a neutral relative, and not the spouse alone. A spouse who serves as sole trustee for the children's inheritance has an obvious conflict, however decent they are.
