Married couples are often told to share one living trust. It is a sensible default for spouses who have the same children and combined finances. In a second marriage, with two sets of children and a new spouse, it can be the wrong default, and the reasons are in a few short sentences of the trust document that most people never read.

This page compares a joint trust with separate trusts, shows how each behaves at the first death, and explains when each makes sense. It is part of our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## Joint trust vs. separate trusts: how do they differ?

A joint trust is a single revocable trust created by both spouses, holding property from both. Separate trusts are two trusts, one per spouse, each holding that spouse's property. The difference that matters is not the paperwork but what happens when one spouse dies and the survivor still has the power to change things.

| Feature | Joint trust | Separate trusts |
|---|---|---|
| Number of documents | One, plus separate wills | Two, plus separate wills |
| Typical cost | Lower | Higher, but often modest |
| Who controls it while both are alive | Both, usually acting together or either one | Each spouse alone for their own trust |
| What happens at first death | Depends on drafting: may split, may stay fully flexible | Decedent's trust becomes irrevocable on its own terms |
| Risk for first spouse's children | Higher if the survivor can amend everything | Lower, because the survivor has no power over the other trust |
| Handling of shared assets | Simple, one place for the house and joint accounts | Requires deciding how to divide or co-own |
| Fits best | Couples with the same children, or community property states | Spouses with separate children and separate property |

Neither structure protects your children by itself. What protects them is a provision that makes the decedent's share irrevocable at death and names the people who receive it afterward.

## What happens to a joint trust at the first death?

It depends on whether the trust says it divides or stays whole. Many joint trusts simply continue as one trust for the survivor, who may then amend it, revoke it and leave everything to a new beneficiary list. This is the risk most blended families do not see. Martin signs a joint trust with Lena that names his daughters and her son as shared beneficiaries. After Martin dies, Lena amends the trust to cut out his daughters. If the trust allowed it, the amendment is valid.

A properly drafted joint trust for a blended family does the opposite. At the first death it splits into a survivor's trust, which the survivor can still amend, and a decedent's trust, which becomes irrevocable. The decedent's trust then follows its own instructions, which usually give the spouse income or use of property for life and name the decedent's children as remainder beneficiaries. This is the familiar A/B structure, and when federal tax rules are the aim it is called a bypass or credit-shelter trust. A version that qualifies for the marital deduction is the QTIP trust described in our page on [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts).

Look for these sentences in any joint trust you are asked to sign:

1. A statement of what becomes irrevocable at the first death.
2. A statement of who may amend the survivor's trust, and whether they can change the remainder beneficiaries of the decedent's share.
3. A rule on how the property is divided between the two sub-trusts, and who decides.
4. A name for a successor trustee that is not only the survivor.

If those provisions are missing or vague, the joint trust is a weak fit for a second marriage.

## When do separate trusts make more sense?

Separate trusts make more sense when each spouse has their own children, their own property and a desire to keep those lines clear. Each spouse signs a revocable trust funded with their own assets, and the trust names that spouse's children as ultimate beneficiaries. The trusts can still provide for the survivor: each says that after death the surviving spouse has the right to live in the home, or receive income, or both.

Separate trusts have advantages beyond control:

- **Clarity about what belongs to whom.** Nobody has to argue about whether a house bought before the marriage became shared.
- **Simple divorce.** If the marriage ends, each person's trust is already separate.
- **Different trustees.** Each can choose a trustee who suits their own children.
- **Different timing.** Each spouse's plan can be changed by that spouse without the other's signature while both are alive.

The downside is coordination. Jointly owned property, such as a house, must be held in a way that works with both trusts, for example as tenants in common with each share in each trust. A couple who files joint taxes and pays shared expenses still has to decide how those costs are divided. Our page on [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) shows how titling works for each asset.

## When does a joint trust make more sense?

A joint trust makes more sense when the couple's finances are fully combined and their goals are aligned. That can be true of a second marriage if the spouses have no children from before, or if all the children are shared, or if the couple live in a community property state and most property is already jointly owned.

A joint trust is also reasonable if it is built with the A/B split from the start, with the decedent's share locked in. Elena and Walter, both 70, have a combined estate of about $1.4 million and four adult children between them. They want the survivor to have full access for life, with half the remaining estate going to each side's children. A joint trust that splits at the first death, names a neutral trustee for the decedent's share and gives the survivor limited rights over principal does the job at a lower cost than two trusts.

Do not choose a joint trust only because it is cheaper. If the cost of two trusts is the stated reason, ask an attorney to price both. The difference is often small.

## What about taxes?

For most families the tax differences between joint and separate trusts are small. A revocable trust, joint or separate, is treated as part of your own estate and has no separate income tax identity while you are alive. With the federal estate tax basic exclusion at $15 million per person in 2026, and portability available for spouses, most blended families will owe no federal estate tax. See [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) and the [federal exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

Two points are worth keeping in mind. First, a few states have estate or inheritance taxes with much lower thresholds. Our [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) shows which. Second, when the decedent's share becomes irrevocable, it files its own tax returns, which adds some cost and paperwork for the trustee.

## Can you combine both approaches?

Yes, and many blended families do. A common arrangement is:

1. Each spouse has a separate revocable trust for their separate property and their children's inheritance.
2. A third trust, or the survivor's right of residence, covers the shared home.
3. Each trust gives the survivor limited rights, such as income for life or a defined period of occupancy.
4. A neutral trustee or co-trustee manages whatever is held for the first spouse's children while the survivor is alive.

This approach keeps the boundaries clear without leaving the survivor with nothing. It does cost more to set up and maintain, and it works only if the assets are actually retitled. Our page on [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) gives the basics.

## Mistakes that undo the plan

- **Signing a standard joint trust off the internet.** Template documents assume a shared family and rarely lock anything at the first death.
- **Naming the spouse as sole trustee of the children's inheritance.** A conflict of interest is built in. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).
- **Not funding the trust.** A trust with nothing in it does nothing. See [what happens if you never funded your trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust).
- **Forgetting beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts and insurance ignore the trust unless it is named.
- **Never reading the amendment clause.** The most important sentence in a joint trust is often a short one.
