The family home is often the biggest asset, and in a second marriage it is also the most emotional. Your spouse needs somewhere to live. Your children may see the house as part of their parent's legacy. The law does not decide between those claims. You have to.

This page explains the main ways to handle the home, the practical questions people forget, and the tradeoffs of each. It is part of our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## What happens to the house if you do nothing?

If you do nothing, the house passes according to how it is titled. If you and your spouse own it as joint tenants with right of survivorship, or as tenants by the entirety in states that offer it, the survivor owns it entirely the moment the first spouse dies. No will or trust can change that. Your children inherit nothing, and your spouse can sell it, leave it to someone else, or leave it to their own children.

If you own the home alone and die without a will, state intestacy law divides the estate, and the home may end up shared among your spouse and your children. If you left a will giving it to your children, your spouse often has protections, including homestead rights and, in many states, an [elective share](/glossary/elective-share).

Here is the common surprise. Gina, 61, owns the home she lived in with her first husband and moved her second husband, Paul, in after the wedding. They never changed the title. She assumes her children will inherit the house. Because only Gina's name is on the deed and her will leaves it to her kids, they do, but Paul may have a right to stay for some time, and he can sometimes claim a share of the estate. Without a plan, the result depends on state law and on how willing everyone is to cooperate.

## What are your options for the home?

You have five realistic choices. The right one depends on how much each person needs, the home's value, and your other assets.

| Option | How it works | Benefit | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leave it to your spouse outright | Spouse inherits full title | Simple, spouse is secure | Children may be cut out entirely |
| Right of residence in a trust | Trust holds the home; spouse lives there; children receive it later | Both are protected | Needs trust and a trustee; costs to maintain |
| Life estate by deed | Spouse has lifetime use; children are remaindermen | Direct and inexpensive | Less flexible; the deed is hard to change |
| Tenants in common | Each owns a share and may leave it separately | Each controls their own share | A co-owner can force a sale |
| Buyout with life insurance | Spouse inherits home; insurance pays children | Clean split | Premiums; policy needs to stay in force |

### A trust with a right of residence

This is the approach many attorneys recommend, because you can customize it. The house goes into your [revocable trust](/glossary/revocable-trust), and at your death the trust says your spouse may live there for life, or until they remarry, move out, or are away for a set time. After that, the house is sold or distributed to your children. The trust can also say that if your spouse chooses to downsize, the sale proceeds can be used to buy a smaller home that is held in the same trust. A trust of this kind can also be built into a [QTIP trust](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) when you want estate tax deferral.

### A life estate by deed

You deed the house so that your spouse has a life estate and your children hold the remainder. Because the children already own their interest, no probate is needed. The downside is that the deed is difficult to change, and a life estate can complicate selling or refinancing since the children must join in. It can also raise Medicaid and tax questions. See [leaving your home to children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children).

### Co-ownership as tenants in common

You and your spouse own the house as tenants in common, each with a defined share. Each owner can leave their share by will. This keeps your share out of your spouse's control, but your children then co-own a home with your spouse, and any co-owner can ask a court to order a sale. That is a poor setup unless relationships are strong and the agreement spells out who lives there and who pays.

### A buyout

You leave the house to your spouse but buy a life insurance policy naming your children as beneficiaries, sized to match the home's value or their share. The children are made whole in cash, and the spouse is not burdened. See [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

## What details should the documents settle?

Most disputes come from things the documents never mentioned. Settle these in writing:

1. **Who pays property tax, insurance and repairs.** Usually the spouse as the occupant, with the trust covering major repairs. If the spouse cannot pay, say what happens.
2. **What counts as a trigger.** Remarriage, moving out, living with a new partner, entering a care facility. Be specific about how long an absence is allowed.
3. **Whether the spouse may rent the home.** The rent might belong to the spouse, the trust, or be split.
4. **Whether the spouse can sell and buy another.** Allow it, or say the home must be kept. Without that choice, an aging spouse may be stuck in a house they cannot manage.
5. **Who decides on long-term care.** If your spouse needs a nursing home, a trust may allow the home to be sold to pay for care. Our guide on [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) explains the Medicaid issues.
6. **What the children receive at the end.** The house itself, its sale proceeds, or a payment to equalize.
7. **Who holds the keys and who can inspect.** Children may want to confirm the property is maintained.

## What about taxes and a mortgage?

On the tax side, property that your children inherit at your death usually receives a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) to its value then, which can eliminate capital gains tax on sale. A lifetime gift to children does not get that step-up. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). Some jurisdictions reassess property taxes on a transfer, and many provide exceptions for transfers to a spouse or children, so confirm local rules.

Federal law generally prevents a lender from calling a residential mortgage due solely because you put the home into your own living trust, or because a spouse or child inherits it. Still, tell the lender and update homeowner's insurance whenever title changes. Check title insurance too, as the policy may continue only if the transfer is made correctly.

If a spouse or child must assume the mortgage, the payments come from somewhere. Treat the loan as part of the plan.

## How do you reach agreement as a family?

Talk early. Your spouse and children will each have fears: your spouse that they will be evicted, your children that the house will vanish. Telling them what you decided, and why, removes most of the suspicion. Put it in writing and consider a family meeting with your attorney present. Our guide on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) offers practical advice.
