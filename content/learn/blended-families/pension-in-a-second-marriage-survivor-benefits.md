Your pension is probably the one asset that your will cannot touch and your beneficiary form may not control. If you have remarried, that is a problem or a relief, depending on what you want for your spouse and for children from your first family.

This page covers what a pension pays when you are married, how a first spouse's claim fits in, and the choices that decide whether anything is left for your children. It is part of our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## What happens to my pension if I remarry?

A new marriage changes who the plan must protect. For a private pension covered by federal law, a married participant's default is a joint-and-survivor annuity: monthly payments for your life, then continuing payments to your spouse after you die. To pick a different payout, such as a larger single-life check, your spouse must consent in writing, and the plan generally requires a notary or plan representative to witness the signature.

That default is why a pension rarely works as a gift to children. When Hassan, 64, retires from a manufacturing company with a pension of $3,100 a month, his second wife Linda holds a right to a survivor benefit. His two sons from his first marriage have none, even if Hassan would prefer it. If Linda signs the waiver, Hassan collects more each month, and Linda gets nothing from the pension after he dies. Whether she should sign depends on what else she has, and she should decide with her own advice.

## Does a first spouse have a claim on the pension?

Possibly, and the claim can come before your new spouse's. If your divorce judgment divided the pension, the plan will have received a qualified domestic relations order (QDRO) naming your first spouse as the person who receives a share. A QDRO can give the former spouse a portion of your monthly payments, a survivor benefit, or both.

When a QDRO awards the first spouse the survivor benefit, your second spouse cannot receive it for that portion, no matter what you intended. Ask the plan administrator for a copy of every order on file. People often forget what their divorce paperwork said twenty years ago, and the plan will not remind them until a claim is made.

If your divorce agreement divided the pension but no order was ever sent to the plan, that is a different problem. Fixing it can take a family law attorney and, at times, a court, so deal with it before retirement, not after.

## What are the pension payout choices?

| Option | Monthly check | After you die | Children receive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single life | Highest | Nothing | Nothing |
| Joint and 50% survivor | Reduced | Spouse receives half | Nothing, unless the spouse leaves it |
| Joint and 100% survivor | Lowest | Spouse keeps full amount | Nothing, unless the spouse leaves it |
| Period certain (for example 10 years) | Somewhat reduced | Continues to named beneficiary for the rest of the period | Possible, if the plan lets you name them |
| Lump sum, if offered | Paid once | Money is yours to leave | Yes, through the rolled-over account |

A lump sum, where a plan offers one, changes the picture entirely. Rolled into an IRA, the money can be left to anyone, though a spouse's rights depend on the type of account. See [retirement accounts in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/retirement-accounts-in-a-second-marriage). Taking a lump sum gives up a lifetime income that cannot run out, which is a serious trade-off for a spouse who depends on it.

## What about government pensions in Illinois and New Jersey?

Public plans are not covered by the federal rules above, so each system sets its own definition of a survivor, its own options at retirement and its own treatment of a divorce order. Teachers, police, firefighters and state workers belong to different systems, and the forms differ.

Our pages on [Illinois pension survivor benefits](/learn/illinois/illinois-pension-survivor-benefits-imrf-trs-surs-sers) and [New Jersey pension survivor benefits](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-pension-survivor-benefits-pers-tpaf-pfrs) cover the main systems. Read the plan's own member handbook before you rely on any summary, ours included, because eligibility often depends on when you married and how long.

## Will remarriage affect Social Security survivor benefits?

It can, depending on your age. A surviving spouse who remarries before age 60 generally loses the right to survivor benefits on the first spouse's record while the new marriage lasts. At 60 or later, remarriage does not stop them. Those rules come from the Social Security Administration (ssa.gov), and there are separate ones for disabled survivors.

Example only. Rosa, 58, is a widow collecting nothing yet, planning to marry Frank. If she waits two years, she keeps the option of claiming survivor benefits from her late husband's record. For some people that difference is worth more than the timing of a wedding. The same caution applies to some pensions that stop paying a survivor benefit on remarriage, so ask before you set a date.

## How do you protect children when the pension goes to a spouse?

You use the assets the pension does not control. There are four standard tools.

1. **Life insurance.** A policy owned by you and payable to your children replaces what the pension will not leave them. Our page on [life insurance in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/life-insurance-in-a-blended-family) shows how to size it.
2. **The house and savings.** Leave these in a trust or by will to your children, with your spouse protected by a [life estate](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse) or a trust. See [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage).
3. **A negotiated waiver.** If your spouse has strong income of their own, they may agree to waive, and you may agree to something in return in a written agreement.
4. **A premarital or postnuptial agreement.** See [prenups and your estate plan](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans). A waiver of pension rights signed before the wedding usually does not count, so the spouse must sign again after the marriage.

## When should you ask these questions?

Early, and in writing. Request the summary plan description, any QDRO on file, and a benefit estimate for each payout option at least six months before your planned retirement date. Plans send election forms with a short deadline, and a spouse who is surprised by a waiver request at the kitchen table will sign nothing or sign without understanding it.

Put the numbers side by side. In Hassan's case, giving up the survivor benefit might raise his check by a few hundred dollars a month. Over twenty years that is real money, but Linda could lose a lifetime income she may need in her eighties. A policy bought with part of the extra monthly amount can cover that gap and leave his sons something besides. Many couples find that the waiver, plus insurance, beats either extreme.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the single-life option without a spouse's signed waiver, then finding out the plan will not honor it.
- Assuming the pension counts as part of the estate that the will divides.
- Forgetting an old QDRO.
- Reading only the summary, not the plan's actual election form, before the retirement date.
- Treating the lump sum as free money. It is taxable when not rolled over.

Once a pension begins, the election is usually permanent, and plans rarely allow changes if you remarry later. That makes the choice at retirement one of the few irreversible decisions in a second-marriage plan.
