A QTIP trust solves a specific problem in second marriages: how to provide for a spouse without giving them the power to disinherit your children. If you have been told you need one, or you are trying to figure out whether you do, this page explains how it works, what it costs, and where it can go wrong.

It is one of several tools covered in our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families), and it is usually paired with the planning steps in [how to make sure children from a first marriage inherit](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage).

## What is a QTIP trust?

A QTIP trust is an irrevocable trust, created at your death, that provides for your surviving spouse for life and then distributes the remainder to the people you named. The letters stand for qualified terminable interest property. The name refers to the spouse's interest: it is real, but it ends when the spouse dies.

During the spouse's life, the trustee manages the assets and pays out income. The surviving spouse does not own the property and cannot give it away, sell it for their own purposes, or leave it by their own will. At the spouse's death, the remaining assets go exactly as you specified.

Think of it as a promise written in advance. You promise your spouse support, and you promise your children the rest, and a trustee holds both promises.

## How does a QTIP trust work, step by step?

1. **You sign the trust.** The QTIP provisions can be a section inside your [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) or a separate trust created by your will. Before your death, you can change it freely.
2. **You die.** The part of the trust designated for your spouse becomes irrevocable. The trustee takes over.
3. **The trustee funds the QTIP.** Assets are transferred to the QTIP share, and a federal estate tax return (Form 706) is filed if the QTIP election is made. A return may be filed even when no tax is due, to make the election and sometimes to elect portability.
4. **The trustee manages and distributes.** At least once a year, all net income goes to your spouse. Principal may be paid as the trust allows.
5. **Your spouse dies.** The remaining trust property passes to your remainder beneficiaries, often your children, in the shares you chose.

## What does the law require?

To qualify for the marital deduction, which defers estate tax, the trust must meet several requirements:

- Your spouse must be entitled to all income from the trust for life, payable at least annually.
- No one can appoint trust property to anyone other than your spouse during your spouse's life.
- The property must pass from you, and your spouse must be a US citizen (or the trust must use a special structure called a QDOT).
- Your executor must make the QTIP election on the estate tax return.

If any of these fails, the trust still exists, but the tax deferral may be lost.

## Who pays the tax, and does a QTIP still matter at $15 million?

For federal purposes, there is no tax at the first death because of the [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction). The assets are then counted as part of your surviving spouse's estate when they die. As of 2026, the federal exclusion is $15 million per person, so most families will owe nothing either way. See the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

That means the QTIP's tax function matters less than it once did, and its control function matters more. The trust is chosen because you want your children to inherit, not because you are trying to save tax. Estates close to or over the exemption, or in states with their own estate tax and a lower threshold, may still benefit from careful use of the election. See [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) for the way spouses combine their exclusions, and state estate and inheritance taxes for state rules.

One benefit applies at the second death: because QTIP assets are included in the survivor's estate, beneficiaries generally receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) to market value, which can erase income tax on gains.

## What is the difference between QTIP, marital and credit shelter trusts?

| Trust | Spouse's rights | Who decides the remainder | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| QTIP trust | All income for life; principal as allowed | First spouse to die | Second marriages, control |
| General power of appointment marital trust | All income plus power to direct the remainder | Surviving spouse | First marriages with full trust |
| Credit shelter (bypass) trust | Benefits as the trust allows | First spouse to die | Using the first spouse's exclusion |
| Outright gift to spouse | Full ownership | Surviving spouse | Simple estates, high trust |

Some plans combine these. A larger estate may place part in a bypass trust and part in a QTIP, each with different terms.

## What decisions matter most when drafting?

**The trustee.** If the surviving spouse is also trustee, the children may worry about the principal. If a child is trustee, the spouse may feel overseen. Many second-marriage plans name a professional trustee or an independent individual. Our [guide to choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) covers the options.

**The standard for principal.** "Health, education, maintenance and support" is a common standard, and it is looser than "emergencies only." Tighter language protects the remainder, looser language protects the spouse. Draft toward whichever you care about more, with the likely cost of the surviving spouse's later years in mind.

**The home.** A QTIP can hold the family home and let the spouse live there. Decide who pays property taxes, insurance and repairs, and what happens if the spouse moves. See [who gets the house in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage).

**Income-producing property.** The spouse is entitled to income. A trust holding only non-income property, such as undeveloped land, can create conflict unless the trustee has authority to convert it or pay an equivalent amount.

**Retirement accounts.** Naming a QTIP as the beneficiary of an IRA or 401(k) lets you control the remainder, but the trust must be drafted to qualify as a see-through trust. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

## What are the downsides?

- **Cost and complexity.** A QTIP trust means drafting fees now and administration later: a trustee, tax filings and records.
- **Less flexibility for your spouse.** Your spouse receives income but not control. Some spouses feel that as a lack of trust, so talk it through first.
- **Possible friction.** Your spouse wants more income and your children want to preserve principal. A neutral trustee and clear language help.
- **Rules change.** Estate tax law has changed several times, and the plan should be reviewed periodically.

## Is a QTIP trust right for you?

It is often the right fit when you have children from a prior relationship, your spouse does not have the same children, and you want assurance that they will receive the remainder. It is less necessary if your spouse is also the parent of every beneficiary, or if your assets are modest and a simpler plan works. Our guide to [prenups and postnups](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans) explains how a written agreement can support or replace some of the same goals.
