You remarried. You had a will, or you meant to get one, and life moved on. Now you wonder what would really happen if you died tomorrow: would your new spouse get everything, would your kids from your first marriage get anything, and does the old will even count?

The answers depend on your state and on how your accounts are titled. This page covers the two cases, an old will and no will, shows where the surprises hide, and ends with a plain look at whether a blended family needs a trust.

## The short version

- An old will usually stays valid after remarriage. It does not update itself.
- With no will, state law divides your property, and in a blended family that often means your spouse and your children share it.
- Beneficiary forms, joint accounts and a house held jointly bypass both the will and state law.
- A new spouse may have rights the will cannot override.
- Updating takes a few documents and a few forms, and it is cheaper than the dispute that follows if you don't.

## Does remarriage cancel an existing will?

In most states, no. Marriage by itself does not revoke a will. Divorce is different: most states treat your ex-spouse as if they had died before you for the will's gifts, so an ex named in the will drops out. But nothing in the law says "new spouse added." The will is read exactly as written.

That produces two odd outcomes, both common.

- **The will names an ex-spouse and leaves nothing to the new one.** The ex's gifts usually fail, the backup beneficiaries take, and the new spouse is left to claim whatever the state gives a spouse.
- **The will leaves everything to the children and says nothing about the spouse.** The will is valid, but many states let a spouse who married later claim a share, either as an omitted spouse or through the [elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse). Your children get what is left.

Neither result is what most people mean to happen. If your last will was signed before your second marriage, treat it as out of date. Our page on [dying with an outdated will](/learn/what-if/outdated-will) shows how that plays out.

## What if you have no will at all?

State law then writes the will for you, through [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession). In a blended family the rule depends on whose children survive you.

| Your family | What many states do |
|---|---|
| Spouse, and all your children are also your spouse's | Spouse often takes everything. |
| Spouse, and at least one child from a prior relationship | Spouse takes a share, often a set amount plus part of the rest, and the children split the remainder. |
| Spouse, no children | Spouse often takes all or most, with parents sometimes sharing. |
| No spouse, children from two marriages | Children share equally. |
| Stepchildren only | Stepchildren generally inherit nothing. |

Those are patterns, not promises. Each state sets its own fractions, and a few states, including community property states, change the picture. Read [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for the general rules, and the [stepchildren guide](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) for why a stepchild who lived in your home for 20 years may still receive nothing.

### Example: Gail's house and two sets of children

Gail, 63, owned a paid-off home worth about $380,000 and a $150,000 IRA. Her first husband had died. She married Tom, who had a daughter, and they moved into Gail's house. Gail's son, Eli, assumed the house would be his someday. She never changed her will, a short one from 2009 that left everything to Eli. When Gail died, the will was valid. But Tom, who was left out, claimed his statutory share of the estate, and Tom's rights to stay in the home complicated a sale. Eli received the IRA, because the 2009 beneficiary form still named him, and a smaller part of the estate than he expected.

A few hours of planning years earlier would have kept the house available for Tom to live in and still gone to Eli.

## Which assets ignore the will and state law?

Check these before relying on anything above.

1. **Retirement accounts.** The form names the beneficiary. For many employer plans, federal law makes the spouse the default unless they sign a waiver. See [retirement accounts in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/retirement-accounts-in-a-second-marriage).
2. **Life insurance.** Same rule, and an old form may still name the ex.
3. **Joint accounts and property.** Whoever survives owns it, so a house put in both names passes to the second spouse, not the first spouse's children.
4. **Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death registrations.**
5. **Trust assets.** The trust document controls.

For many blended families, these assets make up most of what is inherited. A will that nobody coordinated with them is a decoration.

## Should a blended family have a trust?

You do not need one, but it deserves serious thought if any of these are true.

- You want your spouse to have income or a home for life, with the rest going to your children.
- You worry the survivor will change the plan.
- The family home is the main asset.
- Your children and your spouse are close in age.

A will can name your children as beneficiaries, but it hands assets to the spouse outright if you give them anything. A trust lets you give the spouse use of the assets for life and name who receives what is left, with a trustee who answers to the document. Options include a [QTIP trust](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts), a [life estate](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse) in the home, and separate trusts for each spouse, compared in [joint vs. separate trusts](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage). A trust costs more than a will and requires retitling assets. If your estate is simple and your spouse and children get along, a will with the right beneficiary forms may be enough.

## What to do now

1. **List everything you own** and how it is titled: your name, joint, in trust, or with a beneficiary.
2. **Write down who you want to receive each item,** and what your spouse needs to live on.
3. **Decide who serves** as executor, trustee and agent. A child, a spouse and a neutral person each carry trade-offs.
4. **Sign a new will** (and trust, if used) that names your spouse and children as you intend.
5. **Change every beneficiary form** to match.
6. **Review a prenup or postnup,** because it may limit what you can leave. See [prenups and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).
7. **Tell your family the outline** of the plan.

## How state law changes this

Rules differ on whether marriage affects an old will, how much a spouse takes with no will, how large an elective share is, and whether a spouse can waive rights in a prenup. Community property states treat the marital estate differently. You can read the guide for [your state](/estate-planning/california) as one example, and our [plan finder](/plan-finder) can point to the right next step.
