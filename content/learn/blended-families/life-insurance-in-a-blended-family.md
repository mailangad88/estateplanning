You own a house with your second spouse. You want your wife to stay in it for as long as she lives. You also want your two sons to receive what the house is worth. Both wishes cannot be satisfied by the house alone. Life insurance is often the missing piece, because it turns an illiquid problem into cash.

This page explains how blended families use life insurance, who to name, when to put a trust in the middle, and the errors that leave the wrong person with the check. It is part of our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## Why is life insurance useful in a blended family?

Life insurance lets you leave two groups something of equal value without forcing either to share a single asset. In a first-marriage family, the survivor usually inherits everything and passes it to the shared children. In a blended family, the survivor's children may be different, so the trade-off between the spouse and the children is real.

A policy solves three problems at once:

- **It equalizes.** Your spouse keeps the house or the business, and your children receive an insurance payout in a similar amount.
- **It is fast.** Proceeds usually arrive within weeks of the claim, outside probate. Your children do not wait for the house to sell.
- **It is private and clear.** The beneficiary is on the form, so there is little room for argument about intent.

The cost is the premium, and it rises with age and poor health. For that reason, people often buy a policy in their fifties or early sixties, while it is still affordable, or use an existing policy.

## Who should you name as beneficiary?

You should name the people you want paid, in the order and shares you want, using their full legal names. The form overrides your will, which is why the choice has to be deliberate. Our page on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) explains why the will is irrelevant here.

| Goal | Typical naming | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse stays in the house, children receive its value later | Children named on a policy equal to the home's equity | Spouse may need cash for upkeep and taxes |
| Spouse has income for life, children get the rest | Policy payable to a trust for the spouse, remainder to children | Needs a trustee and careful drafting |
| Cover the mortgage if you die | Spouse named on a term policy | Does not help children at all |
| Support young children from a first marriage | Children's trust named, adult trustee | Naming a minor directly forces court involvement |
| Equalize between biological and stepchildren | Each child named for a stated share | Update when anyone is born or dies |

Always name contingent beneficiaries. If your only named beneficiary dies before you, the proceeds may default to your estate, which means probate and creditor claims. See [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and our page on [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

## How do you size a policy for a blended family?

Size the policy by the gap you want to close, not by a rule of thumb. Here is a simple way to work it out.

1. Write down what your spouse will receive if you die first: the house, joint accounts, retirement accounts, a survivor pension.
2. Write down what your children will receive from the same event.
3. Decide what you consider fair. Equal dollars, a smaller share for stepchildren, or each parent providing for their own children.
4. Subtract. The difference is the amount a policy needs to cover.
5. Check the premium against your budget, and compare a term policy for the years of greatest need with a permanent policy for a lifetime need.

Our [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) can help with the arithmetic. If your children are young, add the cost of raising them to the number, and read about [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) before choosing how it is held.

## Should a trust own or receive the policy?

A trust is worth considering when the amount is large, the children are young, or you do not trust the family to manage the money well. There are two ways to use one.

**A trust as beneficiary.** You keep ownership of the policy, and name a trust as the beneficiary. The trustee holds and distributes the money under your rules. This is flexible, and a revocable or testamentary trust is enough. It does not remove the policy from your taxable estate.

**An irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT).** The trust owns the policy from the start. Because you do not own it, the proceeds usually fall outside your taxable estate, and the trust document controls who receives them. Transferring an existing policy to an ILIT generally starts a three-year clock during which the proceeds are still pulled back into your estate if you die. Our page on [irrevocable life insurance trusts](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) explains the mechanics.

With the federal estate tax basic exclusion at $15 million per person as of 2026, most blended families do not need an ILIT to avoid tax. The reason to use one is control: it can protect proceeds from a child's creditors or divorce, and it can say that a stepchild shares equally with a biological child.

## What about taxes?

Life insurance proceeds are generally not taxed as income to the beneficiary, which makes them useful for equalizing against taxable retirement accounts. If you own the policy at death, however, the proceeds count toward your estate for federal estate tax purposes, and a few states tax estates well below the federal level. Use the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) to see whether your state is one of them.

A good comparison: a $250,000 policy paid to a child is worth $250,000. A $250,000 traditional IRA paid to the same child is worth less after income tax. If you are trying to treat children equally, count the after-tax value.

## A worked example

Carlos, 60, and Beth, 57, married five years ago. Carlos has a daughter, Ana, and Beth has a son, Luke. They own a house worth $520,000 with a $180,000 mortgage, and Carlos has a $300,000 401(k). Carlos wants Beth to stay in the house for life and Ana to receive her inheritance.

Carlos buys a 20-year term policy with a $350,000 death benefit and names a trust for Ana as beneficiary. His brother is the trustee. A second, smaller policy of $180,000 pays off the mortgage and names Beth. The 401(k) is split, with Beth consenting in writing to a share for Ana.

If Carlos had not bought the policy, the plan would have forced a choice between selling the house and shortchanging Ana. Because the insurance pays cash, nobody has to be forced out of anything.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the old form in place.** A policy bought during the first marriage may still name the first spouse. A divorce does not remove them from every form. See [what happens if your ex-spouse is still your beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).
- **Naming "my children" without names.** Stepchildren, half-siblings and later births complicate the class. Name people.
- **Forgetting a divorce agreement.** A prior decree may require you to keep a policy for the former spouse or children of that marriage.
- **Naming the estate.** This exposes the proceeds to probate and debts.
- **Not telling anyone.** Beneficiaries cannot claim a policy they do not know exists. Keep a list with your documents.
