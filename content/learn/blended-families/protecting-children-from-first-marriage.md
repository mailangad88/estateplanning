You remarried, and you love your spouse. You also have children from your first marriage, and you want to know they will not be left out if you die first. Both wishes are reasonable, and the law does not protect them automatically.

This page explains why the default rules put children at risk, the planning tools that fix it, and the mistakes that quietly undo a good plan. It is the starting point for the rest of our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## Why do children from a first marriage lose out by default?

Children lose out because almost every default rule favors the surviving spouse, and a spouse can pass what they receive to anyone. Three things combine to cause this.

**Intestacy favors the spouse.** If you die without a will, state law divides your estate. Many states give a spouse everything when all of your children are also the spouse's children. When some children are from another relationship, many states reduce the spouse's share and split the rest with the children, but the numbers vary a great deal. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for the general rules.

**Joint ownership skips your will.** A house or bank account titled as joint tenancy with right of survivorship goes straight to the surviving owner. Your will, however carefully written, never touches it.

**Beneficiary forms control accounts.** A 401(k), IRA or life insurance policy goes to the person named on the form. Many people name a new spouse at the wedding and never think about it again. For a 401(k) covered by federal law, the spouse is the required beneficiary unless they sign a written waiver.

Once your spouse owns an asset outright, your wishes end. If your spouse later remarries, changes their will, or simply leaves everything to their own children, your children have no claim.

Consider David, 63, who has two adult children from his first marriage. He and his wife Karen own their house jointly, his will leaves everything to her, and his IRA names her. When David dies, Karen owns everything. Five years later she leaves it all to her own daughter. David's children inherit nothing, though nobody did anything wrong.

## What tools protect both your spouse and your children?

The core tools are a trust that supports your spouse for life and then passes to your children, direct designations for specific assets, and life insurance. Most plans use a mix.

### A trust with your spouse as lifetime beneficiary

You leave some or all of your assets in trust. The trustee pays income, and sometimes principal, to your spouse for life. When your spouse dies, what remains goes to your children. Your spouse is supported, and you decide who gets the rest.

This arrangement is often built as a [QTIP or marital trust](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts), which also defers federal estate tax. It can be written into a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) that becomes irrevocable at your death, or into a will as a testamentary trust. A living trust avoids a court-supervised process for those assets. Our guide to [wills versus trusts](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) compares the two.

### Direct gifts to children now

You do not have to put everything in trust. Some people leave a defined sum or a specific asset to the children immediately and give the spouse the rest. This works when the estate is large enough to cover both, and it spares children a long wait.

### Life insurance

A policy that names your children as beneficiaries pays them directly and quickly. Used well, it lets you leave your spouse the house and retirement savings while still giving your children a meaningful inheritance. See [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) for how to name them correctly, especially when a child is a minor.

### Retitling assets and updating forms

The plan has to line up with how assets are owned. This is the step people skip. A trust can say your children inherit, but if the brokerage account is jointly owned with your spouse, it passes to your spouse and the trust never sees it.

## How do the main options compare?

| Approach | Spouse gets | Children get | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everything to spouse outright | Full control | Whatever spouse decides | Children can be cut out entirely |
| Everything to children outright | Nothing, unless other assets | Everything now | Spouse may lose housing and income; spouse's legal rights may override |
| Trust for spouse, remainder to children | Income, sometimes principal, for life | What remains at spouse's death | Conflict over spending; trustee choice matters |
| Split: some to spouse outright, some to children | Control of their share | Their share now | Needs enough assets; spouse's share is unprotected |
| Life insurance to children | Everything else | Death benefit | Premium cost; policy must stay in force |

No option is right for everyone. A couple with a modest estate and one house has different needs than a couple with several properties and a business.

## What can a surviving spouse claim regardless of your plan?

In most states a surviving spouse has minimum rights that your plan cannot simply override. If you leave your spouse less than the minimum, they can usually choose the legal minimum instead.

In most states outside the community property group, this is called the [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) or forced share. Depending on the state, it can be a fixed fraction of the estate or a sliding amount tied to the length of the marriage, and some states count trusts and joint accounts when calculating it. Community property states, including Texas and California, treat most property earned during the marriage as owned half by each spouse, so the starting point is different. See our pages on Texas and California for how those states handle it.

A spouse can give up these rights in writing. That is the purpose of many [prenuptial and postnuptial agreements](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans). If you did not sign one, plan around the minimum, because a plan built to disinherit a spouse is likely to be challenged.

## How do you carry this out? A step-by-step list

1. **List every asset and how it is titled.** Home, bank accounts, brokerage accounts, retirement plans, life insurance, business interests, vehicles. Note who is named as owner or beneficiary today.
2. **Decide what each person should receive.** Be specific about housing, income, and the final division. A written goal such as "Karen lives in the house for life, and the house then goes to my two children equally" is easier to draft than a general wish.
3. **Choose the structure.** Most families end up with a trust for the spouse's benefit, plus direct gifts or insurance for children.
4. **Pick a trustee.** Think about this carefully, as the next section explains.
5. **Retitle and redesignate.** Change deeds, account titles and beneficiary forms so they follow the plan. Ask your spouse to sign any waiver a retirement plan requires.
6. **Tell the key people.** Your spouse and children should hear the outline from you rather than discover it at a funeral.
7. **Review after any life change.** See when to update your estate plan.

## Who should serve as trustee?

Your trustee decides how much your spouse receives and protects what is left for your children, so the person should not be the one most likely to benefit or lose. If your spouse serves as sole trustee, your children may worry that the principal is being spent. If an adult child serves, your spouse may feel supervised by a stepchild.

Common solutions include a bank or trust company, a trusted friend or relative who is not in the family dispute, or co-trustees where a spouse and a child both must agree on major decisions. Whatever you choose, write clear standards: whether the trustee may spend principal for the spouse's health or comfort, how often the trustee reports to the children, and what happens if the trustee cannot serve. Our guide to [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) goes through the tradeoffs, and the successor trustee definition explains who steps in.

## What mistakes undo the plan?

- **Leaving a joint account untouched.** It goes to the survivor no matter what the trust says.
- **Forgetting the retirement account.** A named spouse inherits it outright and can name new beneficiaries. See [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).
- **Vague language.** "To my family" invites argument. Name people and say how shares are divided.
- **Not keeping the plan current.** An old will naming an ex-spouse may be partly revoked by divorce, but a will that ignores a new marriage can lead to surprises. See estate planning after divorce and estate planning for newlyweds.
