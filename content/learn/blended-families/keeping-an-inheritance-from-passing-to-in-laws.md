Rosa, 72, leaves her house and $400,000 to her only son, Daniel, outright. Daniel is 44 and has been married to Kyle for six years. Three years after Rosa dies, Daniel is killed in a car accident. He had no will. Under Illinois rules, everything Daniel owned, including what he got from Rosa, goes to Kyle. Rosa's sister, who had planned to leave her own estate to Daniel's future children, has no claim on any of it.

That is how most money ends up with in-laws: not through a bad plan, but through no plan beyond the first hand-off. This page explains the two ways it happens, when a child dies before their spouse and when your own spouse leaves your money to their own family, and what actually prevents it. Part of our [blended family planning guide](/learn/blended-families). This is general education, not legal advice.

## What happens to a child's inheritance if they die before their spouse?

Whatever your child owns when they die passes under their own will or, with no will, under state intestacy law. The default order favors the surviving spouse. Your money, once handed over outright, loses its connection to you.

| Child's situation at death | Illinois default | Who is left out |
|---|---|---|
| Married, no children | Surviving spouse takes everything | Parents, siblings |
| Married, with children | Spouse takes half, children split half | Parents, siblings |
| Unmarried, no children | Parents, then siblings | The partner |

New Jersey's formula is different in detail, with a fixed dollar amount for the spouse in some cases, but the pattern is the same: the spouse comes first, and a spouse with no shared children often takes the whole estate. The rules look reasonable until you ask where the money came from. If it came from you and you meant it for your child's own children, the spouse's share quietly overrides that.

The page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) has the full order.

## Can a child's will fix this?

Only if your child makes one, and only partly. A will can leave your child's property to their own children, siblings or you, but the spouse can often claim a statutory share instead, as we describe in the article on the [spouse's elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse). A married person cannot easily cut a spouse out entirely. Asking an adult child to rely on their own will is a weak plan, because most thirty-year-olds do not have one.

You have more control at the moment of your own gift.

## How do you stop the money from leaving your family?

Leave it in trust instead of outright. This is the central idea, and everything else is detail.

1. **Choose a trust, not a check.** A trust for your child holds the money and pays them what you decide: income, support for health and education, or a lump sum at a set age.
2. **Name the final beneficiaries.** Say who receives what remains when your child dies, such as the grandchildren, your other children or a charity. Without this, the money passes through your child's estate and the spouse gets first claim again.
3. **Pick a trustee.** If your child is the only trustee, they can usually spend and retitle the money. A sibling, a bank or an independent person keeps the structure intact.
4. **Fund it.** The trust works only if the assets are actually in it or it is named as beneficiary of the account.
5. **Check the forms.** An IRA or life insurance policy with your child named directly will skip the trust. Beneficiary forms are where this plan most often breaks.

Our article on [protecting an inheritance from a child's divorce, addiction or bad spouse](/learn/trusts/keeping-an-inheritance-safe-from-a-childs-divorce-addiction-or-bad-spouse) covers how such a trust is drafted, and the same language protects against in-laws after a death. Assets a child never owned outright are generally outside the child's estate and outside the reach of a spouse's claim, which is why the trust has to come before the money does.

## What if your spouse leaves your money to their family?

You have the same problem in a second marriage, only more directly. Say you leave your house to your wife. She now owns it outright. She can sell it, spend the proceeds, or leave it to her own children from a first marriage, and yours may receive nothing. The law treats this as her choice, not a failure of the system.

Three tools address that:

- **A QTIP or marital trust.** Your spouse receives income for life, you decide who gets the remainder. Our page on [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) explains how it works.
- **A life estate.** Your spouse lives in the house for life, and it passes to your children on death. See [life estate for a surviving spouse](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse).
- **Separate trusts for each spouse.** Each partner's money goes to their own children after the survivor's lifetime. This is compared in [joint versus separate trusts for remarriage](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage).

If you want your spouse to be secure but not to control where the money lands, the trust is the only structure that reliably does both.

## What about a spouse who remarries?

A widow who inherits outright and remarries has now put your money in play for a new spouse's rights. In many cases she can add her new husband to the deed without anyone knowing, and what was yours becomes joint property that passes to him. The what-if article on [a surviving spouse who remarries](/learn/what-if/surviving-spouse-remarries-after-you-die) shows how that unfolds. A trust that ends or changes when she remarries is the usual remedy, though some people prefer one that does not, so that she is not punished for forming a new family.

## Does an inheritance stay separate in a marriage?

Usually, if you keep it that way. In both Illinois and New Jersey, property you receive by inheritance is generally yours alone in a divorce, and the court begins from that. The word "generally" is doing work, because the protection weakens when you deposit the money into a joint account, use it to improve a jointly owned home or retitle assets into both names. The rules for that are in [keeping an inheritance separate](/learn/blended-families/keeping-an-inheritance-separate-in-a-blended-family).

That is a rule about divorce. At death, a surviving spouse has separate rights, discussed above, that do not care whether the money came from an inheritance.

## A short checklist

Use this list for each person you intend to leave money to.

- Is this person likely to marry, or already married to someone you would not choose?
- Do you want their spouse to benefit if they die first?
- Is the gift going outright, or into a trust?
- Who is the trustee, and can the beneficiary override them?
- Who receives what remains after the beneficiary dies?
- Do the deed, IRAs, life insurance and bank accounts name the trust or the person?

You can reach many of these answers in a single meeting with an attorney. Seeing which accounts are going to whom helps, and the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you list them.

## Is a trust always worth the extra step?

Not always. A modest bank account to a thirty-year-old you trust rarely justifies a trust. A house, a family business or a sum that took you a lifetime to build probably does, particularly when there are children from more than one marriage. The cost of a trust is time and trustee fees. The cost of skipping it can be your family's entire inheritance routed through someone you never met. To see how this compares with other tools, look at [will versus trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust).
