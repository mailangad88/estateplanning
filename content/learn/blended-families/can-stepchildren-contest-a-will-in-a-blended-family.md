A husband dies and leaves his house and savings to his second wife. His two sons from his first marriage are furious. His wife's daughter, who lived with them for fifteen years, is hurt in a different way, because she got a watch and nothing else. Which of them can take the will to court, and would any of them win?

The answer turns on standing, meaning a legal stake in the outcome, and on grounds, meaning a recognized legal defect in the document. This page explains both for a blended family, then shows the steps that make a plan hard to knock down. For the broader picture, start with our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## Who has standing to contest a will in a blended family?

Only an "interested person" can contest a will, and that term is narrower than "someone who is upset." In general an interested person is an heir, or a beneficiary named in the current will or in an earlier one, whose share would change if the will fell.

Apply that to the usual cast:

| Person | Heir by default? | Usually has standing? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Child from a first marriage | Yes | Yes | An heir, and would inherit if the will failed |
| Surviving second spouse | Yes | Yes | An heir, and may hold elective share rights |
| Stepchild, not adopted | No | Only if named in a will | Intestacy law skips stepchildren |
| Stepchild named in an earlier will | No | Often yes | Would take under the earlier will if the new one fell |
| Half-sibling | Yes, as a blood relative | Yes | Counts as a sibling for intestacy in most states |
| Stepchild's own child | No | No | Two steps removed |

The second to last row is where plans come apart. Suppose Walter signed a will in 2019 that gave a third of his estate to his stepdaughter Carla. In 2025, after a quarrel, he signed a new will that cut her out. If the 2025 will were thrown out, the 2019 will would come back, and Carla would take a third. That gives her standing. A stepchild who was never in any will has nothing to gain and is typically turned away at the door. You can read why in our piece on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance).

## What grounds actually win?

A court asks whether the document is your valid will. It does not ask whether it is fair. The grounds that matter are these.

1. **Lack of capacity.** You did not understand what you owned, who your family was, or what the document did on the day you signed.
2. **Undue influence.** Someone replaced your wishes with their own. Courts look hardest when the person who benefited was close to you, helped arrange the signing and kept others away. A new spouse in that position draws scrutiny. Our article on [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) explains the presumption many states apply.
3. **Fraud or forgery.** You were tricked about what you signed, or the signature is not yours.
4. **A signing defect.** Missing witnesses, a missing page or an unsigned document. See [will signing defects](/learn/wills/will-signing-defects-witnesses-dates-and-missing-pages).

Leaving your children less than your spouse is not a ground. Neither is a cold tone or an old promise that never reached paper. A father can say "you will get the house" for years and then leave it to his wife. Unless the promise was put in a binding contract, the will controls.

## Can my children from a first marriage sue my second spouse?

Yes, but what they sue about decides whether it goes anywhere. The common claims are:

- **A will or trust contest** on the grounds above.
- **Breach of fiduciary duty**, if the spouse was trustee or agent under a power of attorney and moved money for their own benefit. This one is real and increasingly common. Our guide on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) covers the warning signs.
- **Enforcing a binding agreement.** If the couple signed a contract to leave property in a certain way, or the children are remainder beneficiaries of an irrevocable trust, the spouse cannot simply ignore it. Mutual wills can work this way in some states; read [joint will versus mutual will](/learn/wills/joint-will-vs-mutual-will) before relying on one.
- **Claims about the spouse's own rights.** The spouse may have an elective share, and the children may argue about what counts toward it. See [the spouse's elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse).

What the children cannot do is overturn a valid will because the second spouse inherited "too much." If the first parent wanted the spouse to take everything, that is a permitted choice. The better time to protect children is while the parent is alive, with a trust. Our article on [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) shows how.

## How long do people have to file in Illinois and New Jersey?

Short deadlines apply, and they run from probate, not from death.

In Illinois, an interested person must file a petition to contest the will within 6 months after the will is admitted to probate (755 ILCS 5/8-1). Failure to give someone notice does not extend that period. The mechanics are in our [Illinois six-month will contest article](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline).

In New Jersey, a person who expects to object can file a caveat with the county Surrogate to pause the executor's appointment. After probate, a complaint generally must be filed in Superior Court within four months (R. 4:85-1). Our [New Jersey will contest guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats) covers caveats and undue influence.

Trusts run on separate clocks, and a trust that holds most of the assets may be the better target for a challenger. If you think you have a claim, do not wait for the family to settle down.

## How do you make your plan hard to contest?

A fight is rarely won on the facts of a single afternoon. It is won on the paper trail. These steps cost little and matter a great deal.

1. **Use your own attorney.** Not the attorney who works for your new spouse, and not a document the spouse's family arranged.
2. **Sign without a beneficiary in the room.** The spouse can drive you to the appointment but should wait outside.
3. **Write a short explanation.** Two paragraphs saying why you chose as you did, in your own words, signed and dated. It does not bind anyone, but it answers the question a judge asks first.
4. **Document capacity.** If you are in your late seventies or have had a diagnosis, ask your doctor to note your orientation and understanding near the signing date.
5. **Name the stepchildren you mean to include.** Silence invites a fight. A modest gift with a kind word often ends it.
6. **Tell your children.** They do not need to like it. They should not be surprised by it.
7. **Consider a revocable trust.** It keeps the plan private and avoids a public probate file, though it can still be challenged. See [can a living trust be contested](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested).

### A worked example

Ahmed, 74, has a $700,000 estate: a $350,000 house, $250,000 in investments and a $100,000 IRA. He is married to Nadia, 68. He has two grown sons. Nadia has a daughter, Leila, 40, who has been in the house since she was 16. Ahmed's first draft leaves everything to Nadia and says nothing else.

His attorney points out the weak spots. His sons will read the plan as being decided by Nadia, and Leila has no standing at all. They change it. The investments go into a trust for Nadia's life, then to the sons. The house goes to Nadia with a right to remain for life. Leila receives $25,000 named in the will. Ahmed signs alone with the attorney, and writes a letter to his sons about why. The sons may still be unhappy. They have nothing to contest, and every person has been named and heard.

## What if you are the one thinking of contesting?

Weigh the cost honestly. Contests are slow, public and expensive, and many end in settlement. Ask first whether you have standing and which ground you could prove. Collect the documents: earlier wills, medical records, emails around the signing and the lawyer's file. Then speak to an attorney before the deadline runs. Our guide to [what to do when you are left out of a will](/learn/after-a-death/left-out-of-the-will-what-a-child-or-relative-can-do) is a useful place to begin.
