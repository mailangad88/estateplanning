A common second-marriage plan reads like this. When I die, my assets go into a trust. My wife lives on the income and can stay in the house. When she dies, what is left goes to my children. Then comes the question that stalls many couples: who runs the trust in between?

The most natural answer is the wife. She knows the finances, she lives with the result, and no one else is as close. The trouble is that she is also the person with the most to gain from stretching the money her way. This page covers what the law lets you do, where it goes wrong, and four setups that keep the peace. It belongs to our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## Is it legal for a spouse to be both trustee and beneficiary?

Yes. A trustee can also be a beneficiary, including the sole lifetime beneficiary, as long as the trust document does not forbid it and the trustee follows the trust's terms. Courts do not treat the dual role as automatically improper. They look at how the person acted.

What the law demands is that the trustee honor several duties. The trustee must act in good faith, follow the document, keep trust property separate from personal property, and treat the beneficiaries impartially. Impartial matters here. A spouse trustee must give real weight to the children's remainder interest, not just to their own comfort. Our [trustee duties article for New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-trustee-duties-to-beneficiaries-under-the-uniform-trust-code) and the [Illinois duty to inform beneficiaries](/learn/illinois/illinois-trustee-duty-to-inform-beneficiaries) explain what each state requires.

## Where does a spouse trustee go wrong?

Most problems are not theft. They are choices that look reasonable from one chair and unfair from the other.

- **Spending principal freely.** If the trust lets the spouse draw principal "as needed," a generous reading can eat the children's share over twenty years.
- **Investing for income only.** The spouse wants income, so the trust holds bonds and high-yield stocks that never grow. Inflation then shrinks what the children will receive.
- **Favoring the house.** The spouse keeps an expensive house funded from the trust, to the exclusion of other uses.
- **Paying the spouse's own expenses.** Gifts to the spouse's relatives, trips, or help for a stepchild appear as trust spending.
- **Silence.** Not sharing statements, which breeds suspicion even when nothing is wrong.

Consider Greg and Maria, both in their seventies. Greg leaves $800,000 in a trust for Maria's life, remainder to his two daughters. Maria is sole trustee. Over eleven years she draws $40,000 a year and the trust gets no growth. When Maria dies the daughters find $360,000 and a house with deferred repairs. They suspect waste. Maria, in truth, spent what the document allowed. A tighter standard and an independent co-trustee would have ended the dispute before it started.

## How can you limit a spouse trustee's powers?

You can write the limits into the trust, and the drafting choices make the difference. These are the ones our attorneys discuss most often.

| Choice | Looser setup | Tighter setup |
|---|---|---|
| Access to principal | Any amount the spouse wants | Only for health, education, maintenance and support |
| Who decides on principal | The spouse alone | An independent trustee, or both together |
| Sale of the house | Spouse decides | Co-trustee must approve, proceeds stay in trust |
| Investments | Spouse chooses | Prudent investor standard, with a balanced mix of income and growth |
| Reports | Whenever asked | Annual statement to the children |
| Removal | Court only | Children, by majority, may replace an independent trustee |

The phrase "health, education, maintenance and support" is a recognized standard. It lets the spouse use principal for ordinary needs of life but not for luxuries or gifts. It also has tax value, because it keeps the trust property out of the spouse's own estate in a credit shelter arrangement.

## What are the four common setups?

<!-- visual: decision slug=estate-planning-for-second-marriages part=map -->

1. **Spouse alone as trustee, with tight limits.** Works when the children trust the spouse and the assets are modest. Use the health, education, maintenance and support standard and a reporting duty.
2. **Spouse and an independent co-trustee.** The spouse handles day-to-day needs, and the co-trustee must agree to principal withdrawals and to sale of the house. A trusted friend, a professional or a corporate trustee all fit. This is the setup we see most often. See [co-trustees and changing a trustee](/learn/trusts/co-trustees-and-changing-a-trustee).
3. **Independent trustee only.** The spouse is the beneficiary and asks for what they need. It removes the conflict. It can feel like a loss of control for the spouse, so tell them about it before they sign.
4. **A child as trustee, with the spouse as lifetime beneficiary.** Less common, because the child then decides how much the stepparent receives. If you choose it, set a written standard so that the child cannot cut the spouse off.

Which fits depends on the size of the estate, the temperature of the family and how well the spouse and children know each other. Bring those facts to the meeting. They matter more than the legal vocabulary.

### What about a QTIP trust?

A QTIP trust is built for exactly this plan, where the spouse receives all income for life and you decide who gets the principal after. The spouse may serve as trustee, but the document should say who replaces them and how. Because the QTIP rules require the spouse to get all income at least annually, you cannot let a co-trustee withhold income. You can, however, give a co-trustee control over principal. We walk through the mechanics in our [QTIP and marital trust guide](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts).

## What happens when the spouse can no longer serve?

A spouse trustee can become ill, remarry, or die. The document should name successors in order, and it should not let the spouse pick a successor without limits. Otherwise a new spouse can end up directing trust assets meant for your children.

Write four things down:

1. Who succeeds the spouse, and in what order.
2. That a remarriage by the spouse removes their power to appoint trustees.
3. How a trustee may be removed for cause, and by whom.
4. What happens if the spouse develops dementia. Our article on [a spouse with dementia and children from before](/learn/blended-families/when-your-spouse-has-dementia-and-you-have-children-from-before) shows why this matters.

## What do the children need to be able to do?

Remainder beneficiaries have rights, but the rights are only as strong as the document. A well-drafted trust gives children a clear route to information, a say in choosing an independent trustee, and a way to raise concerns before they harden into a lawsuit.

Ask your attorney to include an annual statement to the remainder beneficiaries, a plain statement of what counts as principal and income, and a requirement that the trustee tell the children if the house is listed for sale. None of these costs the spouse a thing when things go well.

If you are the child reading this because a stepparent is already serving, you can usually ask for a copy of the trust and a statement of its assets and receipts. If that is refused, a trust attorney can advise on the next step. Our guide on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) lists the duties that apply.
