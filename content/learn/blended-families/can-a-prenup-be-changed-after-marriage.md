Tom and Renata married four years ago. Both had been married before, and they signed a prenup that kept his rental duplex and her retirement savings in separate columns. Now Renata wants to loosen it, so that Tom keeps living in her house if she dies first. Tom wonders if the old paper can simply be set aside because they both agree.

It can, but there is a right way to do it. This page covers how to change or cancel a prenup after the wedding, what a court looks for when one is challenged, and the retirement-plan trap that catches careful couples. It sits in our [blended family planning guide](/learn/blended-families) and builds on our broader article on [prenups, postnups and your estate plan](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans). This is general education, not legal advice.

## Can you change a prenup after you are married?

Yes, if both spouses agree and sign a written amendment. Illinois and New Jersey both follow the uniform approach: a premarital agreement can be amended or revoked after the wedding only by a written agreement signed by the spouses, and the new paper does not need any payment or trade to be valid. A promise at the kitchen table is not enough, even when both people remember it the same way.

Three practical points matter here.

First, put the change in a document that refers to the original by its date and says which paragraphs change. A vague note that "we both want to be flexible" gives a judge nothing to apply.

Second, treat the amendment as a new negotiation. If one spouse now has far more information or leverage than the other, the amendment can be attacked later on the same grounds as the original.

Third, keep every signed version together. When Tom and Renata's attorney drafted the amendment, it replaced one clause on the house and left the rest alone, so the signed original and the signed amendment have to be read as a pair.

## What makes a prenup enforceable?

A prenup is enforced when it is in writing, both people signed it, the signature was voluntary and the financial picture was fairly disclosed. Courts in both states can refuse to enforce a term that was signed under pressure, or that was so one-sided at the moment of enforcement that it shocks the conscience, particularly when the weaker side was kept in the dark about assets.

| Factor a court looks at | What helps enforcement | What invites a challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Signed weeks before the wedding, with time to read and reflect | Handed over a day or two before the ceremony, with guests already invited |
| Disclosure | Lists of assets, debts and income attached to the agreement | Vague schedules, an omitted business or account |
| Lawyers | Each spouse had their own attorney | One lawyer for both, or none for the spouse who gave up more |
| Fairness | Terms a reasonable person would sign with full information | One spouse left with nothing and no way to support themselves |
| Signing | Signed by both, witnessed or notarized as the form requires | Unsigned drafts, or a version nobody can find |

One local detail: Illinois lets a court look at a waiver of spousal support if enforcing it would leave a spouse in real hardship, even when the rest of the agreement stands. New Jersey takes a similar view of terms that are unconscionable when enforcement is sought. If your prenup waives support, ask a lawyer how those rules apply to you.

## Does each spouse need their own lawyer?

Not by statute in most places, but it is a very good idea. The main protection against a later claim that "I did not understand what I was signing" is a record that each person had independent advice. A prenup is a contract between two people whose interests are opposed on the subject of the contract, and one attorney cannot advise both.

Our attorneys often see couples try to save money with one lawyer and one shared draft. It rarely saves anything. If the document is ever tested, the cost of defending it is far higher than the cost of a second attorney at the start. For the general picture of what legal work costs, see [how much estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost). Prenup fees vary by complexity and by firm, and a couple with a business and two properties will pay more than a couple with one bank account each.

## What if you need to change the prenup because your lives changed?

Some changes are routine. Common reasons couples revisit an agreement:

- A child is born and one spouse leaves work to raise the child.
- A house bought before the marriage becomes the family home.
- One spouse sells a business or inherits money.
- The couple moves from one state to another.
- One spouse develops a serious illness, and the other becomes a caregiver.

None of these cancels the prenup. They are good reasons to sit down with an attorney and decide whether the old terms still do what you meant. A postnup or amendment can add a gradual change, such as raising a spouse's share of the marital home by a set percentage each year of marriage.

Sunset terms are worth considering at this point. A clause that says certain waivers fall away after fifteen years, or when the couple has a child together, avoids a rewrite later.

## Why a prenup cannot waive a 401(k) by itself

This is the part careful couples miss. Federal law protects a spouse's right to survivor benefits in most employer retirement plans, such as a 401(k) or a pension. A spouse can give up that right, but the waiver must be signed by the spouse, in writing, with a notary or a plan representative, and it has to be signed after the wedding, because before the wedding the signer is not yet a spouse.

So a prenup that says "I waive all rights to Renata's 401(k)" does not, on its own, stop Tom from being the default beneficiary of that plan. Renata's plan will pay the account to Tom unless he signs the plan's own spousal consent form after they are married and she names someone else. Our article on [retirement accounts in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/retirement-accounts-in-a-second-marriage) goes through that form, and [whether your spouse must approve your beneficiary choices](/learn/beneficiary-designations/does-your-spouse-have-to-approve-your-beneficiary-choices) covers it from the beneficiary side.

IRAs are different. They are not covered by the same federal consent rule, so a prenup waiver may carry more weight, but the account custodian still pays whoever is named on its form. The document that controls the money is the beneficiary designation, not the prenup. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) walks you through the accounts that most often get out of step.

## How does a prenup fit with your will and trust?

A prenup says what each spouse can claim from the other. It does not distribute anything. At death, your will, trust and beneficiary forms still do that, and the prenup only limits what the surviving spouse can ask for on top of them.

The most useful checklist after signing or amending a prenup is short.

1. Reread the prenup alongside your will and ask whether each one leaves the same person the same thing.
2. Check every beneficiary form for the retirement accounts and life insurance.
3. Confirm the title on the house and any rental property matches what the prenup assumes.
4. Look at whether your spouse needs to sign a consent or a waiver, and when.
5. Put the signed prenup and all amendments in the same place as your will and tell your executor where it is.

A prenup that gives a spouse almost nothing, paired with a will that leaves that spouse everything, creates a conflict that someone will have to sort out after your death. An attorney can align them. For the spouse's statutory share at death, which a prenup can also waive, read about the [elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse).

## Can a prenup be challenged after a spouse dies?

Yes, by the surviving spouse or sometimes by the children, though the person who signed it and their lawyer are no longer there to explain. Challenges usually claim the signer did not get full disclosure, was pressured, or could not read the document. The more the file shows, such as separate attorney letters, dated drafts and attached asset lists, the harder those claims become.

If you are the spouse whose rights were limited, do not assume the agreement is final just because it exists. Bring the document and the date to an attorney soon after the death, because estate deadlines for claims are short. In Illinois, for example, a surviving spouse who wants to renounce a will has a limited window after the will is admitted, and a prenup waiver may or may not block that. Do not guess at it.

## Should you get a postnup instead?

If you are already married and never signed anything, a postnuptial agreement does the same job. It is signed after the wedding, so courts look at it more carefully, but the two-lawyer approach and full disclosure carry over. Couples often choose one after an inheritance, a business sale or a move. If you are considering one, start by listing what each of you owns and who you want to receive it, then use the [plan finder](/plan-finder) to see which documents fit.
