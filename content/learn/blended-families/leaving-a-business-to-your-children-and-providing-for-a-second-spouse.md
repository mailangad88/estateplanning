You built the company. Your children from your first marriage work in it or expect to own it someday. Your second spouse lives on its income and did not help build it. If you die and the plan is "everything to my spouse," the business can end up with her family. If the plan is "everything to the kids," she can be left without a paycheck or a home.

This page is for owners in that bind, and it sits within our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families). It explains which tools let the children keep the company, how a spouse is still provided for, and what a surviving spouse can claim no matter what you write. The general business transfer questions are in our guide to [passing a family business to your children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children). This page adds the second-marriage problem.

## How do I leave my business to my children and provide for my second spouse?

Separate the two things a business gives you: ownership and income. Give your children the ownership, and give your spouse a claim on income that ends at her death, backed by something other than the company where possible. A trust is the standard container. The business interests go into a trust that pays her for life, and the trust hands them to your children when she dies.

Her income can come from three places: distributions from the business, a fixed annuity-like payment from the trust, or life insurance that pays her directly. The more it comes from outside the business, the less she depends on how well your son runs the company.

## What are the main structures?

| Structure | Spouse gets | Children get | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trust with income to spouse for life | Distributions of income | Ownership after her death | Children wait; a poor trustee choice starts a fight |
| Voting and non-voting split | Non-voting shares or income | Voting control to the child who runs it | Needs clear dividend rules |
| Buy-sell with life insurance | Cash from the buyout | Company stays in the family | Insurance must be bought and kept up |
| Lifetime gift to children | Nothing from the business | Ownership now | Basis, gift tax filing, loss of control |
| Everything to spouse, kids trust her | Full ownership | Nothing enforceable | She can sell or leave it to anyone |

The last row is the most common and the one our attorneys warn against. Under [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage), the pattern is the same for a house or an investment account, but with a company the gap is larger because the value is tied up in one asset.

## How does a trust handle a business that has to be run?

A trust owns the interest and a trustee votes it. Pick the trustee carefully. A surviving spouse who is also the trustee can vote herself a larger payout. A child trustee can reinvest everything and pay her nothing. Many families name a neutral co-trustee, such as a trust company or an accountant who knows the business, and give the child who works there a defined role as manager but not trustee.

If the company is an S corporation, the trust has to qualify to hold the shares. That usually means an election called a QSST or an ESBT, filed on time, and the trust document has to be written with those rules in mind. A wrong trust can end the S status. Tell your attorney and your accountant that the company is an S corporation before anything is drafted.

Our article on [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) explains the type of trust that gives a spouse income for life and then sends the property to the children you choose.

## How do voting and non-voting interests help?

They let one child run the company without giving the others less value. In an LLC, you create voting units and non-voting units. In a corporation, you create voting and non-voting stock, with S corporation limits. The working child receives the voting interest. Everyone else, including a trust for your spouse, receives non-voting interests that share profits.

Here is an example, with invented numbers. Dan, 63, owns a plumbing company valued at $1,800,000. His wife Carol, 58, is not the mother of his children Mia and Ben. Mia runs the office and Ben works in the field. Dan splits ownership into 1,000 units, of which 100 carry the vote.

- The 100 voting units go to Mia and Ben equally, so they decide how the company is run.
- The 900 non-voting units go to a trust. Carol receives the income from those units for life, and Mia and Ben split the units when she dies.
- A $600,000 life insurance policy pays Carol directly, so she has cash that does not depend on the company.

The company never has to be sold to pay Carol, and the children keep the business together. Carol's income depends on distributions, so Dan's operating agreement says the company must distribute at least enough each year to cover taxes and an agreed minimum to the trust. That one sentence prevents most of the fights.

## What can a surviving spouse claim no matter what?

A statutory share. In Illinois, a spouse who is left less can renounce the will and take one-third of the estate if the decedent left descendants (755 ILCS 5/2-8), as explained in our article on [spouse rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death). New Jersey gives a surviving spouse an elective share of one-third of an augmented estate, described in our guide to the [New Jersey elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse). If the company is the biggest asset, a claim like that can force a payout from the business.

Three answers work. The spouse signs a waiver in a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement, with full financial disclosure. Or the plan gives her enough, through the trust, insurance or both, that renouncing is not attractive. Or you accept that she may claim a share and keep insurance on hand to pay it. A waiver signed without seeing the company's value tends to be challenged. See how a prenuptial agreement interacts with your plan in our guide to [prenups and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).

## How do I pay for it? The role of life insurance

Insurance converts a hard-to-split company into cash. Two uses stand out. First, a policy that pays your spouse so the business can go to the children untouched. Second, a policy that pays children who are not in the business, so the working child can own the company and the others are not shortchanged. Our guide to [life insurance in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/life-insurance-in-a-blended-family) covers the beneficiary mistakes that sink both uses.

A buy-sell agreement is the document that makes the payment automatic. When one owner dies, the company or the other owners buy the interest at a formula price. Read more in [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).

## A checklist to take to your attorney

1. A recent valuation, or a formula for one, and a list of who works in the business.
2. The entity type and the operating agreement or bylaws, including transfer limits.
3. Every beneficiary form and life policy, with current names.
4. Your marriage agreement, if any.
5. What your spouse needs each year to live, and where she would live.
6. Which child should run the company, and whether the others agree.
7. Whether your spouse has her own plan and her own children.

Bring your accountant to at least one meeting. Valuation, basis and S corporation rules sit with them as much as with us.
