You have remarried, and you want two things at once: your spouse should be comfortable if you die first, and your children from before should still end up with the bulk of what you built. An A-B trust is one of the oldest ways to do both, and it works because part of the money is locked the moment you die.

This page explains how the two halves work, what your spouse can and cannot touch, how to choose the split, and where plans go wrong. It sits in our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## What is an A-B trust in a second marriage?

It is a revocable living trust that divides into two trusts at the first death. The A trust (sometimes called the survivor's trust) holds the surviving spouse's own share and can stay fully in their control. The B trust (the bypass or credit shelter trust) holds the share of the person who died, and it becomes irrevocable. The surviving spouse benefits from B but does not own it.

That is the difference that matters to a blended family. Money that goes to a spouse outright becomes theirs, and they can leave it to anyone. Money in B follows your instructions, whatever your spouse later decides.

| | A trust | B trust |
|---|---|---|
| Holds | Survivor's own share | Share of the spouse who died first |
| Can the survivor change it? | Yes, until their own death | No |
| Who gets what is left | Whoever the survivor names | Your chosen beneficiaries, such as your children |
| Typical use of the money | Whatever the survivor wants | Support, health care, housing |

## How does the B trust pay your spouse?

The trust document sets the standard, and it should be written down in plain terms. The usual choices are these.

1. **Income only.** Your spouse receives every dollar of interest and dividends. Principal stays untouched for your children.
2. **Income plus principal for support.** The trustee may also pay principal for health, education, maintenance and support. This is the standard lawyers call HEMS, and it gives your spouse a safety net without a blank check.
3. **A fixed monthly amount.** Useful when the spouse needs predictable income and the children want predictable limits.
4. **Right to live in the house.** The B trust owns the home, your spouse lives there, and the trust pays or shares the taxes and repairs. Our page on a [life estate for a surviving spouse](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse) covers how that compares.

The tighter the standard, the more your children are protected. The looser it is, the safer your spouse feels. There is no neutral setting, and this is the conversation couples most often avoid.

## Who should be the trustee?

Not automatically the surviving spouse. If your spouse is both the person who benefits from the trust and the person who decides what the trust pays, the children depend on goodwill. That goodwill is often real. It is also exactly what disappears after a long illness, a new relationship or a family argument.

Options our attorneys see work:

- An adult child from your first marriage as sole trustee. Your spouse must ask for principal, which can strain the relationship.
- A neutral person or a bank as trustee. Costs more, reduces friction.
- Your spouse and a child as co-trustees, with the child's agreement needed for principal payments.

Read [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) before deciding, and give the trustee a written way to say no that does not sound like an accusation.

## How do you decide what goes into B?

You pick a formula. There are three common ones.

**A fixed dollar amount.** Example only: Peter, 66, and Alma, 62, have a combined estate of $1.4 million. Peter has two daughters. His trust says that at his death $600,000 of his assets go to B. The rest goes to Alma. Simple, but inflation and market changes can make the number wrong.

**A percentage.** B holds 60 percent of Peter's share. This tracks the size of the estate as it grows or shrinks.

**Specific assets.** The house and Peter's brokerage account go to B, his retirement accounts go to Alma. This avoids hard arguments about valuation and matches the asset to its purpose.

For larger estates, a formula clause can move the dividing line to use the estate tax exclusion exactly. The page on [bypass trust versus portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability) explains when that is worth the trouble. With the federal exclusion at $15,000,000 per person in 2026, most families will not owe federal estate tax, so the split is more about family than tax. New Jersey and Illinois each have a state estate tax with a lower threshold, so check the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) if your total estate is above a few million dollars.

## A worked example

Example only. Peter and Alma live in a New Jersey ranch house worth $520,000, with $380,000 in investments and $500,000 in Peter's 401(k). Peter wants Alma to stay in the house for life and wants his daughters, Dana and Joy, to receive his share after that.

His plan: the house and half of the investments (Peter's half) fund B. Alma gets income from B, and the trustee, Dana, may pay principal for Alma's medical needs. The 401(k) names Alma directly, so she can roll it over and use it for her own retirement. When Alma dies, B passes equally to Dana and Joy.

If Peter had left everything to Alma outright, she could have sold the house and changed her will. She might never have done that. The trust means nobody has to rely on her not doing it.

## What goes wrong with A-B trusts?

Most failures are not about the trust language.

- **The trust is never funded.** The deed to the house stays in Peter's name, the accounts are never retitled, and at death the assets go through probate and bypass the trust entirely. See [what funding a trust means](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
- **Beneficiary forms contradict the plan.** A retirement account naming Alma outright can swell her side, and the balance between A and B shifts.
- **Nobody tells the spouse.** Alma learns of the restrictions after Peter dies. Talk about it while both of you can.
- **Spouse and children have no practical way to reach each other.** Give the trustee a duty to send annual statements. See [trustee duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account).
- **Over-engineering a small estate.** A couple with $300,000 may do better with a simple trust or a right to live in the house.

## What does your spouse need to understand before you sign?

Three things, said out loud. First, the B trust is not a punishment. It exists because you want both families looked after, and the trustee is there to pay for real needs. Second, the rules for spending principal should be written so that nobody has to guess. A trustee who has to decide whether a new roof is "support" will make a better decision with an example in the document than without one. Third, your spouse should have their own attorney read the plan. A spouse who later says they never understood it is a spouse who may bring a claim for an elective share, and a well-documented, independent review is the best answer to that.

Also decide what happens if the trust runs short. Some couples let the trustee sell the house and buy a smaller one, with the proceeds staying inside B. Others set a floor, such as a minimum monthly payment, that the trustee must meet before any principal goes to the children. Putting that in writing is far kinder than leaving it to be argued over by the people left behind.

## Should you use a joint trust instead?

A single joint trust that becomes irrevocable at the first death can do the same job, and one document is cheaper to maintain. The risk is a drafting mistake that leaves the survivor free to amend. Our page on [joint versus separate trusts for remarriage](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage) sets out how to choose.
