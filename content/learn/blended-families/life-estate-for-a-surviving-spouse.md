A common wish in a second marriage sounds simple: my wife stays in the house as long as she lives, and when she dies, the house goes to my children. A life estate is the oldest legal tool for exactly that.

This page explains how a life estate works for a surviving spouse, how to set one up, what it costs in tax and flexibility, and when a trust does the job better. It builds on our broader page about [who gets the house in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage), which compares all the options, and sits in our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## What is a life estate?

A life estate is a way of dividing ownership of real estate over time. One person, the life tenant, has the right to use the property for their lifetime. Another person, the remainderman, owns the future interest and becomes the full owner when the life tenant dies. No court proceeding is needed to make that transfer, because the life interest simply ends.

For a blended family, the arrangement looks like this. You give your spouse a life estate in the home. Your children are the remaindermen. Your spouse lives there until death, and then the home belongs to your children outright, without probate for that property.

You can create a life estate in two ways:

- **By will.** The will says that your spouse has the right to live in the house for life and that the house then goes to your children. The will has to go through probate, and the house passes under court supervision.
- **By deed.** You sign and record a deed that gives your spouse a life estate and your children the remainder. This takes effect on your death if you retain a life estate yourself, or immediately if you give the interest away now.

## How is a life estate set up, step by step?

The mechanics are simple, but each step has a choice that matters.

1. **Decide who the life tenant is and who the remaindermen are.** Name people, not a class. "My three children" can become a dispute if one dies before you.
2. **Choose a will or a deed.** A will keeps the property in your control until death. A deed lets the property skip probate, but gives up some control now.
3. **Write the rules.** Say who pays taxes, insurance and repairs, whether the spouse can rent the home out, and what happens if the spouse moves out or remarries.
4. **Prepare the deed or will.** In most states a deed must be signed before a notary and recorded at the county recorder's office. Use the exact legal description from your current deed.
5. **Notify the lender and insurer.** A deed transfer can trigger a due-on-sale clause in some mortgages, although exceptions exist for transfers to a spouse. Tell the insurer who is covered.
6. **Keep the documents.** Give a copy to the trustee or executor and to the spouse.

## What does the life tenant have to do, and what can't they do?

The life tenant has the right to live in the home, but also duties. They generally pay property taxes, insurance and ordinary maintenance. They cannot waste the property, meaning they cannot let it fall apart or strip it of value. They cannot sell the house alone, or take out a mortgage that would outlast their life without the remainder owners' agreement.

This is where life estates cause the most friction. Priya has a life estate in the home she shared with Dev. His two sons are the remaindermen. The roof needs replacing and costs $18,000. Who pays? Many states say the life tenant pays for ordinary repairs and the remaindermen for major ones, but the rules vary and the deed may be silent. Without clear instructions, the family may end up in court over a roof.

A table of common issues:

| Issue | Life tenant | Remainderman |
|---|---|---|
| Live in the home | Yes | No, until the life tenant dies |
| Pay property taxes and insurance | Usually yes | Usually no |
| Major repairs | Depends on state and deed | Depends on state and deed |
| Rent the home | Often allowed, income to the tenant | Usually no income |
| Sell the whole property | Not alone | Not alone |
| Take out a mortgage | Not without consent | Not without consent |

## What are the tax effects of a life estate?

The main tax benefit of a life estate created at your death is the step-up in basis. Property you own at death generally receives a new tax basis equal to its value on the date of death. If you keep control and the home passes to your children at your spouse's later death, the children may owe little or no capital gains tax on a sale. See our page on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) for the rules, and talk to a tax adviser, because partial step-ups can apply when a life estate is created by gift during life.

If you give away the remainder during life, the children generally keep your original cost basis in that part of the property. A home bought for $120,000 and now worth $600,000 could create a large gain if sold. That is one reason many attorneys prefer to create the life estate at death rather than by lifetime gift.

Property tax relief, such as a homestead exemption, can also be affected by a deed. In some states a life tenant keeps the exemption, and in others it is lost or reduced. Check with the county before you record.

## When does a life estate go wrong?

A life estate has a rigid structure, and rigid structures fail in predictable ways.

- **The spouse needs to move.** A surviving spouse who needs assisted living or a smaller place cannot sell the house without the children. If the children refuse, the spouse is stuck, and if they agree, the sale proceeds must be divided according to the value of each interest.
- **A child dies, divorces or is sued.** A remainder interest can pass to the child's spouse or be reached by the child's creditors. Name contingent remaindermen and keep your documents current.
- **Medicaid planning.** A deed that transfers the remainder may be treated as a gift for Medicaid purposes and start the look-back period. See [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).
- **Neglect.** A life tenant who cannot afford upkeep may let the house run down, and the children bear the loss.
- **Family conflict.** The children may feel the spouse is in "their" house. The spouse may resent restrictions. Clear terms reduce this.

## When is a trust a better answer?

A trust with a right of residence is more flexible than a life estate, and it is generally the better choice when the home is a large share of the estate. The trustee, not the spouse and children, controls the property. The trust can allow a sale, buy a smaller home for the spouse, pay for care, and then divide the proceeds. It can also name a neutral trustee, which a life estate cannot do.

The tradeoff is cost and administration. A trust has to be drafted, funded and managed, and the trustee has duties. Our page on [leaving your house to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) compares deeds, trusts and wills, and [how to transfer a house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) explains the process.

## A worked example

Hank, 72, and Rosa, 68, married eight years ago. Hank's house, which he owned before the marriage, is worth $430,000. He has two daughters, and Rosa has a son and a home of her own that she rents out. Hank wants Rosa to live in his house as long as she wishes, and he wants his daughters to inherit it afterward.

Hank's will gives Rosa a life estate in the house. It says she pays the property taxes and insurance, the daughters pay for any repair over $5,000, Rosa may rent a room, and the life estate ends if she moves out for more than twelve months. The remainder goes to the daughters equally, with contingent beneficiaries for their children.

When Hank dies, the will goes through probate and the house is retitled with the life estate recorded. When Rosa dies years later, the daughters record a death certificate and become full owners. The planning was simple, cheap and clear. If Rosa had needed to move into a care facility at 85, however, the family would have had to agree on a sale, which is the risk Hank accepted.
