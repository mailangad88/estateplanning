Many people who remarry want one simple thing from their estate plan: their own children should end up with their assets, and the new spouse should be looked after but not hold the whole estate. Some go further and want to leave the spouse nothing at all. Before you write that plan, you need to know what the law will let you do.

This page explains the spousal protections that sit underneath every will and trust, how they work in different kinds of states, what a waiver can and cannot do, and the options that protect your children without a fight. It belongs to our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## Can you legally disinherit your spouse?

In nearly every state, no. You can write a will that leaves your spouse nothing, but the spouse can usually ask a court to override it and award a minimum share. The law treats marriage as an economic partnership, and it does not let one partner remove the other from it by signing a document in private.

Two separate systems are at work. Most states follow the common law approach and give the survivor an elective share, also called a forced share or statutory share. A smaller group of states, the community property states, start from a different idea: whatever either spouse earned during the marriage belongs to both. Our glossary entries on [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) and [community property](/glossary/community-property) define both terms.

Either way, the result is similar. Your spouse has a claim that does not depend on your goodwill.

## How does the elective share work?

The elective share lets a surviving spouse choose between two things: whatever the will and the beneficiary forms give them, or a percentage of the estate fixed by statute. The spouse files a petition within a deadline, and if the statutory number is higher, the other gifts shrink to cover the difference.

The details vary a great deal, which is why general rules mislead people. These are the points that differ from state to state:

- **The percentage.** Some states use a flat fraction of the estate. Others use a sliding scale that grows with the length of the marriage.
- **What counts.** Older statutes looked only at the probate estate. Newer ones use an augmented estate that pulls in trust assets, joint accounts, retirement plans and gifts made shortly before death, so that moving property out of your name does not defeat the claim.
- **What the spouse already received.** Property the spouse gets outside the will, such as a jointly owned house, is often credited against the share.
- **Who pays.** The shortfall is taken from the other beneficiaries, sometimes in proportion to what each would have received.

The practical lesson is that a plan built around "everything outside probate" is less safe than it looks. A state with an augmented estate can reach the revocable trust you funded to avoid the problem.

Here is a short comparison of how the three situations tend to play out.

| Situation | What the spouse can claim | Typical risk to your plan |
|---|---|---|
| Common law state, probate estate only | A percentage of probated assets | Moving assets into joint names or a trust may avoid the claim |
| Common law state, augmented estate | A percentage of a wider pool, including trusts and some gifts | Little room to shrink the pool by retitling |
| Community property state | Half of community property, plus survivor rights in the rest | You can only plan around your half |

See your own state guide under [estate planning by state](/estate-planning) for local details. Illinois, for example, has its own rules on a spouse renouncing a will, covered in our page on [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death).

## What other rights does a surviving spouse have?

A surviving spouse often has rights on top of the elective share, and these can be reached even when the will is silent. Many of them are small in dollars but large in their effect on timing.

1. **Homestead or right to remain.** Several states let the spouse stay in the family home for a period, or for life, regardless of the will.
2. **Family allowance.** A court can award living expenses to the spouse while the estate is open.
3. **Exempt property.** A fixed amount of household items, a car and cash may go to the spouse before creditors or other heirs.
4. **Pretermitted spouse share.** In many states, a spouse who married after the will was signed may receive an intestate share unless the document shows the omission was deliberate.
5. **Retirement plan protection.** Federal law requires the spouse's written consent before a 401(k) can pass to anyone else. See our page on [retirement accounts in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/retirement-accounts-in-a-second-marriage).

Together these mean that "disinheriting" a spouse is rarely clean. Even if the main claim is waived, smaller rights may remain unless the waiver names them.

## How can a spouse waive these rights?

A spouse gives up the elective share and related rights by signing a written waiver, usually inside a prenuptial agreement or a postnuptial agreement. A handshake or a promise does not count. Our page on [prenups, postnups and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans) covers the agreement itself in detail.

Courts look at the same few things when a waiver is challenged:

- Was it in writing and signed by both spouses?
- Was it voluntary, with no pressure or last-minute surprise?
- Did each spouse receive a fair and reasonable disclosure of the other's assets, or waive disclosure in writing?
- Did each spouse have the chance to consult their own lawyer?
- Was it fair when signed, and not so lopsided that it shocks the court?

Timing matters. A prenup handed over the week before the wedding is easier to attack than one signed months earlier. A postnuptial waiver, signed after the marriage, needs a good reason for the spouse to agree, and it deserves independent counsel on each side.

A waiver also does not reach everything automatically. A spouse who waives rights in the estate may still be the named beneficiary on a plan or the joint owner of a house, and those forms still control. Waive, then fix the forms so they match.

## What if your spouse will not sign a waiver?

If the spouse will not waive, the realistic goal is not disinheritance but control. You give the spouse something real and keep the final destination of the assets in your hands. Most people find this is enough, and most spouses find it acceptable.

The standard tool is a trust. After your death, assets go into a trust that pays your spouse income, and principal if needed, for life. When the spouse dies, the remaining property goes to your children. Our page on [how to make sure children from a first marriage inherit](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) explains the structure, and the page on [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) covers the tax-driven version. In some states a trust of this kind satisfies or reduces the elective share claim, and in others the spouse can still elect against it. That is a question for a local attorney, not a rule to assume.

Other approaches work in combination:

- Leave your spouse the family home through a right of residence rather than ownership.
- Fund part of the plan with life insurance payable to your children, so the estate does not need to be split. See [life insurance in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/life-insurance-in-a-blended-family).
- Give the spouse a defined sum or percentage in exchange for a waiver of the rest.

## Worked example

Daniel, 67, and Priya, 61, married six years ago. Each has adult children from a first marriage. Daniel owns a rental house and an investment account worth about $900,000, mostly in his own name, and he wants it all to go to his two daughters. His will leaves Priya nothing, because she has her own pension and a home.

When Daniel dies, Priya consults an attorney. Their state allows an elective share, measured against an augmented estate, and the marriage is long enough that the percentage is meaningful. She files, and a court awards her a share of the investment account and the rental property value. Daniel's daughters receive less than he planned, after months of litigation.

What could Daniel have done? He could have asked Priya to sign a waiver, with her own attorney, in exchange for a clear promise: the right to live in the rental house for ten years and a modest cash gift. Or he could have created a trust that paid her a monthly income for life, with the remainder passing to his daughters. Either route is far less expensive than the court fight.

## What are the common mistakes?

The mistakes below come up most often when someone tries to leave a spouse out.

1. Assuming that a will clause saying "my spouse has been provided for elsewhere" settles the question. It does not, unless the spouse actually received something.
2. Retitling assets into joint names with a child to avoid the claim. See the [risks of joint ownership with a child](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).
3. Skipping disclosure in a prenup, which weakens the waiver.
4. Forgetting that a move to another state may change which rules apply. Our page on [updating your plan when you move](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) is a good checklist.
5. Never telling the spouse. A plan that is a surprise at the funeral is more likely to be contested.
