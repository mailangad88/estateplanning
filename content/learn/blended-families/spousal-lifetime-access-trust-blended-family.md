You remarried, you have children from your first marriage, and you have enough saved that the federal estate tax is not a far-off idea. Your new spouse should be looked after. Your children should still end up with what you worked for. And a friend has mentioned an odd acronym: SLAT.

A spousal lifetime access trust can serve both goals, but it is a heavy tool. This page explains how it works, what it costs you in control, where a second marriage makes the risks sharper, and when a simpler trust does the job.

## What is a spousal lifetime access trust?

A SLAT is an [irrevocable trust](/glossary/irrevocable-trust) that one spouse, the donor, creates and funds during life for the benefit of the other spouse. The spouse can receive distributions, usually for health, education, maintenance and support. When the spouse dies, what remains goes to the people the donor named, often children or a trust for them.

Two features make it different from the revocable living trust most couples know.

1. **The gift is complete.** The assets leave your estate for tax purposes, along with their future growth, as described in our guide to [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts).
2. **Your spouse is a beneficiary.** That is the "access." If you need money in retirement, the household can draw on the trust through your spouse.

The IRS allows this because the spouse is a separate person. You cannot be a beneficiary of a trust you fund yourself and still keep it out of your estate, but your spouse can be.

## How does a SLAT work, step by step?

1. **Choose the donor.** One spouse, usually the one with more separate property.
2. **Draft the trust.** It names the spouse as beneficiary, sets the standard for distributions, names a trustee and names who receives the remainder.
3. **Sign and fund it.** You give cash, investments or other assets by transfer to the trust.
4. **File a gift tax return.** The gift uses part of your lifetime exclusion. In 2026 the federal basic exclusion is $15 million per person, and the annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient.
5. **Live with it.** The trustee manages the assets. Income is often taxed to the donor as a grantor trust, which lets the trust grow without its own tax bill, though you pay the tax.
6. **At the spouse's death,** the trust continues for, or pays out to, the remainder beneficiaries you chose.

## Why would a blended family use a SLAT?

Most SLATs are sold on tax grounds. In a second marriage there is a second reason that may matter more: a SLAT locks in where the assets go.

If you leave everything outright to your new spouse, they can leave it to anyone later, including their own children. The [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) guide covers the usual ways to prevent that. A SLAT is one of them, and unlike a will it works during life. Because the trust is irrevocable, the remainder beneficiaries can't be changed by you or by your spouse. Your children have a trust document rather than a promise.

### Example: Raymond and Joan

Raymond, 66, has $22 million, mostly from selling a business, and two adult children from his first marriage. He remarried Joan, 61, who has modest savings and a daughter of her own. Raymond funds a SLAT with $8 million of brokerage assets. Joan may receive distributions for her support, decided by an independent trustee. At Joan's death, the remaining assets are divided between Raymond's two children. Raymond keeps the other $14 million outside the trust for his own needs. The $8 million, and everything it earns, is out of his taxable estate. Joan is cared for. And Raymond's children cannot be cut out by a later will or a change of heart.

Without the trust, Raymond's children would rely on a will or a trust created at death, which Joan could attack by claiming her statutory share.

## What are the risks?

None of these are small.

- **Divorce.** If the marriage ends, a former spouse may remain a beneficiary of an irrevocable trust. Many SLATs include a clause that ends the spouse's interest on divorce, but some state laws and some trust documents do not. Plan for it even if you cannot imagine it, because the trust cannot be rewritten later.
- **Death of the beneficiary spouse.** If your spouse dies first, your access through them ends. You have given the assets away.
- **No control.** You cannot take the money back. A change of mind, a business setback or a health crisis can leave the household short.
- **Capital gains.** Assets in the trust do not get a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) at your death. Your children may owe tax on gains later, where assets held until death would have been reset.
- **Friction between spouses.** A spouse who sees a large sum controlled by a trustee may feel shut out. The trust works best when both people have agreed to it with their own lawyers' advice.
- **Reciprocal trusts.** If both of you create SLATs for each other that are nearly the same, the IRS may disregard them.

## Does a SLAT make sense for you?

The honest answer for most families is no. With a $15 million exclusion per person and the option of portability between spouses, a married couple can currently shelter around $30 million. If your combined estate is well below that, a SLAT trades control and basis step-up for a tax benefit you may never need. Read the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) guide to see where you stand, and check your state, since some states tax estates at much lower levels.

A SLAT tends to be considered when:

- Your estate is, or may become, well above the federal exclusion, or you live in a state with its own estate tax.
- You have a clear second reason, such as locking in the remainder for children from a prior marriage.
- You can afford to give away the funded amount without worrying about your own lifetime needs.
- Your marriage is stable, and your spouse understands the arrangement.

## What are the alternatives?

| Tool | When it's created | Control for the donor | Main use in a second marriage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revocable living trust | Now | Full, until death | Organizes the plan, no tax benefit. |
| QTIP trust | At death (or now, if funded) | Chooses remainder beneficiaries | Supports spouse, protects children. |
| Separate trusts for each spouse | Now | Full | Keeps each spouse's property apart. |
| SLAT | Now | None once funded | Removes assets from the estate and fixes the remainder. |
| Life insurance trust | Now | Limited | Funds an equal inheritance for children. |

Our guides to [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) and [joint vs. separate trusts for remarriage](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage) explain the two most common alternatives. If equalizing the inheritance is the goal, [life insurance in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/life-insurance-in-a-blended-family) is often cheaper than a SLAT.

## Questions to ask before you sign

- If we divorce, what happens to my spouse's interest?
- Who is the trustee, and who can replace them?
- What standard controls distributions, and can my spouse demand them?
- Are the remainder beneficiaries my children only, or can the trust also benefit stepchildren?
- How much can I give while keeping enough outside the trust?
- Do the numbers work after losing the step-up in basis?
