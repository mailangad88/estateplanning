You have raised your spouse's child for twenty years, and the law still calls that child a stranger. If you die without a will, a stepchild inherits nothing. Some stepparents hear that and ask whether they can simply adopt the child, even as an adult.

You can, in both Illinois and New Jersey, and it works. It also changes more than the inheritance. This page covers what adoption does, what it can cost your family, the steps, and the cheaper routes that reach the same goal.

## Does adopting an adult stepchild make them an heir?

Yes. A final adoption order makes the adopted person the child of the adopting parent for nearly every legal purpose. That includes intestate succession, class gifts like "to my children" in your own will and trusts, and survivor benefits that follow the word "child."

Compare that to the default. Without adoption, a stepchild is outside the list of heirs. Our guide to [who counts as an heir in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/who-counts-as-an-heir-in-a-blended-family) shows the full table, and [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) explains the document-based fixes.

## Can a stepchild inherit from a stepparent with no will?

Not unless adopted. Take Joanne, 64, married to Pete for 25 years. Pete's daughter Kayla, 33, grew up in their house. Joanne dies without a will, with a $400,000 estate in her own name. Illinois sends everything to Pete, because she has no descendants. When Pete later dies, Kayla inherits from her father, but never from Joanne.

Now suppose Joanne adopted Kayla at 31. In Illinois, a spouse who survives with descendants takes one-half and the descendants take one-half (755 ILCS 5/2-1). Kayla would receive $200,000 at once, and Pete would receive $200,000 instead of $400,000. That is the first thing to weigh: adoption helps the child and can squeeze the spouse, especially when there is no will. With a will that says "everything to my husband, then to Kayla," the trade-off disappears.

## How does an adult adoption work in Illinois and New Jersey?

Both states let a petition for adoption of an adult go to court, and both require the adult's consent. The details below are general. Local forms and the court that hears the case change, so an attorney should confirm them.

1. **Confirm the goal.** Write down what you want Kayla to receive and from whom, and check whether a will gets there.
2. **Get consent in writing.** The adult being adopted must agree. So should your spouse, even if the law does not strictly demand it, since the adoption changes your spouse's share.
3. **File a petition** in the county court that handles adoptions where you or the adult lives.
4. **Attend a short hearing.** Judges commonly ask each person whether the adoption is voluntary and whether anyone is pressuring them.
5. **Receive the order** and, if wanted, ask for an amended birth record. Then update every will, trust and beneficiary form to match.

The case is usually shorter than a child adoption because no home study is involved. The cost is attorney time and a filing fee, which varies by county.

## What can go wrong with an adult adoption?

Four things come up in practice.

- **Your spouse's share can drop.** The Illinois example above is the standard one. In New Jersey, a spouse who survives alongside children who are not the spouse's own is also treated differently from a spouse who survives with shared children only, so check the current rule.
- **Other children get a new sibling.** Their intestate share shrinks. If adoption looks timed to an estate, a sibling may challenge it. The more it looks like a family relationship that already existed, the better it holds up.
- **Old documents can read oddly.** A grandparent's trust that benefits "descendants" may or may not include a person adopted as an adult. Courts in some states exclude adults adopted late from older class gifts. The adoptee could gain a parent and lose a trust share.
- **It cannot be reversed.** A will is a pencil. An adoption is a pen.

Another point that surprises people: New Jersey already classifies stepchildren with children for inheritance tax. Under our [New Jersey inheritance tax guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax), stepchildren are Class A and owe nothing, so adopting a stepchild saves no New Jersey tax. Illinois has no inheritance tax, though its estate tax kicks in at a lower level than the federal one; see [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).

## What are the alternatives to adopting a stepchild?

Most families get what they want without a court case.

| Tool | What it does | Can you change it later? |
|---|---|---|
| Will naming the stepchild | Leaves a set share or a fixed gift | Yes |
| Revocable trust | Names the stepchild as a beneficiary, possibly after your spouse's lifetime | Yes |
| Beneficiary form on an IRA or life policy | Pays the stepchild directly, outside probate | Yes |
| Defined "children" clause | Says "my children includes my stepchildren Kayla and Marcus" | Yes |
| Adult adoption | Makes the stepchild a legal child for all purposes | No |

A trust is the sharpest tool when you want your spouse looked after first and the stepchild second. Our article on [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) shows how that structure works, and it applies equally to stepchildren.

Adoption does one thing the others cannot. It lets a stepchild inherit from your relatives through you. A grandmother's will that says "to my grandchildren" will, in many states, include a person adopted by her son, whatever the adopted person's age. If that matters to the family, adoption has a real benefit. If it does not, skip it.

## How do you decide whether to adopt?

Ask four questions. Is there a will or trust that already names the stepchild? Would your spouse's share shrink if you died without one? Do other children, including the stepchild's other parent's side, understand and accept it? Does the stepchild want it, apart from the money? When the honest answers are "no, yes, not sure, and yes," adoption often reflects the relationship and the cost is a conversation worth having.

## What mistakes do families make with stepchild adoptions?

The most common one is adopting and then leaving every other document alone. After the order is signed, the new child shows up in a few places automatically and in many places not at all. Beneficiary forms still name the old people. A trust written before the adoption may define "children" as biological only. A prenuptial agreement may waive rights in ways the adoption was never meant to touch.

The second mistake is doing it in a hurry. An adoption filed a month after a serious diagnosis looks like estate planning and invites a challenge from a sibling. If the relationship has been a parent-child one for years, do it while everyone is healthy and put the reasons in a short signed letter.

The third is skipping the spouse. Even when the law does not require your spouse's agreement, a surprise at the kitchen table is how a decent plan turns into a family fight.

## What if the stepchild is the one asking how to inherit?

If you are the stepchild, the legal answer is simple: you inherit only if your stepparent names you or adopts you. If the stepparent has already died without a will, [what happens when a stepparent dies without a will](/learn/what-if/stepparent-dies-without-a-will) lists what is still possible, which is often little. If your stepparent is alive, say so gently. Many stepparents assume the family "knows" and never put it on paper.
