You want your money to reach your children, and you worry about two places it could leak. One is the new marriage, yours or a parent's. The other is the old marriage, a child's, because a divorce could split what you meant as a family inheritance. In a [blended family](/learn/blended-families) both risks can be present at once.

The legal tools are the same for each: keep the property separate, put it in the right container, and put every promise in writing. This page explains how each works, where it fails and how a family business changes the picture.

## Does an inheritance stay separate in a marriage?

In most states, yes. Property one spouse inherits is treated as that spouse's separate property, along with property owned before the marriage. In community property states the same general rule applies, though the details differ. Our page on [whether an inheritance is marital property](/learn/after-a-death/is-an-inheritance-marital-property) explains the framework.

The trouble is that separate property loses its protection when it is mixed in. The common ways this happens are:

- Depositing inherited cash into a joint checking account.
- Using it to pay down a mortgage on a home both spouses own.
- Adding a spouse's name to a deed or account as a gesture of trust.
- Using separate funds to renovate a shared house without a written record.

Lawyers call this commingling or transmutation. Once it happens, separating the money again can be difficult or impossible, and a court may treat it as a gift to the marriage. Take Sofia, who inherited $200,000 from her father and put it into the joint account she shares with her husband. Five years later they divorce. She will have to trace the money to prove it was hers, and she may lose that argument.

## How do you protect a child's inheritance from their divorce?

Leave it in trust. An outright gift is exposed, because the child can mix it, share title or lose track of it. A trust with an independent trustee keeps the assets in the trust's name, so they are never part of the child's marital estate while they sit there. A spendthrift clause adds protection against a child's creditors and, in many cases, against a spouse's claim to the trust's principal. Our page on [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) covers how this works, and [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) covers age-based and lifetime versions.

A few points make the difference between a trust that works and one that merely exists.

1. **Choose a trustee other than the child's spouse.** A spouse as trustee defeats the point.
2. **Make distributions discretionary.** If the trustee decides, the child has no fixed entitlement for a court to divide.
3. **Do not lend or give trust assets to the couple jointly.** A house bought through the trust is better than one bought with a check to both spouses.
4. **Say who gets what is left.** If the child dies before the remainder is paid, your document, not state law, should name the next beneficiary, such as the child's own children.

A trust cannot promise that a judge will ignore the money when dividing property or setting support, because courts can consider the income a spouse receives. But it moves the starting point heavily in the child's favor. Our guide on [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) gives the parent's side of the same issue.

## How do you keep a house separate before remarrying?

If you own a house now and want your children to get it, the title is the first question. Keep it in your name or in your revocable trust. Do not add a new spouse to the deed, even for convenience, unless you are content to give half the house to that person. Several choices are available, including a [life estate](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse) for your spouse, a trust that allows your spouse to live there for life, or a prenup. Our page on the [family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) compares them.

Selling before remarrying is a separate question, and the answer is usually no. Selling converts a house into cash, and cash is easier to mix with joint money. The decision to sell should be driven by what you need, not by the wedding date.

Housing for the surviving spouse also deserves honest thought. A plan that makes a spouse leave the house within months of your death may be legal and still harsh. Many parents choose a middle path: the spouse can stay for life or for a set number of years, then the house goes to the children.

## What happens to the family business in a second marriage?

A business adds three decisions that a house does not: who owns it, who runs it and who is paid from it. Often those are three different people. A common blended-family pattern gives the children the ownership, lets the one child who works in the business run it, and gives the surviving spouse an income stream, such as a payout or a note. A buy-sell agreement or a trust can implement this. Nonvoting shares let the spouse share in profits without running things. Life insurance can equalize the inheritance among children who are not in the business. Our guide to [passing a family business to children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children) describes the options.

Without a plan, state law may give the spouse a large share of the business. A surviving spouse who is also an owner may then share control with the children, or may sell. That outcome surprises many children, who assumed the business was theirs.

## Can a promise be enforced?

Parents love to say it: "Don't worry, the farm goes to you." It usually doesn't hold. A spoken promise about land generally fails for lack of a signed writing, and an agreement to make a will must usually be in writing in most states. Even then, the promise often limits only what is left after the promisor's death and does not stop a lifetime sale.

If a promise matters, sign a document. That might be a will, a trust, a [prenuptial agreement](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans) or a postnuptial agreement. A joint will with a contract not to revoke is possible, but it can lock a surviving spouse into a plan that stops working. See [joint will vs mutual will](/learn/wills/joint-will-vs-mutual-will) before using one.

## What if a parent remarries and you are the child?

You may be on the receiving end. If your father remarries and his new wife ends up with everything, your options are limited, and often none. Our page on [a parent who remarries and the new spouse inherits everything](/learn/what-if/parent-remarries-and-new-spouse-inherits-everything) lays out when a child has a claim. Beyond that, the best protection is a respectful conversation while your parent can still make changes.

## What should you do now?

1. List what you own and how each item is titled.
2. Mark items that are separate property and keep them out of joint accounts.
3. Choose which assets for children should be in trust and who the trustee will be.
4. Decide what the surviving spouse needs: housing, income or both.
5. Put every promise in a signed document.
6. Review the plan whenever a child marries, divorces or has a child.
