For many couples in their fifties and sixties, the retirement accounts are the biggest asset, bigger than the house. They are also the asset that ignores your will. If you have remarried and want your own children to receive part of that money, the beneficiary form is the document that decides it, and it is one you can fix in an afternoon.

This page covers who receives a retirement account in a second marriage, how spousal consent works, the choices for naming beneficiaries, and the tax traps for children who inherit. It is part of our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).

## Who gets my retirement account if I remarry?

Whoever is named on the beneficiary form gets the account, and a new marriage can change who the law treats as entitled to it. If you never named anyone, the plan's default order applies, and the default is almost always the surviving spouse. That is why the same question has different answers for a 401(k) and for an IRA.

| Account type | Spouse's rights | Can you name your children instead? |
|---|---|---|
| 401(k), 403(b), pension | Spouse is the beneficiary by federal law unless they consent in writing | Only with the spouse's written, witnessed consent |
| IRA, Roth IRA | No federal consent rule in most states | Yes, but community property and some other states give the spouse a claim |
| Annuity | Depends on the contract and state law | Usually yes, read the contract |
| Inherited from a prior spouse | Your own beneficiary choice | Yes |

Outside of consent rules, the form wins. If your will says "my retirement account goes to my sons" and the form says "Elaine, spouse," Elaine receives the account. Our page on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) explains why.

## How does spousal consent work for a 401(k)?

Federal law protects the spouse of a 401(k) participant by making the spouse the beneficiary unless they waive that right correctly. A valid waiver must be in writing, name the specific person or class of beneficiaries, and be signed in front of a notary or a plan representative. A casual statement such as "I don't need it" does not count.

Three practical points follow.

1. **A consent given before the wedding usually does not count.** In most plans a person is not yet a spouse, so a premarital waiver does not bind them. The spouse has to sign after the marriage.
2. **Consent is tied to the form.** If you later change the beneficiary again, ask whether the earlier consent still works. Often it does not.
3. **The plan sets the rules.** Employers use their own forms, and some plans, such as certain profit-sharing plans, are exempt from the spousal requirement. Ask the plan administrator for the form and the exact rule.

A spouse may agree to sign for good reasons: they have their own retirement money, they prefer to receive life insurance, or they accept a trust that supports them. Ask early, and let them take advice from their own attorney. A signature obtained by pressure invites a later challenge.

## What are the ways to name beneficiaries in a blended family?

There are four common patterns. None is right for everyone, and many couples use a mix across different accounts.

### Name the spouse outright

This is simple and gives the spouse the most flexibility. The surviving spouse can roll the account into their own IRA, delay withdrawals, and name new beneficiaries. It is also the riskiest option for children from a first marriage, because once the money is in the spouse's name, nothing obliges them to leave it to your children. Use this when you trust the spouse completely or the account is small.

### Split the account by percentage

You name your spouse for part, say 50 percent, and your children for the rest. At your death, each beneficiary has their own account and their own choices. The children can stretch withdrawals over the 10-year period that applies to most non-spouse heirs, and the spouse keeps real money. It is easy and cheap, and for many couples it is the best answer. The risk is that your spouse may need more than their share, so consider other assets that will support them.

### Name your spouse for some accounts and your children for others

If you have several accounts, you can name your spouse on the IRA and your children on a different account, or name your children on your Roth and your spouse on the traditional IRA. This keeps the forms simple, though you must check that each account's rules allow it, and a 401(k) still needs consent.

### Name a trust

A trust beneficiary lets you pay your spouse income from the account for life and send what remains to your children. This is the retirement-account version of the structure described in our page on [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage). It gives control, but it needs careful drafting. The trust has to meet the rules for identifiable beneficiaries so that payouts are not forced out faster than necessary, and trust income can reach the highest tax bracket quickly. Read [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) before you sign anything.

## What does the 10-year rule mean for my children?

Most adult children who inherit a retirement account must empty it within 10 years of the owner's death, and the spouse is an exception. A surviving spouse is an eligible designated beneficiary with extra options, including treating the account as their own. Our page on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) covers the timing in detail.

For a second family this matters in two ways. First, your children cannot hold the money for decades, so a $400,000 account becomes taxable income inside a decade. Second, the spouse's flexibility is far greater, which is why a rollover can be so powerful and so final.

Retirement money also does not receive a step-up in basis. Unlike a house or stock, it carries its tax with it. If you leave your spouse a $300,000 brokerage account and your children a $300,000 traditional IRA, the children receive less after tax. Many families give the children the assets that step up, and the spouse the retirement money, or the reverse, depending on ages and tax brackets. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## A worked example

Marcus, 63, and Elaine, 59, married four years ago. Marcus has two sons from his first marriage. His 401(k) is worth about $520,000, and he has a $180,000 IRA. His beneficiary forms still name his late first wife's estate for the IRA and, on the 401(k), nobody, because he assumed the will would cover it.

Marcus fixes this in three steps. He asks Elaine to sign a consent on the 401(k) naming his sons for 60 percent and Elaine for 40 percent, with her own attorney reviewing it. He names Elaine on the IRA, because she has little retirement money of her own. And he ties the plan to the house: Elaine will have a right to live there for life, with the home passing to his sons afterward.

If Marcus had done nothing, Elaine would have received the 401(k) automatically, rolled it into her IRA, and his sons would have depended on her will. If he had named the sons on the IRA but not asked Elaine to sign for the 401(k), the consent rule would have prevented it from working.

## What mistakes do people make?

- **Leaving an ex-spouse on the form.** Divorce does not cancel a beneficiary designation on every plan. Our page on [what happens if your ex-spouse is still your beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) shows how it plays out.
- **Forgetting contingent beneficiaries.** If your first-choice beneficiary dies before you, a missing backup sends the account to the default. See [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).
- **Updating the will and not the form.** The form wins.
- **Naming children without a plan for minors.** A minor cannot own an account directly. Use a trust or a custodian arrangement.
- **Ignoring taxes.** Equal percentages on a traditional IRA and a Roth are not equal after tax.
