Nobody plans a second marriage around a diagnosis. You married Dale at 62, you each brought grown children, you agreed on a will and a house, and for six years it worked. Now Dale forgets the stove is on, and his daughter has started asking who holds his checkbook.

A spouse's dementia puts three pressures on a blended family at once: who makes decisions, who pays for care, and whose children end up with what is left. This page walks through each. It builds on the broader [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families), and it assumes the marriage stays intact.

## What should you check first?

Start by finding out what your spouse can still sign. Dementia is a spectrum, and legal capacity is judged document by document and day by day. Someone in early stages can often sign a power of attorney, because that only requires understanding who they trust. A complicated new trust asks for more.

Take stock of what exists. Do not assume. In a second marriage, each spouse often signed their paperwork years apart, before the marriage, with a different attorney.

1. Find the financial power of attorney, and see who is named as agent and who is the backup.
2. Find the healthcare power of attorney and living will, and check whether the agent is you or a child.
3. Pull each spouse's will or trust, and read who gets what and who is the executor or trustee.
4. List every account with a beneficiary form, and see who is named on each.
5. Check how the house and the main bank accounts are titled.
6. Ask your spouse's doctor to write a short note on cognition, dated close to any signing.

If a document is missing and your spouse can still understand it, sign it now. Our guide on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) explains how capacity is judged and how to document it. If it is already too late, [what happens with no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) describes the court route.

## Who should be the agent, you or a child?

In most couples the well spouse is the natural agent. In a blended family that choice deserves a second look, because you and your stepchildren may not trust each other. Your spouse's children see you managing money that they expect to inherit. You see yourself paying for care.

Naming the spouse as primary agent is usually right, since you live with the person and know the bills. Naming one of your spouse's children as backup, or as a co-agent for investment decisions only, can lower the suspicion. Some families add a rule that the agent sends the children an annual summary of income and spending. That costs little and prevents most accusations. If your spouse's documents name only you and there is no backup, a gap opens when you get sick too.

Our page on [decision-makers in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/decision-makers-in-a-blended-family) compares roles in more detail.

## How do you pay for care without losing your children's inheritance?

Long-term care is where a second marriage gets expensive fast. A memory care unit can cost more per month than many households live on. Medicare does not cover most custodial care, so families pay privately or turn to Medicaid.

Medicaid has special rules for married couples, usually called spousal impoverishment rules. They let the spouse who stays at home keep a share of the couple's countable assets and some income, so that one person's care does not wipe out the other. The exact dollar limits are set federally and then adjusted each year, and states choose within a range. Check the current figures for your state with an attorney rather than relying on a number you saw online.

Transfers between spouses are generally exempt from the Medicaid look-back, which is why many couples retitle assets to the healthy spouse. In a blended family, that move has a catch. The assets then sit in your name, and if you die first they may be left to a spouse who cannot manage them. Our guide to [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples) covers the mechanics, and [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) covers the house.

| Choice | What it does | Risk in a blended family |
|---|---|---|
| Move assets to the well spouse | Often allowed between spouses | Your death first leaves the ill spouse with property they cannot manage |
| Keep assets in joint names | Simple, no retitling | Joint property can pass entirely to the survivor, away from your children |
| Pay privately from savings | Keeps full choice of facility | Can drain what you meant for your children |
| Apply for Medicaid after spend-down | Public program pays the bulk of care | Estate recovery may reach the ill spouse's assets after death |

## What should you do with your own will?

Change it now, while you are healthy. The usual second-marriage plan, where everything goes outright to the survivor, is risky when the survivor has dementia. Imagine you die first. Your estate goes outright to Dale, who cannot invest it, and whose care provider may then count it against Medicaid eligibility. When Dale dies, it passes under his will, which may favor his children entirely.

A better design is a trust in your will for Dale's benefit. The trustee, someone you choose and not Dale, pays for his extra needs and can be told to use the principal if the income is not enough. After his death, whatever is left goes to your children. Because Dale never owned the money, it stays out of his own estate and out of his reach as a Medicaid asset in many states. Whether a particular trust avoids being counted depends on how it is drafted and on state rules, which is why the drafting matters. Our page on [joint and separate trusts for remarriage](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage) shows how these are built.

Be careful with the surviving spouse's right to claim part of your estate, often called the [elective share](/glossary/elective-share). If Dale is left less than the statutory share, the agent acting for him may be expected to claim it, especially if Medicaid is paying. That can undo a careful plan. Read our guide to [the spousal elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) and ask how your state handles an incapacitated spouse.

## What if your spouse's own plan is the problem?

You cannot rewrite your spouse's will for them. If Dale's will leaves everything to his daughter, or leaves it to you outright, it stays that way unless he can still sign changes. Where capacity is borderline, an attorney may meet with Dale alone, ask open questions, and record the meeting.

Where capacity is gone, the options narrow. Some states let a court approve a limited change through a guardian or conservator, called substituted judgment, but it takes a hearing and an independent attorney for Dale. The process is slow and public, and his children may oppose it. This is why early action matters more than perfect drafting.

## A worked example

Marcus, 68, is married to Elena, 66. Marcus has two sons, and Elena has a daughter. Elena is diagnosed with early dementia, and her neurologist agrees she can still sign documents.

Within a month, Elena names Marcus as her financial agent and her daughter Priya as backup. She names Marcus as healthcare agent. She keeps her will, which leaves her IRA to Priya and the rest to Marcus. Marcus rewrites his own will: the house stays in a trust for Elena to live in for life, a trustee other than Elena pays for her care out of his savings, and the remainder goes to his sons. Priya receives a yearly letter showing what was spent. When Elena's care needs grow, nobody has to go to court.

## How do you keep the families talking?

Stepchildren often learn about a diagnosis late and from someone else. Tell them yourself, early, and share the list of documents. Agree who talks to doctors. If tempers are already high, a family meeting with an attorney or a geriatric care manager helps, since outsiders say hard things more easily. Our guide on [preventing inheritance fights in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/preventing-inheritance-fights-in-a-blended-family) has more suggestions.
