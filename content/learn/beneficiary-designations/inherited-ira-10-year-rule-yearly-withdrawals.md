You inherited an IRA, or you are leaving one, and you keep hearing "10 years" without anyone saying what you actually have to do in those 10 years. The answer is different from person to person. It turns on who the beneficiary is, whether the owner had started taking required distributions, and whether the account is traditional or Roth.

This page walks through those three questions in order. If you want the bigger picture of how [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) work, start with our guide to [naming beneficiaries on retirement accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

## What does the 10-year rule actually require?

The rule says the whole balance must be out of the inherited account by December 31 of the tenth year after the owner died. If Gordon dies in March 2026, his daughter's deadline is December 31, 2036. The year of death does not count as year one.

Nothing in the rule makes the money come out at the end. You could take it all in year ten, spread it evenly, or take it in lumps whenever your income is low. That freedom is real, and it is also the trap. A $500,000 traditional IRA withdrawn in a single year stacks on top of that year's paycheck, and a large share can be taxed at the higher federal brackets. Heirs who plan the withdrawals, especially around a lower-income year such as a sabbatical or the first years of retirement, often pay noticeably less in total.

Withdrawals from an inherited traditional IRA are ordinary income. They are not subject to the 10 percent early withdrawal penalty, no matter how young you are. We cover how the income tax works for each kind of asset in [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## When do I need to take a withdrawal every year?

You need yearly withdrawals only if the owner died on or after their required beginning date. That date is April 1 of the year after the owner reached the age when required minimum distributions start. Under current law that age is 73, rising to 75 for people born in 1960 or later.

| Owner's situation at death | Heir's yearly withdrawals | Deadline to empty the account |
|---|---|---|
| Died before required distributions began | None required | End of year 10 |
| Died after required distributions began | A minimum in years 1 to 9 | End of year 10 |
| Roth IRA, any age at death | None required for a non-spouse | End of year 10 |

The IRS finalized this reading in regulations that took effect for deaths in 2025 and later, after several years of confusion in which the agency waived penalties. If you inherited earlier, you may have been covered by that relief. Ask the custodian or a tax professional which year applies to you.

Missing a required yearly amount triggers an excise tax on the shortfall. Current law sets it at 25 percent, dropping to 10 percent if you fix the mistake promptly. Custodians often calculate the figure for you, but the responsibility is yours.

### A worked example

Linda's father, Henry, died at 79. He had been taking required distributions for six years. Linda, 50, inherits his $400,000 traditional IRA. She must take a minimum amount each year for nine years, based on a life expectancy table, and empty the rest in year ten. If Henry had died at 68 instead, Linda could have left the money untouched for nine years and withdrawn it all in year ten, though that would have been an unwise tax plan.

## Who is a designated beneficiary, and who is not?

The tax code sorts heirs into three groups, and the group decides the schedule.

- **Eligible designated beneficiaries** are living people who get the longer, lifetime payout. They are a surviving spouse, a minor child of the owner, a disabled or chronically ill person as the tax code defines those terms, and anyone not more than 10 years younger than the owner, such as a sibling.
- **Designated beneficiaries** are other living people named on the form, like an adult child, a niece, or a friend. They get the 10-year rule.
- **Non-designated beneficiaries** are the owner's estate, a charity, and trusts that fail certain tests. They get no 10-year window at all. If the owner died before required distributions began, they generally must empty the account within five years. If the owner had begun distributions, the payout can follow what remained of the owner's life expectancy.

This is why leaving the form blank, or naming the estate, can cost your family. A person named on the form gets up to 10 years. An estate may get five.

### The minor child exception is short

A minor child of the owner can take withdrawals over their life expectancy until reaching majority, which the IRS treats as age 21. Then the 10-year clock starts. Grandchildren do not qualify, only the owner's own children. If you are thinking about leaving an IRA to a grandchild, the 10-year rule applies from day one, and a trust may be worth considering. Our article on [leaving an inheritance to grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/leaving-an-inheritance-to-grandchildren) covers the options.

### Disabled and chronically ill heirs

A beneficiary who qualifies as disabled or chronically ill can stretch. The tax code has strict definitions and the custodian will usually want medical documentation. If the heir also receives means-tested benefits, the money often belongs in a properly drafted trust, because a large IRA in the heir's own name can threaten that eligibility. See [special needs trusts and SSI](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules).

## How is a Roth IRA different?

The owner of a Roth IRA never has required distributions during life. For that reason, a non-spouse heir treats the death as occurring before required distributions began, so no yearly withdrawals are due. The balance still has to be gone by the end of year ten.

Withdrawals are generally tax-free if the Roth had been open at least five years, counting from the first contribution or conversion. If it had not, earnings taken out early may be taxable until the five-year mark. Many heirs leave a Roth alone until year ten, since growth inside it is untouched by tax. A spouse can roll a Roth into their own and avoid the 10-year rule altogether, which is covered in our article on [spousal rollover vs. inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira).

## What if I want to name a successor beneficiary?

When you inherit an IRA you become the owner of an inherited account, and you can name your own beneficiary on it. Do it. If you skip it and you die, the account may land in your estate, and the custodian's default terms apply.

The successor does not start over. A grandchild who inherits what is left of an adult child's inherited IRA generally has to finish within whatever remains of the original 10 years, and may also owe yearly amounts if they applied to you. Ask the custodian how it applies to your account before you name someone.

## Steps to take after you inherit an IRA

1. Get a certified death certificate and contact the custodian. Ask them to confirm the account type, the owner's date of birth, and whether required distributions had begun.
2. Confirm which category you fall into: eligible designated, designated, or non-designated.
3. Retitle the account as an inherited IRA. Never take a check payable to you from an inherited retirement account unless you intend to withdraw it.
4. Calculate the yearly minimum if one applies, and set a calendar reminder for the first one. The first year's deadline is December 31 of the year after death.
5. Write a withdrawal plan with a tax preparer. Look at your expected income for the next 10 years, not just this year.
6. Name a successor beneficiary on the new account.

If you are a charity beneficiary, or you are an heir who does not need the money, a [disclaimer](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) is a possible option. It must be done before you take any benefit and within nine months of death, and the account then passes to the next person on the form.

## What should owners do about the 10-year rule now?

The rule is a good reason to look at who is on your IRA form and how much tax your heirs would pay. Some people leave traditional IRA money to a child in a lower bracket and give other assets to a child in a higher one. Some convert part of a traditional IRA to Roth over several years so heirs inherit tax-free money. Some leave traditional IRA balances to charity, since a charity pays no income tax and can use [qualified charitable distributions](/learn/estate-tax/donor-advised-funds-and-qcds) in life. None of these is right for everyone, and a Roth conversion adds to your own tax bill today.

If you want a trust to receive an IRA, it needs language written for the 10-year rules. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) before you change a form.
