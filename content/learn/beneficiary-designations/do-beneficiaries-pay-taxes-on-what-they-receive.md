Someone you love named you on an account, and now a form from a custodian asks how much tax to withhold. Or you are the owner, and you want to know whether the person you named will get a bill. The honest answer is that the same dollar can be taxed in four different ways, depending on what kind of account it came from.

This page goes asset by asset, for things that pass by beneficiary form. For the general picture of taxes on inheritances, including state inheritance taxes, see our article on [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs). To understand how forms work in the first place, start with the [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## The short answer by type of asset

| Asset passed by beneficiary form | Income tax to the beneficiary | Other tax points |
|---|---|---|
| Life insurance death benefit | Generally none | May count in the owner's taxable estate; interest on delayed payouts is taxable |
| Traditional IRA or 401(k) | Ordinary income as withdrawn | 10-year rule for most heirs |
| Roth IRA or Roth 401(k) | Generally none if the five-year test is met | 10-year rule for most heirs |
| Annuity | Gain is ordinary income | Premiums paid in come back tax-free |
| POD bank account | None on the principal; interest after death is taxable | No step-up needed, since cash has no gain |
| TOD brokerage account | None on transfer | Basis resets to value at death |
| TOD deed on real estate | None on transfer | Basis resets to value at death |

Two separate taxes sit above this table. The **federal estate tax** is paid by the estate before the beneficiary receives the asset, and only if the estate is above $15,000,000 per person in 2026. A few states also have an estate tax, and a handful have an inheritance tax paid by the heir, with lower exemptions. See our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Is life insurance taxable to the person who receives it?

The death benefit is generally not income. A $500,000 policy pays $500,000 to the named beneficiary, and nothing is reported on their income tax return. The exceptions are narrow:

- **Interest.** If the insurer holds the money and pays it out over time, the interest it adds is taxable. A lump sum avoids that.
- **Transfer for value.** If someone bought the policy from the owner for money, part of the proceeds can be taxable. This mostly shows up in business deals.
- **Estate tax.** The death benefit is counted in your gross estate when you own the policy or keep control over it. For most families this makes no difference, because the estate is far below $15 million. Wealthier families often use an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) to keep the policy out of the estate.

The details on naming and paperwork are in our article on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

## Is inherited retirement money taxed?

Yes, if it is pre-tax. Traditional IRA and 401(k) dollars were never taxed on the way in, so the tax comes on the way out, at the beneficiary's own rate. Whoever receives the account reports each withdrawal as ordinary income. There is no early withdrawal penalty on an inherited account, even for a young heir.

Joyce, 45, inherits $300,000 from her mother's IRA. Her salary puts her in a middle bracket. If she empties it in year ten, the lump sum may push much of it into a higher one. If she takes $40,000 to $50,000 a year, she may stay in a lower bracket on most of it. The same dollar, taxed differently, because of timing. We explain the schedule in [the 10-year rule and yearly withdrawals](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) and the broader rules in [naming beneficiaries on retirement accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

Roth accounts are the opposite. Contributions went in after tax, so qualified withdrawals come out tax-free, though the same 10-year deadline applies.

One more point. There is no step-up in basis for retirement accounts. The tax code treats them as income owed to the owner, which is why a $200,000 IRA is not worth $200,000 after tax.

## How are annuities taxed to a beneficiary?

An annuity pays out premiums and growth. A non-qualified annuity, bought with after-tax money, returns the premiums tax-free and taxes the growth as ordinary income. A beneficiary can usually take the money as a lump sum, over five years, or as lifetime payments, and the spread changes the tax. An annuity held inside an IRA is taxed like the IRA: fully, as ordinary income.

Unlike stocks or a house, annuities do not get a step-up in basis. A surprising number of heirs expect one because the policy sat inside a "life insurance company." Check the annuity statement for the cost basis, and ask the issuer for a Form 1099-R at year end.

## Do TOD accounts and TOD deeds get a step-up in basis?

Yes, and this is the best news in the table. [Step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) means the heir's cost basis becomes the value on the day the owner died, not what the owner paid. Securities held in a transfer-on-death brokerage account or a house passing by [TOD deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) qualify, because the owner kept full control until death and the asset was part of the estate.

| | Passed at death by TOD | Given as a gift during life |
|---|---|---|
| Heir's cost basis | Value at date of death | The giver's original cost |
| Example: bought for $100,000, worth $450,000 | Basis $450,000, sale at $450,000 produces no gain | Basis $100,000, a sale produces $350,000 of gain |

That gap is why we often steer parents away from adding a child to the deed while they are alive. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) for the full picture and the exceptions.

## What other taxes might a beneficiary see?

- **Income earned after death.** Interest and dividends the account earns after the owner dies belong to the beneficiary and are reported by them.
- **State taxes.** A small number of states tax inheritances, usually with lower rates for spouses and children and higher ones for distant relatives.
- **Capital gains on later growth.** Growth after the date of death is taxed when the heir sells.
- **Withholding.** Retirement custodians often withhold at a default rate on a payout. You can usually choose a different rate, and the final tax is settled on your return.

## What should owners do now?

1. Pick which assets go to which person with taxes in mind. A traditional IRA left to a charity pays no income tax, while a house with a step-up is the easiest asset for a child to receive.
2. Do not name the estate on retirement accounts. It gives up the lifetime options and may speed up the tax.
3. Think about Roth conversions in lower-income years, if your heirs are in higher brackets.
4. Keep a one-page list showing the type and approximate basis of each asset, so heirs and their tax preparer can work from it.

Confirm any plan with a tax professional, since the numbers move.
