A beneficiary form looks simple: a few boxes and a signature line. The trouble starts when the person you want to name is not a person. A trust, a charity or a cousin in another country each has its own way of being identified, and an institution cannot pay "my family trust" or "the shelter on Maple Street" without being sure who that is.

This article is about wording. If you are still deciding whether to use a trust or which accounts allow a beneficiary at all, start with our guide to [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) and the list of [accounts that can have a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/which-accounts-can-have-a-beneficiary). The pillar page on [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) gives the overview.

## What wording do I use to name a trust?

Use the full title, the date, and the trustee. Those three items are what let the institution find the trust and confirm who can act for it. Copy them from the first page or the signature page of the signed trust, not from memory.

A good entry looks like this:

> The Maria and Luis Alvarez Revocable Trust dated March 4, 2024, Maria Alvarez and Luis Alvarez, Trustees

Notice what is missing: no nickname, no "family trust," no percentage sign beside the trust's name unless several beneficiaries share the account. If the trust is named alongside others, add the percentage the way you would for a person.

Some forms ask for a tax ID. A revocable trust usually uses its owner's Social Security number during life, but after the owner's death the trust needs its own number. That is handled by the trustee, not by you on the form. See our article on [a trust's EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election). If the trust already has an EIN, enter it where asked.

### Which trust, and why?

Naming a revocable living trust makes sense when you want one set of instructions to control the money, for instance to keep it in trust for a young child, to split it among several heirs, or to give a trustee control over a beneficiary who cannot manage it. Naming a trust can also keep a vulnerable person on benefits. Our article on [naming a minor, incapacitated or benefits-receiving beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary) explains that.

For a retirement account, the trust has to be written with the account in mind. A trust that does not qualify can force a faster payout and a larger tax bill. Show the trust to an attorney before you sign the form.

## Can I name a trust on a bank or brokerage account?

Yes to both, with two different habits. For a bank account, the options are to add a payable-on-death beneficiary and name the trust, or to retitle the account in the name of the trust's trustee. Retitling is cleaner when the trust is the real owner of your money, since it removes the need for a form. For a brokerage account, a TOD designation naming the trust works well, and the firm may ask for a certification of trust, which is a short summary signed by the trustee that proves the trust exists.

| Account | Common way to involve the trust | What the institution usually wants |
|---|---|---|
| Checking or savings | POD to the trust, or retitle into the trust | Full trust name and date |
| Brokerage | TOD to the trust | Trust name, date, sometimes a certification |
| IRA or 401(k) | Beneficiary designation | Trust name, date, copy of the trust after death |
| Life insurance | Beneficiary designation | Trust name, date, trustee |

Our page on [POD and TOD accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) explains the difference between the two and how a beneficiary claims the account.

One caution: a trust only works as a beneficiary if it exists and is signed on the day you die. If you name a trust you plan to create later, the form may fail. Sign the trust first.

## What wording do I use to name a charity?

Name the charity by its legal name and its tax ID, and add its address. Many charities go by a shorter name than the one on their registration. "The Humane Society" could be several organizations. A tax ID, called an EIN, is a nine-digit number the charity can give you; it appears on the charity's own giving page or on request, and it lets the institution confirm which organization you mean.

A sample entry:

> Riverbend Community Food Bank, Inc., EIN 00-0000000, 410 Mill Road, Springfield, Illinois

Use the real number, not the placeholder above.

Charities are a smart choice for pre-tax retirement accounts. A charity does not pay income tax on what it receives, while a family member would. Many people leave the house or cash to family and the IRA to charity for this reason. Our guide to [charitable gifts and the tax rules](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-gifts-and-bequests-gift-tax-estate-tax-and-income-tax) covers the details.

### How do I name several charities?

List each one on its own line with a percentage, and make the percentages total 100. For example, 50% to a hospital foundation, 30% to a school and 20% to a church. If you would rather split equally and let the form do the math, say "in equal shares."

Then plan for change. Charities merge, rename or close. Ask whether the institution will pay the successor organization, and consider a contingent beneficiary or a clause such as "if any charity listed is no longer in existence, its share goes to the others in proportion." Not every form has room for that sentence. If yours does not, call the institution and ask how it handles it. Our article on [a charity named in a will that no longer exists](/learn/what-if/charity-named-in-will-no-longer-exists) shows how the same problem plays out in court.

## Can I name a beneficiary who lives overseas?

Usually yes, with some extra steps. A bank, brokerage or insurer will generally pay a person abroad, though it may ask for a passport copy, proof of address, and a wire or international check. Payment is slower than a domestic transfer. Fees apply, and some countries limit incoming funds.

Three points are worth checking before you name someone overseas:

- **Identity.** Give the full legal name as it appears on their passport, along with their date of birth and the country they live in. Spelling differences are a common cause of delay.
- **Tax.** A pre-tax retirement account paid to a non-resident can face withholding in the United States, often at a flat rate unless a tax treaty lowers it. The beneficiary may also owe tax in their home country. The rules differ from the ones that apply to a U.S. resident.
- **Account type.** Some custodians restrict or decline to open an inherited account for someone with a foreign address, and may pay out the balance in a lump sum instead.

If your beneficiary is a non-citizen in the United States, or you are, the rules change again. See our article on [estate planning for immigrants and non-citizens](/learn/basics/estate-planning-for-immigrants-and-non-citizens).

## A checklist before you sign

1. Pull the exact legal name of the trust or charity from a signed document.
2. Include the date, the trustee or the tax ID, and a current address.
3. Put percentages on each line and make the column total 100%.
4. Name a backup for each person or entity, or state what happens if one is gone.
5. Ask the institution what it requires from the trustee or charity after your death.
6. Keep the confirmation with your estate planning papers.
7. Check that the same names appear in your will or trust. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps with this step.

## How state law changes this

Banks and brokers apply state law to POD and TOD accounts, and the rules on trusts, charities and non-resident beneficiaries differ in small ways. Some states require a certain form of notice, and some set time limits for a trustee to prove the trust. For Illinois and New Jersey, our guide to [Illinois payable-on-death and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts) and the one on [transfer on death options in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-on-death-options-in-new-jersey) describe how each state treats these accounts.
