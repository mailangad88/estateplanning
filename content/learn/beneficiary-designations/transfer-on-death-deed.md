Your home is probably the asset your family cares about most, and it is often the one that triggers probate. A transfer-on-death deed is a simple tool that lets the house pass to your chosen person without a court case. It is not right for everyone, and it works differently from state to state.

This article explains how TOD deeds work, how they compare with other options, and the problems to check for. For the broader menu of ways to skip probate, see our guide to [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

## What is a transfer-on-death deed?

A TOD deed is a recorded document in which you, the owner, name one or more beneficiaries who will receive the property at your death. Some states call it a beneficiary deed or a Lady Bird deed (a version with extra creditor and Medicaid features used in a few states). Whatever the name, the key features are the same:

- It takes effect only at your death.
- It is revocable. You can cancel it, replace it, or sell the property without the beneficiary's consent.
- The beneficiary has no ownership, no right to live there, and no say while you are alive.
- The property avoids probate.

Maya, 74, lives in a three-bedroom house worth $380,000. She wants her daughter Nora to inherit it. If she signs and records a TOD deed naming Nora, then after Maya dies, Nora files a death certificate and a short form with the county recorder and becomes the owner, usually without a court.

## Which states allow TOD deeds?

Most states now allow some form of TOD deed. Many have adopted the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act, while others created their own statutes. Some states do not authorize them at all, and a few allow them only in limited situations. Because this changes, check the rules for your state through your county recorder or an attorney, and see our [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) for how individual states handle estate planning.

Property in a state that does not authorize TOD deeds can often be handled with a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) instead. If you own real estate in more than one state, you may need a different approach for each, which we cover in [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

## What are the steps to record one?

1. Confirm your state allows a TOD deed and ask the county recorder for any required form or language.
2. Find your current deed and legal description of the property. Use it exactly.
3. Decide on beneficiaries, shares, and a contingent beneficiary. Write full legal names.
4. Sign the deed in front of a notary. Some states also require witnesses.
5. Record the deed with the county recorder (or equivalent office) while you are alive and competent. In most states a deed that is not recorded before death is invalid.
6. Keep a copy with your estate documents, and note it in your records so your executor knows it exists.
7. Revisit the deed after any big change: marriage, divorce, death of a beneficiary, a sale, or a refinance.

## What are the advantages?

**No probate for the house.** The beneficiary can usually take title with a death certificate and a short affidavit.

**Low cost.** A deed is far less expensive than a trust when the house is the only asset you are trying to protect.

**A step-up in basis.** Because the owner keeps title until death, the beneficiary generally inherits at the home's value on the date of death. If the home was bought for $90,000 and is worth $380,000, the beneficiary starts with a $380,000 basis and may owe little or no capital gains tax on a quick sale. That is the opposite of a lifetime gift, where the receiver takes your old basis. Read more in [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

**Flexibility.** You can sell or borrow against the house without asking anyone.

## What are the risks?

### Creditors, Medicaid, and long-term care

A TOD deed does not remove the house from your estate. If you receive Medicaid long-term care, many states can seek repayment from your estate after death, and some states define "estate" to include property that passes by TOD deed. See [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) for tools that do change the result.

### Several beneficiaries

If you name three children, they become co-owners. They may disagree about whether to sell or who pays property taxes, and one beneficiary who cannot be located can hold up a sale. Consider whether a trust, or an arrangement for one child to buy out the others, is better.

### Beneficiaries who are minors or vulnerable

A young child or a beneficiary receiving public benefits should not own real estate directly. A trust can be named instead if your state allows it.

### Mortgages, liens, and title insurance

A mortgage stays with the property after your death, and the beneficiary takes the house subject to it. Federal law generally limits a lender's ability to call a loan because of a transfer to a relative at the owner's death, but check your loan terms. Some title insurers have limits on coverage after a TOD transfer, so ask before you record.

### Mismatch with your will

A TOD deed overrides your will for that property. If your will says "divide everything equally" but the deed names one child, that child gets the house and your other children may get less. For how this plays out, see [leaving your home to children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children).

### Co-owners and spouses

If you own the house with a spouse, check whether your state treats the home as jointly owned or community property. A deed signed by only one spouse may not control the whole property. If your spouse is a co-owner by [tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety) or [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy), the survivorship rule usually takes priority over a TOD deed signed by one owner.

## Is a TOD deed or a trust better?

| | TOD deed | Revocable living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Low | Higher upfront |
| Skips probate on the house | Yes (if state allows) | Yes |
| Works if you become incapacitated | Does not help | A successor trustee can manage the house |
| Controls timing or conditions | No | Yes |
| Handles minors or special needs beneficiaries | Poorly | Well |
| Covers other assets | No, only that property | Yes, if funded |

A TOD deed is a good fit for a single home going to one adult child. A trust is often better when there are several beneficiaries, multiple properties, or concern about incapacity. See [will vs. trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) for the broader comparison.

## What happens after the owner dies?

The beneficiary does not have to open a probate case, but they do have to act. In most states they record a certified death certificate and, often, an affidavit or similar form with the county recorder, within a period the statute sets. Some states give creditors or other interested parties a window to object. Until the paperwork is recorded, the house may be hard to sell or insure in the beneficiary's name. The beneficiary should also contact the mortgage servicer, change the property insurance, and keep paying property taxes. If several beneficiaries inherit, they should agree early on who handles the sale or upkeep. Sorting this out before the death, through a conversation or a written family plan, saves a great deal of stress.
