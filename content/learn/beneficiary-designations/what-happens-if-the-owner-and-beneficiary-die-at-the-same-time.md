Tom and Ellen are driving home from a wedding when a truck crosses the median. Tom dies at the scene. Ellen dies in the hospital the next morning. Each is the sole beneficiary of the other's $500,000 life insurance policy, and neither named a backup. Whose family gets the money?

Without a plan, the answer depends on how the law counts those twenty hours. This page explains how survival rules work, what the 120-hour rule does, and how to word your forms so a tragedy does not also become a legal puzzle. For the broader picture of who is named on your accounts, see our [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## What happens if the owner and the beneficiary die at the same time?

When no one can show who lived longer, the law treats the beneficiary as having died first. The money then goes to the contingent beneficiary. If there is no contingent, the account usually falls into the owner's estate, and the owner's will or state law decides who receives it.

Most states apply this logic through a survival statute. The older "simultaneous death" idea was narrow. Modern versions add a time cushion, so that a person who does not clearly outlive the other by a set period is treated as having died first. The common cushion is 120 hours.

## What is the 120-hour rule?

It is a survival requirement. To count as a survivor, a person must be alive 120 hours, which is five days, after the other person's death. If the beneficiary dies sooner, the law treats the beneficiary as having died first.

The rule exists to stop a bad result. Without it, Ellen's survival of a single night would send Tom's money to Ellen, and then through Ellen's estate to her heirs, who might be her adult children from a first marriage rather than Tom's family. Each family's wishes could be reversed by a few hours.

New Jersey's version, N.J.S.A. 3B:3-32, says that a person who cannot be shown by clear and convincing evidence to have outlived the other by 120 hours is treated as having died first. Our article on [when a beneficiary dies first in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/when-a-beneficiary-dies-first-in-new-jersey) explains how that rule works for gifts in a will. Illinois also has survival rules, and the details differ. Because the period and the documents covered vary by statute, do not assume the same answer applies to your insurance policy or IRA.

## Does it apply to life insurance, IRAs and bank accounts?

Not always in the same way. Statutes that cover wills do not automatically control a contract with an insurer or a custodian. Many policies and account agreements contain their own survival clause, such as "the beneficiary must survive the insured by 30 days." Some say nothing. Where they say nothing, state law fills the gap, and the answer may depend on which law applies.

| Asset | Who decides survival | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Life insurance | Policy terms, then state law | Any survival clause in the policy or beneficiary form |
| IRA or 401(k) | Plan or custodian agreement, then state law | Default beneficiary rules in the plan document |
| Payable-on-death account | Bank agreement, then state law | Whether the form lets you name a contingent |
| Joint account with survivorship | State law on joint ownership | Our [joint ownership article](/learn/beneficiary-designations/joint-ownership-vs-beneficiary-designation) |
| Will gift | State survival statute | A survival clause in the will itself |

The practical advice is the same each time. Read the form, and when it is unclear, ask the company to explain in writing how it handles two deaths within days of each other.

## What if the beneficiary lives a few days longer?

Then the law may treat them as the survivor, and the account becomes part of their estate. That is the scenario that worries families, because the money follows the beneficiary's plan and not the owner's.

Take Priya and Dev. Dev has a daughter from his first marriage and names Priya as sole beneficiary of his IRA. Dev dies in a car crash. Priya lives nine days. Under a 120-hour rule she survived, so the IRA is hers, and it passes under her will to her sister. Dev's daughter receives nothing from it, even though Dev may have assumed that his daughter was next in line. A contingent beneficiary on the IRA form would have prevented this. Our overview of [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) shows how to word the line.

## What happens if there is no contingent beneficiary?

The money generally goes to the owner's estate and through probate, which adds delay and cost. For retirement accounts, an estate beneficiary can also shorten the withdrawal period. Our article on [what happens if there is no beneficiary on a bank or brokerage account](/learn/beneficiary-designations/what-happens-if-there-is-no-beneficiary-on-a-bank-or-brokerage-account) explains the default order, and the blog post on [what happens if a beneficiary dies before you](/blog/what-happens-if-a-beneficiary-dies-before-you) covers the more common case where the deaths are years apart.

## What if both spouses die and there are young children?

This is the case that makes the paperwork matter. If both parents die, the contingent beneficiary on each form should be someone who can actually receive and manage money for a child. A minor cannot collect an insurance payout, so the insurer may wait for a court to appoint someone, and that takes months. Naming a trust for the children, with a trustee you have chosen, keeps the proceeds out of that process. Our scenario page on [what happens when both parents die without a will](/learn/what-if/both-parents-die-without-a-will) shows what the court route looks like, and the guardian question belongs in the same conversation.

Check the pairing too. If you name a trust on your forms and your spouse names a sibling on theirs, the same accident can send the money to two places that were never meant to meet.

## How do you protect your family from this?

Five steps do most of the work.

1. **Name a contingent beneficiary on every account and policy.** This is the main fix.
2. **Add a survival period** if the form allows one, often 30 or 60 days, so the money does not pass through a person who was alive only briefly.
3. **Check each document's survival language.** The will, the trust, the insurance policy and the IRA custodian can each say something different.
4. **Coordinate with your spouse's plan.** If both plans use the same wording, you do not end up with opposite results.
5. **Review after major events.** Our guide to [how often to review beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-often-to-review-beneficiary-designations) gives a schedule.

### A note on survival periods

A longer period is not always better. A 60-day clause could leave money in limbo if the beneficiary is the person who needs it for rent. For a spouse who relies on the funds, many couples choose 30 days; for families with prior children, a longer cushion may feel safer. An attorney can match the period to the risk. If a minor could receive the money, the [naming a minor as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary) article explains why a trust or custodian is needed.

## How state law changes this

States differ on the length of the survival period, the standard of proof, whether the rule covers insurance, retirement accounts and joint property, and how a contract's own terms interact with the statute. Our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) guide covers the state we serve.
