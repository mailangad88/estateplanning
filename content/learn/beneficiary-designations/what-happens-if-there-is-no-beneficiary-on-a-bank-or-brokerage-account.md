Walt, 72, is a widower in Joliet with $62,000 in a savings account and $140,000 in a brokerage account. He has a will that leaves everything to his three children equally. He assumes the bank and the broker know that. They do not. Neither account has a beneficiary, and when Walt dies, both are locked until someone is appointed to deal with them.

This page explains what happens to accounts with no beneficiary, why it matters, and how to fix it. It belongs with the other tools in our [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## What happens to a bank account with no beneficiary?

If the account is in one person's name and no beneficiary is listed, it becomes part of that person's estate. The bank freezes it once it learns of the death. Family members cannot withdraw from it, even to pay funeral bills, unless they bring court papers (letters of office) showing someone has authority to act for the estate. In practice, that means the family must open a probate case.

Once the executor has letters, the executor collects the balance, pays valid debts, and distributes what remains under the will. Because the account is a probate asset, it is also reachable by estate creditors. A payable-on-death account is different: it passes directly to the named person without probate. For a deeper look at the two types, see [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).

## What about a brokerage account?

The result is the same. A brokerage account with no TOD registration is a probate asset. The firm will not retitle the shares or sell them for the family until the executor presents letters and a death certificate. The market keeps moving in the meantime, so a family that waits months for letters can watch an account rise or fall with no one able to act.

TOD stands for transfer on death. It is how a brokerage lets you register securities in beneficiary form. New Jersey adopted the Uniform TOD Security Registration Act (N.J.S.A. 3B:30-5), and Illinois allows the same kind of registration. Ask your broker for the TOD form, which is usually a one-page document.

## When does a bank or brokerage account not need a beneficiary?

Some accounts already have a built-in successor, so check the title before you worry.

| How the account is titled | Without a beneficiary, who gets it? | Probate needed? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Individual | Your estate | Usually yes |
| Joint with right of survivorship | The surviving co-owner | No |
| Joint without survivorship (tenants in common) | Your share goes to your estate | Usually yes |
| POD or TOD with named beneficiary | The named beneficiary | No |
| Held in a living trust | As the trust directs | No |
| Custodial (UTMA) for a minor | The minor | Rarely |

A joint account solves some problems and creates others, such as exposure to the co-owner's creditors and a risk that the co-owner takes it all. Our page on [joint ownership vs. beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations/joint-ownership-vs-beneficiary-designation) compares them.

## Can the family skip probate if the account is small?

Sometimes. Both Illinois and New Jersey have small-estate shortcuts, but they are narrow.

Illinois allows a small estate affidavit when the estate's personal property is under $150,000, with registered motor vehicles not counted, under 755 ILCS 5/25-1. The affidavit goes to the bank or broker, who must pay or deliver the property to the person named. Walt's two accounts total $202,000, which is over the limit, so his children could not use it. See the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit).

New Jersey's rule applies to intestate estates, meaning no will. A surviving spouse or partner can use an affidavit for estates of $50,000 or less (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-3), and heirs can use it for $20,000 or less if there is no spouse or partner (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-4). If the person left a will, the process differs. See [the New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit).

The lesson from Walt's numbers is simple. A small account might qualify for a shortcut. A larger one almost certainly will not.

## Why do so many accounts have no beneficiary?

Mostly by accident. A bank opens an account in ten minutes, and the beneficiary box on the signature card is optional, so it stays blank. A brokerage transfer from an old employer plan lands in a new account with no form. After a spouse dies, the surviving spouse often keeps using the joint account and never updates the title. Divorce adds another gap: a decree may split the property but leave the beneficiary lines untouched. Our attorneys regularly find a blank or outdated beneficiary line among the statements clients bring to a first meeting. The fix is cheap, which makes the omission worth ten minutes of your time.

## How do you add a beneficiary to a bank or brokerage account?

These steps work for nearly every institution.

1. **List every account.** Include old savings, CDs, brokerage and health savings accounts.
2. **Ask for the form.** Request a POD form from the bank or a TOD beneficiary form from the broker. Many are online.
3. **Choose primary and contingent beneficiaries.** Use full legal names. Add a contingent in case the first has died. See [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).
4. **Pick the shares.** Equal shares are simplest. Percentages must total 100.
5. **Sign and return the form.** Keep the confirmation.
6. **Match your will.** If your will says one thing and your accounts say another, the account forms control.

Walt does this in an afternoon. He names his three children equally, with the grandchildren as contingent beneficiaries per stirpes. Next time he reviews his plan, he checks the same accounts again.

## What should you watch out for?

- **A POD can override a will.** If you name only your daughter on the bank form while the will splits everything three ways, the daughter takes the whole account. Your other children can do nothing about it.
- **A beneficiary with a disability or a minor.** Naming a child under 18 or a person receiving benefits can create problems. Read [naming a minor, incapacitated or benefits recipient as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary).
- **Cash needs.** If every account passes by beneficiary, the estate may have too little cash for debts, taxes or funeral costs. Keep enough in the probate estate or in a trust.
- **Joint accounts in disguise.** Adding a child to an account for convenience makes a joint owner, not a beneficiary.

## How state law changes this

Illinois governs bank beneficiaries under the Trust and Payable on Death Accounts Act, and the statute sends proceeds to the last holder's estate if no beneficiary survives. New Jersey treats POD accounts through banking rules and TOD securities through its Uniform TOD Security Registration Act.
