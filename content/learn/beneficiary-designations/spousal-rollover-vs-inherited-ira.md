When a spouse dies, the retirement accounts often become the surviving spouse's main source of income. One of the first decisions is whether to roll the IRA into your own name or leave it as an inherited account. Both are legal. They lead to different tax outcomes, and the wrong one for your age can cost real money.

This article compares the two, explains where children and other heirs fit, and covers the Roth questions that come up. For an overview of how retirement account beneficiaries work, start with [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) and our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## What is a spousal rollover?

A spousal rollover means the surviving spouse moves the inherited IRA into an IRA in the spouse's own name, or retitles it, and treats it as theirs from then on. A spouse can usually do this with a traditional IRA, a Roth IRA, or an employer plan such as a 401(k) (by moving it into an IRA). Only a spouse has this choice.

After the rollover, the account follows the same rules as any IRA you opened yourself: your beneficiaries, your required withdrawal age, and your 59½ penalty rule.

## What is the alternative?

The spouse can instead keep the money in an inherited IRA, titled something like "Dale Ortiz (deceased) for the benefit of Meg Ortiz." The account stays tied to the original owner's death date. The spouse can take money out at any age without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty, because the rules for inherited accounts apply.

## Which is better?

It depends mostly on your age and whether you need the money now.

| Question | Spousal rollover | Keep as inherited IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Under 59½ and need cash soon? | Withdrawals generally trigger a 10% penalty plus tax | No 10% penalty on withdrawals |
| Do not need the money for years? | Required withdrawals start at your own required age | Required withdrawals can be delayed until the year the deceased would have reached theirs |
| Younger than the deceased? | Often better, since your own later start age gives more years of growth | Can be worse, since the clock tied to the deceased may run earlier |
| Want to name your own beneficiaries? | Yes, your children become heirs under your form | Yes, but they inherit on the inherited account's terms |
| Want to convert to Roth? | Yes, can convert like your own IRA | Generally no |

Two examples show how the choice can swing.

Meg, 54, loses her husband Dale. Meg still works but expects to retire at 58. If she rolls over Dale's $500,000 IRA and then needs money at 56, she owes a 10% penalty on top of income tax. If she keeps it as an inherited IRA, she can withdraw what she needs at 56 without the penalty. Once she reaches 59½, she can roll it into her own IRA. Many advisors suggest she keep part of the money in each.

Lorraine, 66, inherits her husband's IRA. She has her own pension and does not need the funds. A rollover lets her postpone withdrawals as long as the law allows and name her children as her own beneficiaries. The inherited route would give no extra benefit.

## What about the 10-year rule for children?

Children and most other non-spouse heirs cannot roll the account into their own IRA. They open an inherited IRA and generally must empty it within 10 years under the SECURE Act. A minor child of the owner, a disabled or chronically ill person, and someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner are exceptions, which we explain in [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

For an employer plan, a non-spouse beneficiary can usually move the balance by direct trustee-to-trustee transfer into an inherited IRA. Taking a check payable to yourself generally cuts off that option and creates taxable income. Always ask for a direct transfer.

### Do heirs have to take yearly withdrawals under the 10-year rule?

Not always. If the owner died before starting required distributions, the heir may leave the money alone until the end of year 10. If the owner had already started them, the heir generally must take yearly withdrawals too, and then empty the account by the end of year 10. A missed distribution can trigger the penalty described in the FAQ above, so confirm which category applies.

## How does an inherited Roth IRA work?

A Roth IRA is funded with after-tax money, so qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free to the heir. A Roth never requires the original owner to withdraw, so an heir who is a non-spouse has a 10-year deadline but usually no required yearly amounts. That gives a lot of room: leaving the money to grow tax-free for the full 10 years is a common approach.

A spouse who rolls a Roth into their own Roth can also skip lifetime required withdrawals, which makes it one of the more tax-friendly assets to leave to a surviving spouse. The Roth five-year rule still applies to whether earnings come out tax-free.

## Is the choice permanent?

Partly. A spouse who keeps the account as inherited can usually still roll it into their own IRA later, which is why the inherited route is often a safe first move for someone under 59½. The reverse is not true. Once you have rolled the money into your own IRA, it is yours, and you cannot go back to inherited status. Some spouses also split the account, rolling part over and leaving part inherited. That keeps a pool available for early cash needs and still lets the rest grow.

There is also a tax angle most people miss. Because the surviving spouse is the beneficiary, estate tax is rarely an issue thanks to the unlimited marital deduction. The real cost is income tax, and it arrives whenever money leaves a traditional IRA. A slower schedule is not always better if your income will be higher later. Sometimes taking more in a lower-income year is the cheaper path.

## What mistakes happen most often?

- Taking a check instead of asking for a direct transfer, which can end the chance of a rollover.
- Rolling over at 50 and then needing money.
- Failing to name new beneficiaries after the rollover, so the account falls to a default.
- Missing the first-year withdrawal when the owner was already taking required distributions.
- Not asking whether the custodian title matches the choice made.

## Steps for a surviving spouse

1. Wait before moving anything. Obtain certified death certificates and the account statements.
2. Ask the custodian for the beneficiary form on file and confirm you are named.
3. Write down your age, income needs for the next five years, and whether you plan to work.
4. Talk to a tax professional about which choice fits your age and cash needs.
5. If you roll over, immediately name your own beneficiaries and a contingent.
6. Put reminders on your calendar for any distribution due dates.
