Parents name their kids on life insurance. Grandparents name grandchildren on IRAs. A father names his adult son with a disability on everything because he is the one who needs it most. All of that is reasonable, and in each case the form alone is not enough. When the money arrives, someone has to be legally able to receive and manage it.

This page deals with three kinds of beneficiaries who cannot simply be handed a check: children, adults who cannot manage money, and people who receive means-tested benefits. For the wider subject, see our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## What happens if a minor is named as a beneficiary?

The beneficiary is entitled to the money, but a child cannot sign a receipt or manage an account. So the company waits for an adult with legal authority. Without one named on the form, that usually means a court proceeding to appoint a conservator or guardian of the estate. It is called different things by state. The judge may require a bond, regular accountings and court approval for spending, and the cost comes out of the child's money.

Then comes the second problem. When the child reaches the age of majority, the court-supervised account typically ends and the child gets everything. Picture Danielle, 17, whose father's $400,000 policy was paid to a conservator. On her eighteenth birthday the balance is hers. Danielle may be a sensible young woman. Many 18-year-olds are not ready for that amount, and it can also affect financial aid. We tell the story in more detail in [what happens when a minor inherits without a trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust).

## What are my options for a child beneficiary?

| Option | How it works | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name a minor directly | Court appoints a conservator | Nothing planned | Cost, delay, full payout at majority |
| UTMA custodian on the form | An adult you choose holds the money for the child | Smaller sums | Child gets control at 18 to 25, depending on state |
| Trust for the child | A trustee follows your terms and ages you set | Larger sums, family disputes | Trust must be written and funded |
| Spouse or relative as beneficiary, informally | They are asked to use it for the child | Nothing in writing | No legal duty, and their own creditors can reach it |

The informal route deserves a warning. We often hear "I'll name my wife and she'll take care of the kids." It works until she remarries, becomes ill, or dies. Money named to an adult belongs to that adult.

For modest amounts, a UTMA custodian is simple. The wording on the form usually follows a pattern such as: "Anna Reyes, as custodian for Ben Reyes under the [State] Uniform Transfers to Minors Act." Name a successor custodian too. Check whether the institution accepts that wording, because some accounts, like IRAs and 529 plans, handle it differently. Our glossary entry for [UTMA](/glossary/utma) is a quick reference.

For larger sums, a trust is the better tool. Our page on [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) covers the details, including the special rules for IRAs, and [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) compares the choices across your whole plan.

## How do I name someone to manage a child's life insurance money?

You cannot name a guardian on the form, and a guardian of the child's person has no automatic power over the child's money. Pick one of two forms:

1. **A trust.** Name the trust as beneficiary, with a trustee you trust. The trust says when and how the child receives money, for example for education and health at first, then a third at 25, a third at 30, and the rest at 35.
2. **A custodian.** Name the adult as custodian for the child, with the state's UTMA wording.

Then use your will to name the guardian of the child. These are two separate decisions made in two separate documents, and the same person does not have to do both. Many parents choose a sibling to raise the children and a financially steady friend to manage the money. Compare how this works for each [life insurance beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

## What if the beneficiary is an adult who cannot manage money?

Say your brother Walt has advanced dementia and you are his beneficiary, or the reverse. The paying company will not accept his signature if he lacks capacity. The usual routes are:

- **An agent under a durable power of attorney,** if Walt signed one while he had capacity and it covers claims and receiving money.
- **A court-appointed conservator or guardian,** if there is no power of attorney.

Neither the agent nor the conservator has a right to keep the money. They manage it for Walt. And neither can redirect Walt's own beneficiary choices in most cases, a question we cover in [whether a power of attorney can change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary).

If you are the owner and know that a beneficiary will probably be unable to manage money, name a trust. A trustee can step in on day one with no court filing, and the money is protected from a scammer or a poor decision.

## What if the beneficiary receives SSI or Medicaid?

Supplemental Security Income and most Medicaid programs are means-tested. A direct inheritance counts. SSI generally allows $2,000 in countable resources for an individual, and Medicaid asset limits are usually similar in the states that apply them. A $60,000 inheritance paid to a disabled adult can end benefits until the money is spent down, and spending it down can mean paying for care the program would have covered.

The fix is to leave the money to a **third-party special needs trust** rather than to the person. A trustee holds the funds and pays for items that make life better, such as a phone, therapy, travel, a computer or a vehicle, without counting as the person's income in most cases. Details matter, and rules differ between SSI and Medicaid, so see [special needs trusts and SSI and Medicaid rules](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) before you draft.

Here are three mistakes we see:

- Naming the disabled person directly "because they're the one who needs it."
- Naming a trust that has no special needs language, or naming the wrong kind of trust.
- Leaving out the contingent line, so a trust fails and the money goes outright to the person or the estate.

If the beneficiary already received an inheritance directly and is on benefits, act quickly. In some situations the money can be placed into a first-party special needs trust or an ABLE account, but time and dollar limits apply. Read [first-party vs. third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) and talk with an attorney.

## How state law changes this

States set the age at which UTMA custodianship ends and the amount a court will let a parent receive for a child without appointing a conservator. Medicaid asset limits and the way trusts are treated also differ. Look at your state guide, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), and confirm with an attorney before you rely on any form wording.
