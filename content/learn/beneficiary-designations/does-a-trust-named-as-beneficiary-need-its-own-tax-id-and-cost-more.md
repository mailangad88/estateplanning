Naming a trust as the beneficiary of a retirement account or policy sounds like one extra line on a form. In practice it adds a second legal entity to your family's life after you die, and that entity files tax returns, needs a bank account and has to be run by somebody.

That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to know what you are buying. This page answers two things people ask before they sign: does the trust need its own tax ID, and what does it cost to administer? For the planning decision itself, see our guide to [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary), and for the form wording, [beneficiary form wording for trusts, charities and overseas beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/beneficiary-form-wording-for-trusts-charities-and-overseas-beneficiaries).

## Does a trust named as beneficiary need its own tax ID?

Usually, after your death, yes. While you are alive, a revocable living trust uses your Social Security number because it is treated as you for income tax. When you die the trust becomes irrevocable and the tax picture changes. The successor trustee applies for an employer identification number (EIN) from the IRS. Banks, brokerages and IRA custodians will not release an inherited account to a trust without one.

Two practical points follow.

- **On the form,** you normally give the trust's exact legal name and date, for example "The Dolores Reyes Revocable Trust dated March 3, 2024." A trust created inside a will (a testamentary trust) has its own naming rules, so the wording has to match the document.
- **After death,** the trustee needs the death certificate, the trust document (or a certification of trust), a new EIN and the custodian's claim forms. Our guide to the [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election) covers the timing, and [how to claim an account as a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-to-claim-an-account-as-a-beneficiary) walks through the custodian side.

A person named directly gets the account on a Social Security number and a claim form. A trustee has more steps, and each one takes some time.

## What does it cost to name a trust?

There are four kinds of cost, and only the first is paid upfront.

1. **Drafting.** The trust has to exist before the form is filled in, and a trust meant to receive retirement money needs specific provisions. This is a one-time cost and is usually part of a broader plan.
2. **Trustee time.** After death someone must claim the accounts, keep records and make distributions. A family member can serve; a professional trustee is paid. If you name a family trustee, give them a written list of accounts.
3. **Tax returns.** An irrevocable trust files its own income tax return each year it has income. In Illinois that is the Form IL-1041 (see [Illinois Form IL-1041](/learn/illinois/illinois-form-il-1041-estate-and-trust-income-tax-return)), and in New Jersey a fiduciary return. Accountants charge for this work.
4. **Accounting and record keeping.** Even when a court is not involved, the trustee owes beneficiaries reports and clear records.

We do not quote fee ranges because they vary by region, size and complexity. What can be said is that the cost grows with the number of years the trust stays open. A trust that holds money for a minor until 25 costs more to run than one that distributes everything within a year.

## Why do retirement accounts make it more complicated?

Because inherited retirement money carries a tax clock. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years (the SECURE Act rule), and an account paid to a trust has to fit the same limits. Our article on the [inherited IRA 10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) explains the withdrawal rules.

Trusts reach the top federal income tax bracket at far lower income levels than individuals do. If a trust receives an IRA withdrawal and keeps it, that money may be taxed at those compressed rates. If the trust passes the withdrawal to the beneficiary the same year, the beneficiary pays at the beneficiary's own rate. That choice is built into the trust:

| Trust design | What happens to withdrawals | Cost trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Conduit | Must be paid out to the beneficiary | Lower tax, less protection |
| Accumulation | Trustee may keep them inside | More protection, higher tax risk |
| Individual beneficiary, no trust | Paid directly | Simplest and cheapest, no control |

Here is an example with invented numbers. Marta leaves a $400,000 IRA to a trust for her grandson Eli, age 19. The trustee takes $40,000 out in the first year. If the trust keeps the money, it is taxed inside the trust at trust rates. If the trust is a conduit and passes it on, Eli reports it, probably at a lower rate, but he now holds $40,000 at age 20. Which outcome Marta prefers is the real planning question, and the tax ID and returns follow from it.

## Is life insurance cheaper to leave to a trust?

Usually yes. Life insurance death benefits are generally not income to the trust, so there is little annual tax work. The trustee collects the proceeds, deposits them in a trust account, and distributes under the document. The expense is mostly drafting and a trustee's time. Our guide to [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) explains the forms, and [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) covers what to name behind the trust.

## When is a trust worth the cost?

When the people who would otherwise inherit directly are not ready for it. Typical cases are:

- A minor or young adult. Without a trust a court may appoint a guardian of the money. See [naming a minor, incapacitated or benefits-receiving person as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary).
- A beneficiary receiving means-tested benefits, where a special needs trust protects eligibility.
- A beneficiary in debt, in a shaky marriage or with a history of addiction. Our piece on [what creditors can reach](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits) explains why direct inheritance can be exposed.
- A blended family where you want a spouse supported and children protected.

For a responsible adult child with a stable life, the answer is often no. Naming the child directly is simpler and costs nothing. If you doubt, an attorney can compare both paths using your actual accounts.

## What should I hand my trustee so the cost stays down?

Most avoidable cost is time spent hunting for paperwork. A one-page file for your successor trustee saves hours of billable work later. Include the following.

- A signed copy of the trust, or a certification of trust that proves its existence without revealing every term.
- A list of each account or policy that names the trust, with the custodian's phone number and the policy or account number.
- The name of the attorney and accountant who know the plan.
- A reminder that the trust needs an EIN before a custodian will pay out.
- A note saying how fast the 10-year clock matters for retirement money.

Review the list whenever you change a beneficiary line. Our overview of [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) shows which accounts commonly name a trust, and [how often to review beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-often-to-review-beneficiary-designations) gives a schedule. A trust nobody can find is a trust that costs the most.

## How state law changes this

State law decides whether a trustee must report to beneficiaries, how a fiduciary return is filed, and what protection the trust offers from creditors. Federal rules control the IRA payout period and the EIN. Our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides cover the states we serve.
