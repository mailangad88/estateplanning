If you have been told that the simplest way to keep your family out of probate is to "just put a name on your accounts," you have heard about POD and TOD designations. They are free, quick, and often useful. They also have limits that surprise people, especially when they are used for most of an estate.

This article explains how they work, when they help, and how to avoid the problems that appear years later. For the other ways to skip probate, see [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

## What is a POD account?

A payable-on-death account is a bank account where you name one or more people who will receive the balance when you die. It works for checking, savings, money market accounts, and certificates of deposit. You fill out a short form at the bank, or add a beneficiary online.

While you are alive, you own the money outright. You can deposit, withdraw, change the beneficiary, or close the account without telling anyone. When you die, the beneficiary shows the bank a certified death certificate and photo ID, and the bank releases the funds. No court order is needed.

## What is a TOD account?

A transfer-on-death account is the same idea for investments. Nearly every state has adopted a version of the Uniform TOD Security Registration Act, which lets brokerage firms register stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and brokerage accounts in a way that passes to a named person at death. The beneficiary typically opens an account in their own name and the firm moves the assets over, without a sale. The beneficiary receives the benefit of the stepped-up value of the assets at death; our article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains why that matters.

Real estate has its own version, covered in [transfer-on-death deeds for real estate](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed). Retirement accounts and life insurance use beneficiary forms, covered in [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

## Why use POD or TOD?

- **They skip probate.** Money reaches your beneficiary in days or weeks, not months.
- **They are free.** Banks and brokers rarely charge for adding a beneficiary.
- **You keep control.** Unlike a joint account, the beneficiary has no access while you live.
- **They are private.** Probate records are public; a POD transfer is not.
- **They are useful for smaller estates.** In an estate where probate would cost more than it is worth, naming beneficiaries on cash and investments may be enough, sometimes paired with a [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for anything left over.

## What is the difference between POD and a joint account?

| Feature | POD or TOD account | Joint account |
|---|---|---|
| Who can withdraw while you are alive | Only you | Either owner |
| Can the other person's creditors reach it | No | Often yes |
| Effect of the other person's divorce | None | Can be at risk |
| Can you change your mind | Yes, anytime | Removing an owner needs their cooperation or closing the account |
| Skips probate at death | Yes | Yes, to the surviving owner |
| Gift tax questions when added | None | Possible, depending on the account |

Many people add a child as a joint owner to "make things easy." That can backfire: the child's lawsuit, divorce, or bankruptcy can touch the account, and the child can legally empty it. For more on that, read about [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

## What can go wrong?

### Your will and your POD accounts disagree

Because these accounts pass outside the will, they can undo it. Take Joan, 71, with $400,000 in savings and a home worth $300,000. Her will leaves everything equally to her son and daughter. Years ago she put her son on the savings account as POD beneficiary because he lived nearby. At her death, her son receives all $400,000, and the house is the only thing the will divides. Her daughter receives about $150,000 against her brother's $550,000. Joan never meant that.

Ask yourself whether the total effect of all your designations matches your plan. Our article on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) covers more of these gaps.

### A shortage of money for debts, taxes, and expenses

If most of your assets pass by POD and TOD, there may be too little in the probate estate to pay final bills, funeral costs, and taxes. Some states allow creditors or the executor to reach non-probate assets in that situation, which can lead to disputes among beneficiaries. A backup source of cash, or a plan stating who pays what, helps.

### Unequal splits when a beneficiary dies first

If you name "my three kids" on an account and one child dies before you, the survivors may take larger shares or the deceased child's children may be left out, depending on how the form and the bank handle it. Choose a payout method such as per stirpes or per capita deliberately; see [primary vs. contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

### Minors and people with disabilities

A child under 18 cannot collect directly, so the bank or broker may require a court-supervised guardian. A beneficiary who receives public benefits could lose eligibility. In both cases a trust is safer, and you can name it as the POD or TOD beneficiary if the institution allows it. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

### Forgotten and out-of-date forms

Banks merge, accounts get renewed, and beneficiary records can be lost. Ask for a written confirmation every few years.

## How do I set up a POD or TOD designation?

1. Ask the bank or brokerage for its POD or TOD beneficiary form, or find it in your online account settings.
2. Write each beneficiary's full legal name, relationship, and share percentages. Add a contingent beneficiary if the form allows.
3. If your state is a community property state or you are married, confirm whether your spouse has rights in the account before you name someone else.
4. Submit the form and ask for a written confirmation.
5. Record the account, the beneficiary, and where the confirmation is kept in your estate planning file.
6. Review whenever you marry, divorce, have a child, or experience a death in the family.

If you have a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), many attorneys instead put accounts in the trust's name so one set of instructions governs everything. That avoids the mismatches described above.

## How does a beneficiary claim the account?

Claiming is usually simple, which is the point. The beneficiary contacts the bank or brokerage, sends a certified death certificate and a copy of photo ID, and completes the institution's claim form. For a POD account, the bank often pays within days. For a TOD brokerage account, the beneficiary typically opens an account in their own name, and the firm re-registers the securities there. Joint POD beneficiaries each receive their own share. If a beneficiary cannot be found or has died with no backup, the institution may hold the funds until a court or an executor tells it where to send them. A short list of your accounts and the people named, kept with your will, helps your executor and your beneficiaries know what to look for.
