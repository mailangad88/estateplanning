Marriage changes who has a claim on your money, but not who is named on your accounts. That mismatch causes more trouble than almost anything else in this cluster. A woman remarries and assumes her new husband is protected. A man wants to leave his IRA to his daughter and does not know his wife has rights. A widow keeps a form naming a husband who died six years ago.

This page covers the spouse-related questions about [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations): when a spouse must consent, what remarriage and widowhood do to the forms, and where community property comes in.

## Does my spouse have to approve my beneficiary on a 401(k)?

For most employer plans, yes. The federal law that governs private-sector plans, ERISA, says a married participant's spouse is the beneficiary of the account unless the spouse agrees otherwise in writing. The waiver must name the new beneficiary or acknowledge that the spouse is giving up the right, and a notary or a plan representative must witness the signature. Anything less and the plan will pay the spouse.

Two details trip people up:

- A spouse who signs a waiver on one day cannot always be held to it later if the participant changes beneficiaries again. Many plans require a new consent for each change.
- Government and church plans, and some 403(b) plans, are not covered by ERISA, so the rule may differ. Ask the plan administrator.

Pensions have a similar rule. Most defined benefit plans require a survivor benefit for a spouse unless the spouse signs a waiver.

### How the consent works in practice

1. Ask the plan administrator for its spousal consent form. Do not use a generic one.
2. Complete the new beneficiary form first, so the consent can name exactly who and what share.
3. Have your spouse sign in front of a notary, or in front of the plan's representative if the plan allows that.
4. Return both forms together, and ask for written confirmation that the plan accepted them.

If your spouse refuses, you have your answer: the plan will treat the spouse as the beneficiary. Some people then compensate through other assets, such as life insurance or a house, so the overall result still looks the way they intended.

## Does an IRA or life insurance policy need the spouse's consent?

Not under federal law. An IRA custodian will take a form that names your daughter, your friend or a charity, without your spouse's signature, and the same goes for most life insurance policies. That is why people call IRAs more flexible.

Flexible does not mean risk-free. Your spouse may have a claim through other channels:

- **Community property.** In the nine community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin), earnings during the marriage are generally owned equally. A spouse may be able to claim half of an IRA funded during the marriage, no matter what the form says.
- **Elective share.** Most other states let a surviving spouse claim a fraction of the estate, and some count non-probate assets in the calculation. See our article on the [spouse's elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse).
- **Divorce.** In a divorce, an IRA built during the marriage is typically divided.

If you want to name someone besides your spouse, the safer course is a written agreement, signed by your spouse, that explains why, ideally after each of you has had legal advice.

## Who is protected when you name a partner, not a spouse?

Lee, 61, has been separated from his wife for eleven years but never divorced. He names his partner, Dana, on his IRA. If he lives in a non-community-property state, the bank may pay Dana. His wife might still sue under an elective share or separation-related claim. If he names Dana on a 401(k), the plan will refuse unless his wife signs the waiver, which she may not want to do.

Unmarried couples have the opposite problem. Without a form, a partner receives nothing. [Estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) walks through the documents they need.

## What happens if I remarry and don't change the form?

Nothing changes. A beneficiary designation is a contract, and marriage does not amend it. Consider three situations.

| Situation | Who gets the account |
|---|---|
| First spouse still named, now divorced | Often the ex, though some state laws void the designation, and employer plans may ignore those laws |
| First spouse named, deceased, no contingent | The default order, often your estate, not your new spouse |
| Children named, new spouse not | The children, subject to any consent rule or community property claim for the new spouse |

Rhonda, 63, remarries at 60. She leaves her IRA form naming her three children, which is exactly what she wants. Her 401(k) is another matter, because federal law now makes her new husband the beneficiary until he signs a waiver. She should decide on purpose. Our article on [retirement accounts in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/retirement-accounts-in-a-second-marriage) goes through common arrangements, and [remarrying without updating your will](/learn/blended-families/remarried-without-updating-your-will) covers the will side.

For divorced readers, the page on an [ex-spouse who is still the beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) shows what happens and what can be fixed.

## What if my spouse died and I never updated my forms?

Your late spouse is treated as having died before you. The next name on the form takes the asset. If there is no contingent name, the plan's default applies and it often points to your estate. A rollover, an inherited IRA or a pension survivor benefit may also change, and the death may change which tax options you have. Start with the page on [estate planning after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies).

A short list for a widowed owner:

1. Gather the forms for every retirement account, life insurance policy, annuity and bank account with a payable-on-death line.
2. Cross out your spouse as primary and name your children, a trust, or another person.
3. Add a contingent line to each.
4. Move any jointly held bank accounts or a house into your name alone or into a trust, as appropriate.
5. Update your will and any trust with the new picture.

## What about stepchildren?

A stepchild is not an heir under the intestacy law of most states, and phrases like "my children" on a form often mean only biological or adopted children. If you want a stepdaughter to share, name her by full name and relationship, with a percentage. Our article on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) covers wills and trusts, too.

## How state law changes this

States differ in how they treat a spouse's rights over non-probate assets. Community property states give an automatic ownership interest. Some other states have an "augmented estate" that adds back beneficiary-designated property when calculating a spouse's share. Several states cancel a former spouse's designation by statute after divorce, but ERISA plans often disregard those statutes. If you have moved states, or hold accounts in more than one, have an attorney check how your state's rules apply. Our [state guides](/estate-planning/california) are a starting point.
