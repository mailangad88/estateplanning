A beneficiary form has room for a person's name, but many families would be better served by naming a trust. A trust can hold money for a child until adulthood, protect an heir from a creditor, or ensure that a second spouse is supported while the children from a first marriage still inherit.

The catch is that the rules differ by asset. A trust on a life insurance policy is straightforward. A trust on an IRA is a drafting exercise that can go badly if done casually. This article explains both. For a general introduction to how forms interact with your plan, see [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## When does naming a trust make sense?

A trust is worth the added cost and complexity in these situations:

- **Minor children.** The trustee can manage the money until the child reaches an age you choose, instead of a court-supervised guardianship or a lump sum at 18. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).
- **A beneficiary with a disability.** A [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) lets a person keep Medicaid or SSI while receiving extra support.
- **Blended families.** A trust can pay a surviving spouse for life and then pass the rest to children from an earlier marriage.
- **Spendthrift or vulnerable heirs.** A trust with a [spendthrift clause](/glossary/spendthrift-clause) can shield assets from creditors and from a beneficiary's own poor choices.
- **Large sums.** Even capable adults sometimes prefer staggered distributions or professional management.
- **Divorce protection.** Assets held in trust for a child can help keep them separate from a future spouse.

A trust is usually unnecessary when beneficiaries are adults who handle money well, the amounts are modest, and simplicity matters more than control.

## How does a trust work for life insurance?

For life insurance, naming a trust is generally simple. The insurer pays the trustee, and the trustee follows the trust's instructions. Death benefits are generally not subject to income tax, so there is no payout schedule to manage. The main points are:

1. The trust must exist and be signed before death.
2. The form must show the exact legal name, date, and trustee. A form that says "the family trust" invites delays.
3. Name a successor trustee in the trust document in case the first one cannot serve.
4. Consider whether the trustee is prepared to handle a large sum. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).

Our article on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) covers other common mistakes.

## How does a trust work for an IRA or 401(k)?

This is where trouble starts. Retirement plans, under the SECURE Act and IRS regulations, assign payout periods based on the beneficiary. If a trust is the beneficiary, the plan looks through the trust to the individuals behind it, but only if the trust qualifies. A qualifying "see-through" or "look-through" trust generally must:

- be valid under state law,
- be irrevocable (or become irrevocable at the owner's death),
- have identifiable beneficiaries, and
- deliver a copy of the trust or a list of beneficiaries to the plan administrator by the deadline set in the rules.

If the trust fails, the plan may treat the account as having no designated beneficiary, which can mean a faster payout and a higher tax bill than naming individuals directly.

### Conduit trusts and accumulation trusts

| | Conduit trust | Accumulation trust |
|---|---|---|
| What happens to withdrawals | Passed straight out to the beneficiary | Can be kept inside the trust |
| Who pays income tax | The beneficiary, at personal rates | The trust, at trust rates, unless distributed |
| Protection from creditors or poor spending | Limited, because the money leaves the trust | Stronger |
| Complexity | Lower | Higher |

Since most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited account within 10 years, a conduit trust can end up paying everything out within that window anyway. That defeats much of the trust's purpose. An accumulation trust gives the trustee more control but may pay more tax: federal trust tax brackets reach the top rate at a far lower income than brackets for individuals, so money left inside the trust can be heavily taxed. Trustees often distribute enough to the beneficiary to use lower personal brackets while keeping the rest protected.

### An example

Raj, 61, has a $900,000 IRA. His daughter Priya is 29 and doing well financially, and his son Dev is 24 and has struggled with debt. Raj names Priya directly on 50% of the IRA. For Dev's half, he names an accumulation trust with a trustee who can pace distributions. Dev will still have to receive the funds within 10 years, but the trustee can manage when and how much, and can pay expenses directly. Raj's attorney drafts the trust with the IRA rules in mind and lists the trust's exact name on the beneficiary form.

### Special rules for disabled and chronically ill beneficiaries

Federal law allows some trusts for disabled or chronically ill beneficiaries to use a payout period based on the beneficiary's life expectancy, instead of the 10-year rule. These "applicable multi-beneficiary trusts" require specific language, so use an attorney.

## What mistakes cause trouble?

- **Naming a trust that does not exist yet**, or naming one with the wrong name.
- **Using a revocable trust that was not written to receive retirement accounts.** Many standard living trusts lack the language to qualify as a see-through trust or to handle required distributions.
- **Naming the trust but forgetting to give the plan administrator a copy** by the deadline.
- **Ignoring the tax cost of accumulating income.** If a trust keeps retirement withdrawals, the trust pays tax at compressed rates.
- **Choosing the wrong trustee.** A relative with no investment or tax experience may struggle with required distributions and tax filings.
- **Forgetting a spouse's rights.** Federal law requires spousal consent for most 401(k)s to name anyone but the spouse, and a trust is no exception.
- **Not coordinating with your other documents.** A trust named on a form but not matching your will or [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can leave a hole in your plan.

## How do I set this up?

1. List every account you want a trust to receive.
2. Decide which beneficiaries need protection or control, and which can inherit directly.
3. Ask an attorney to draft or review the trust, with the retirement accounts in mind.
4. Sign the trust and confirm it has a taxpayer ID and a named successor trustee.
5. Update each beneficiary form with the trust's exact name and date.
6. Give the trustee a list of the accounts and contact information.
7. Review when tax law changes, when beneficiaries' situations change, or every few years.

## What does a trustee need to know?

If you name a trust, the person you choose as trustee will have to act quickly after your death. Give them a plain list of the accounts and policies that name the trust, the contact for each institution, and a copy of the trust. For retirement accounts, the trustee must provide documentation to the plan within the deadline set by IRS rules, open an inherited account titled correctly, take required distributions on time, and file a trust income tax return each year. A trustee who misses a required withdrawal can cost the beneficiary an excise tax. If you have chosen a family member who has never done this, consider naming a professional co-trustee or including a clause that lets the trustee hire a tax advisor and pay for it from the trust.
