Most people name a beneficiary and stop. But the question that does the real work is "What if that person is not here?" A contingent beneficiary answers it. The words "per stirpes" and "per capita" answer a second, harder question: when one of several beneficiaries dies first, who gets their share?

Both choices take two minutes on a form, and both can decide whether an account passes cleanly or ends up in court. This article explains each one. For the bigger picture, see our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## What is the difference between primary and contingent?

Think of it as a line of people waiting.

- **Primary beneficiaries** are first. If they survive you, they receive the asset, and the contingent beneficiaries receive nothing.
- **Contingent (or secondary) beneficiaries** receive the asset only if no primary beneficiary is alive or able to accept it. The term "contingent" means "depends on something happening."

Most forms also allow a third level, sometimes called tertiary. Use it if you want a charity or a trust to be the final backup.

You can name several people at each level. For example, 50% to your daughter and 50% to your son as primary, and a named niece as contingent. The percentages at each level must total 100%.

Our glossary entry on [contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary) has the short definition.

## What happens if there is no contingent beneficiary?

If every named beneficiary has died or declines, the asset usually follows the institution's default order. A common default is spouse, then children, then your estate. If it falls to the estate, the asset must go through probate. That brings court oversight, delay, public records, and creditor claims, none of which you were trying to invite when you filled out the form. See [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

If your estate ends up as the beneficiary and you have no will, your state's intestacy rules decide who inherits. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains how that works.

## What does per stirpes mean?

"Per stirpes" is Latin for "by the branch." Under a per stirpes designation, if a beneficiary dies before you, that person's share is passed to their own children, divided equally. Each branch of the family keeps its portion.

### Example

Helen, 80, names her three children, Carol, Dan, and Eve, as equal beneficiaries of a $300,000 account, per stirpes. Carol died three years ago and left two children, Sam and Tess.

- Dan receives $100,000.
- Eve receives $100,000.
- Sam and Tess receive $50,000 each, which is Carol's one-third.

## What does per capita mean?

"Per capita" means "by the head." With a per capita designation, the share of a beneficiary who died before you is divided among the surviving beneficiaries at that level.

Using the same family, if Helen's form says per capita, Dan and Eve each receive $150,000. Sam and Tess receive nothing from this account, because they were never named.

(The glossary has short definitions for [per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes) and [per capita](/glossary/per-capita). Be aware that some legal sources use "per capita" in a slightly different technical sense, such as "per capita with representation." Use clear plain-English wording on your forms and attach a note if the institution has a separate option.)

## Which should I choose?

| | Per stirpes | Per capita |
|---|---|---|
| Deceased beneficiary's share goes to | That person's children | The other surviving beneficiaries |
| Best when | You want each branch treated equally | You want to keep it simple or you are close to the surviving beneficiaries |
| Risk | Shares may go to young grandchildren who need a trust or a custodian | Grandchildren may feel left out |
| Typical use | Children from different households, or large age differences | Friends, siblings, or a small group |

If your estate plan treats each child's family as a branch, per stirpes usually matches that goal. If you are naming friends or charities, per capita often makes more sense. In either case, make sure it fits your other documents. Many wills and trusts use "descendants per stirpes" language, and it helps to have the beneficiary forms say the same thing.

## What if a beneficiary is a minor?

A grandchild who inherits under per stirpes may be a young child. The institution usually will not pay a minor directly, so a court might appoint a guardian of the estate. To avoid this, consider naming a trust as a contingent beneficiary or setting up a custodial account in advance. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) and [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

New parents often make these updates for the first time, so our guide to [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) is a good companion read.

## Can a beneficiary say no? (Disclaimers)

Yes. A beneficiary can make a qualified [disclaimer](/glossary/disclaimer), which means refusing the inheritance without taking any benefit from it. The money passes as if the disclaiming person had died before you, so it goes to the next in line. Disclaimers are often used to move an asset to a younger generation or to use a lower tax bracket. They must be in writing and made within nine months of death, and the person must not have accepted the asset or its benefits. Having a contingent beneficiary on your form gives this tool somewhere to land.

## How do I fill out the form well? A checklist

1. Name a primary beneficiary and, separately, at least one contingent beneficiary.
2. Use full legal names, not nicknames, plus relationship and date of birth if the form asks.
3. Give percentages for each level and check that each level totals 100%.
4. Choose per stirpes or per capita, and confirm how the institution defines the terms in its own paperwork.
5. If the form has no option, ask the institution how it handles a deceased beneficiary's share.
6. Consider a trust for minors or vulnerable beneficiaries.
7. Check accounts, policies, and annuities one by one, including [retirement accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) and [life insurance](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).
8. Keep confirmations and repeat the review after any marriage, divorce, birth, or death.

## What if my family situation is complicated?

Blended families and estranged relatives call for extra care. Suppose Ken has a daughter from his first marriage and a wife, Rosa, who has children of her own. If Ken names only Rosa as primary and "my children" as contingent, the stepchildren may or may not count, depending on the institution's definitions. Naming each person by full legal name avoids that. If a beneficiary has a gambling, substance, or creditor problem, a trust as contingent beneficiary lets a trustee pay out slowly. And if you want to leave someone out on purpose, simply not naming them on a form works, but a short note in your estate planning file can head off a later dispute.
