Nobody sends you a reminder to check your beneficiaries. The bank does not ask. Your employer's benefits portal shows a name from a form you filled in on your first day, possibly fifteen years ago. The form just sits there, and on the day it is needed it will be followed exactly as written.

This article gives you a schedule, a list of events that should send you back to the forms, and a way to keep the records so that a review takes an hour and not a weekend. It sits alongside our broader guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) and the general question of [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## How often should I review my beneficiaries?

Every three years is a reasonable floor, and once a year is better if you have many accounts. The reason to pick a fixed date is that slow changes never feel urgent. A nephew you named at 12 is now 25. A charity merges with another one. Your employer changes its 401(k) provider and migrates the old forms, sometimes losing the contingent names in the move.

Tie the review to something you already do. Some people use the week they file taxes, because the 1099s and 5498 forms give a list of every account. Others use open enrollment, because the benefits portal is already open. Pick one and write it down.

If you want a nudge, our [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) can set it up.

## Which life events should trigger a review?

Anything that changes who is in your life or what you own. The table below shows the events that most often make a form wrong, and what to look at.

| Event | What to check |
|---|---|
| Marriage | Spouse as primary on retirement accounts and insurance; ex-partners removed |
| Divorce or separation | Ex-spouse's name on every form; state rules may not remove them (see below) |
| Birth or adoption | Children added or per stirpes wording; a trust for minors |
| Death of a beneficiary | Backup moved up; new backup named |
| Death of a spouse | Every account where the spouse was the primary |
| A beneficiary's disability, bankruptcy or addiction | Whether a trust should hold the share |
| New account, rollover, inheritance | Fresh forms; a rollover can reset the beneficiary |
| Job change | Old 401(k) forms; new employer's group life insurance |
| Move to a new state | Whether your documents still fit local law |
| Sale of a business or large windfall | Shares, trust structure and taxes |
| Estrangement or a change of heart | Whether a name should come off |

Divorce is the one that causes the most damage. A decree may say each spouse waives rights to the other's accounts, but a custodian will follow the form on file unless a court order or state law clearly forces the change. Our article on [an ex-spouse who is still a beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) walks through when the law removes the name and when it cannot, and [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) gives the full list of things to redo.

## How do I make sure my forms match my will?

Read them side by side, once, and answer one question: if both documents were carried out exactly as written, would my wishes be met?

Here is how to check, step by step.

1. List every asset that passes by form: retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, bank and brokerage accounts with a payable-on-death or transfer-on-death name, and any real estate with a transfer on death deed.
2. List the assets that pass by your will or trust: the house if it is titled solo, personal property, accounts without a named beneficiary.
3. Write who receives each one.
4. Compare the two lists. Look for mismatches in people, percentages and backups.
5. Look for a gap, an asset on neither list or one that names a person you no longer want.
6. Fix the form, not the will, whenever the asset passes by form. The will cannot override it.

Sofia, 62, has a will that splits everything equally between her two sons. Her largest asset is a $400,000 IRA that names only her older son. Her will looks fair. The IRA does not, and a will review alone would never show it. The difference between the two routes is explained in [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets). If you want to leave unequal amounts on purpose, see [leaving unequal shares with beneficiary forms](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-unequal-shares-with-beneficiary-forms).

## How should I keep a record of my beneficiary designations?

Keep one document that lists every account and when you last confirmed it. The format matters less than the habit. A spreadsheet, a printed page in your estate planning binder, or a note in a password manager all work.

For each account, write down these five things.

- The institution and the type of account or policy.
- The last four digits of the account number, not the whole number.
- Primary and contingent names, with percentages.
- The date you last confirmed the form.
- Where the confirmation is saved.

Save the confirmation, not a memory of it. Most institutions send an email or a PDF when you update a form, and some websites show the current beneficiary page with a date. Take a screenshot. If you ever need to prove what you filed, that page is your evidence. Our guide on [how to organize your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents) covers where to keep the file.

## Should I tell my beneficiaries what is on the list?

Tell them where it is. You do not have to tell them how much. Many families spend months looking for an old 401(k) or a policy bought decades ago, and the delay is avoidable.

A workable middle path: tell the person you trust most, usually your executor or successor trustee, that the list exists and where it is kept. Put the institution names and account types in a letter of instruction, and keep the account numbers and logins elsewhere. See our article on [the letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) for what to put in it.

It also helps to tell a beneficiary directly when the choice is unusual. If you are leaving your daughter more than your son because of what you have already helped him with, a short conversation, or a note kept with your will, tends to prevent hurt feelings later.

## What do I do when I find a mistake?

Fix it the same week, in writing, with the institution's own form. A phone call, an email to a friend at the bank or a note in your will does not change the record.

1. Download the current form from the institution, or ask for it.
2. Fill in full legal names, relationships, percentages and, where there is a space, the per stirpes or per capita choice.
3. Submit it the way the institution requires, and ask for a confirmation.
4. If your plan is through an employer and you are married, ask whether spousal consent is needed. Our article on [whether your spouse has to approve your beneficiary choices](/learn/beneficiary-designations/does-your-spouse-have-to-approve-your-beneficiary-choices) explains who needs to sign.
5. Update your record and your calendar.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether a divorce automatically cancels a former spouse's role on a beneficiary form. Some states revoke it for many non-probate assets, some only for life insurance and some not at all, and federal rules for employer retirement plans can override state law. Rules for community property, spousal rights and the effect of a will also vary. Illinois and New Jersey have their own treatment of these questions, so if you moved to or from either state, check with an attorney. You can start with our overview of [estate planning in Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) or [estate planning in New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey).
