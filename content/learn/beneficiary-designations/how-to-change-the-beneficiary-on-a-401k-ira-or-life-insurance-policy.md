Changing a beneficiary takes about fifteen minutes, and most people put it off for years. If you want to change the beneficiary on your 401(k), IRA or life insurance policy, you do it on the form or website of the company that holds the account, not in your will and not by telling a relative.

This page walks through the process for each account type, explains when a spouse has to sign, and covers the cases people actually ask us about: removing someone, dealing with an ex-spouse, and what happens if you never get to it. For the broader picture, start with our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## How do I change the beneficiary on my 401(k)?

Log in to the plan's website (Fidelity, Vanguard, Empower and similar recordkeepers all work this way), find "beneficiaries" under your profile or account settings, and enter the new names, relationships, birth dates and percentages. If the portal does not offer the change you want, call the plan and ask for a paper form.

A 401(k) is governed by a federal law called ERISA, and that matters in one way people miss. If you are married, the plan generally treats your spouse as the beneficiary unless your spouse signs a waiver. The waiver has to be written, and the signature has to be witnessed by a notary or a plan representative. A text message saying "I'm fine with that" does nothing. If you name your sister as 100% beneficiary and your husband never signed, the plan can pay him anyway.

Our article on [whether your spouse has to approve your beneficiary choices](/learn/beneficiary-designations/does-your-spouse-have-to-approve-your-beneficiary-choices) covers the waiver in more detail.

## How do I change the beneficiary on an IRA?

An IRA is simpler because federal law does not require spousal consent. You fill out the custodian's beneficiary form, usually online, and the new form replaces the old one. A few states with community property rules give a spouse some claim regardless, but Illinois and New Jersey are not community property states.

Check one thing before you click submit: some custodians treat "per stirpes" as a checkbox and others as a line of text. If you want a deceased child's share to go to that child's children, make sure the form really says so. Our article on [contingent beneficiaries and per stirpes wording](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) shows what a good form looks like.

## How do I change the beneficiary on a life insurance policy?

Ask the insurer for a "change of beneficiary" form, or download it from the policyholder portal. Policies bought through an employer often have the form on the benefits site, separate from the 401(k).

The details that trip people up:

- Use the exact legal name and relationship of each person, and add a date of birth or last four digits of a Social Security number if the form asks.
- Sign it yourself. If you own the policy and someone else is the insured, the owner signs, not the insured.
- Mail it with tracking, or upload it through the portal, and ask for a written confirmation.
- If the policy was assigned as collateral for a loan, the lender may have a claim that comes ahead of any beneficiary.

More on policy-specific traps is in our guide to [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

## How do I remove a beneficiary?

You cannot subtract a name from a form. You replace the whole designation with a new one that leaves that person out, and the new form cancels the old one in full.

Suppose Dale, 52, has named his three adult children at 33%, 33% and 34%. His daughter Renee and he have not spoken in six years, and he wants her out. Dale logs in, enters his son and his other daughter at 50% each, and submits. He does not need Renee's permission and she is not told. If he also wants her to be sure to receive nothing under his will, that needs a separate edit to the will, which our article on [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) explains.

One caution: if you remove a child and that child has children of their own, say so deliberately on the form. Many people mean "no money to Renee" and do not mean "no money to Renee's kids," or the other way around. Either is a legitimate choice, but the form should state it.

## What is an irrevocable beneficiary?

An irrevocable beneficiary is someone whose designation you cannot change without their written consent. It shows up most often in life insurance, where a divorce decree or a business loan may require it. If you are not sure whether anyone on a policy is irrevocable, look for the words "irrevocable" or "may not be changed without consent" on the original application, or ask the insurer to confirm in writing.

Most beneficiary forms for retirement accounts are revocable, so this comes up much less often there.

## What happens if I forgot to update my beneficiary after a divorce?

Often the ex-spouse is still paid. The answer depends on the asset, which is why divorce is the most expensive time to forget a form.

| Asset | Does divorce automatically cancel an ex-spouse's designation? |
|---|---|
| 401(k) or pension under ERISA | No. The Supreme Court has held that the plan documents control, so a state divorce law does not help. |
| IRA | Depends on state law and the divorce decree; do not rely on it. |
| Individual life insurance | Some states cancel it automatically, many only in certain cases. |
| Bank POD account | Varies by state and by the bank's own rules. |

Both Illinois and New Jersey have rules that can cancel an ex-spouse's designation on some non-employer assets, but neither state's rule reaches an ERISA plan, and a rule that applies is not a substitute for a form that is correct. If your divorce is pending, read [estate planning during a separation](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-during-separation-or-a-pending-divorce), because some courts freeze changes while a case is open.

## A checklist for any beneficiary change

1. Pull your current designation from each account and write down what it says today.
2. Decide the new primary and contingent beneficiaries, with percentages that add to 100% at each level.
3. If you are married and the account is an employer plan, get your spouse's notarized or plan-witnessed waiver before you submit.
4. Submit the change through the portal or by tracked mail.
5. Save the confirmation page or letter with your other estate papers.
6. Look at the account again in three months and confirm the new names show.
7. Update the will or trust so it matches, since [a will and a beneficiary form can disagree](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form) and the form wins.

## What if I am changing it because someone died?

A death is the other common trigger. If your beneficiary has died, do not wait for the next review. Name a new primary now, because many default orders would otherwise push the money into your estate and into probate. For the common questions about a beneficiary who dies first, see our blog post on [what happens if a beneficiary dies before you](/blog/what-happens-if-a-beneficiary-dies-before-you).
