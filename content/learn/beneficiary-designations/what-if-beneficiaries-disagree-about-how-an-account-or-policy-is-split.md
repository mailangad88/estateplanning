Omar named "my children" as the beneficiaries of his $300,000 life insurance policy and never listed names. When he died, his daughter Fatima assumed three equal shares of $100,000. His son Jamal pointed out that their brother Sami had died two years earlier, leaving two young daughters, and said the policy should be split in two. Fatima said the grandchildren were not named. The insurer received both letters and stopped.

This is the typical co-beneficiary dispute. Nobody is a villain. The form was vague, and each person read it in their own favor. This page explains who decides, what insurers and plans do when claims conflict, and how families settle without a lawsuit. It is part of our [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Who decides how the money is split?

The document decides. Not the will, not the family, and not the person who would be most upset. A life insurance policy, retirement plan or annuity pays according to the beneficiary designation on file, read with the contract or plan terms. If the form says 50/25/25, the company pays 50/25/25.

Where families run into trouble is the wording. Phrases like "my children," "equally" or "to my heirs" leave open questions: does that include a stepchild, a child who has died, or an adopted child? The contract's definitions answer some of them, and state law fills the rest.

Ask for these three things first:

1. A certified copy of the designation form on file, not the family's memory of it.
2. The policy or plan document's definition of "children," "per stirpes" and "survivors."
3. A written statement from the company of how it intends to pay.

With Omar's policy in hand, the lawyer for the family could see that the company treats "children" as living children unless the form adds "per stirpes." Fatima was right on the wording. If the form had said "per stirpes," Sami's two daughters would have split his $100,000 share. Our guide to [contingent beneficiaries and per stirpes](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) explains the difference.

## What happens when two beneficiaries make competing claims?

Companies avoid paying the wrong person, because paying twice is expensive. If the claims conflict, one of three things usually happens.

| What the company does | When | What it means for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pays undisputed shares, holds the disputed share | The dispute covers only part of the money | Some beneficiaries are paid on time |
| Holds everything and waits for an agreement | Both sides say they own the whole | Long delays, no interest in some cases |
| Files an interpleader in court | The conflict cannot be resolved | A judge decides; the company steps out |

Interpleader is the legal route when an insurer or plan does not want to pick a side. The company deposits the money with the court and asks the judge to decide who is entitled. In Illinois the case goes to the circuit court, and in New Jersey to the Superior Court, often the Chancery Division. Plan on months, not weeks. Fees and costs can come out of the pot, which shrinks everyone's share.

## Does a deceased beneficiary's share go to the others?

Often not, though people assume it does. What happens depends on three pieces of wording.

- **"To my surviving children, equally."** The share of the child who died goes to the survivors. Omar's form did not say this.
- **"Per stirpes."** The share passes down to the deceased child's own children. Sami's daughters would take it.
- **No survivorship wording.** The company's default order applies, and that can send the share to the other beneficiaries or to the estate.

Retirement plans are less forgiving, because many plan documents set their own rules and the beneficiary form often lacks space to spell out instructions. A review with the plan administrator can prevent surprises. The article on [how often to review your designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-often-to-review-beneficiary-designations) gives a schedule.

## How can the beneficiaries resolve it without court?

Most disputes end faster and cheaper by agreement. Try these steps in order.

1. **Get the facts in writing.** Everyone reads the same form and the same contract language.
2. **Talk to the company.** Ask what it will pay now, what it will hold, and what documents it needs to release the held share.
3. **Offer a compromise on paper.** Fatima and Jamal might agree that Sami's daughters should get something. If the nieces' share is paid to them directly by the company, the paperwork is clean. If Jamal instead hands part of his own share to the girls after he is paid, that transfer may count as a gift, and money for children usually needs a custodian or a trust.
4. **Try mediation.** A neutral mediator costs far less than a contested case, and most mediators are attorneys.
5. **Use a court only for a real conflict.** Where the form is truly ambiguous or someone alleges pressure, a judge may need to rule.

Keep in mind that a beneficiary who wants to step aside has to follow strict rules for a qualified disclaimer. Our page on [disclaiming an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) explains how, and the state-specific rules are in the [Illinois](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois) and [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-new-jersey) guides.

## What about retirement accounts shared by several beneficiaries?

For IRAs and employer plans, the cleanest way to end friction is to divide the account. Each beneficiary can usually ask the custodian to split the account into separate inherited accounts, so each person controls their own share, chooses their own investments and takes their own required distributions. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must still empty the account within 10 years of the owner's death, but a split means one person's choices no longer affect another's tax picture. Our page on the [10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) explains how it works.

## What mistakes make a dispute worse?

- **Paying a share before the claim is resolved.** If one beneficiary receives money and a court later reallocates it, getting it back is slow and unpleasant.
- **Signing the company's release without reading it.** A release may waive claims to a larger share. Read before you sign.
- **Arguing over the owner's intent when the form is clear.** Memories of what Dad meant do not change what he signed.
- **Waiting too long.** Plans and courts have deadlines, and evidence such as banking records and doctors' notes disappears. If you suspect pressure on the owner, move quickly and talk to an attorney.

## How can an account owner prevent a dispute?

Owners control most of this. Use full names, not "my children." Say what happens if a beneficiary dies first. Choose percentages that add to 100. Tell the people named.
