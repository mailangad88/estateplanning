Parents have reasons for leaving children different amounts. One child spent years as the caregiver. One has a disability or a hard financial history. One already received help with a down payment. Whatever the reason, the tool that carries it out is often a beneficiary form, and the forms have some quirks that surprise people.

This article covers how to set uneven shares on a form, what goes wrong when you try to shortcut it, and how to check that the whole plan adds up. If you want the broader picture of how these forms work, start with our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations). For unequal gifts through a will, see [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).

## The short version

- You can set any split you like, in whole or fractional percentages, on retirement accounts, life insurance, bank and brokerage accounts with payable-on-death or transfer-on-death features.
- Each tier has to total 100%. Primary beneficiaries add to 100, and contingent beneficiaries add to 100 separately.
- The forms control these assets. Your will does not.
- Think in total value, after tax, across every account and the house, not account by account.

## How do I set unequal shares on a beneficiary form?

Every form has a line for each beneficiary, with a name, relationship and a percentage or fraction. To favor one child, give that child a larger number. The math is the only requirement.

Take Renata, 71, with three children. Her daughter Carla moved in three years ago and handles her care. Renata wants Carla to receive 50% of her IRA, and her sons Marcus and Dev to receive 25% each. She writes that on the form, adds a contingent line for each child's children, and keeps a copy. That is all there is to it.

A few practical points:

1. Use full legal names and a date of birth if the form asks. "My son Marcus" can be read two ways if you have two sons named Marcus in the family tree.
2. Decide what happens when a child dies before you. If Carla does not outlive Renata, should her 50% go to Carla's kids, or be split between the two brothers? Pick per stirpes or per capita, and read [primary vs. contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) before you decide.
3. Do each account on its own. A common mistake is changing the IRA and forgetting the old life insurance policy, which still says "equally."
4. Save the confirmation page or letter. The company's record is what counts.

## Why is naming one child to "share with the others" a bad idea?

Because on paper, that child just inherited everything. This arrangement is common, especially when one child lives nearby and the parent trusts them. It is also where many sibling disputes begin.

Here is how it fails. Anil, 79, names his daughter Priya as the sole beneficiary of a $400,000 account and tells her, "Split it three ways after I'm gone." Priya means to. But three things can derail that: she gets divorced before the money moves, a creditor gets a judgment against her, or she dies before she distributes it. Even if none of those happen, the money she gives her brothers may count as a gift from her, which has its own tax paperwork. And if she decides she needs it, her brothers have no claim at all.

None of this means Priya is untrustworthy. It means the structure puts all the risk on the relationship. If you want someone to carry out your wishes, use a trustee, who has legal duties to follow your instructions. Our article on [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) explains how that works for retirement accounts and other assets.

## How do the forms fit with my will?

They do not talk to each other, and that is the whole problem. A beneficiary form passes the asset directly, without probate. Your will governs only what is left in your name alone. If your will says "everything equally to my three children" but the IRA names Carla alone, Carla gets the IRA and the will does not offset it. We explain the split in [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

You can make it come out evenly or unevenly on purpose. A rough worksheet helps:

| Asset | Value | Who is named | Child A | Child B | Child C |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IRA | $300,000, pre-tax. | By form. | 50% | 25% | 25% |
| Life insurance | $150,000 | By form. | 0% | 50% | 50% |
| House | $400,000 | By will or deed. | 100% | 0% | 0% |
| Bank account | $50,000 | POD form. | 34% | 33% | 33% |

Total this up for each child before you sign anything. The numbers often surprise people, because a 50% share of an IRA is taxable income when withdrawn, while a house typically receives a step-up in basis. In practice, a dollar of IRA is usually worth less to a child than a dollar of home equity. If you want true fairness, give more of the IRA to the child in a lower tax bracket or give the larger percentage to the child you want to compensate.

## What if I want to explain my reasons?

A short letter kept with your estate papers helps. Beneficiary forms do not allow explanations, and silence invites guessing. Something like "Carla received a larger share because she gave up her job to care for me" prevents the story from becoming "Mom must have been confused." It is not legally binding, but it can discourage a challenge. A person's mental state and outside pressure are the usual grounds for contesting a change, so a written explanation made while you are well, dated, and perhaps reviewed by your attorney carries weight.

One more habit worth building: when you change one form, pull up the list of every other account and ask whether it still fits the new split. Families rarely fight about the account that was updated. They fight about the forgotten one, usually an old workplace policy or a small brokerage account nobody mentioned.

## What should I do if a parent already named one child alone?

1. Do not assume the worst. Ask whether the designation was part of a plan, such as a will that gives the other children a larger share of other assets.
2. Get the paperwork. As a child, you may not be able to see the form until after death, but the executor can request it.
3. Look at the dates. A designation made years ago looks very different from one signed a month before a diagnosis.
4. If you suspect pressure or confusion, speak with an attorney before the funds are paid out. Deadlines are short in some states, and money that has been paid is much harder to recover.

Our article on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) covers one common way these changes happen without the owner's real consent.

## How state law changes this

State law affects spousal rights, how an ex-spouse's name is treated and the time limits for challenging a change. In community property states, a spouse may have a claim on some retirement money earned during the marriage even when another person is named. Many employer retirement plans require a spouse's signed consent to name anyone else. If you live in a state with special rules, such as [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) or [California](/estate-planning/california), check the state guide before changing anything.
