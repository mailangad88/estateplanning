Life insurance is one of the simplest ways to leave money to someone: a form, a name, and a check. It is also one of the easiest to get wrong, because the form sits in a drawer or a company portal for decades while your life changes around it.

This article walks through the mistakes we see most often and how to avoid each one. For how all of these forms fit together, see our overview of [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Who gets the money if my will says something different?

The insurance company pays the person named on the policy. A life insurance policy is a contract, and the beneficiary designation is part of it. Your [will](/learn/wills) is not consulted unless the policy pays out to your estate, which usually means something went wrong.

Consider Marcus, 44. His will leaves everything equally to his three children. His $500,000 term policy, bought years ago through work, names only his oldest child, Alicia, because the other two were not yet born. When Marcus dies, Alicia is entitled to the whole $500,000. There is no legal duty for her to share it, even though Marcus would have wanted her to.

## What mistakes should I avoid?

### Naming a minor child directly

Insurers will not pay a young child. If a minor is the beneficiary, the money typically has to be held by a court-supervised guardian of the property or by a custodian under your state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act. Court oversight costs time and fees, and many states hand over the balance at 18 or 21 whether or not the child is ready. A better path is to name a trust for the child or to set up a custodial account in advance. Our article on [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) compares the options.

### Leaving out a contingent beneficiary

If your only beneficiary dies before you and you have not named a backup, the policy often defaults to your estate. That means probate, delay, and potential creditor claims. A contingent line costs nothing. See [primary vs. contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) for how to word it, including the difference between per stirpes and per capita.

### Naming your estate

People sometimes name "my estate" thinking it keeps everything simple. It does the opposite. The proceeds become part of the probate estate, so they are delayed and subject to your debts. Name a person or a trust instead.

### Forgetting about an ex-spouse

Divorce does not always cancel a beneficiary designation. Some states have statutes that treat an ex-spouse as if they had died first, but employer-provided group life insurance is usually governed by federal law (ERISA), which generally requires the insurer to follow the paper form. After a divorce, change every form. The same goes for a separation agreement: it may say who must keep coverage in place for children, so read it before changing anything.

### Using vague descriptions

"My children" or "my family" invites disputes about who counts, especially in blended families. Use full legal names, relationships, and percentages. If you want shares to pass to grandchildren when a child dies first, say so with "per stirpes."

### Not updating after major changes

Birth, adoption, marriage, divorce, a death in the family, a falling out, a big change in anyone's finances: any of these is a reason to look at the forms.

## When should a trust be the beneficiary?

A trust makes sense when you want to control timing or protect the proceeds. Common reasons:

- Your children are young and you want an adult trustee to manage the money until they reach ages you choose.
- A beneficiary receives means-tested benefits, and a direct inheritance could end eligibility. A special needs trust can avoid that.
- You have children from more than one relationship and want to guarantee a fair split.
- A beneficiary struggles with debt, addiction, or a risky marriage.

If you go this route, make sure the trust is signed and in existence before you name it, and use its exact legal name and date on the form. Our guide to [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) covers the details. For families with large policies, an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) can also own the policy itself to keep it out of a taxable estate.

## Are life insurance proceeds taxable?

Generally, a death benefit paid to a named beneficiary is not subject to federal income tax. Estate tax is separate. If you own the policy when you die, the death benefit counts toward your gross estate. For 2026, the federal estate tax exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, so most families owe nothing. The [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) article explains how the numbers work. A handful of states tax smaller estates, and some inheritance tax states treat insurance differently, so see our summary of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## How do I check my policies? A short checklist

1. List every policy you own: individual, term, whole life, employer group coverage, and any credit union or association policy.
2. Request a current beneficiary confirmation from each insurer or HR department. Do not assume the form matches your memory.
3. For each policy, confirm that a primary and a contingent beneficiary are named.
4. Replace any minor child, ex-spouse, or "estate" designation with a person or properly named trust.
5. Confirm that percentages add up to 100% for each tier.
6. Save the confirmation pages with your estate documents and tell your executor where the policies are.
7. Put a reminder on your calendar to repeat this every three years or after any family change.

## What if the insurer cannot find the policy?

Families sometimes discover that a loved one had coverage only after death. Ask the employer's HR department, look through bank and credit card statements for premium payments, check the person's mail for annual statements, and search your state's unclaimed property office. Several states also offer free policy locator services. Keeping a list of policies where your executor can find it prevents this problem entirely.

## What should my family do after a death?

The beneficiary, not the executor, files the claim. They contact the insurer, complete a claim form, and send a certified death certificate, often several copies. Most claims are paid within a few weeks, though a death within the first two years of a policy can trigger a closer review called the contestability period. Interest usually accrues on the benefit from the date of death. Beneficiaries can often choose a lump sum or installments, and a lump sum is the common choice. If more than one beneficiary is named, each files a claim for their own share. Keep the confirmation letters, because the proceeds can affect later decisions about debts, taxes, and other inheritances.
