If you have an IRA, a 401(k), a 403(b), or a similar account, there is a good chance it is the largest thing you will leave behind besides your home. It is also the asset most likely to go to the wrong person, because it does not follow your will. It follows a one-page form you may have signed 20 years ago.

This article explains how those forms work, what the 10-year rule means for the people who inherit, and how to choose beneficiaries on purpose. For the bigger picture of forms that override wills, see our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Why does the beneficiary form beat my will?

A retirement account is a contract between you and the plan administrator or IRA custodian. The contract says the balance is paid to the person named on the form when you die. The account never becomes part of your [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate) as long as a living beneficiary exists, so your will has no say. We cover this split in more detail in [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

Here is what that looks like in real life. Walter, 67, divorced in 2009 and wrote a new will leaving everything to his two daughters. He never changed the beneficiary on his old 401(k), which still names his ex-wife. When Walter dies, the plan will usually pay his ex-wife. Some states have laws that cancel an ex-spouse's designation after divorce, but those laws often do not reach employer plans governed by federal law. The safe course is to fix the form itself.

## What is the 10-year rule?

The SECURE Act, passed in 2019 and adjusted by later legislation, ended the "stretch IRA" for most heirs. As of 2026, a non-spouse beneficiary generally has to empty an inherited IRA or 401(k) by December 31 of the tenth year after the owner's death. There is no rule saying when in those 10 years you must take money out, with one important exception below.

The exception is for owners who had already reached their required beginning date for required minimum distributions (RMDs). In that case, IRS final regulations generally require the beneficiary to take an annual withdrawal in years 1 through 9 and then clear the balance in year 10. Missing a yearly withdrawal can trigger an excise tax, so heirs should confirm the rules with a tax professional.

The tax effect is the part families notice. Withdrawals from a traditional IRA or 401(k) are taxed as ordinary income. An inherited Roth IRA is generally tax-free to take out, though the 10-year deadline still applies.

### An example

Elena, 52, inherits her father's $600,000 traditional IRA. If she waits until year 10 and takes it all at once, the lump sum lands on top of her salary in a single tax year and could push much of it into a high bracket. If she spreads withdrawals over several years, especially lower-income years, she can often reduce the total tax. The 10-year clock gives her a choice, but it does not give her a tax break.

## Who gets better options than the 10-year rule?

Federal law carves out a group called eligible designated beneficiaries. They can use a longer schedule, often based on their own life expectancy:

- A surviving spouse
- A child of the owner who has not reached the age of majority (the exception ends when the child does, and then the 10-year clock starts)
- A disabled or chronically ill individual, as defined by the tax code
- Anyone not more than 10 years younger than the owner, such as a sibling

A surviving spouse has the most choices. They can roll the account into their own IRA and treat it as theirs, delay distributions until their own required age, or keep it as an inherited account. Which one is best depends on the spouse's age and whether they need the money soon.

## How do I choose my beneficiaries?

Start by listing every retirement account and who is currently named. Then decide who should receive each one, and in what shares. These questions help:

1. Who needs the money, and who needs it flexibly? A spouse who may live decades longer often needs the full rollover option.
2. Who is in a lower tax bracket? Leaving traditional IRA money to a child in a lower bracket can mean less tax than leaving it to one in the top bracket.
3. Would it make sense to leave pre-tax accounts to one person and other assets, like a house, to another? Splitting by tax type can be fairer than splitting by account balance.
4. Is any beneficiary a minor, or someone who may not manage a lump sum well? If so, a trust may help, though it must be written carefully. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).
5. Is anyone receiving government benefits? An inheritance can end eligibility unless it goes into the right kind of trust.

Charities are often best funded from traditional IRAs, because a qualified charity owes no income tax on what it receives, while an individual would. That is a reason many people use retirement accounts for gifts to charity and other assets for family.

## What are the most common mistakes?

**Leaving the form blank.** If no one is named, many plans default to your estate. Then the account goes through probate, and under the 10-year rule it may be forced onto a faster payout schedule than a named person would have had.

**Naming your estate.** This usually causes the same problems as a blank form, plus creditor exposure in some states.

**Naming minor children directly.** A custodian cannot pay a child. A court may have to appoint a guardian of the property, and the child may receive the entire balance at 18 or 21. Our article on [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) explains the options.

**Forgetting the contingent line.** Without a [contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary), a single death before yours can undo your plan. See [primary vs. contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

**Not updating after life events.** Marriage, divorce, births, deaths, and estrangements all call for a fresh look.

**Ignoring spousal rights.** Under federal law, the spouse is the automatic beneficiary of most 401(k) plans unless they give written consent to someone else. In community property states, a spouse may also have a claim on IRA funds earned during the marriage. If you want to name someone other than your spouse, talk to an attorney first.

## How do I change or confirm a beneficiary?

1. Log in to each account or call the plan administrator and ask for the current beneficiary form. Ask them to email you the designation on file, not just a summary.
2. Complete a new form, using full legal names, relationships, and percentages that total 100% for primary and for contingent beneficiaries.
3. Describe shares the way your plan allows. Many forms let you choose "per stirpes," which means a deceased beneficiary's share goes to their children.
4. Submit the form exactly as the plan requires, with spousal consent signatures if needed.
5. Save the confirmation. Keep a copy with your estate planning documents and tell your executor or successor trustee where the accounts are.

Your beneficiary forms and your estate plan should be reviewed together. A [will](/learn/wills) or [trust](/learn/trusts) that says one thing while the forms say another is one of the most common reasons families end up in court.
