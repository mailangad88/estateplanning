A beneficiary form is the most powerful piece of paper in many estates. It passes an IRA, a life insurance policy or a bank account straight to a named person, without a will and without a court. That power is also why families fight over it.

If you are wondering whether a form can be undone, or whether yours could be, this page, part of our [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) library, covers the real grounds, what evidence matters, and what ordinary mistakes do not break a form.

## The short version

- A designation is presumed valid. The person who challenges it must prove the problem.
- The main grounds are undue influence, lack of capacity, fraud, forgery and, for a killer, the slayer rule.
- Small errors in a name or a date rarely invalidate a designation when the intent is clear.
- You can lower the odds of a fight by documenting why and how you made the choice.

## Who can challenge a beneficiary designation?

Someone who would gain if the form were set aside, such as a prior beneficiary, an heir, or the estate. A neighbor with a hunch has no standing. The usual challenger is the person who was named on an earlier form, or an heir who would have inherited under the will.

Take Helen, 79, who for years named her two sons equally on her IRA. After a hospital stay she signs a new form, naming a home health aide as the sole beneficiary. When Helen dies, both sons can ask a court to examine the change. They will need evidence, not just disappointment.

## What is undue influence?

Undue influence means someone pressured or manipulated the owner so strongly that the form reflected the influencer's wishes, not the owner's. The law looks for several signals together: a vulnerable owner, a person in a position of trust, that person's involvement in making the change, an unnatural result, and secrecy.

The pattern shows up in real files.

- The new beneficiary drove the owner to the bank or insurer and stayed in the room.
- The owner was recently ill, isolated or dependent on the new beneficiary.
- The change cut out close family with no explanation.
- The new form was filed shortly after a medical event.

Some states treat a confidential relationship, such as a caregiver or an agent under a power of attorney who profits from the change, as raising a presumption that shifts the burden. See [can a power of attorney or guardian change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary) for the agent side, and the glossary entry on [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence).

## What if the owner lacked capacity?

The test resembles the one for a will: did the owner understand what they were doing, the nature of their property, and who their natural beneficiaries were? Many courts apply a standard close to the one for a contract, which can be a bit stricter than a will, but the variation among states is real.

What decides these cases is evidence from the time of signing. A physician's note from that month, a nurse's chart, a social worker's visit, and the testimony of the bank employee who witnessed the signature all matter. A diagnosis of dementia from last year does not prove incapacity on the day of the change, because people with early memory loss can have clear periods. Our page on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) explains how clinicians assess capacity and why timing matters.

## What does the slayer rule do?

Almost every state has a slayer statute. A person who intentionally and unlawfully kills the owner cannot collect from the owner's death, including life insurance, retirement accounts and inheritance. The law treats the killer as though they died before the owner, which usually sends the asset to the contingent beneficiary or the estate.

Three points are worth knowing.

1. It requires an intentional killing. Causing a death in a car accident, or a different felony, does not trigger it.
2. A criminal conviction is generally strong proof, but some states allow a civil court to find the same facts by a lower standard if no conviction exists.
3. The rule bars the killer, not the killer's own family. If the killer's child is a separate contingent beneficiary, the child may still take.

This is also why a named contingent beneficiary matters. [Contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) explains who is next in line.

## Which mistakes are harmless and which are not?

| Problem on the form | Usual result |
|---|---|
| Misspelled name, nickname or old surname | Fixed with ID or a court order if the person is clearly identifiable |
| Missing date or middle initial | Generally accepted if intent is clear |
| No signature | The form may be rejected by the plan |
| Unsigned spousal consent on an employer plan | The designation may not be effective against a spouse |
| Form never filed with the plan or insurer | The old designation controls |
| Percentages that do not add to 100 | The administrator may reject or hold the funds |
| Ex-spouse still listed | Depends on state law and the plan |
| Beneficiary is a trust that does not exist yet | May fail, so check the trust is signed |

A form that is filed late, or never reaches the company, is a surprising source of disputes. A change you meant to make, but did not complete before death, generally does not count. The company follows its records.

For the divorce situation, some states cancel an ex-spouse's designation on certain accounts. Plans governed by federal law, however, may follow the form on file, so check each account.

## How do I defend a designation I made?

If you expect a family member to object, prepare while you are healthy. Practical steps:

1. **Tell the people involved.** Silence invites suspicion. A conversation with your children, even a short one, makes the later story easier.
2. **Write down your reasons** in a signed note or letter, kept with your estate documents.
3. **Complete the form yourself,** without the beneficiary present, and get a confirmation from the plan.
4. **Ask your doctor** for a short note about your mental state if you are older or recently ill.
5. **Use a lawyer** when the change is unequal. See [leaving unequal shares with beneficiary forms](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-unequal-shares-with-beneficiary-forms).

## What if I think a designation is wrong?

Move fast. Time limits for challenging a designation vary by state and by claim, and the account may be paid out quickly. The steps usually go like this.

1. Tell the plan administrator or insurer in writing that you dispute the claim, which may cause them to freeze the funds.
2. Gather the old and new forms, medical records, bank records, and names of witnesses.
3. Talk to an attorney about your state's deadline and standard.
4. If the company has already paid, the fight is against the recipient, which is harder to enforce.

Challenges are expensive and uncertain. Many families settle after a lawyer reviews the evidence. If the dispute involves a will too, [how to object to probate](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate) covers the parallel process.
