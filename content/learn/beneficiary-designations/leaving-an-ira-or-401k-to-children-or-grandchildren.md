Most married people name a spouse on their retirement accounts, and for good reason. A surviving spouse gets the most flexible options. But some people want the children to inherit directly: a second marriage, a spouse with plenty of their own money, or a plan to move a Roth IRA to the next generation. Others want to skip a generation and name grandchildren.

Those choices are allowed, with some rules attached. This article covers who must agree, what the children or grandchildren can actually do with the account, how Roth money is taxed, and what to watch for with young or overseas heirs. For the general rules, start with our guide to [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) and the pillar on [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Can I leave my IRA to my children instead of my spouse?

Yes for an IRA in most states, and only with consent for an employer plan. The IRA is a contract between you and the custodian, and you decide who is named. In a community property state, a spouse may have a claim to part of the account regardless of the form, so check local law if you live in or moved from one.

A 401(k), 403(b) and many pensions are different. Federal law generally makes your spouse the beneficiary of the plan unless the spouse signs a written consent, usually witnessed by a notary or a plan representative, agreeing to another choice. A consent signed before marriage does not count, and a verbal agreement does not count. Our article on [whether your spouse has to approve your beneficiary choices](/learn/beneficiary-designations/does-your-spouse-have-to-approve-your-beneficiary-choices) goes through who signs and when.

Here is a common version. Tom, 66, remarried at 60. His new wife, Lena, has her own pension and a paid-off house. Tom's $700,000 401(k) is the main thing he wants his two adult children to have. He can do that only if Lena signs the plan's consent form. Tom's IRA, by contrast, he can leave to his children without her signature.

If you leave the account to children and nothing to your spouse, consider what else your spouse will live on. Life insurance, a trust, or the house can fill the gap. Our page on [estate planning for second marriages](/learn/blended-families) is a place to start.

## What can children do with an inherited retirement account?

They have fewer choices than a spouse. A spouse can roll the account into their own IRA and keep delaying withdrawals. A child cannot. A child inherits the account as a beneficiary and has to follow a payout schedule.

| | Surviving spouse | Adult child or grandchild |
|---|---|---|
| Treat the account as their own | Yes, by rollover | No |
| Inherited IRA with stretch options | Yes | Generally no |
| Payout deadline | Often none until their own required age | Usually 10 years after the year of death |
| Yearly withdrawals | Based on their own age | Required in years 1 to 9 if the owner had already started required withdrawals |

Our comparison of [a spousal rollover and an inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira) shows the spouse side in full, and [the 10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals) explains when yearly withdrawals apply.

### Can a non-spouse roll over an inherited 401(k)?

No, not into their own account. A non-spouse beneficiary of a 401(k) can ask the plan for a direct transfer into an inherited IRA, which is an account titled in the owner's name for the benefit of the heir. The steps look like this.

1. Notify the plan administrator and send a death certificate.
2. Ask for the beneficiary claim packet and the plan's rules on non-spouse transfers.
3. Open an inherited IRA at a custodian you choose, and give the plan its exact titling.
4. Ask the plan to send the money directly to the new custodian. Do not take a check in your own name.
5. Choose investments, and calendar the 10-year deadline.

If the check is made out to you, the plan may withhold tax and the entire amount counts as income that year. A non-spouse beneficiary cannot undo that by rolling the money back in later.

## Are inherited Roth IRA withdrawals tax-free?

Usually, yes. Most children and grandchildren who inherit a Roth IRA pay no income tax when they take the money out, provided the account meets a five-year test. The test is that the Roth IRA must have been opened, by the original owner, at least five tax years before the withdrawal. If it has not been, the contributions can come out tax-free but the earnings may be taxed until the period is complete.

The 10-year rule still applies. The heir must empty the account by the end of the tenth year after the owner's death, but because a Roth owner is treated as never having started required withdrawals, no yearly amounts are generally required during those years. A child can wait and take it all in year 10, and in that time the money continues to grow tax-free.

That is why many families leave Roth money to the next generation and spend, or give to charity, the traditional IRA money. A traditional IRA left to a child in a high-earning year can produce a larger tax bill than expected. See our article on [whether beneficiaries pay taxes on what they receive](/learn/beneficiary-designations/do-beneficiaries-pay-taxes-on-what-they-receive) for the full picture.

## Can I leave my IRA to my grandchildren?

Yes, and it often works well, but three things need planning.

**Minors cannot manage the account.** A custodian will not usually release money to a child under 18 or 21, depending on the state. Without a trust, a custodian under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, or a court-appointed guardian, the account can get stuck. Naming a trust for the grandchild is the usual answer. Our article on [naming a minor or incapacitated person as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary) explains how.

**A grandchild is not an eligible designated beneficiary.** Only the owner's own minor child gets the minor-child exception. A grandchild generally falls under the 10-year rule from the start, so the account cannot be stretched for decades.

**The generation-skipping transfer tax may apply.** A gift to a person two generations below you can be subject to this separate tax, but it only matters if the total passes the exemption, which matches the $15 million federal estate exclusion per person in 2026. For almost all families it is not an issue. See our article on the [generation-skipping transfer tax](/learn/estate-tax/generation-skipping-transfer-tax) and our guide to [leaving an inheritance to grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/leaving-an-inheritance-to-grandchildren).

If a child dies before you, per stirpes wording sends that child's share to the grandchildren automatically. See [primary vs. contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

## Can a nonresident alien inherit an IRA?

Generally yes, but the details are different. A grandchild or child who lives outside the United States, or who is not a U.S. citizen, can usually be named. Several things change:

- The custodian may require extra identity papers and a foreign address check.
- Payments to someone who is not a U.S. tax resident can be subject to withholding, commonly at a flat rate unless a tax treaty reduces it.
- The heir may owe income tax in their home country as well.
- Some custodians will not open an inherited IRA for an overseas address and will pay the balance out in one sum, which can cause a large tax bill.

Raise this with the custodian before you name the person. See our guide on [estate planning for immigrants and non-citizens](/learn/basics/estate-planning-for-immigrants-and-non-citizens).

## Are inherited retirement accounts safe from creditors?

Often not as safe as the owner's own account. In 2014 the U.S. Supreme Court held that an inherited IRA is not protected as "retirement funds" in the heir's bankruptcy under the federal bankruptcy code. Some states protect inherited accounts by their own law, and others do not. If your child is in a risky job, in a bad marriage, or has debts, a trust can offer more protection. We cover the details in our article on [whether creditors can take what a beneficiary inherits](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits).

## How state law changes this

State law affects spousal rights in community property states, creditor protection for inherited accounts, and state income tax on withdrawals. New Jersey has an inheritance tax that can reach some transfers to non-family beneficiaries. Illinois has an estate tax with a lower threshold than the federal one. Read our overview of [New Jersey estate planning](/estate-planning/new-jersey) and [Illinois estate planning](/estate-planning/illinois) if you live in either state.
