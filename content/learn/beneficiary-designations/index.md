Most people think their will decides who gets their things. For a large share of what they own, it does not. Retirement accounts, life insurance, and many bank and brokerage accounts pass to whoever is named on a form, and that form wins over the will every time.

This guide explains how beneficiary designations work, why they cause so many family disputes, and how to build a set of forms that matches your plan. Each section links to an article that goes deeper on one type of asset.

## What is a beneficiary designation?

A beneficiary designation is a signed instruction to a bank, brokerage, insurer, or retirement plan, naming who is to receive the asset when you die. When you die, the institution verifies the death and pays that person. No judge is involved, no executor needs to sign, and the asset is not part of your [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate).

That speed is the reason beneficiary designations are popular. It is also the reason they are risky: the institution does not ask whether the form is out of date, whether it matches your will, or whether the person named is still the right choice. It pays the name on the paper.

Here is a realistic example. Linda, 63, has a will that splits her estate equally among her three children. Her assets are a $350,000 house, a $500,000 IRA naming her eldest child, Chris, and a $250,000 life insurance policy that names her late husband and no one else. When Linda dies, Chris receives the IRA, the policy falls into her estate (because the only named beneficiary is dead), and the house and policy proceeds are split three ways. Her children receive very different amounts, and the estate has to go through probate. Nothing in the will was wrong. The forms were.

## Which assets pass by beneficiary designation?

| Asset | How the beneficiary is named | What it avoids |
|---|---|---|
| IRA, 401(k), 403(b), pension | Plan or custodian beneficiary form | Probate |
| Life insurance, annuities | Insurer's beneficiary form | Probate |
| Bank account (checking, savings, CD) | Payable-on-death (POD) form | Probate |
| Brokerage account | Transfer-on-death (TOD) registration | Probate |
| Real estate | Transfer-on-death deed, where allowed | Probate |
| Jointly owned property | Right of survivorship | Probate |
| Assets in a trust | Trust terms | Probate, if the trust is funded |

Anything not on that list, such as a car in your sole name or a bank account without a beneficiary, usually passes under your will and goes through probate. Our guide to [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) shows how to sort your own list.

## Why does the form beat the will?

The institution is bound by its contract with you, and a beneficiary form is part of that contract. A will only governs property you owned in your own name at death that has no other instructions attached. Many lawyers describe it as the "last in line." Our overview of [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) covers this and other limits.

This is also why a [will](/learn/wills) or [trust](/learn/trusts) alone is never a complete estate plan. The plan includes your forms.

## What are the most common mistakes?

**1. Outdated forms.** Divorces, deaths, and estrangements happen, and forms stay the same. Some states cancel an ex-spouse's designation after divorce, but employer plans governed by federal law often ignore that. The only safe option is to update the form.

**2. No contingent beneficiary.** If the primary beneficiary has died, an asset with no backup usually defaults to your estate. See [primary vs. contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

**3. Naming a minor child.** Financial institutions generally will not pay a minor directly, and a court may have to appoint someone to manage the money. The child may also receive everything at 18 or 21. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

**4. Naming "my estate."** This often triggers probate for an asset that could have bypassed it, and it can expose the money to creditors.

**5. Unequal results from unequal forms.** If each child is named on a different account, the totals may not match your intentions, as in Linda's case.

**6. Forgetting that a trust needs to be named.** A trust holds only what is titled in its name or directed to it by form. Assets you never moved into the trust, or never pointed to it, pass elsewhere. See [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

**7. Ignoring the spouse's rights.** Federal law makes a spouse the automatic beneficiary of most 401(k)s unless they consent in writing, and community property states give a spouse an interest in some assets. Our state guides, such as [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), note how these rules differ.

**8. Disregarding the tax rules.** Retirement accounts carry income tax and distribution rules that other assets do not.

## How do retirement accounts work?

Retirement accounts are usually the largest beneficiary-designated assets, and they carry unique tax rules. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA or 401(k) within 10 years of the owner's death under the SECURE Act. A surviving spouse can roll the account into their own IRA and has the most flexibility. A few other people, including a minor child of the owner, a disabled or chronically ill person, and someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner, may qualify for longer payouts.

Because withdrawals from a traditional IRA are taxed as ordinary income, who you name can change how much your family keeps. Our article on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) walks through the 10-year rule, how to choose, and how to confirm what is on file.

## How does life insurance work?

Life insurance pays the named beneficiary, generally free of income tax. The main errors are naming a minor, leaving out a contingent beneficiary, forgetting about an ex-spouse, and naming the estate. We cover these in [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries), including how to find a policy a loved one may have left behind and when an irrevocable trust should own the policy.

## What are POD and TOD accounts?

A payable-on-death account is a bank account that goes to a named person at your death. A transfer-on-death account is a brokerage account or securities registration that does the same. You keep full control while alive, the beneficiary has no access, and the asset skips probate. They are free and fast, but they can quietly undercut a will if they hold most of your money. See [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) for details and a comparison with joint accounts.

## What about my house?

In states that allow it, a transfer-on-death deed names who will receive your home without probate. You stay the owner, can sell or borrow, and can revoke the deed. The beneficiary generally receives a step-up in basis at your death. But the deed has traps with creditors, Medicaid recovery, and co-owners. Read [transfer-on-death deeds for real estate](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) before recording one.

## When should a trust be the beneficiary?

A trust can be named on most beneficiary forms, which lets you control timing and protect heirs. It is a strong choice for minors, beneficiaries with disabilities, blended families, and heirs who may struggle with money. The rules for naming a trust on an IRA are strict, and a poorly drafted trust can speed up taxes. For insurance and bank accounts the process is simpler. Our article on [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) compares conduit and accumulation trusts and lists the drafting traps. If a beneficiary receives public benefits, also see [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).

## What does per stirpes mean on a form?

When a beneficiary dies before you, the wording on the form decides who receives their share. "Per stirpes" passes it to that person's children. "Per capita" gives it to the remaining beneficiaries. Many forms do not offer this choice, and each institution has its own default. Our article on [primary and contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) explains both and how to fill out the form.

## How do I review my beneficiaries? A step-by-step process

1. **Make a list of every account and policy.** Include old employer plans, IRAs, annuities, insurance through work, credit union accounts, and any account you rarely look at.
2. **Request the current beneficiary on file for each.** Do not rely on memory. Ask for a confirmation in writing.
3. **Write down what you want.** Decide who should receive each asset, in what shares, and who the backups are.
4. **Add up what each person receives.** Include the will or trust. Check whether the total outcome is fair and intentional.
5. **Spot the problems.** Look for minors, ex-spouses, estates, blank forms, and missing contingents.
6. **Update the forms.** Use full legal names and clear percentages. Follow the institution's signing requirements, and get any spousal consent needed.
7. **Coordinate with your will or trust.** If your plan changed, update the other documents at the same time.
8. **Store the confirmations and tell your executor.** A list of accounts and contacts saves your family weeks of searching.
9. **Repeat.** Calendar a review for every three years and after life events.

## How do beneficiary designations connect to tax?

Beneficiary designations do not avoid tax. Retirement accounts carry income tax for the beneficiary, and life insurance you own counts toward your gross estate. The federal estate tax exclusion in 2026 is $15,000,000 per person, so most families owe nothing, but some states tax much smaller estates. Our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) covers the federal rules, and [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) shows where state tax may apply.

Heirs who inherit assets by beneficiary designation also benefit differently from tax rules. Most inherited property, but not retirement accounts, gets a step-up in basis. For more on what a beneficiary must do after receiving an asset, see [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## Where do beneficiary designations fit in the whole plan?

Think of an estate plan as a set of tools, each of which controls a different group of assets:

- The **will** controls assets in your name alone with no other instruction.
- A **revocable living trust** controls what you have moved into it.
- **Beneficiary forms** control retirement accounts, insurance, and POD or TOD assets.
- **Joint ownership** controls assets held with survivorship.
- **Powers of attorney and health care directives** cover decisions while you are alive.

A gap or conflict between them is where families get hurt. A thorough review lines them up, and it is one of the least expensive things an attorney does. If you are just starting, our [basics guide](/learn/basics) explains the full set of documents.

Two situations cause most beneficiary problems we see: [an ex-spouse who is still named](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) and [an account with no beneficiary at all](/learn/what-if/no-beneficiary-named). Both are covered in our guide to [what happens when planning waits](/learn/what-if).
