A parent has died and you think there might be a life insurance policy, an old 401(k) or a bank account with your name on it. Or you know you are named, and you do not know what to do next. Either way, the money is not going to find you. A claim has to be made.

This article, part of our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations), walks through how to find accounts, who to contact, what the paperwork looks like, how long it takes and what to do when something goes wrong. If you are also dealing with funeral arrangements and bills, our [checklist for when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) covers the rest.

## How do I find out whether I am a beneficiary?

Start with the people and papers closest to the person who died.

1. **Ask the executor or the family member handling the paperwork.** The executor may already have a list of accounts. See [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) for what that person should be gathering.
2. **Look through the mail and files.** Annual statements, a 1099 tax form, and letters about beneficiary updates all show an institution and an account number.
3. **Check tax returns.** Interest and dividend income on last year's return points to banks and brokerages. A retirement plan distribution points to an IRA or 401(k).
4. **Search for lost life insurance.** The National Association of Insurance Commissioners runs a free life insurance policy locator, and many states let you search for unclaimed policy proceeds.
5. **Search unclaimed property.** Every state runs a database, and the national site missingmoney.com searches many states at once. Old paychecks, utility deposits and dormant accounts end up there.
6. **Contact former employers.** A pension or 401(k) from a past job may still exist, and the plan administrator can tell you.

Be wary of anyone who offers to find money for a percentage. Searching is free and you can do it yourself.

A word on privacy. A company will not tell a stranger who the beneficiary is, even after death, but it will usually tell someone who shows a death certificate and a legitimate reason, such as being the executor. Whether it confirms you personally are named depends on the company.

## What does a company need to pay me?

Each company has its own packet, but the contents are similar:

| Document | What it is for |
|---|---|
| Certified death certificate | Proof of death. Order several copies, since each company keeps one. |
| Claim form | Names the claimant and how to pay. |
| Photo ID | Matches you to the form on file. |
| Account or policy number | Helps them find the record fast. |
| Tax form such as W-9 | So the company can report any taxable payment. |
| Spouse or trust paperwork | Only if you are claiming as a spouse, or a trustee claiming for a trust |

For a retirement account, you will also decide how to receive it, as discussed in [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries). Taking a lump-sum check on an IRA triggers income tax right away, so ask about options before you sign. For a bank account with a payable-on-death designation, the process is usually shorter. See [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).

## Do I have to wait for probate?

No. This is the part that surprises people. Beneficiary forms pass the asset outside probate, so you do not need letters from the court to claim an account that names you. The company wants the form on file and your proof of identity, not the will. The executor may still need to know about it, because the asset can affect final taxes and, in some cases, the estate's ability to pay debts. You can read more in [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account).

## How long will it take?

A realistic range for a clean claim is a few weeks. Insurers often pay within a month or two of a complete claim. Retirement account custodians move at their own pace, and a large brokerage may take a couple of weeks to set up an inherited account in your name. Delays tend to come from:

- A missing certified death certificate or a mismatch in names.
- A policy that is within its contestability period.
- A form that was never processed.
- Two people who both claim to be the beneficiary.
- A beneficiary who cannot be located.

None of those mean you lose the money. They mean you need more paperwork.

## What if something goes wrong?

**The form cannot be found.** Ask the company to search its records, including imaging systems and prior administrators. If the owner changed jobs or the company was acquired, the records may sit with the successor. Meanwhile, the plan or policy usually has a default order that applies.

**Your name is misspelled or the relationship is wrong.** If it clearly refers to you, companies often accept ID plus a short signed statement. If another person might fit the description, they may require a court order.

**The company says someone else is named.** Ask for a copy of the form, the date it was signed and whether it was changed recently. If the date raises questions, such as a change made after a dementia diagnosis, talk to an attorney. Our article on [whether a power of attorney can change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary) explains how those changes are challenged.

**Two beneficiaries disagree about the split.** The form usually controls, and the company will not referee. If the percentages are unclear, the company may hold the funds until all claimants sign or a court rules.

**No living beneficiary exists.** The money generally falls to the estate and into probate. [Life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) covers what that looks like for policies.

## Should I tell my own beneficiaries they are named?

Most of the time, yes, in general terms. You do not have to share amounts. But a child who knows an account exists and which company holds it can begin the claim within days of a death. A child who has never heard of it may not find it for years. Telling people also avoids the opposite problem, where a beneficiary learns of the form at the funeral and assumes the worst about why they were or were not named. If the split is uneven, a short conversation or letter explaining why is worth the awkwardness.

## What happens with taxes?

Life insurance proceeds are generally not income to the beneficiary. Retirement accounts are different, since withdrawals from a traditional IRA or 401(k) are taxed as ordinary income. Bank accounts are not taxed on the principal. Many inherited assets get a stepped-up basis, which can reduce tax when sold. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). Keep all statements for your tax preparer.

## What should I tell my own family?

If you are the one who will leave accounts to others, do the people who follow you a favor. Keep a simple list of institutions, account types and where papers are, and let your executor know where it is. It does not need balances or passwords. Most of the searching above exists because nobody wrote the list down.
