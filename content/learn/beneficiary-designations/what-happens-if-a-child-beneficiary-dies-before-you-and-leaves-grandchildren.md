Your three children are named on your IRA, each for a third. Then your oldest dies, leaving two young kids. You may assume those two kids now take their parent's third. On many forms, they do not.

This page explains what usually happens when a named child dies first, how per stirpes wording changes it, and the form-level fixes that keep a deceased child's share in that child's family. It is general education for Illinois and New Jersey readers, not legal advice.

## What happens when a beneficiary dies before the account owner?

The beneficiary is treated as if they never existed, and the share goes elsewhere. Where it goes depends on the form. If you named contingent beneficiaries, they step up. If you named several primary beneficiaries without per stirpes wording, the usual rule is that the surviving primaries split the whole account. If nobody named survives, the account usually falls back to the company's default: a spouse, then children, or your estate.

This is a rule about the contract with the bank, insurer or plan, and not about family fairness. Our overview of [primary and contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) shows the pattern. A will does not override it, because a form with a named beneficiary passes outside the will. See [which controls: your will, a trust, a joint account or a beneficiary form](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form).

## What does per stirpes mean for a deceased child's share?

Per stirpes means "by branch." Each branch of the family keeps the share its head would have received. If a child has died, that child's children divide that child's share.

Take an example with invented numbers. Renata, 72, has a $450,000 IRA with her three children, Paul, Lena and Mateo, named for one third each. Mateo dies at 44, leaving two children, Sofia and Dani. Renata dies a year later.

| Form wording | Paul | Lena | Sofia | Dani |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No contingent, no per stirpes | $225,000 | $225,000 | $0 | $0 |
| Per stirpes | $150,000 | $150,000 | $75,000 | $75,000 |
| Contingent: "Mateo's children" | $150,000 | $150,000 | $75,000 | $75,000 |

In the first case, Sofia and Dani lose the whole share through a form decision made years before. In the second and third, they receive $75,000 each. A per capita form that lets grandchildren share equally with their surviving aunt and uncle would give each of the four people $112,500, which is a third option that few owners intend. Our article on [leaving an inheritance to grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/leaving-an-inheritance-to-grandchildren) covers the family side of that choice.

## Do company forms let you choose per stirpes?

Some do and some do not. Many life insurers and brokerages have a box for per stirpes on the beneficiary page. Others, including many employer 401(k) plans, offer only a list of names, with a default that the company applies when a named person dies. Read the form's fine print, call the plan administrator, and ask what happens to a share when one of several primary beneficiaries has died. Get the answer in writing.

If the form has no box, there are three practical options.

1. **Name the grandchildren directly as contingent beneficiaries.** Write each full name and date of birth, with the share each gets, for example "Sofia Reyes, 50% of Mateo's share." Update the form whenever a grandchild is born.
2. **Name a trust as contingent beneficiary** for the grandchildren, written to be accepted by the plan. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).
3. **Add a written attachment** if the company allows one. Some will accept a typed addendum signed by you; others reject anything off the form.

Our guide to [beneficiary form wording](/learn/beneficiary-designations/beneficiary-form-wording-for-trusts-charities-and-overseas-beneficiaries) helps with names and addresses.

## What about minor grandchildren?

A child under 18 cannot take control of an account. If the money goes to them outright, the company will usually require a court-appointed guardian or custodian before paying out. Naming a trustee for the grandchildren in a trust, or a custodian under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, avoids a court process in most cases. Read [naming a minor or incapacitated person as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary) and [what happens when a minor inherits a house or retirement account](/learn/guardianship/what-happens-when-a-minor-inherits-a-house-or-retirement-account).

Retirement accounts add a tax clock. Grandchildren are generally not "eligible designated beneficiaries," so they usually must withdraw the whole inherited account within 10 years. The special rules for a minor child apply to the owner's own minor child, not a grandchild. Our article on [leaving an IRA or 401(k) to children or grandchildren](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-an-ira-or-401k-to-children-or-grandchildren) shows how that works year by year.

## Is there a difference between a bank account, a policy and an IRA?

Yes, in the details. A payable-on-death bank account tends to follow the bank's own form and state law. A life insurance policy follows the policy contract, and an insurer will often pay the contingent beneficiary or the estate if the primary has died. A retirement plan follows its plan document, which may default to a spouse. If you own all three, check all three. The fastest review is the [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit).

Some states have statutes that apply "anti-lapse" rules to certain non-probate transfers, and others apply them only to wills. Illinois and New Jersey have anti-lapse rules for wills, and our article on [when a beneficiary dies first in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/when-a-beneficiary-dies-first-in-new-jersey) describes the New Jersey version. Do not assume a statute will rescue a form. The safest course is to write the result you want.

## What goes wrong when the surviving parent is the contingent beneficiary?

A common setup is a child named as primary, with that child's spouse as contingent. It looks tidy until the child dies. The widowed son-in-law now owns the money outright, and it can pass to a new spouse, a new family or a creditor, while the grandchildren get nothing from you. Mariam, 66, learned this when her daughter died and she realized her IRA form still named the daughter's husband as backup. She changed the form that week to name her two grandsons, each as one half of the daughter's share, with a trustee managing the money until age 25. The change took one signed page and cost nothing.

## What should you do today?

- Pull every beneficiary form for IRAs, 401(k)s, annuities, life insurance and POD accounts.
- Check which primary beneficiaries have died, divorced or fallen out of touch.
- Decide whether grandchildren should take a deceased child's share, and add them by name or per stirpes.
- Add a trust or custodian for any grandchild under 18 or any with special needs.
- Save a dated copy of each confirmation.
- Set a reminder to review after every birth, death or marriage; the [how often to review beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-often-to-review-beneficiary-designations) article suggests a schedule.
