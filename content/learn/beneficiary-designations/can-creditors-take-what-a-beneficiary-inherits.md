Two different worries tend to be tangled together here. One is about the person who died: they had debts, and you are named on their account. The other is about you: you are about to inherit, and you have a lawsuit, a divorce, or a back-tax bill hanging over you.

The answers are not the same, so this article takes them one at a time. For how beneficiary designations work in general, see our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Do I have to pay the debts of the person who died?

Not out of your own money, in nearly all cases. Debts belong to the person who incurred them and are paid from their estate. A beneficiary who simply receives an account owes nothing for the owner's credit card balances, medical bills or personal loans.

You can become responsible in a few narrow ways: you co-signed the loan, you shared a joint account or card, your state has a filial responsibility law for certain care costs, or you promised in writing to pay. Debt collectors sometimes call family members and imply an obligation. You are generally allowed to say you are not the executor and will not discuss it, and to refer them to the estate. Our article on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) lays out the order creditors get paid.

### Can the estate reach money that passed by beneficiary form?

Usually not, but there are exceptions. Accounts that pass by a form skip probate, so the executor generally does not control them. Many states, however, allow a court to reach non-probate assets in limited cases where the probate estate cannot cover funeral costs, taxes, or support for a surviving spouse or child. Medicaid estate recovery may also reach some non-probate assets in states that define the estate broadly. Life insurance and retirement accounts often have extra protections. Because this varies, an attorney should review the rules in your state before an executor or heir takes a position.

Consider Sofia, whose father Paul died with $40,000 in credit card debt and almost no probate assets. Paul had named Sofia on a $90,000 IRA. In most states Sofia keeps the IRA, and the card companies absorb the loss. If instead Paul had named his estate, the same IRA would have been available to pay the cards. That is one reason the form matters.

## Can my own creditors take what I inherit?

Often yes. Once the money is yours, ordinary creditors can pursue it the same way they pursue your paycheck or savings. A judgment creditor can garnish a bank account that includes an inheritance. Child support and back taxes usually get priority and move quickly, and a tax lien can attach to inherited assets.

The picture is more mixed for particular account types:

- **Cash in a bank account:** little protection. It is treated like any other savings.
- **Inherited IRA or 401(k) in your name:** not protected in federal bankruptcy. Some states protect it by statute.
- **Spousal rollover into your own IRA:** usually protected like your own retirement account.
- **Life insurance proceeds:** varies by state, and often protected only for a limited amount or time.
- **Real estate:** may be shielded by a homestead exemption, with limits.
- **Assets left in a spendthrift trust:** strong protection if the trust is drafted properly.

## Are inherited IRAs protected from bankruptcy?

Not under federal law. In 2014 the U.S. Supreme Court held, in *Clark v. Rameker*, that money in an inherited IRA is not "retirement funds" for the federal bankruptcy exemption, because the heir can withdraw it at any time and has to take it out on a schedule. So a non-spouse heir who files for bankruptcy may lose the account to creditors.

Some states have changed their own laws to protect inherited IRAs, and a few protect them only up to a cap. If you are a spouse and roll the account into your own IRA, the usual protection for your own IRA applies. That distinction is one reason spouses pick the rollover. We compare the choices in [spousal rollover vs. inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira).

## Are inherited accounts protected in a divorce?

Sometimes, but do not count on it. In most states, an inheritance received by one spouse is treated as that spouse's separate property. The shield erodes if you deposit it into a joint account, use it to pay off a joint mortgage, or let it blend with marital funds. Courts also look at inheritances when dividing property or setting support, even when they do not divide the inheritance itself.

If you expect to inherit and are married, or about to be, keep the money in an account with only your name, avoid mixing, and ask an attorney about a prenuptial agreement. In community property states such as [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), an inheritance is generally still separate, but the income it produces may not be.

## What about back taxes, child support and lawsuits?

These move to the front of the line. A person who owes child support can have an inheritance intercepted, and many states run payouts of life insurance and unclaimed money past child support records. The IRS can levy bank accounts, including one that just received an inheritance. A plaintiff with a judgment can freeze an account until a court sorts it out. None of this requires the deceased to have done anything wrong. It follows the heir's own history.

There is a timing point worth knowing. Money is easiest to protect before it arrives. After it lands in an account in your name, the options narrow to exemptions and legal arguments. Before it arrives, a parent or grandparent can route it to a trust, or you can sometimes disclaim it so that it passes to the next person in line. Disclaiming means giving it up completely, so it only makes sense when you are comfortable with where it goes.

## Can a trust protect a beneficiary's inheritance?

Yes, and this is one of the main reasons to name one. If an account pays into a trust that has a [spendthrift clause](/glossary/spendthrift-clause), the trustee holds the assets, and the beneficiary's creditors usually cannot demand them before they are paid out. A trust can also hold the share for a beneficiary with creditor trouble, an unstable marriage, or a gambling or substance problem. The trade-off is that the trustee has to follow the document, and retirement accounts paid to a trust have tax rules that need careful drafting. Read [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) and [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) before you decide. You can also see why the protection has limits in [does a living trust protect assets from creditors](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-protect-assets-from-creditors).

## What should I do if I am inheriting and have debts?

1. Talk to an attorney before you accept or move any money. A beneficiary can sometimes disclaim an inheritance, but only before accepting it, and disclaiming does not always work against federal tax liens or Medicaid claims.
2. Do not pay old debts of the deceased from your own funds without advice.
3. Keep inherited funds separate and documented.
4. If you owe child support or back taxes, expect those claims to reach the inheritance.
5. Ask whether a spendthrift trust could be set up for future inheritances, since this is a conversation for the person leaving the money.
