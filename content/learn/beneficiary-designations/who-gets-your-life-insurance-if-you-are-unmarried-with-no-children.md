If you are single and have no children, your life insurance goes to the person or charity you name on the beneficiary form. No one else has a claim to it, including a sibling or a parent who assumes they are next in line. That freedom is the whole point of the form, and also the risk: the form is the only thing speaking for you.

This page explains who actually receives the money in each situation, what happens when the form is blank or out of date, and how to set it up so your choice is the one that gets paid. It sits inside our [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Who gets my life insurance if I am unmarried with no kids?

The person named as primary beneficiary gets it, and nobody else. A policy is a contract between you and the insurer, and the beneficiary line tells the insurer where to send the check. Your will, your family tree and your relationships do not enter into it.

That means a long-term partner you never married receives nothing unless named. So does a close friend, a godchild or a stepchild. Meanwhile, a former partner still named from ten years ago will be paid, even if you split up and even if your parents are furious.

Marital status matters in only one way here. A spouse has special protections on employer plans and in some estates, and you have no spouse, so nothing overrides your choice. If you are weighing whether to marry, read [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) for how the rules differ.

## What if no beneficiary is named, or the named person died?

Then the policy's default order applies. The order is written in the contract, and for group life insurance through your job it is written in the plan document. It varies, and a typical version looks like this:

1. Surviving spouse.
2. Surviving children, in equal shares.
3. Surviving parents.
4. Surviving siblings.
5. Your estate.

A single person with no children skips the first two steps. Some policies stop at "estate" instead of listing parents and siblings, so you cannot tell from this list which version you have. Ask the insurer or HR for the written default order.

If the money lands in your estate, three bad things can follow. It goes through probate, which takes months in Illinois and New Jersey. It becomes available to pay your creditors. And it passes under your will, or under state law if there is no will. Our article on [never naming a beneficiary](/learn/what-if/no-beneficiary-named) walks through that path in detail.

## Who inherits if there is no will either?

State intestacy law fills the gap, and for a person with no spouse and no children it points to your parents and siblings. In Illinois the order, with no spouse and no descendants, runs to parents, brothers and sisters, and their descendants. New Jersey also looks first to parents, then to siblings and their children, and then further out.

An unmarried partner of any length is not an heir in either state. Neither is a friend. Consider Priya, 41, who has a $250,000 employer life policy, no children and a partner of eleven years named Marcus. Her form lists her mother, who died last year. Because Priya never updated it and never made a will, the policy defaults to her estate, her father and her brother inherit, and Marcus gets nothing. A new form naming Marcus and a contingent beneficiary would have prevented the whole result.

## Which choices work for a single person with no children?

| You want to protect | A workable setup |
|---|---|
| A long-term partner | Name them as primary by full legal name and relationship. Add a will and a durable power of attorney, since neither is automatic. |
| Parents who depend on you | Name them directly, or use a trust if one has dementia or receives needs-based benefits. |
| A sibling's children | Name the children in shares, or a trust that a trustee manages until each turns the age you set. |
| A charity | Name it with its exact legal name and tax ID, and a contingent person in case it closes. |
| A pet | Name a caretaker and a pet trust, because the animal cannot take the money. |
| Someone on SSI or Medicaid | A special needs trust as beneficiary, so the payout does not end benefits. |

Naming a minor directly is the mistake we see most. A court-supervised guardianship of the money can result, and the child may receive everything at 18 or 21. [Trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) are the usual fix.

## Should the policy go into a trust?

For most single people a direct beneficiary is enough. A trust earns its cost when the person you are protecting cannot manage a lump sum, when you want the money paid out over years, or when your estate is big enough for tax planning. Only large estates face federal tax, since the 2026 exclusion is $15,000,000 per person. State taxes bite earlier. Illinois has its own estate tax with a much lower threshold, and New Jersey has an inheritance tax on transfers to some relatives, so look at [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) if your total assets are significant.

## Can my family challenge who I named?

Rarely. A beneficiary form is a contract, and courts enforce it as written unless someone proves fraud, undue influence or that you lacked capacity when you signed. Relatives who feel shortchanged have no right to a share simply because they are related. If you name a friend over a sibling, write down why while you remember, and keep the dated form. Our article on [when a beneficiary designation can be challenged](/learn/beneficiary-designations/when-a-beneficiary-designation-can-be-challenged) lists the grounds.

## Does it matter if the policy is through work or bought on my own?

It matters for the default order and for how you change the form. An individual policy has a contract you can read, and you change the beneficiary with the insurer. A group policy through your employer has a plan document, a separate portal, and often a lapse date: the coverage can end when you leave the job, and a conversion window is short. Many single people forget the group policy exists, so their family finds it only when a final paycheck stub mentions a deduction. List every policy, group and individual, in one place and tell your executor where it lives.

## A checklist before you file the form

- Primary beneficiary: full legal name, relationship, date of birth.
- Contingent beneficiary: someone who is not in the same household or accident risk as the primary.
- Percentages that add to 100% at each level.
- Written confirmation from the insurer that the change was recorded.
- A will that names an executor, so you are not relying on state law for anything the policy misses.
- A note to your executor about which policies exist and where the statements are.

If you also carry a retirement account, check that its beneficiaries match your intent, since [retirement account beneficiary forms](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) follow their own rules.
