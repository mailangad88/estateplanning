A common piece of advice goes around families: put your daughter's name on your bank account so she can pay your bills, and the money skips probate when you die. It sounds efficient, and sometimes it is. It is also one of the more frequent ways a good plan goes sideways.

Adding someone as joint owner and naming someone as beneficiary look similar on a bank form. They have different effects while you are alive and after you are gone. This page, part of our [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) library, compares them side by side.

## The short version

- Joint ownership gives the other person rights today. A beneficiary has none until your death.
- A joint owner's creditors, divorce and mistakes can reach the account.
- Both methods can skip probate, but only a beneficiary designation leaves you in sole control.
- Whichever you choose, check how it fits with your will and any trust.

## How do the two options differ?

| Question | Joint with survivorship | Beneficiary or POD/TOD |
|---|---|---|
| Who can withdraw during your life? | Either owner | Only you |
| Whose creditors can reach the account? | Both owners' | Yours only |
| What happens at your death? | Survivor owns it | Named person claims it |
| Can you change your mind? | Not alone; the joint owner has rights | Yes, at any time |
| Does the account skip probate? | Yes | Yes |
| Gift tax issues? | Possible on some assets | None until death |
| Control if you lose capacity | The joint owner can act | Your power of attorney acts |
| Fit for unequal shares | Poor | Good, with percentages |

For the ownership forms in more detail, see [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) and the page on [which accounts can have a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/which-accounts-can-have-a-beneficiary).

## What goes wrong with joint accounts?

Joint ownership works well between spouses. Between a parent and an adult child it creates problems that the form never mentions.

Here is Raymond, 76, who has three children. He adds his daughter Nia to his checking and savings, since she lives nearby and helps him. Raymond intends everything to be split three ways. When he dies, the bank pays the accounts to Nia, because that is how survivorship works. Her brothers learn the account was never part of the estate. Nia may feel a moral duty to share, or not. The law does not require her to.

Other risks:

1. **Creditors.** If Nia is sued or owes back taxes, the account may be fair game, even if the money was all Raymond's.
2. **Divorce.** In a divorce, a court may consider the account when dividing property, depending on state law.
3. **Withdrawals.** Nia can empty the account while Raymond is alive. Banks do not ask whose money it was.
4. **Medicaid.** Adding an owner can look like a transfer, which matters for eligibility. See [Medicaid look-back](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).
5. **Accidental disinheritance.** A joint account passes by survivorship even if your will says otherwise.

Real estate has its own rules. Adding a child to a deed makes a present gift of part of the house, and the child may lose the step-up in basis on that share. Our page on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) covers that trap.

## What about a convenience account?

If the goal is help paying bills, there are better tools than joint ownership.

- A **durable financial power of attorney** lets a trusted person act for you if you cannot, without owning anything. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
- Many banks offer a **convenience signer** or an agent designation, which allows access without ownership.
- A **revocable trust** with you as trustee lets a successor step in on incapacity.

Some states presume that a surviving joint owner takes the money, while others let the estate show that the account was for convenience only. That fight is expensive and usually avoidable.

## When is joint ownership the right call?

For spouses, it often is. Joint ownership with survivorship, or tenancy by the entirety for real estate in states that offer it, gives the survivor immediate control. Unmarried partners sometimes use it for a shared household account when each contributes and each trusts the other.

For parent and child, it is the right call only if you genuinely want the child to be an owner today and accept the risks. If your estate involves more than one child, or any chance of a remarriage, beneficiary forms are cleaner.

## How do beneficiary designations work instead?

You name one or more people on the account. While you live, they have no rights. At your death, they claim the account with a death certificate and ID. In bank accounts the form is usually called payable-on-death (POD). On brokerage accounts it is transfer-on-death (TOD), and retirement accounts and life insurance use a beneficiary form. [Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) walks through the setup.

Good practice:

1. Name primary and contingent beneficiaries, with percentages that total 100.
2. Say how a share passes if a beneficiary dies first, such as to that person's children.
3. Keep a list of accounts with the date you last reviewed each.
4. Review after a birth, death, marriage or divorce.
5. Tell your family where the list is.

## Should I use a TOD account or a revocable trust?

A TOD or POD registration is quick, usually free, and works well for one or two accounts going to adults. It does not help if a beneficiary is a minor or has special needs, if you want money paid out in stages, or if you want to plan for your own incapacity. A [revocable trust](/learn/trusts) can do all of that, and it also ties the accounts to your wider plan. A [transfer on death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) plays the same role for a house in states that allow it, and [is a TOD deed better than a trust](/learn/probate/is-a-tod-deed-better-than-a-trust) compares them.

A trust can be named as beneficiary on an account if your institution allows it. Check [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) for the wording and the pitfalls.

## What about a beneficiary who lives abroad?

Many institutions will pay a beneficiary who lives overseas, but they require extra identity checks and may withhold tax on certain payments. For retirement accounts, a non-resident beneficiary can face withholding and different rules, so confirm with the plan and with a tax adviser before relying on it. For the general tax picture, see [do beneficiaries pay taxes on what they receive](/learn/beneficiary-designations/do-beneficiaries-pay-taxes-on-what-they-receive).
