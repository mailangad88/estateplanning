Someone you love can no longer manage their own money, and you are holding a power of attorney. A beneficiary form is out of date, or a relative is pressing for a change. Or the reverse: your parent changed everything last spring, and now you wonder who really made that decision.

The short answer is that beneficiary designations are among the most personal choices a person makes, and the law treats them that way. This article explains what an agent or guardian can do, where the limits are, and how these changes get challenged. For the background on how the forms work, see our guide to [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Can a power of attorney change my beneficiary?

Only if the document clearly says so. A [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets an agent pay bills, manage accounts and handle property. Changing who inherits is a different kind of act. It does not help the owner while alive. It rearranges what happens at death. Because of that, many states require that the document grant the power in so many words, and many lawyers draft it as a separate, limited authority or leave it out.

Even with the authority, an agent faces limits:

- The agent owes a [fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty) to the owner, which means acting for the owner and not for themselves.
- The agent should follow what the owner would have wanted, based on earlier estate planning and past statements.
- A change that breaks from an established plan invites questions.

Our guide to [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) lists the other limits, including making or changing a will.

### What do companies actually do?

In practice, many insurers and retirement plan administrators will not take a beneficiary change from an agent at all. Their forms ask for the owner's signature, and their legal departments have seen fraud cases. Some will accept it with a notarized copy of the power of attorney, a certification and a letter explaining the reason. Others refuse unless a court approves. Expect a delay and a request for more paperwork.

## Can an agent name themselves as beneficiary?

In most states, no, or only under very narrow conditions. This is called self-dealing. Suppose Gerald is the agent for his mother, Lois, who has Alzheimer's. Gerald has two sisters. He signs a form moving Lois's $250,000 annuity from "my three children equally" to "Gerald." Courts see a pattern like this often. The change benefits the agent, it departs from a prior plan, and the owner could not confirm it. A judge is likely to treat it as presumptively improper, and Gerald could be ordered to repay the money, removed as agent, or reported to authorities.

There is a narrow exception. If the owner's power of attorney or earlier written plan explicitly allowed the agent to receive gifts, and the amount is consistent with the owner's long-standing pattern, a court may allow it. The burden is on the agent.

## What about a guardian or conservator?

A court-appointed guardian or conservator is more closely supervised than an agent. Because the court oversees the estate, the guardian generally cannot change a beneficiary without a judge's approval, and judges tend to leave the existing designations alone. The usual reason to ask is a real change in circumstances, such as an ex-spouse who is still named or a beneficiary who has died. Our article on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) compares the two roles.

## How do I contest a change made by someone with dementia?

You challenge it in court, usually on one of two grounds.

1. **Lack of capacity.** The owner did not understand what they were signing, who their family was, or what they owned on the day they signed. Dementia is not an automatic bar. Many people with early memory loss have clear days. What counts is the day of the signing. See [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) for the general standard.
2. **Undue influence.** Someone with power over the owner pressured them into a change they would not otherwise have made. [Undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) is easier to prove when the person who benefited also arranged the appointment, drove the owner there, was in the room, or controlled who could visit.

### Steps to take

1. Ask the executor or the company for the beneficiary history, including dates and the signed form. If a company will not release it to you, an attorney can often get it with a court order.
2. Collect medical records from around the date of the change, along with notes from caregivers and doctors.
3. Write a timeline: when the diagnosis came, when the change was made, who helped.
4. Do not wait. Payouts happen fast, and funds that have been distributed to a beneficiary are harder to recover. Time limits vary by state.
5. Talk to an attorney who handles contested estates before you confront the person named.

A related situation, a parent with dementia who never signed a power of attorney, is covered in [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

## What if my parent changed everything shortly before death?

A late change is a flag, not a verdict. Many people change a beneficiary during a final illness for good reasons: a divorce they never got around to handling, a death in the family, or a rift that finally ended. Courts weigh several things.

| What the court looks at | Points toward a valid change. | Points toward a challenge. |
|---|---|---|
| Who arranged it | The owner called the company directly. | The beneficiary scheduled and attended. |
| Prior plans | The change matches earlier wills or statements. | It reverses a long-standing plan. |
| Health on the date | Doctor's notes show clear thinking. | Notes show confusion that same week. |
| Secrecy | The owner told others about it. | Other family were kept away. |
| Who benefits | A longtime supporter of the owner. | The caregiver or agent who gained control. |

## Can I change a beneficiary without telling the old one?

Yes, in nearly all cases. A beneficiary of a revocable designation has no right to notice. The company will not call them, and you need not. The exceptions are court orders and agreements, for example a divorce decree that requires you to keep your ex-spouse named on a life policy to secure support. Ignoring one can create a claim against your estate even if the form says otherwise.

One practical note for agents: if you think a change really is needed, write down why before you act, tell the other family members who would be affected, and ask the company what it requires. A transparent, documented request is far less likely to be attacked later than a quiet one, and it protects you personally if a sibling later asks questions.

## How can I protect my own wishes?

- Review your beneficiaries while you are well, and keep proof. A dated confirmation from the company shows what you intended.
- Choose your agent with care, and consider adding limits in the power of attorney, such as a ban on changing beneficiaries or making gifts to the agent. Our article on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) discusses safeguards.
- Let a second person, such as a sibling or accountant, see statements.
- Look at signs of [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) early.
