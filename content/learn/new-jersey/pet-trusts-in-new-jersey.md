Pets are property in the eyes of the law. You cannot leave your dog a bank account, and a verbal promise from a neighbor to take your cat is not something anyone can enforce. New Jersey gives you a way to turn that promise into a plan: a trust for the care of an animal, authorized by the state's version of the Uniform Trust Code.

Our national article on [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) explains the idea. This one is about what New Jersey's statute says, how it works with the rest of a New Jersey plan and where the limits are. It is part of our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What does New Jersey law say about pet trusts?

The rule is N.J.S.A. 3B:31-24, titled "Trust for care of animal." It was enacted as part of the Uniform Trust Code, which is Chapter 31 of Title 3B, in P.L. 2015, c.276, approved January 19, 2016. The Code took effect on the 180th day after enactment.

The section does four things.

1. It allows a trust for the care of an animal alive during your lifetime.
2. It says the trust ends when that animal dies, or when the last surviving animal dies if the trust covers more than one.
3. It names who can enforce the trust.
4. It controls what happens to money the animal does not need.

Notice what the statute does not do. It does not say how much money you may put in, and it does not describe the caregiver's duties. Those details are yours to write, and they decide whether the plan works.

## Does the pet have to be alive when I sign?

The trust covers an animal alive during your lifetime. That is a real limit for people who will adopt again. If you have a nine-year-old retriever now and expect to have another dog at 75, the trust you sign today covers the retriever. A dog you adopt later is better handled by updating the trust or by a gift in your will to a named person with instructions.

It also means you can cover several animals at once, such as two cats and a horse. The trust lasts until the last one dies, so think about the animals' different lifespans. A parrot can outlive its owner by decades. A trust that covers a parrot and a ten-year-old cat will run on well after the cat is gone.

## Who enforces a New Jersey pet trust?

This is the part most home-made plans leave out. An animal cannot sue. The statute fills the gap: the settlor can enforce the trust, and so can a person named in its terms. If the trust names no one, a court can appoint a person. Anyone with an interest in the animal's welfare can ask the court to appoint an enforcer, or to remove one.

In practice, name two roles on purpose.

| Role | What they do | Who often fills it |
|---|---|---|
| Trustee | Holds and pays out the money | A trusted relative, friend or a professional |
| Caregiver | Has the animal day to day | A person who has already agreed |
| Enforcer | Checks that care matches the trust | A person who is not the caregiver |

Keeping the caregiver and the enforcer apart is the point. If the person who has the dog is also the only one who can complain about the dog's care, nobody is checking.

## What happens to money the animal does not need?

Trust property may be used only for the animal's care. That is the central rule, and it protects the animal from a trustee who treats the fund as spending money.

The statute also gives a way out for excess. If a court finds that the trust property is worth more than the intended use requires, the excess is no longer limited to that use. When the trust ends or any property is left over, it goes to you if you are living, or otherwise to your estate, unless the trust terms provide differently.

That last clause is the planning point. If you say nothing, the leftover money returns to your estate and passes under your will or, without one, under intestacy. Most owners prefer to name a person or an animal welfare charity to take what is left. A named recipient also gives the caregiver a reason to keep costs honest, since it is not the caregiver's money.

## How much should a pet trust hold?

The statute gives no figure, and nobody can give you one without knowing the animal. Work it out like this:

1. Add up yearly costs: food, routine veterinary care, grooming, boarding and pet insurance.
2. Estimate the animal's remaining life, using the vet's view of its breed and age.
3. Multiply, and add a cushion for emergencies. Surgery on a large dog can cost more than a year of ordinary care.
4. Subtract any gift you plan to make to the caregiver for their trouble, if you want that made separately.

A trust that holds far more than the animal can use invites exactly the excess question described above. A trust that holds too little leaves the caregiver choosing between the animal and their own budget.

Imagine Pauline, 68, who has two rescue greyhounds and a nephew in Montclair who adores them. She leaves $40,000 in a trust named in her will, with her nephew as caregiver, a friend as trustee and her sister as enforcer. When the second dog dies, the remaining money goes to a greyhound rescue. Every role is filled, and nobody is left guessing what she wanted. Those are invented numbers. Yours will depend on your animals.

## Will vs. living trust: when does the money arrive?

A pet trust can be written into a will, which makes it take effect only after you die and after probate. Or it can sit in a living trust you sign now. The difference matters for pets because they need care the moment you cannot give it. If you go into the hospital, a testamentary trust has not started yet.

For that reason, many owners combine tools. A pet trust in the will or living trust handles death. A [durable power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) with clear authority to pay pet expenses handles incapacity. And a signed note naming an emergency contact handles the first 48 hours. The [living trust](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) article explains the trade-offs of a trust that starts now. A will can also create a trust, which then goes through the county Surrogate like the rest of the estate. See [New Jersey probate](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process).

## What a pet trust cannot do

- It cannot make a reluctant caregiver loving. Ask first, and name a backup.
- It should not be counted on to cover animals you adopt after signing. Update it each time.
- It cannot cut anyone out of the estate. Spouses and others keep their rights. See [New Jersey's elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse) for how a spouse's claim interacts with a trust.
