Most people spend years hoping to inherit something. A smaller group sits across from a lawyer and asks how to turn it down. A brother who is already comfortable may want his share to go to his children. A man with a judgment against him may not want the money to land in his bank account. A niece may not want a house that carries a heavy mortgage and a failing roof.

New Jersey has a statute for exactly this, in chapter 9 of Title 3B. This article explains how the disclaimer works, what ruins one, and where the property goes afterward. The general idea is covered in our national piece on [how to disclaim an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance). Here we stay with New Jersey law and the state guide at [New Jersey estate planning and probate](/learn/new-jersey).

## What does it mean to disclaim in New Jersey?

A disclaimer is a refusal to accept property, not a gift of it. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:9-8, a person who disclaims is treated as never having received the interest, and the property passes as if the person had died first. That phrase does the work. Nothing passes through your hands, so nothing is taxed as a gift from you.

The statute reaches well beyond wills. You can disclaim a share under a will or trust, an intestate share, a surviving joint tenant's interest, a payable-on-death account, and benefits under a contract or insurance policy (N.J.S.A. 3B:9-2). You may refuse everything or part: a fraction, a dollar amount, one named asset or a limited interest.

## How do you do it? A step-by-step path

1. **Do nothing with the asset.** Do not deposit the check, move into the house, collect rent or sign a deed. Acceptance is the single most common way a disclaimer is lost.
2. **Read what happens next.** Check the will's backup clause, or the intestacy chart in [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey). Make sure you accept the result.
3. **Sign a written disclaimer before a notary.** It must describe the property, give the municipality and county if it is real estate, and declare the refusal and its extent (N.J.S.A. 3B:9-3).
4. **File and deliver it.** For a will or an intestacy, file with the Surrogate where the estate is opened and send a copy to the executor or administrator. For other assets, deliver it to the bank, insurer or person who holds the asset. Delivery can be in person, by registered or certified mail, or any other way reasonably likely to reach them (N.J.S.A. 3B:9-6).
5. **Let the fiduciary tell the next taker.** The executor must promptly notify the people who now take the property. A slip in giving that notice does not undo the disclaimer.

The county clerk or register of deeds keeps a book of disclaimers for real property, and those records are open to the public (N.J.S.A. 3B:9-7). If you disclaim a house, expect that the refusal will be a matter of public record.

## Who gets the property after a disclaimer?

The next person in line, as if you were out of the picture. If you were left a share by a parent's will and the will has no backup clause, your own children generally step into your place when the law lets descendants take by representation. If you were the only survivor of a joint account, the money goes to the estate of the last other owner. Where several joint owners survive, it goes to them equally.

| Situation | Where a disclaimed share usually goes |
|---|---|
| Will names you and a backup beneficiary | To the backup, as written |
| Will names you, no backup, and you have children | Often to your children, by representation |
| No will, you are one of three children | Split among the other heirs, with your children in your place if the law allows |
| Joint account, you are the only survivor | The estate of the last other owner |
| Life insurance with no contingent beneficiary | Usually the estate, or as the policy terms provide |

The table is a guide. A will can override the default, and the answer for your family depends on the exact wording. Our article on [when a beneficiary dies first](/learn/new-jersey/when-a-beneficiary-dies-first-in-new-jersey) shows how the same backup rules work when the beneficiary is deceased rather than declining.

Think about taxes before choosing. Where the property lands may change who owes New Jersey inheritance tax, because the rate depends on the relationship between the decedent and the person who actually receives the property. Run the comparison with our guide to [the New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) before you sign.

## What can bar a disclaimer?

Several things, and most of them are actions that feel harmless at the time. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:9-9, any of these bars the right.

- Transferring, assigning, conveying, encumbering or pledging the property, or contracting to do so.
- Signing a written waiver of the right to disclaim.
- Accepting the property or a benefit from it after you know it has been left to you.
- A sale after a creditor has seized the property under court process.
- A transfer that is a fraud on creditors under the Uniform Voidable Transactions Act.

If you already took part of what you were left, you can still disclaim what you have not received. Once you disclaim or waive in writing, you are bound, and so is anyone claiming through you (N.J.S.A. 3B:9-10).

Take Daniel, whose uncle left him a rental duplex. For two months he collected the rent and paid the plumber from it. When the tax bill arrived, he asked about disclaiming. By then he had accepted a benefit from the property, and the disclaimer would be hard to defend. A disclaimer is a decision you make early, before the first check.

## Can you disclaim to avoid a creditor or Medicaid?

Not safely. The creditor bar above is aimed at exactly that. If you owe a judgment and disclaim to keep the money out of reach, a creditor can argue the disclaimer was a fraudulent transfer. The same logic worries people receiving Medicaid. Benefits programs can treat a refused inheritance as an asset the person gave away, which can cause a penalty period. We cover the general rules in our article on [Medicaid and gifting](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period). Talk to an attorney before anyone who receives or may need benefits disclaims.

For general discussion of what creditors can reach once you accept, see [can creditors take what a beneficiary inherits](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits).

## What is the nine-month federal rule?

For the federal gift tax, a disclaimer counts as qualified only if several conditions are met. Under the Treasury regulations (26 C.F.R. 25.2518-2) it must be irrevocable, in writing, and delivered within nine months of the transfer, which for an inheritance is the date of death. Someone under 21 has until nine months after turning 21. You must not have accepted the interest or its benefits, and the property must pass to someone else without your directing it. A surviving spouse has a special rule when the property passes to the spouse as a result of the disclaimer.

If you miss these conditions, the state-law disclaimer may still be valid, but the IRS can treat the property as passing to you first and then as a gift from you to the next taker. That is the reason many attorneys calendar nine months from death and draft within the first few months.

## When a guardian or agent wants to disclaim

A fiduciary, or an agent under a power of attorney, can disclaim for someone else, but the statute generally requires court approval first, unless the governing document expressly gives that authority (N.J.S.A. 3B:9-4). The court considers whether the disclaimer is advisable and whether it harms the person represented. If you manage a parent's affairs, read our article on the [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) to see what an agent can and cannot do.
