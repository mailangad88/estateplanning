Your mother has been diagnosed with dementia, the bills are piling up, and someone has to be able to talk to the bank. The decision in front of you is whether a power of attorney will do or whether you need a court to appoint a guardian. In New Jersey the answer comes down to one question: can your parent still sign a power of attorney?

Our [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) page explains the court process in detail and [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) covers the Act. This page helps you choose between them. It is part of our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## How do you decide between a power of attorney and guardianship?

Work through three questions in order.

1. **Is a signed durable power of attorney already in place, along with an advance directive?** If yes, find the originals and see whether the agent is willing and able. You may need nothing from a court.
2. **If not, can your parent still sign one?** If an attorney believes your parent understands the document and who is being named, sign it now. This is faster, cheaper and private.
3. **If your parent cannot sign, or the document exists but is failing, is there a decision that cannot wait?** A house must be sold, a facility needs consent, an account is being drained, or a bank will not accept the agent. That is guardianship territory.

## When is a power of attorney enough?

A power of attorney is enough when it exists, is durable, and the agent can use it. It must be in writing, signed and acknowledged (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.9). It survives incapacity only if the language says so (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.2). Your parent's dementia does not cancel it. Banks may still hesitate over an old form, and our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) covers that. Third parties may rely on a copy (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.11).

Health decisions need a separate document. The agent under a financial power cannot be assumed to speak for medical care, so check for an advance directive naming a health care representative, described in [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives). If none exists, our page on [who makes medical decisions without a health care proxy](/learn/new-jersey/who-makes-medical-decisions-in-new-jersey-without-a-health-care-proxy) explains the gap.

### What if your parent can still sign but has dementia?

Then move quickly. The Act sets no dementia cutoff. What matters is whether your parent understands the document when it is signed. Many families learn that a morning appointment goes better than an afternoon one, and that a doctor's note written the same day helps if anyone later questions the signing. Our article on [creating a trust or power of attorney with dementia](/learn/trusts/creating-a-trust-with-dementia-or-a-power-of-attorney) goes through the capacity question.

Steps for a power of attorney:

1. Call an estate planning attorney and describe the diagnosis honestly.
2. Choose the agent and at least one backup. Our guide to [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) helps.
3. Include durability language so the document survives incapacity.
4. Decide about gifts and pay in the document, since the Act is silent on gifts and a court may award reasonable pay if the document says nothing (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.12).
5. Sign before a notary with the original in hand.
6. Give copies to the agent and to banks, and sign the advance directive at the same visit.

## When is guardianship needed?

Guardianship is needed when your parent cannot sign a power of attorney and nobody else has legal authority. It also fits when an existing power of attorney is being abused. A court can determine a person incapacitated and appoint a guardian (N.J.S.A. 3B:12-25), and it can appoint a limited guardian if the person can still manage some tasks (N.J.S.A. 3B:12-24.1).

Two cases we see: Ruth, 83, has advanced Alzheimer's, owns a house, and has never signed anything. Her son cannot list the house or pay the assisted living bill from her account. That is a guardianship of the estate. Another family has a power of attorney, but the agent, a nephew, has stopped paying the nursing home. If their mother can no longer revoke the document, a guardian gives the family someone with authority over the agent, because the agent must answer to the guardian once one is appointed.

Whether you need the estate side depends on the assets. The New Jersey Courts' guide says it is generally needed if the person has earnings or pension income, real property, or stocks and bonds. It is generally not needed if the person has no assets or only collects Social Security. Social Security uses its own process; see [how a power of attorney works with Social Security, Medicare and the IRS](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-a-power-of-attorney-work-with-social-security-medicare-and-the-irs).

### What are the steps for a New Jersey guardianship?

The Courts' self-help guide describes filing with the Surrogate's Office in the county where the person lives, with the case heard in Superior Court. In order:

1. **Get two medical certifications.** Two physicians, or a physician and a psychologist, must each examine your parent within 30 days before filing.
2. **Assemble the forms.** The Courts list a Verified Complaint, the two certifications and a Case Information Statement. They also list a Certification of Assets, required even if you are not seeking guardianship of the estate, and a criminal and civil judgment history for the proposed guardian. The last two are an Order Fixing Guardianship Hearing Date and a proposed Judgment.
3. **File with the Surrogate.** Make at least two copies, submit the originals and one copy, and pay the $200 filing fee, in person or by mail.
4. **Serve notice.** Your parent must be served in person at least 20 days before the hearing, and relatives and any agent get notice by certified mail (R. 4:86-4).
5. **The lawyer for your parent reports.** The court appoints a lawyer, who interviews your parent and others involved in care and writes a report, including whether a less restrictive option exists.
6. **Attend the hearing.** You must prove incapacity and the need for a guardian. If you succeed, the judge signs the judgment.
7. **Qualify within 30 days.** Sign the acceptance, acknowledge the training guide, complete background screening if it applies, and post a bond if the judge orders one. Letters of Guardianship are issued after you qualify, and you need them before acting.

A guardian of the estate files an inventory within 90 days of the judgment. Periodic reports follow as the judgment sets them, and the Courts list reports at $5 per page. Our [guardian duties and accountings in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-guardian-duties-reports-and-accountings-after-appointment) page explains the reporting.

If the court finds your parent still handles some matters, expect a limited order. Parents often keep a say about where they live while losing control of money.

## What does each route cost and take away?

A power of attorney costs a lawyer's drafting fee, which we cover in [how much a power of attorney costs](/learn/power-of-attorney/how-much-does-a-power-of-attorney-cost), and it leaves your parent's legal rights alone. Guardianship costs the filing fee, fees for medical certifications, possibly your lawyer, and the reports that follow. It also changes your parent's legal status, and that is a real loss, which the Courts' process recognizes by requiring a lawyer for your parent.

Because of that, the court looks for less restrictive options. Our guide to [guardianship of an adult parent](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-of-an-adult-parent-who-can-petition-and-what-the-court-needs) and the national article on [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) go deeper on the comparison.

## What if siblings disagree?

Disagreement tends to push families into guardianship, where a judge hears everyone. Try for one family meeting with the facts on the table: what the documents say, what the bills are, who lives closest. If a sibling is misusing money, see our guide to [challenging a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-challenge-or-remove-a-power-of-attorney-agent-when-siblings-disagree) and the New Jersey page on [reporting elder financial abuse](/learn/new-jersey/how-to-report-elder-financial-abuse-in-new-jersey-adult-protective-services).
