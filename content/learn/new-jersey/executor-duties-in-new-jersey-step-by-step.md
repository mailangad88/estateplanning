Being named executor in a New Jersey will feels like an honor for about a week. Then the paperwork arrives. The good news is that the job has a sequence, and the county Surrogate, the Division of Taxation and your own beneficiaries each ask for something at a predictable point.

This article walks that sequence in the order you will meet it. It builds on the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process), which covers the filing itself, and on our general list of [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties). The rest of the state picture is in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What are the steps, in order?

| Step | Who you deal with | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Qualify and get letters | County Surrogate | After the 10th day following death |
| 2. Order short certificates | County Surrogate | With or right after probate |
| 3. Mail notice of probate | Beneficiaries and next of kin | Within 60 days of probate |
| 4. Tax return or waivers | Division of Taxation | Return and tax due in 8 months |
| 5. Pay debts and expenses | Creditors, funeral home, accountant | Through the nine-month claim window |
| 6. Account and distribute | Beneficiaries, Surrogate | After releases are signed |

## How does an executor qualify with the Surrogate?

You bring the original will, a certified death certificate and identification to the Surrogate of the county where the person lived. County Surrogates say probate and letters come only after the tenth day after death, so the first week is for gathering paper. If the will has a self-proving affidavit the visit is quick. If it does not, a witness may have to appear.

Bond is worth a sentence. The Morris County Surrogate's guide recommends that a will let the executor serve without bond, and the Burlington County guide describes a surety bond as something an administrator, who is appointed when there is no will, usually must furnish. If the will waives bond, you skip that cost.

Counties keep their own fee schedules. Gloucester County, for example, lists $100 for probate of a two-page will, with $5 for each additional page, and says that fee includes one short certificate. Your own Surrogate's website lists the current numbers.

## What is a short certificate, and how many do you need?

A short certificate is the Surrogate's proof that you are the person with authority over one asset in the decedent's sole name. Burlington County says short certificates are needed to transfer stock, close bank accounts, retitle a car and sell real estate. Monmouth County describes it as evidence of authority to transfer or sell an asset, and says you need one for each such asset.

Two practical points come from the Monmouth guidance. A certificate is typically good for up to a year, but some institutions accept only one dated within 60 days. And Monmouth charges $5 per certificate. So do not order forty on day one. Make the list first.

Sit down with the statements and sort assets before you order. Our [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) helps separate what has a beneficiary from what runs through you. Anything with a named beneficiary or a joint owner who survives does not need a short certificate.

## What must you do within 60 days of probate?

Mail a written notice of probate to every beneficiary in the will and every next of kin. The Morris County guide gives the deadline as 60 days after probate and says proof of the mailing is filed with the Surrogate. The notice says the will was probated, who the executor is, and where to get a copy. Burlington County charges a filing fee per page for the proof of mailing, so ask the Surrogate what to enclose.

Do this early. Mailing the notices in week two instead of week eight gives an unhappy relative less to complain about, and it starts the conversation about timing and expectations. The notice mechanics, including unknown addresses and gifts to charity, are in the [probate process article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process).

## How do the inheritance tax return and waivers work?

The Division of Taxation decides which form fits. If tax may be due, the executor files Form IT-R. A full return is required whenever any beneficiary falls in Class C, D or E, even if no tax ends up due. The return and any tax are due eight months after death, tax not paid by then bears 10% annual interest, and you can extend the return with Form IT-EXT but not the payment. Returns cannot be filed electronically.

If everything goes to Class A beneficiaries such as a spouse or children, a waiver may be enough. Form L-8 is a self-executing affidavit for banks, brokers and transfer agents, filed with the institution and not the Division. Real estate is different. The Division's executor guide says transfers of real estate always need a waiver from the Division, either by filing a return or, for a fully exempt estate, with Form L-9.

Banks may release up to 50% of the funds on hand before a waiver arrives, only to the executor, administrator or a joint owner. The same guide says plan for several months after filing, that audits add more, and that returns are handled in the order received. Details on rates and classes are in the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article, and the house-specific wrinkle is in [inheriting a house in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/inheriting-a-house-in-new-jersey).

## How should you pay debts and expenses?

Open an estate checking account with the estate's tax ID number and run every payment through it. Pay the funeral bill, the last illness, administration costs and taxes from estate funds. The Burlington County guide lists those same categories as the executor's job and adds that you must file the required state and federal tax forms.

Creditors have a set window to present claims, which the [commissions and timeline article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline) explains, and our national piece on the [probate creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) shows the common pattern. A parent who received Medicaid adds a special creditor, covered in [New Jersey estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery).

Consider Denise, executor for her father. He left a house, two bank accounts and a small brokerage account, all to her and her brother. She orders four short certificates, mails notices in the second week, and files Form L-9 because both children are Class A. She holds back a reserve until the claim window closes. Nothing dramatic happens, and that is the point.

## How does the estate close?

Closing means two things: an accounting, and a release. Most New Jersey estates close informally. The Mercer County Surrogate says most accountings are informal and that an executor generally cannot be required to account until a year has passed. A formal accounting is a court proceeding, started by verified complaint and order to show cause, and the Mercer County page lists a $175 filing fee.

The ordinary shortcut is a refunding bond and release signed by each beneficiary. Burlington County says it dispenses with a formal accounting, approves what the executor did and releases the executor from further liability, while each beneficiary promises to give back a proportionate share if a legitimate debt or tax appears later. Morris County says all executors are required to obtain one from the beneficiaries. The forms are filed with the Surrogate for a fee, and Burlington, Morris and Atlantic list $10 for a form of two pages or fewer.

Do not hand out the money first. The full walk-through, including who signs for a minor, is in [refunding bonds and releases when an estate closes](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-refunding-bonds-and-releases-when-an-estate-closes). If you are weighing your own pay, see [what an executor may take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate).
