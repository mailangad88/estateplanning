A parent spends her last three years in a nursing home on Medicaid. She dies owning a modest house. The children assume the house is theirs, because everyone told them it did not count when she applied. Then a letter arrives from the state.

That letter is Medicaid estate recovery. The rules are specific, and several protections exist if you know about them in time. This article uses the Division of Medical Assistance and Health Services (DMAHS) Medicaid Communication 17-15, last updated in February 2020, and the regulation it cites, N.J.A.C. 10:49-14.1. Rules can change, so confirm the current version with DMAHS or an attorney. For how a person qualifies in the first place, read [New Jersey Medicaid and nursing home planning](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning); for the national picture, see our [estate recovery overview](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery).

## What does New Jersey recover, and from whom?

The state may recover from the estates of Medicaid beneficiaries and former beneficiaries for payments made for services received on or after age 55. The communication says recovery covers all services, not just institutional care. It includes managed care capitation payments, even when the person used out-of-network providers, and payments under the Medicaid waiver programs. The two named exceptions are the state-funded Home Care Expansion Program and Jersey Assistance for Community Caregiving. Medicare cost-sharing paid under the Medicare Savings Programs has not been subject to recovery since January 1, 2010.

So the claim can be much larger than the nursing home bills a family remembers. It can include the monthly managed care payments the state made on the person's behalf.

## What counts as the estate?

Broader than a probate lawyer would define it. The regulation points to N.J.S.A. 3B:1-1 and says the estate includes real and personal property and any asset in which the person held legal title or an interest at death. That reaches the person's share of property that passed to survivors through joint tenancy, tenancy in common, survivorship, a life estate, a living trust or similar arrangement.

| Asset | Reached by recovery? |
|---|---|
| Primary residence | Yes, even though it was exempt for eligibility |
| Bank accounts, solely or jointly held | Yes |
| Trusts, stocks, bonds | Yes, as listed in the state's communication |
| Life insurance paid to the estate (including as a default beneficiary) | Yes |
| Personal needs allowance balance | Yes, turned over to the executor |
| Unused funeral trust or burial policy funds | Excess forwarded to DMAHS under P.L. 1999, c. 193 |
| A life estate that ended at death | Excluded |

Annuities work differently. The state is to be named the remainder beneficiary for the total Medicaid paid, and annuities are not subject to a lien. This is why a [Medicaid-compliant annuity](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-compliant-annuity) comes with a beneficiary condition.

This matters for the reader who thinks a living trust or joint ownership solved the problem. Under the state's definition, those arrangements do not put the share out of reach.

## Who is protected?

Recovery is deferred while a spouse survives. It is also deferred while a surviving child is under 21, or is blind or permanently and totally disabled under the Social Security standard (42 U.S.C. 1382c). When that protection ends, because the spouse dies, the child turns 21 or the disabled child dies or recovers, DMAHS may recover from what is left at that time.

Deferred is the right word. If Maria is the one who received Medicaid and her husband outlives her, the state waits. When he dies, whatever of her share remains in the estate can be claimed.

The state can also decline to pursue a claim when it decides, in its sole discretion, that recovery is not cost-effective. Do not count on that. It is a judgment call that belongs to the agency.

## What happens with the house and a lien?

DMAHS files a lien for payments received on or after age 55, except annuities. The lien equals all assistance paid for those services. Per the communication, no lien is filed at application. Liens are filed after death, when no exception applies.

There is a home protection. If a family member lived continuously in the beneficiary's primary residence before death and still lives there, DMAHS may record a lien but will not enforce it until the home is voluntarily sold or the resident dies or moves out. A lien also has to describe the beneficiary's own interest. Under the regulation, a tenancy in common reaches 50 percent of equity, while tenancy by the entirety or joint tenancy with survivorship reaches the whole property.

A buyer who pays fair market value in good faith is not pursued. The state looks to the estate instead.

For what happens to the house once it is out of the estate, see [inheriting a house in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/inheriting-a-house-in-new-jersey).

## What is the order of payment?

The communication sets this priority:

1. Reasonable funeral expenses, which may be paid before notifying DMAHS.
2. Reasonable estate administration costs.
3. Debts owed to the Office of the Public Guardian for Elderly Adults.
4. The DMAHS claim, treated as a preferred claim.

Claims with higher federal or state preference, such as an earlier IRS lien, can come ahead of Medicaid. If money is short, debts are paid under N.J.S.A. 3B:22-2. And the key instruction for an executor: no distributions to heirs or creditors before DMAHS is paid if a Medicaid claim exists. An executor who hands out the checking account first can be called to account. Our article on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) covers the general order of payment.

## How does the hardship waiver work?

There are narrow grounds. Undue hardship exists only if the estate is, or would become, the survivors' sole income-producing asset and recovery is likely to make one or more survivors eligible for public assistance or Medicaid. If the hardship came from estate planning that moved assets out to avoid recovery, there is a rebuttable presumption that no undue hardship exists.

The deadlines are short:

- 20 days from receiving written notice of the claim to file a waiver or compromise request with evidence.
- 45 days for DMAHS to decide and notify in writing.
- 20 days from receiving a denial to request a hearing under N.J.A.C. 10:49-10, which goes to the Office of Administrative Law.

The communication says that if the 20-day request deadline is missed, DMAHS cannot grant a hardship waiver. Put the date on the calendar the day the letter arrives.

## What should the executor do first?

Notify DMAHS in writing as soon as possible after death, before spending estate funds other than reasonable funeral costs. The address in the communication is DMAHS, Office of Legal and Regulatory Affairs, Attn: Estates, PO Box 712, Mail Code 6, Trenton, NJ 08625. The executor or another interested party can also ask for a payoff statement.

Notice also starts the three-year clock that limits when the state may file its claim. An executor who stays silent does not make the claim disappear. The estate stays open, the house cannot be cleanly sold and the executor carries the risk.
