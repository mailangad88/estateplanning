People often hear that an irrevocable trust "can never be changed," and for the grantor acting alone, that is close to true. The law does leave a few doors open. New Jersey's Uniform Trust Code, N.J.S.A. 3B:31, was enacted by L. 2015, c. 276, and it sets out how a trust that cannot be revoked can still be adjusted when the family or the facts have changed.

This page sits inside our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey) and covers the four tools in order of cost: an agreement without a court, a consent modification, a court modification and the question of decanting. For background on why people use these trusts, see [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts). For the trustee side, see [New Jersey trustee duties to beneficiaries](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-trustee-duties-to-beneficiaries-under-the-uniform-trust-code).

## Can you change an irrevocable trust in New Jersey?

Usually with other people's agreement, and never in a way that defeats the trust's main purpose. The statute asks two questions every time. Who has to agree? And does the change still serve what the trust was set up to do? That second idea is called a material purpose, and it is the test that decides most disputes.

| Tool | Who acts | Court needed | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nonjudicial settlement agreement (3B:31-11) | Interested persons | No, unless someone asks for approval | Cannot violate a material purpose |
| Consent modification or termination (3B:31-27) | Trustee and all beneficiaries | Not always, but a court can approve | Must be consistent with a material purpose, unless a court finds otherwise |
| Court modification (3B:31-28) | A trustee or beneficiary asks | Yes | Unanticipated circumstances or impracticable administration |
| Reformation for mistake (3B:31-31) | A party asks | Yes | Clear and convincing proof |

## What is a nonjudicial settlement agreement?

It is a written deal among the people with a stake in the trust, made without going to court. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:31-11, interested persons, meaning those whose consent a court would need to approve a settlement, can enter a binding agreement on any matter involving a trust. The code lists proper subjects, including interpreting the trust, approving a trustee's accounting, appointing or accepting the resignation of a trustee and moving the principal place of administration.

The agreement is valid only to the extent it does not violate a material purpose of the trust and includes only terms a court could approve. Any interested person may ask the court to approve it, which is worth doing when the change is a big one or someone may later say they did not agree.

Consider three siblings who are beneficiaries of their father's trust, with a bank as trustee. The bank's fees are high and the trust holds one rental property. All agree to replace the bank with their sister Priya. That is an ordinary use of an agreement. If one sibling later claimed the agreement was unfair, a court approval would have closed that door.

## Can the trustee and beneficiaries agree to modify or end the trust?

Yes. A noncharitable irrevocable trust may be modified or terminated on the consent of the trustee and all beneficiaries, if the change is consistent with a material purpose (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-27). A separate path lets all the beneficiaries ask the court to approve, without the trustee's consent, if the court finds termination is not needed to achieve any material purpose, or that a modification is not inconsistent with one.

Three further points matter.

- **Spendthrift clauses do not block it.** A spendthrift provision is not presumed to be a material purpose. That surprises families who assumed a protective clause meant the trust had to run to the end.
- **Unanimity is not always required.** If not every beneficiary consents, the court may still approve if the trust could have been changed had all agreed, and the interests of those who refuse are adequately protected.
- **Termination has a mechanism.** The trustee distributes the property as the beneficiaries agreed.

A material purpose is read from the trust document and the settlor's reasons. A trust that holds a house for a surviving spouse for life, with the children getting what is left, usually has a purpose of providing for the spouse. Children and the trustee cannot agree to sell and split the proceeds if that defeats it.

## When can a court change a trust?

When something happened that the settlor did not foresee, or the trust has become impractical to run. The court may modify the administrative or dispositive terms, or terminate the trust, if circumstances not anticipated by the settlor mean the change will further the trust's purposes, and it should try to follow the settlor's probable intent (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-28). It may also modify administrative terms if continuing on the existing terms would be impracticable or wasteful, or impair administration.

Think of a trust that lets a trustee invest only in a particular bank's certificates of deposit, written decades ago. The investment rule has become a drag. Changing it is an administrative fix of the kind a court may approve. By contrast, a family that wants the court to rewrite who inherits because they now dislike the split will not get far.

Other court tools, each narrower:

- **Reformation.** A court may correct even clear terms to match the settlor's probable intent, but only with clear and convincing proof of a mistake of fact or law (3B:31-31).
- **Tax objectives.** A court may modify terms to achieve the settlor's tax objectives if that does not contradict probable intent, and it can make the change retroactive (3B:31-33).
- **Small trusts.** A trustee, after notice to qualified beneficiaries, may end a trust worth less than $100,000 if it costs more to run than it justifies (3B:31-30).
- **Combining or dividing.** Trustees may combine trusts or divide one into several, but only if the result does not impair any beneficiary's rights or the trust's purposes (3B:31-34). Dividing a trust can help, for instance, when one beneficiary has special needs and the others do not.

## What about decanting?

Decanting means a trustee uses a power to distribute trust property to a new trust with different terms. The tools above are what the Uniform Trust Code, as enacted in 2015, provides for changing a trust. Decanting is not among its listed sections, and the code's closest power is the right to divide a trust under 3B:31-34. So in New Jersey the safe assumption is that a trustee can decant only if the trust instrument or other law actually gives the power, and only in a way that respects the beneficiaries.

That is a question for an attorney to answer for your document, not a general rule. Our national article on [trust protectors and decanting](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-trust-protector-and-decanting) explains the concept, but it does not say New Jersey law allows it. A trustee who moves assets into a new trust without clear authority risks a claim for breach of trust, with the remedies described in the trustee duties article.

## What do you do before changing an irrevocable trust?

1. Get the full trust instrument and every amendment, and identify the trustee and all beneficiaries, including minor and contingent ones.
2. Write down what the change is meant to fix, in a sentence. If you cannot, wait.
3. Ask what material purpose the trust serves, reading the document for the settlor's reasons.
4. Decide on the route: agreement, consent modification or court petition. A trustee or beneficiary may commence a proceeding to approve or disapprove a modification (3B:31-26).
5. Handle minors and unborn beneficiaries. A parent can represent a child in some cases, and the court can appoint a guardian ad litem when an interest is unrepresented (3B:31-15 to 31-17).
6. Check tax and benefits effects before anyone signs, particularly Medicaid and [special needs planning](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey).
7. Record the result in a signed document and give a copy to every qualified beneficiary.

A frequent error our attorneys see is a family signing a "release and agreement" that was never reviewed, then finding that one adult child, or a trust for a minor, was left out. A court may refuse to be bound by that, and the whole change unravels.
