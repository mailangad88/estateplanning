New Jersey has two registered statuses for couples who are not married: the civil union and the domestic partnership. People often treat them as one thing, and in estate law they mostly work alike. The difference between either of them and having neither is much larger. A couple who has lived together for thirty years without registering has almost none of the protections described here.

This article maps what each status gets, where the dates matter and what an unregistered partner should do instead. The detailed share calculations live in other pages, which are linked where they come up. Start with the [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey) if you want the big picture.

## What is the difference between a civil union and a domestic partnership?

A civil union was created by P.L. 2006, c.103, approved December 21, 2006. The law says civil union couples receive "all of the same benefits, protections and responsibilities" under law as spouses in a marriage (section 4a), including rules on "descent and distribution, intestate succession, survivorship" and on "probate law and procedure, including nonprobate transfer" (section 5). It was written for same-sex couples.

A domestic partnership is older. The Domestic Partnership Act, P.L. 2003, c.246, gave a narrower set of rights, and it was amended when civil unions began on February 19, 2007. Since then, a same-sex couple cannot start a new domestic partnership, and partnerships registered before that date stay valid. Opposite-sex couples who are both 62 or older can still register one.

| | Civil union | Domestic partnership |
|---|---|---|
| Created by | P.L. 2006, c.103 | P.L. 2003, c.246 |
| Who | Same-sex couples | Same-sex couples (existing partnerships) and opposite-sex couples both 62 or older |
| Scope of rights | Same benefits, protections and responsibilities as spouses | A defined list of rights, including inheritance tax, visiting, medical decisions |
| How to start | Under the Civil Union Act | Notarized affidavit filed with a local registrar |
| Cannot hold at the same time | Another union, partnership or marriage | A marriage, civil union or another partnership |

To register a domestic partnership, the couple must share a common residence and show joint financial responsibility, such as a joint deed, lease, bank account or beneficiary designation. They cannot be closely related, and a partnership ended within the last 180 days generally blocks a new one unless a partner died. Ending a domestic partnership requires a filing with the Superior Court.

## How does the inheritance tax treat each status?

Both are in Class A, which pays no New Jersey inheritance tax, but the Division of Taxation attaches dates.

- **Civil union partners** are Class A for civil unions formed after February 19, 2007.
- **Domestic partners** are Class A for partnerships formed after July 10, 2004. The Division says the Domestic Partnership Act exempts transfers by will, survivorship or contract to a surviving domestic partner for decedents who died on or after July 10, 2004.
- **The civil union partner of your child** sits in Class C, just as the spouse of your child does. A domestic partner of a child does not appear in that class.
- **Everyone else** falls in Class D, the catch-all, which includes an unmarried partner with no status.

Pension, annuity and retirement payments from a qualified employer plan are exempt when paid to a surviving spouse or domestic partner (N.J.S.A. 54:34-4). Joint tenancy in a cooperative housing unit used as the couple's principal residence is also excluded from the survivorship tax. Our [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article has the class tables and the filing timeline.

## What does a partner inherit without a will?

A surviving civil union partner or domestic partner has the intestate share of a surviving spouse. A 2024 law makes this explicit. P.L. 2023, c.238, approved January 8, 2024 and effective immediately, amended N.J.S.A. 3B:5-3 and 3B:5-4 so they name civil union partners and domestic partners alongside spouses. The same law amended the elective share section, N.J.S.A. 3B:8-1.

Three practical points follow.

**The status must exist at death.** Proof of the registration or license is the first thing the Surrogate will want.

**A pending case ends the right.** The list of people who do not count as surviving partners includes someone with a pending complaint for divorce, dissolution or termination, and others described in section 3B:5-3(d). The elective share carries the same exclusions.

**The exact fractions are in separate pages.** For the shares, read [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey). For the right to claim against a will, read [the elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse). The same 2024 law also covers the rule that someone responsible for the decedent's intentional killing loses benefits, including those of a partner.

A surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner can also use the affidavit route for a small intestate estate, as the [small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) article explains.

## Who controls the funeral and health care?

Registered status helps most when no one is thinking about paperwork.

For the funeral, N.J.S.A. 45:27-22 ranks the surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner first when the deceased named no one. See [who controls funeral and burial decisions in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-controls-funeral-and-burial-decisions-in-new-jersey).

For health care, licensed facilities must allow a patient's domestic partner to visit (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-12.22). A domestic partner can be named a health care representative in a proxy directive. Be aware that the designation is revoked when the partnership ends unless the directive says otherwise (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-57). A partner may also consent to an autopsy under the Domestic Partnership Act. Our article on [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives) explains the form.

None of this replaces a signed directive. A status gives you a place on a list. A document gives you a decision.

## What does an unmarried partner without a status get?

Almost nothing by default, and this is where the harm happens.

- No intestate share. The statute lists spouses, civil union partners and domestic partners, and a long-term live-in partner is none of them.
- No elective share.
- Class D for the inheritance tax, as the Division's classes describe it.
- No place on the funeral priority list, which starts with a spouse or registered partner and then moves to blood relatives.
- No automatic role in health care decisions beyond what a directive says.

Take Robert, 72, and Anita, 69, who share a house in Cherry Hill. Anita is a widow who is not interested in remarrying and will lose a pension if she does. They qualify for a domestic partnership because both are over 62, and they share the mortgage. Registering gives each the Class A treatment and the intestate and elective share rights. If they decide not to register, they need wills, a power of attorney, health care directives and a funeral appointment, plus beneficiary designations that name each other. Our article on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) and the scenario in [unmarried partner dies without a will](/learn/what-if/unmarried-partner-dies-without-a-will) cover the options.

1. Find your certificate of civil union or domestic partnership and note the date. The inheritance tax classes depend on it.
2. Check beneficiary forms, wills and directives for an old name or a former partner.
3. If you started a termination case, update everything now.
