You can leave a child out of your New Jersey will, and the law will not stop you. What the law will do is let that child ask a judge whether the document really reflects your wishes. Most of the work of a disinheritance is not writing the sentence. It is making sure the sentence survives that question.

This page covers the rule, the drafting steps, the paperwork outside the will that undoes a plan, and what a caveat or a contest looks like from the child's side. If your worry is a baby or adoption after the will was signed, read [children left out of a New Jersey will](/learn/new-jersey/children-left-out-of-a-new-jersey-will) instead. The wider picture is in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## The short version

- Children have no forced share in New Jersey. A surviving spouse or partner does, under the [elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse).
- Name the child in the will and state that the omission is intentional.
- Fix the accounts and forms that pass outside the will. These cause most failed disinheritances.
- Plan for a challenge on capacity or undue influence, not on the wording.
- If the child is not truly cut out, a smaller gift may work better than nothing.

## Is there a forced share for children in New Jersey?

No. The only family member the Probate Code protects against a will that leaves them too little is a surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner, who can elect one-third of the augmented estate (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-1). A child can take under a will, take as an heir if there is no will, or take through a beneficiary form. A child cannot demand a share the document does not give.

Contrast this with the way the default works. Without a will, the intestacy statutes (N.J.S.A. 3B:5-3 and 3B:5-4) send property to a spouse and children in fixed proportions, which is covered in [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey). That is why disinheriting a child by simply doing nothing never works. The child is your heir until a valid document says otherwise.

## How do you disinherit a child in a New Jersey will?

Write it down, on purpose, in a will signed with the formalities in our [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) article. Then follow these steps.

1. **Name the child.** "I intentionally make no provision for my son, Daniel Reyes" is clearer than leaving his name out of a list.
2. **Say it is deliberate.** One sentence. Courts look at whether a child was forgotten or excluded on purpose, and a clear statement answers that.
3. **Skip the grievance.** The will becomes part of the Surrogate's file. A paragraph about old arguments gives the child something to quote and a reason to sue.
4. **Address the grandchildren.** Decide whether Daniel's children take a gift, take a trust, or are also excluded. Then write what you decided.
5. **Choose a residuary beneficiary and a backup.** If everything flows to the other children and one of them dies first, you want a plan for that share. See [when a beneficiary dies first in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/when-a-beneficiary-dies-first-in-new-jersey).
6. **Sign with a self-proving affidavit and keep a file.** Notes from the meeting with the attorney, and a doctor's note if capacity might be questioned, are the evidence a judge will want years later.

Take Walter, 77, who has three children and has not spoken to his eldest, Daniel, for fifteen years. His old will splits everything three ways. He signs a new one that names Daniel, states the omission is intentional and leaves everything to his two other children equally. If Daniel sues, the document answers the easy questions. What is left is whether Walter was free and clear-headed that day.

## What does a child's challenge look like?

A disinherited child has two moves, and each has a different timing.

| Step | When | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Caveat | Before the will is probated | A written objection filed with the county Surrogate. The Rules of Court say the Surrogate's Court may not act while a caveat is in place unless the Superior Court authorizes it (R. 4:82). |
| Complaint | After probate | A lawsuit in the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part, generally filed within four months after the will is probated (R. 4:85-1). |

Our article on [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats) walks through both steps, the Morris County Surrogate's $25 caveat form and the cases on the four-month clock. The part worth knowing here is what the child must prove.

The usual theories are lack of capacity, undue influence and improper signing. Undue influence is the one that targets a disinheritance, because it asks who benefited. If a sibling who drove the parent to the lawyer, handled the money and was in the room ends up with everything, that fact pattern can shift the burden to that sibling to explain. Without a confidential relationship and suspicious circumstances, the contestant carries the burden and must show it clearly.

The best protection is boring. The parent meets the attorney alone. The reasons for the unequal result are consistent with what the parent told other people over the years. The signing is supervised. A child who reads that record usually decides not to spend money on a lawsuit.

## Which documents can undo a disinheritance?

The will controls only what passes under the will. Most of what families own does not. If Daniel is still listed as a payable-on-death beneficiary, as a joint owner, or on a life insurance policy or IRA form, that asset goes to him no matter what the will says. Our guide to [which document controls](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form) explains the order.

Run a short audit before you sign anything:

- Retirement accounts and life insurance: who is named, and who is the backup?
- Bank accounts: any joint owners or payable-on-death payees? The rules for [joint bank accounts after a death in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/joint-bank-accounts-after-a-death-in-new-jersey) can surprise people.
- The house: deed, transfer-on-death deed, or life estate? See [transfer-on-death options in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-on-death-options-in-new-jersey).
- Trust: if you have a [living trust](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey), its terms must match the will.

The reverse also happens. A parent who wants to disinherit one child often leaves that child on an old account as a convenience co-owner. At death the bank pays the survivor, and the will never gets a vote.

## Is a no-contest clause a good idea?

Sometimes, and only if the child is left something worth losing. A clause that says anyone who challenges the will forfeits the gift does nothing to a child who receives nothing. Whether a New Jersey court will enforce one in a given case is a question to put to your attorney. Our general article on the [no-contest clause](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) explains the idea, and a small gift paired with a clause is a middle path when the relationship is strained rather than finished.

## What if you want unequal shares, not a full disinheritance?

That is often what parents actually mean. A child who already received help with a house, or who has a stable income while a sibling does not, may simply take a smaller share. The same drafting rule applies: state the result and, in a sentence, why it is intended. [Disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) covers the national options, and a trust is often better than a larger outright gift for a child with a disability or a money problem, as discussed in [special needs planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey).
