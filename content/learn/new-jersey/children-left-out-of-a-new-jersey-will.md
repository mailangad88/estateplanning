Two different worries bring parents to this page. One is the new baby or adopted child who arrived after the will was signed. The other is an adult son or daughter the parent does not want to leave anything to. New Jersey treats those two situations very differently, and a will that handles one well can fail at the other.

The first is addressed by N.J.S.A. 3B:5-16, the omitted child section. The second is not, which is the point. This article explains who is covered, how the share is figured, and how to disinherit someone so the choice holds up. For the general approach to unequal shares, see our article on [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares), and the [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey) lists the other state articles.

## Who is an omitted child under New Jersey law?

An omitted child is one born or adopted after the will was signed whom the will does not provide for. That is the whole definition in N.J.S.A. 3B:5-16. The child does not have to be the maker's only child, and the will does not have to be old.

Note what the definition leaves out. A child who was alive when you signed is not an omitted child, even if the will never mentions that child. The section protects the surprise of a later arrival, not a child you chose to leave out or forgot about.

One more category is included. If a living child was omitted only because you believed the child was dead, that child is treated like an after-born child.

## How much does an omitted child get?

It depends on whether you had other children when you signed. The statute gives two formulas.

| Your situation when you signed | What the omitted child receives |
|---|---|
| No living children | The share the child would have received if you had died without a will |
| One or more living children | A share of only the part of the estate left to those children, sized as if the omitted child had been included as an equal beneficiary |

In the first case there is an exception. If the will left all or substantially all of the estate to the child's other parent, or to a trust for that parent, and the parent survives and takes, the omitted child receives nothing under the section. That fits the usual young-family will, where each spouse leaves everything to the other.

In the second case, the statute asks the court to keep your plan as intact as it can. The omitted child's interest should match, where feasible, the legal and equitable character of the gifts to the other children, and those gifts are reduced proportionally. Think of it as the existing children making room at the table.

Here is how the second case plays out. Ruth signs a will leaving her whole estate to her two sons, Marcus and Paul, in equal shares. She later adopts a daughter, Elena, and never updates the will. Elena shares in what was left to Marcus and Paul as if she had been named an equal beneficiary, so each of the three takes one third. If Ruth had also left $20,000 to a friend, the friend's gift is not touched, because the section reaches only the portion devised to the children.

Where there were no children at signing, the share comes proportionally from the other beneficiaries according to their interests. Under the first formula, a will that left half to a sister and half to a cousin would shrink each gift equally.

## What cuts off the omitted-child share?

Two things defeat the claim, and both are in subsection (b) of the statute.

1. The will shows the omission was intentional. A sentence such as "I have intentionally made no provision for any child born after the date of this will" does the job, and it is easy to include.
2. You gave the child something outside the will meant to replace a gift in the will. That intent may be shown by your statements, or reasonably inferred from the size of the transfer or other evidence. A life insurance policy payable to the child or a funded account can qualify, but a paper trail helps.

If you want to rely on an outside transfer, say so in the will itself and keep records of the transfer.

## Does the new-spouse rule work the same way?

No, but it is related. N.J.S.A. 3B:5-15 gives a spouse or domestic partner you married or partnered with after signing a floor of the intestate share, with exceptions for a will made in contemplation of the marriage, a will that survives a later marriage, and substitute transfers outside the will. Our article on [changing or revoking a will](/learn/new-jersey/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-new-jersey) explains how marriage and divorce interact with the document, and [marriage, divorce or a new baby and your existing will](/learn/wills/marriage-divorce-or-a-new-baby-and-your-existing-will) gives the national view.

## Can you disinherit an adult child in New Jersey?

Yes, by planning it, though the omitted-child section does nothing to stop you. A child alive when you signed is not protected by 3B:5-16. The spouse's elective share, discussed in [the New Jersey elective share article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse), is a right of a spouse or partner, not of a child.

That does not make a disinheritance safe. The usual attack is on the will itself, through claims of incapacity or undue influence, and our article on [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats) covers the process and deadlines. A will that quietly skips a child invites that attack. A will that names the child, states in one calm sentence that the omission is intentional, and has been signed with proper formalities gives the court less to chew on.

A short list of habits that help:

- Name the child by name and say the omission is deliberate.
- Do not use the will to explain a long family grievance. Reasons in a document that gets filed with the Surrogate can cause more fights.
- Think about a smaller gift if the relationship is strained rather than ended. A modest bequest paired with a [no-contest clause](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) discourages a suit.
- Remember the assets outside the will. A beneficiary form that still lists the child will override the will, as our article on [which document controls](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form) explains.
- If the child has children, decide whether the grandchildren take. Without language, a will that disinherits a child may still leave questions about their share, so state it.

## What if I want to treat children unequally without cutting anyone out?

You can, and the same drafting habit applies: say what you mean. A parent who has already paid for one child's education or helped with a home may want a smaller share for that child. The will can say so, describe it as an advancement or just set percentages. If one child needs more help than another, because of a disability or a money problem, a trust usually works better than a bigger outright gift.
