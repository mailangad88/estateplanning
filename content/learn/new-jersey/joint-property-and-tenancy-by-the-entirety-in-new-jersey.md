How a house or an account is titled decides who gets it at death, and it does so before the will is read. Two people can sign the same will and own the same house. They can still end up with opposite results because one deed said "as joint tenants" and the other said "as tenants in common." This article explains the three common forms of co-ownership in New Jersey and what each does when an owner dies.

It focuses on the New Jersey rules: the statutes that create a tenancy by the entirety, what two Appellate Division decisions say about survivorship, and how the Division of Taxation treats jointly held property. The general national comparison is in [joint tenants versus tenants in common versus entirety](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenants-vs-tenants-in-common-vs-entirety), and the risks of adding a name to a deed are in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks). The whole state picture is in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What are the three ways to co-own property in New Jersey?

Joint tenancy, tenancy in common and tenancy by the entirety. They differ in who can own, whether survivorship applies, and how easily one owner can get out.

| | Joint tenancy | Tenancy in common | Tenancy by the entirety |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who can hold it | Any people | Any people | Spouses only |
| At one owner's death | Survivor takes the whole | Share goes under the will or intestacy | Survivor takes the whole |
| Can one owner leave their share by will | No | Yes | No |
| Can one owner end it alone | Yes, by transferring their interest | Yes, by selling or leaving their share | No, written consent of both is needed |
| Probate for that asset | No | Yes, for the dead owner's share | No |

The Division of Taxation describes the first two in its own glossary. A joint tenant has full rights to the entire property if they outlive the other owners, and joint ownership is shown by "and" or "or" wording. Tenants in common each hold a separate share and have no rights to the others' shares.

## How does joint tenancy work at death?

The survivor takes the whole by operation of law. The Appellate Division put it plainly in *Branco v. Rodrigues*, decided June 20, 2023 and approved for publication: survivorship is "the essence of the joint tenancy estate," and co-owners hold equal, undivided interests.

That means the will is beside the point for the asset. Consider Ray and his brother Denny, who bought a rental duplex in Paterson as joint tenants. Ray's will leaves everything to his two daughters. When Ray dies, the duplex still goes to Denny. The daughters take Ray's other assets, but not the duplex. The reverse is also true. If Denny had quietly deeded his half to a friend years earlier, the joint tenancy would have ended and Ray's half would have been a separate, inheritable interest.

New Jersey has allowed an owner to create a joint tenancy by deeding to himself and another since 1950. The *Branco* court cited N.J.S.A. 46:3-17.1 for that point, noting that before the statute, common law sometimes required a "straw man" conveyance to do the same thing.

Several things follow in practice.

- A joint tenancy can end without anyone telling you. The same decision says it may be converted into a tenancy in common through a unilateral act that destroys one of the unities, such as a transfer of one owner's interest.
- Putting a child on a parent's deed may be treated as a gift. The court said a transfer into joint tenancy may be presumed a gift, rebuttable only with clear and convincing evidence.
- The survivor may owe tax or need a waiver. The Division says waivers are generally required for New Jersey real property of a decedent, with the entirety exception, so do not assume a house owned jointly with a non-spouse moves freely.

## What makes a tenancy by the entirety different?

It exists only between spouses, it cannot be undone by one of them, and the survivor takes everything. In *Jimenez v. Jimenez*, decided May 8, 2018, the court called a tenancy by the entirety "a form of joint property ownership available only to spouses."

New Jersey's statutes make this the default for a married couple. Under N.J.S.A. 46:3-17.2, the tenancy is created when a husband and wife together take title to an interest in real or personal property. It is also created when a husband and wife become lessees of property together. The court read this as a legislative choice to maximize each spouse's protection. The law was approved on January 5, 1988 and applies to tenancies created on or after its effective date.

The key protection is N.J.S.A. 46:3-17.4: "Neither spouse may sever, alienate, or otherwise affect their interest in the tenancy by entirety during the marriage or upon separation without the written consent of both spouses."

The tenancy does not fully shield the property, though. According to *Jimenez*, a judgment creditor of one spouse may levy on that spouse's right of survivorship, and a debtor's interest may be reached by creditors. The court also noted that nothing in its ruling limits a divorce court's power to order a sale of a marital entirety asset. A tenancy by the entirety protects against one spouse acting alone. It is not a promise against every outside claim.

## Does entirety property need a tax waiver?

No. The Division of Taxation says real property held by spouses or civil union partners as tenants by the entirety is not treated as a transfer to the survivor. Its inheritance tax instructions state that such real property in the estate of the spouse who dies first does not need to be reported, and no waiver is required, regardless of the date of death. For a nonresident who owned New Jersey real estate, the same exception applies.

This is a real convenience compared with a house in one spouse's name. For that house, as explained in [inheriting a house in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/inheriting-a-house-in-new-jersey), the executor needs letters and a waiver before a deed can move. With entirety title, the Division's rules do not put that step in the survivor's way. Ask your title company what it wants recorded.

It helps to be exact about the edges. A tenancy by the entirety still does nothing for other assets in the dead spouse's sole name, and it does not change the class of any other heir. The tax itself is explained in [the New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

## What happens to joint bank accounts and other jointly held assets?

For a resident decedent's joint account, the Division says a financial institution may release up to 50% of the balance to the survivor without a formal waiver. It calls this a blanket waiver and points to N.J.A.C. 18:26-11.16. The 50% limit applies to the total in the account, and stocks and bonds are not eligible. Brokerage accounts need a waiver.

Form L-8, a self-executing waiver filed with the financial institution and not the Division, covers certain Class A beneficiaries, but it cannot move real estate. For real estate, the Division's instructions describe Form L-9 as available to a surviving spouse or other Class A beneficiaries, including where the interest was held with another Class A beneficiary.

A payable-on-death account is a different animal. The Division says it needs no waiver if the beneficiary dies first, but if the owner dies first a waiver is needed to transfer the funds. For beneficiary forms versus joint titles, see [joint ownership versus beneficiary designation](/learn/beneficiary-designations/joint-ownership-vs-beneficiary-designation).

Joint ownership also draws attention when a spouse wants a claim against the estate. The augmented estate under the elective share can include property held jointly with a right of survivorship. That is explained in [the New Jersey elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse).

## What happens to a tenant in common's share?

It passes under the owner's will, or to heirs under the intestacy rules. A tenant in common has a separate interest, so the co-owners get nothing automatically. That share is part of the probate estate, which means the executor needs letters from the Surrogate before dealing with it. See [the New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process).

Co-ownership then becomes a family question. If Elena and her sister inherited a house as tenants in common and Elena dies, her share goes to her own children, who become co-owners with their aunt. Without an agreement, nobody has the last word on selling or keeping the house. Our article on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) shows how that tends to go.

## How should you choose?

Ask what the title is supposed to accomplish, and then check whether it does.

1. List every deed and account and write down exactly how it is titled, using the words on the deed ("and," "or," "as tenants in common," "as tenants by the entirety").
2. Match the title to your plan. If a married couple wants the survivor to take the house, entirety title does it.
3. If an unmarried partner or a second spouse is involved, ask what survivorship does to your children's share.
4. Check for creditors. A joint owner with debts, a lawsuit or a business can put the property at risk.
5. Review the title after a divorce, a second marriage or a death. A deed does not update itself.

Adding a child to your deed to avoid probate is one of the most common mistakes. It gives the child a present interest, exposes the house to the child's debts and can lose tax advantages. A funded living trust or another planned approach is often cleaner. [Transfer on death options in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-on-death-options-in-new-jersey) and [living trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) compare them.
