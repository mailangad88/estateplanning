Carol is 66 and has raised her grandson Eli since he was six, after her daughter's addiction made it unsafe for him at home. She has no court order. The school accepts her signature on forms, but the pediatrician has asked twice who has authority, and nobody has told Carol what to do if her daughter reappears and takes Eli away.

New Jersey has a middle road for families like this. It is called kinship legal guardianship, and it sits between informal care and adoption. This page explains who qualifies, what a judge must find, what the order changes and what stays the same. Our general article on [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) covers the broader picture, and the state overview is in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey). The facts below come from the Kinship Legal Guardianship Act, N.J.S.A. 3B:12A-1 and following, and its companion sections.

## What is kinship legal guardianship in New Jersey?

It is a court-ordered guardianship that gives a relative or close family friend a birth parent's decision-making power over a child, without terminating the parents' rights. The Legislature created it for long-term kinship care where adoption is unlikely. The Act's findings describe it as a permanent option beyond custody but short of ending parental rights.

The case is filed in the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Family Part. The Rules of Court set the venue.

## Who qualifies as a caregiver?

The statute defines a caregiver as a person over 18 years old, other than the child's parent, who has a kinship relationship with the child. The child must also have lived in the caregiver's home and been cared for there for either the last six consecutive months or nine of the last 15 months.

The Act requires a "kinship relationship" with the child, and whether a particular aunt, older sibling or family friend qualifies is a legal question, so have an attorney check before you file. Carol is a grandmother, with years of care behind her, so she would not have difficulty on either point.

If the Division of Child Protection and Permanency (DCPP) placed the child with you and the child has lived with you for 12 consecutive months or 15 of the last 22 months, the statute says you need the Division's consent before petitioning for guardianship. If the Division removed the child within the last 12 months or has an open case with the Public Defender involved, the Division does the kinship caregiver assessment at no cost to you.

## What must the court find?

The court must appoint the caregiver if it finds, by clear and convincing evidence, all of the following.

1. Each parent's incapacity is serious enough that the parents are unable, unavailable or unwilling to perform the regular and expected functions of care and support of the child.
2. That inability is unlikely to change in the foreseeable future.
3. Guardianship is in the child's best interests.

The court also cannot award guardianship solely because of parental incapacity. It has to weigh the child's best interests, the kinship caregiver assessment and the caregiver's ability to give a safe and permanent home. It also considers the parents' wishes if known and the child's own wishes if the child is 12 or older. Child abuse record, criminal history and domestic violence checks are part of the picture too. Proper notice must be served on the parents and on anyone else holding custody or parenting time under a court order.

## How do you file? Step by step

Each case differs, but the statute points to this sequence.

1. **Confirm the six-month or nine-of-fifteen-month residence.** Gather school, medical and benefits records that show the child lived with you.
2. **Ask whether DCPP is involved.** If it placed the child with you, ask about consent and the assessment.
3. **Complete the kinship caregiver assessment.** The petition must include it. The statute lists 13 required items, such as your name and address, the family's circumstances, the parents' whereabouts and wishes if known, your ability to provide permanent care and the results of background, child abuse record and home review checks.
4. **File the complaint in the Family Part** for your county. The statute says no filing fee applies.
5. **Serve the parents.** The court will not act without proper notice, in line with the Rules of Court.
6. **Attend the hearing.** The judge looks at the evidence on each finding above.
7. **Keep the order.** Provide it to schools, doctors and insurers.

In cases that did not start with the Division, the assessment is done by an agency under the commissioner's regulations. The statute says the department pays the cost only if a financially eligible person applies for cash assistance under a kinship care or pilot program that requires kinship legal guardianship. Otherwise the caregiver pays. Ask about the cost of the assessment first.

## What does the guardian get to decide?

The kinship legal guardian has the same rights, responsibilities and authority relating to the child as a birth parent. That includes consent for medical care and school decisions, and the day-to-day decisions that informal caregivers struggle with. Two things are carved out. The guardian cannot consent to the child's adoption or to a name change.

## What do the parents keep?

More than most grandparents expect. The birth parent keeps the right to consent to adoption and the obligation to pay child support, and the right to visitation or parenting time as the court decides. If your daughter is in recovery and wants to see Eli, the order does not shut her out. That is why many families prefer this route to adoption. It is also why a judge must be satisfied that the parent's inability is unlikely to change.

The child's rights from the parents continue, too. The statute says appointment does not limit or end the child's rights to benefits derived from the parents, including inheritance and insurance eligibility. If a parent dies, the child is still an heir. If the child inherits money, the guardianship of the person is not enough to manage it, and the question of who handles it is covered in [what happens when a minor inherits money in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/minor-inherits-money-in-new-jersey-guardian-of-the-property).

## How long does it last, and can it be undone?

It lasts until the child turns 18 or stops being continuously enrolled in secondary education, whichever is later, unless the court ends it sooner. Before the child turns 18, the order may be vacated if guardianship is no longer in the child's best interests, or if the guardian failed to provide proper care or is unable or unwilling to, shown by clear and convincing evidence.

## Where does estate planning fit in?

Guardianship solves who decides while you are alive. It does not say what happens if you, the grandparent, become unable to act or die. That is the gap grandparents most often leave open. Our article on [estate planning for grandparents raising grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-grandparents-raising-grandchildren) lists the pieces, and [what to do if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) shows why a backup plan matters. A few practical points follow.

- Put a [power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) and a health care proxy in place so someone can manage your affairs if you fall ill.
- Tell the family who you want to step in for the child, and write it down. Ask an attorney how your wishes interact with the court order.
- Check your own beneficiary forms. A child who cannot manage money should not be named outright on a policy or retirement account.
- Compare this route with custody or adoption in [guardianship vs custody vs adoption](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-vs-custody-vs-adoption).
