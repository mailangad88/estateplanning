If you have children under 18, the most important paragraph in your will is probably the one you write last: who raises them if neither parent can. This page walks through how New Jersey lets you do it, what the second parent has to sign, and what to set up for the years when you are alive but cannot act.

We take the parent's side of the table here. For the court's side, how it picks a guardian when no one was named, see [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named), and for our overview of adult and minor guardianship, see [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey). The main guide to the state is the [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey).

## How do you name a guardian in a New Jersey will?

You put the nomination in your signed and witnessed will. N.J.S.A. 3B:12-13 lets a parent appoint, by will, a guardian of the person of the child, a guardian of the child's estate, or both. It covers children who are under 18 and unmarried when the parent dies, and it reaches a child not yet born.

A short clause is enough, but the clause should name a first choice, a backup, and the roles. The will itself must meet the execution rules in [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements): a signature and two witnesses. A nomination in an unsigned note or an email is not a valid appointment. A handwritten letter to the guardian is still a good idea, but as a companion to the will, as we describe in [a letter of intent for your children's guardian](/learn/guardianship/letter-of-intent-for-your-childrens-guardian).

| Role | What the person does | Who to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Guardian of the person | Raises the child: home, school, medical care | A relative or friend whose values and household you trust |
| Guardian of the estate | Manages money the child owns outright | A careful relative, or the trustee you name in a trust |
| Trustee (separate from guardian) | Holds and pays out money under a trust you create | A person or institution you pick for money skills |

The last row is not in the guardianship statute, but it is the choice that keeps the Surrogate out of your child's finances. A guardian of the estate must hand everything over at 18, which is why our article on [a minor who inherits money](/learn/new-jersey/minor-inherits-money-in-new-jersey-guardian-of-the-property) pushes trusts for anything sizable.

## What if the other parent is still alive?

Then the other parent's consent matters, and many couples overlook it. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:12-14, when the other parent survives the parent who made the appointment, that parent must consent in writing before or when letters are issued. The consent has to be signed and acknowledged before two witnesses who are present at the same time and who sign as witnesses.

In practice, a surviving parent usually keeps the children, and the nomination only matters if that parent is also gone or unable to act. A separate statute, N.J.S.A. 3B:12-15, lets a surviving parent make an appointment too. So the sensible plan is simple. Each parent signs a will naming the same guardian and the same backup, and the parents agree on the names in advance. If you are divorced, or the other parent is not involved, say so to your attorney, because the consent rule and a custody order can pull in different directions.

## Does the guardian have to post a bond?

Usually, but the will can excuse it. A testamentary guardian of a minor must be bonded before receiving letters, following the procedure in N.J.S.A. 3B:15-1 and the sections after it, but a parent's will or a court order can relieve the guardian of that duty (N.J.S.A. 3B:12-16). There are limits. Even when excused, the guardian must still be bonded before controlling property the child receives from sources other than the parent or a life insurance policy on the parent's life. And the will-based excuse does not apply to guardians of minors with a developmental disability, who are governed by the bonding rules in N.J.S.A. 3B:15-1.

A bond is an insurance-type promise, and the guardian pays a premium for it. Saying in your will that bond is waived spares your chosen guardian that cost on the parent-sourced money. It does not erase the court's oversight of what comes from grandparents or lawsuits.

## Will the court always honor the guardian you name?

No. The law gives your choice real weight, but the court keeps a safety valve. N.J.S.A. 3B:12-18 says an appointment of a guardian by will is good and effectual against any other person claiming the guardianship. Yet N.J.S.A. 3B:12-17 lets the Superior Court inquire into a minor's present custody when a will appointing a guardian of the person has been or will be probated, and issue an order for the best interest and welfare of the minor, on notice to the named guardian.

So a relative who thinks your choice is wrong can bring the question to the court, and the judge looks at the child's situation at that time. A guardian chosen eight years ago may have moved, divorced or developed health problems. Our article on [whether a guardian named in a will holds up in court](/learn/guardianship/does-a-guardian-named-in-a-will-hold-up-in-court) goes through that review. For you, the practical lessons are: pick someone who is a plausible caregiver by any family's standards, tell the relatives who might object, and put your reasons in writing.

## What if you are sick or away while you are still alive?

A will does nothing then. For temporary gaps, New Jersey gives parents two tools.

**A standby guardian.** A parent, custodian or guardian can petition the court to appoint a standby guardian, naming the person, the address and qualifications, and the event that triggers authority. The petition may name an alternate (N.J.S.A. 3B:12-72). The statute lists triggers such as a physician's finding that the parent is incapacitated or debilitated, military service, immigration detention, incarceration or pending criminal charges, and death when no testamentary or other permanent arrangement exists (3B:12-69). The court must appoint a proposed guardian who is fit, willing and good for the child. Once the trigger happens the standby guardian can take over at once, and has 90 days to petition for confirmation (3B:12-73). You can revoke in a signed writing filed with the court. The broader picture is in [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians).

**A power of attorney over your child.** Under N.J.S.A. 3B:12-39, a parent can delegate powers over a child's care, custody or property by a properly executed power of attorney. The other parent generally has to consent in writing before two witnesses, unless that parent is deceased, incapacitated or unavailable. The delegation lasts one year, renewable in one-year periods, and the parent keeps authority and can revoke. It is a short bridge.

Take Dana, a single mother with a six-year-old, who has surgery scheduled and no family nearby. A will leaves her cousin Ines as guardian, but the will does not help if Dana is unconscious for two weeks. She signs a power of attorney naming Ines for a year, and keeps a copy in the child's school file. For a longer-term concern, such as a progressive illness, a standby petition is the sturdier route.

## A checklist before you sign

1. Choose a first and second guardian of the person and ask each one directly.
2. Decide who manages money, and whether a trust should hold anything over a modest amount.
3. Decide how the other parent's consent will be handled under N.J.S.A. 3B:12-14.
4. Write the bond waiver, if you want it, into the will.
5. Discuss standby or power of attorney options if you have a health condition or a deployment.
6. Write a letter that tells the guardian about your child, your values and the schools and doctors.
7. Store the original will where it can be found. Our guide to [finding a will after a death in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/how-to-find-a-will-after-a-death-in-new-jersey) shows what families go through when it cannot.
