The house is usually the largest thing an estate owns, and in New Jersey it is also the item that sets the calendar. An executor can find a buyer in a week. Closing, though, waits on the Surrogate, the Division of Taxation and a handful of state forms that do not appear in an ordinary home sale.

This article follows a sale from the executor's first signature to the closing table: who has authority, which tax clearances matter, which fees land on the estate and what changes when the owner lived out of state. It builds on [inheriting a house in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/inheriting-a-house-in-new-jersey), which covers Form L-9 and keeping or dividing the house, and on [the New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax). The state overview is our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Who signs the contract and the deed?

The executor named in the will, or the administrator if there was no will, once the Surrogate has issued letters. Before that, nobody can bind the estate. Our [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) covers how to get letters, and county Surrogates say they are issued only after the tenth day after death.

The authority to sell comes from the fiduciary powers statute, N.J.S.A. 3B:14-23. It lists powers a fiduciary has unless a will, deed, other instrument or court order says otherwise. For real property, as the 1997 amendment (P.L.1997, c.250) shows, those powers include selling at public or private sale, on the terms the fiduciary considers most beneficial to those interested in the property. They apply unless the property is specifically disposed of, and they also include granting mortgages and easements.

That last clause deserves attention. If the will gives the house to a named person, the sale raises a question about whose property it is. Some title companies ask for the devisee's signature or written consent. The statute's text says nothing either way about purchasers or the form of an executor's deed. Ask the buyer's title company what it will require, and settle it before the contract is signed. A sale ordered by a will's directions to the executor is easier than one that cuts against a gift.

Where there are several executors or co-administrators, the title company will usually want all of them to sign. If one has died or will not act, see [what to do when an executor is removed or unable to serve](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

## Does the house need a tax waiver before closing?

Yes, in most cases. The Division of Taxation says a waiver is required for New Jersey real property of a decedent, and only the Inheritance Tax Branch issues it. The Division describes the lien this way: the inheritance tax is a lien on all property the decedent owned at death for 15 years, unless paid earlier or secured by bond. A waiver releases it. Until the State releases the lien, New Jersey real property cannot pass to a beneficiary or the estate.

Three things to know.

- **Spouses.** Real property held as tenants by the entirety is not treated as a transfer to the survivor, and no waiver is needed. [Joint property and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/new-jersey/joint-property-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety-in-new-jersey) explains why.
- **All Class A beneficiaries.** The executor can request the waiver on Form L-9 if every beneficiary is Class A and no tax is due. Otherwise the executor files a full return.
- **Timing.** The Division's guidance says to plan for at least several months after filing, and that audits can add more. Returns are handled in the order received.

The Division does not say whether a sale can close before the waiver is issued, and it does not set an escrow figure. For a nonresident decedent's New Jersey real estate, its FAQ says the Branch does not set the escrow amount, and the title company or the attorneys handling the sale determine it. In practice that means the closing date is a negotiation among the executor, the buyer's lawyer and the title underwriter.

## What does the realty transfer fee cost the estate?

The seller pays it. The Division's page says the seller is statutorily responsible for the realty transfer fee and, where it applies, a supplemental graduated percent fee. Both are based on the consideration stated in the deed. For a sale, that means the executor signs for the estate, and the fee comes out of the estate's proceeds at the table.

The fee is a schedule that rises with price. For a standard transaction totaling up to $350,000, the Division's schedule starts at $2.00 per $500 of consideration for the first $150,000, then $3.35 for $150,000 to $200,000, then $3.90 for $200,000 to $350,000. Above $350,000 the schedule starts at $2.90 and climbs through $6.05 per $500 above $1,000,000. A reduced schedule exists for senior citizens, blind or disabled persons and certain low and moderate income housing. The closing attorney calculates the exact figure.

The graduated percent fee applies on top for certain property classes with consideration over $1 million: 1% on the portion between $1 million and $2 million, and higher rates above that.

### Which deeds are exempt from the fee?

The Division lists the exemptions, and one is directly relevant: deeds from an executor or administrator to a devisee or heir distributing an estate under a will or intestacy law. Others include consideration of $100 or less, transfers between spouses or between parent and child, partition and corrective deeds. A deed claiming a full or partial exemption must be filed with the seller's affidavit of consideration, Form RTF-1.

Keep the line clear. When Maria, an executor, deeds her father's house to herself and her brother as the heirs, that distribution can be exempt. When the same executor sells the house to a stranger for $410,000, it is a sale at a stated price, and the fee applies. Do not restructure a closing to chase a fee without advice.

## What if the owner lived outside New Jersey?

The estate then has a gross income tax filing at closing. The Division says nonresident individuals, estates and trusts that sell or transfer New Jersey real property must make an estimated gross income tax payment under N.J.S.A. 54A:8-8 through 8-10. The payment is due before or at the closing, and the form must be filed whether or not there is a gain.

The Division's nonresident seller page puts the payment at 2% of the consideration. Its Technical Bulletin TB-57R describes the amount as the reportable federal gain multiplied by the highest New Jersey rate, with a floor of 2% of the deed consideration. Ask the settlement agent for the exact amount in your sale.

The forms work like this.

1. **GIT/REP-1**, the Nonresident Seller's Tax Declaration, goes to the settlement agent with the payment at closing, unless the seller is exempt or has a waiver.
2. **GIT/REP-3**, the residency or exemption certification, lets a seller claim an exemption. Box 1 is for a resident taxpayer, which may be an individual, an estate or a trust.
3. **GIT/REP-4**, a waiver request, requires Division approval, and the bulletin says to submit it at least 14 days before closing.
4. **GIT/REP-2** is the receipt for a prepayment made at a Division office.

The settlement agent, usually the buyer's attorney or title company, files the form with the deed and the realty transfer fee affidavit at the county clerk. The bulletin states that a recording officer may not record a deed without the right GIT/REP form and any payment. Excess withholding can later be claimed, for a nonresident on the NJ-1040NR or by form A-3128.

Several exemption boxes on the GIT/REP-3 matter to estates. Box 8 covers real property transferred by an executor or administrator of a decedent to a devisee or heir under the will or New Jersey intestacy law. Box 14 covers a seller who receives no net proceeds, for example where all proceeds go to pay a mortgage. Box 6 is for total consideration of $1,000 or less. If a box fits, check it. If none does, plan on the payment.

One more point from the Division's nonresident decedent FAQ: a nonresident decedent's New Jersey real estate also needs an inheritance tax waiver, by Form IT-NR or L9-NR, and the escrow question above applies. [Out-of-state property and ancillary probate](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-out-of-state-property-and-ancillary-probate) explains the Surrogate side.

## What does a sale look like from start to finish?

Here is the sequence most executors follow.

1. Get letters from the Surrogate, and several copies, after the tenth day.
2. Secure the house, keep insurance current and keep paying the mortgage and utilities. See [the mortgage when the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).
3. File the waiver request (Form L-9 or the full return) early, because it sets the pace.
4. Hire a lawyer or choose the closing attorney, then sign a contract that allows for waiver timing.
5. Settle the deed form, who signs, and any GIT/REP form with the title company.
6. At closing, pay off the mortgage, the realty transfer fee, any estimated gross income tax payment and agent and closing costs from the proceeds.
7. Hold the net proceeds in the estate account until debts, taxes and fees are paid, then distribute. See [executor duties step by step](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step).

### What about capital gains?

Most inherited property gets a step-up in basis to its value at death, so a sale near that value typically produces little gain. [Selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) covers the federal side, and [whether heirs can sell during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) covers the timing question.
