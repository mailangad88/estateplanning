Full administration in New Jersey means a bond, letters from the Surrogate, notices and an accounting. For a small estate, that is more machinery than the situation calls for. The legislature wrote two shortcuts into the probate code, one for a surviving spouse or partner and one for other family members.

This article explains who can use each one, what the limits really are, what you swear to in the affidavit and where the shortcut stops working. It is part of our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What are the two New Jersey affidavit routes?

New Jersey has two routes, and the right one depends on who survived. Both are used when the person died without a will.

| | Spouse or partner route | Next of kin route |
|---|---|---|
| Statute | N.J.S.A. 3B:10-3 | N.J.S.A. 3B:10-4 |
| Who signs | Surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner | One heir |
| Survivors | A spouse or partner exists | No spouse or partner |
| Dollar limit | Total real and personal assets of $50,000 or less | $20,000 or less |
| Other heirs | No consent requirement stated | Remaining heirs consent in writing |
| Bond | Not addressed in the section | Not required |
| Filed with | Surrogate of the county where the decedent lived | Surrogate or Superior Court |

The limits come from P.L. 2015, c. 232, approved January 19, 2016, which amended both sections. Before that, the limits were lower. Anyone using an old form or an old website should check the current dollar figure with the Surrogate.

Our national article on the [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) shows how this tool works in other states, which set very different limits.

## How does the spouse or partner affidavit work?

The surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner signs an affidavit before the Surrogate of the county where the decedent lived. If the decedent was not a New Jersey resident, it goes to the county where assets are located. It can also be filed with the Superior Court. The affidavit must state:

- that the person is the surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner,
- that the estate's value will not exceed $50,000,
- where the decedent lived at death, and
- the nature, location and value of the assets.

The affidavit is filed and recorded with the Surrogate. The statute then lets the survivor take all the assets without administration, and it gives the first $10,000 special protection: that amount is free from the decedent's debts.

The dollar test counts "real and personal assets of the estate." That wording means a house in the decedent's sole name counts toward the $50,000. A couple that owned everything jointly may find there is almost no probate estate at all, and no affidavit is needed. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for what counts and what passes by title.

Consider Helen, whose husband Frank dies without a will. His name alone is on a $28,000 savings account and a $9,000 car. The house is in both names. Helen's total is $37,000, comfortably under $50,000. She uses the spouse route. If Frank's name had also been alone on a $40,000 brokerage account, the total would be $77,000 and Helen would need to ask the Surrogate to open a regular administration.

## How does the next of kin affidavit work?

If there is no spouse or partner and the estate is $20,000 or less, one heir can take the assets for the benefit of all the heirs and creditors without administration or bond. The other heirs must consent in writing (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-4). Atlantic County's Surrogate says it supplies consent forms, and that the office wants documents showing the assets, such as car titles, registrations and bank statements.

The heir signs an affidavit before the Surrogate or the Superior Court listing:

- where the decedent lived at death,
- the names, residences and relationships of all heirs,
- the nature, location and value of the assets, and
- a statement that the value will not exceed $20,000.

Who the heirs are is set by New Jersey's intestacy rules. If you are not sure who counts, start with [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey). One estranged sibling who will not sign is enough to push the matter out of this route.

Anil and his sister Nisha are the only children of a widowed father who dies with $14,000 in a bank account. Anil takes the next of kin route. Nisha signs a consent form. The Surrogate files the affidavit, and Anil collects the account for himself and Nisha. He pays the funeral bill first from the account.

## What do you take on by signing?

You take on the job of an administrator, with the same exposure. Both statutes say that after filing the affidavit, the person has the powers and duties of a duly appointed administrator. The spouse section adds that the person can be sued and required to account as if appointed. In other words, the affidavit is a shortcut for the paperwork, not for the responsibility.

That has practical consequences:

1. Keep every receipt. If a creditor or heir asks where the money went, you need an answer.
2. Pay valid debts before you spend the assets on yourself, and do not distribute until you are sure of what is owed. Our national article on [creditors in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) outlines the general approach.
3. Report assets honestly. The affidavit lists the nature, location and value, and you are swearing to it.
4. Do not use the affidavit if the total is close to the line. If a car is worth more than you think, the estate may not qualify.

## What does the Surrogate need from you?

Bring a death certificate, identification and documents showing what the person owned. Atlantic County's Surrogate lists examples such as car titles, registrations and bank statements. The office does not need an appointment, according to the same page, and posts an information sheet you can complete first. Because Surrogates run their own offices, requirements vary, so call before you go. Your county Surrogate's office can confirm what it accepts.

If you live outside New Jersey, ask the Surrogate whether it will authorize you to sign in your home state. Both statutes allow that, with written permission, before an officer authorized to take such oaths.

## When should you use full probate instead?

Use the regular process when any of these apply:

- There is a will. County Surrogates say a will must be probated.
- The estate is over the limit.
- The heirs disagree or cannot all be found.
- Real estate in the decedent's sole name has to be sold or retitled and a title company will want letters. Ask the company first.
- You are worried about debts or claims.

The ordinary process is described in our [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) article. Even in a small estate, check whether an inheritance tax waiver is needed to release a bank account. The [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article explains the waivers; the Division of Taxation's Form L-8 is the self-executing waiver for bank accounts passing to Class A beneficiaries such as a spouse or children.
