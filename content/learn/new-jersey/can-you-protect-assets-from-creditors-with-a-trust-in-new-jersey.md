Whether a trust can protect assets from creditors in New Jersey depends on who the creditors are and who set up the trust. Your own creditors get very little stopped by a trust you can control. Your heirs' creditors can be held back by a trust you leave them, if it is built correctly. Most of the answers sit in New Jersey's Uniform Trust Code, N.J.S.A. 3B:31.

This page is New Jersey specific and narrower than our national articles on [whether a living trust protects assets from creditors](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-protect-assets-from-creditors) and on [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts). It sits within our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Can your own creditors reach a trust you created?

Yes, if you can take the property back or receive it. The Code addresses two cases under the heading "Creditor's claim against settlor" (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-39).

- **Revocable trust.** During your life the property is subject to claims of your creditors. After your death, property of a trust that was revocable can be reached for your creditors, estate administration costs, funeral expenses and a surviving spouse or civil union partner and children, but only to the extent the probate estate cannot cover them.
- **Irrevocable trust where you are a beneficiary.** A creditor may reach the maximum amount that could be distributed to you or for your benefit. If several people funded the trust, the reach is limited to the share attributable to the person being pursued.

The Code says this applies whether or not the trust has a spendthrift clause. A clause in the document cannot override it. That is the main reason a living trust is not an asset protection tool; our [living trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) page covers what it is good for.

## What about a trust you create for yourself that creditors cannot touch?

The lawyers' name for it is a self-settled trust. Under the section above, the Code gives no exception for a trust you fund and can benefit from, and a creditor may reach what the trustee could pay you. People who want protection therefore have to give up access, and that costs something real. If you cannot receive distributions, the trust cannot easily be reached for your debts, but you cannot use the money either.

Here is a worked example, with invented numbers. Hal, 62, a contractor, moves $400,000 of savings into an irrevocable trust for his daughters. He is not a beneficiary and has no right to take the money back. Three years later a customer wins a judgment against him for $90,000. The trust is out of the customer's reach unless the customer can show the transfer was a fraudulent one. If Hal had named himself as a discretionary beneficiary, the customer could reach the largest amount the trustee could pay him.

Control is the question. Naming a trustee who always does what you say does not change the analysis, and a court looks at what the document allows, not at your family's habits. Our article on [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) describes what you give up.

## When does a transfer to a trust get undone?

When it was made to hinder, delay or defraud a creditor, or when you received too little in return and were in financial trouble. New Jersey's fraudulent transfer statute makes a transfer voidable by a creditor whose claim arose before or after the transfer (N.J.S.A. 25:2-25). It has two routes:

1. **Actual intent.** You transferred with the intent to hinder, delay or defraud a creditor.
2. **Constructive fraud.** You did not receive reasonably equivalent value and you were left with unreasonably small assets for your business, or you expected to take on debts you could not pay.

The creditor must prove either by a preponderance of the evidence. The statute also sets time limits (N.J.S.A. 25:2-31). For a transfer made with actual intent, the claim is extinguished unless brought within four years of the transfer, or within one year of discovery if that is later. A constructive fraud claim under the other subsection also has a four-year limit.

The mistake our attorneys see most often is timing. Someone receives a demand letter, then asks about a trust. At that point a transfer looks like exactly what the statute describes. The same logic applies to long-term care: Medicaid uses its own look-back, 60 months in most states, covered in our article on [the Medicaid asset protection trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) and [Medicaid and nursing home planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning).

## Can a trust protect what you leave to your children from their creditors?

Yes, and this is where trusts do their best work. The tool is a spendthrift clause. In New Jersey it is valid only if it restrains both voluntary and involuntary transfers of the beneficiary's interest, and describing the interest as held in a "spendthrift trust" is enough to do that (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-36). With a valid clause, creditors and assignees generally cannot reach the interest or the trustee's distributions before the beneficiary receives them. The clause stays valid even if the beneficiary is the sole trustee or a co-trustee.

Without a clause, the rule reverses. A creditor may reach a beneficiary's interest by attachment of present or future distributions, and a court can limit the relief (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-35).

| Trust design | What a beneficiary's creditor can do |
|---|---|
| Outright inheritance, no trust | Reach everything once it is received |
| Trust without a spendthrift clause | Attach present and future distributions (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-35) |
| Trust with a valid spendthrift clause | Generally nothing before payout (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-36) |
| Discretionary distributions to the beneficiary | Cannot force the trustee to pay (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-38) |
| Mandatory distribution, overdue | Can reach the overdue amount (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-40) |

The last two rows are the design choices. A discretionary trust keeps the trustee in charge of timing, so a creditor cannot compel a payment, even if the trust gives a standard for distributions and even if the trustee has been abusive. A beneficiary can still sue a trustee for abuse of discretion (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-38). A mandatory payment, like "all income to my son each year," is different. If it is overdue by a reasonable time, a creditor can reach it.

Consider Marcus, 41, who is deep in medical debt. His mother leaves him $300,000 in a discretionary spendthrift trust. A creditor with an $80,000 judgment cannot make the trustee pay. If the trustee pays Marcus's rent and car loan directly, the creditor still has nothing to reach. If the trustee hands Marcus a $50,000 check, that cash is his and is exposed.

Choose the trustee carefully. A brother who is also a lender, or a trustee who cannot say no, undermines the plan. ## What other tools protect assets in New Jersey?

A trust is rarely the first tool. Liability insurance and an umbrella policy come first for lawsuits. For a married couple, how you hold a house matters too: see [joint property and tenancy by the entirety in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/joint-property-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety-in-new-jersey). For a family member with a disability, a special needs trust can be shielded by statute (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-37), and our page on [special needs planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey) explains that. For deaths, creditor claims against an estate follow separate rules in [creditor claims and paying debts of a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate).

Retirement accounts and certain exemptions have their own protections that depend on the type of account and the claim, so do not assume a trust adds anything there. Ask before you retitle an IRA or a 401(k).
