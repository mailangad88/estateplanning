The first piece of paper an executor needs is also the one that holds up everything else: a certified death certificate with a raised seal. The Surrogate wants one, the bank wants one, the MVC wants one. Photocopies do not work.

In New Jersey you cannot simply look the record up. The state's Office of Vital Statistics says vital records are not public records and that state law restricts their release. This page covers who may order, the ways to order, what the state charges, and how to count how many copies your own estate will use. The wider process is in our [New Jersey probate guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process).

## Who can order a New Jersey death certificate?

The state's list for certified copies of a death record is short. These people may order one.

- The person's parent, legal guardian or legal representative.
- The person's spouse or civil union partner, or a child, grandchild or sibling who is of legal age.
- A state or federal agency acting for an official purpose.
- Anyone holding a court order.

That list covers deaths in roughly the last 40 years, which the state treats as non-genealogical records. Older records follow separate genealogical rules, and deaths through 1950 are held by the State Archives rather than Vital Statistics. A niece, a friend or a longtime unmarried partner is not on the list. If that person is the one handling affairs, they get a certified copy by being appointed executor, or by having an eligible relative sign a notarized written release and send ID.

### What proof does an executor or attorney need?

An executor must supply proof of appointment as executor. An attorney acting for the executor must supply proof of the retainer and proof of the executor's appointment. An attorney acting for a family member must show the retainer and the relationship. An attorney who is not the representative of an eligible person needs a court order, and the state says a subpoena is not enough.

The state's most common reason for rejecting a certified copy application is missing proof of relationship. For a child, that is typically the child's own birth certificate showing the parent's name. For a spouse, the marriage or civil union certificate. Send copies of ID, not originals, and the ID must show a current address.

## Where and how do you order?

There are three routes, and each is best for a different problem.

1. **The municipality where the death occurred.** Contact the local registrar. Fees vary by municipality, and same-day service is available in person at the local office. A local copy is valid for most purposes.
2. **The state Office of Vital Statistics.** Order by mail, through VitalChek online (available at any hour), or by phone. VitalChek's phone number is 877-622-7549. The state also runs a walk-in center at 140 E. Front Street in Trenton for same-day service, and it no longer accepts cash.
3. **A funeral director.** The state's page does not discuss them, but funeral homes in practice file the record with the local registrar and often offer to order the first batch. Ask when you make arrangements.

Faxed records are not accepted, and a mailed application with the wrong fee or no ID comes back to you. Cash cannot be mailed, and credit cards cannot be accepted by mail, only through the online site. The mailed certificate goes to the address on your ID unless you send a notarized letter.

## What does a New Jersey death certificate cost?

The state charges $25 for the initial search and one certified copy, and $2 for each additional copy ordered at the same time. VitalChek adds a processing fee, listed as $6 for mail orders and $12.95 for phone orders, and UPS tracking is $18 in the continental United States if you want it. Municipal offices set their own prices. The state's pages do not list processing times, so ask VitalChek or the office when you order.

The cheap time to buy extras is the first order. Ordering one copy now and nine later means nine more searches at $25 each at the state office, while nine extras ordered with the first are $2 each.

## How many copies does an executor need?

Plan on about ten for an average estate, and more if there are many accounts or property in other states. That figure is a practical rule of thumb from our attorneys, not a number the state publishes. Count your own list before you order. Here is one estate, as an example only.

| Who asks for one | Copies |
|---|---|
| County Surrogate (letters, or small estate affidavit) | 1 |
| Bank or credit union, per account | 1 each, 3 accounts |
| Life insurance claim | 1 |
| Pension or retirement plan | 1 |
| Brokerage or IRA custodian | 1 |
| MVC for the car title | 1 |
| Deed transfer or sale of the house | 1 |
| Social Security, final tax return, safe keeping | 2 |
| **Total for this example** | **11** |

## Which version do you want: long form or short?

The state notes that a long-form copy shows the burial location, which helps you find a cemetery plot. If a bank or agency tells you a copy was not accepted, ask what it is missing before you reorder.

## What should you do first if you are the one handling everything?

Call the funeral home and ask three things: whether the record has been filed with the local registrar, how many certified copies they have ordered for you, and what each additional copy costs. Then call the Surrogate of the county where the person lived. County Surrogates say probate and letters come only after the tenth day after death, so you have a week or so to gather paper, and ordering copies is the best use of it.

If you are not on the eligible list yourself, such as a close friend named as executor, ask a spouse or adult child to place the first order before letters issue. Once letters arrive, the executor can order the rest with proof of appointment.

## What if the certificate has a mistake?

A wrong name, birth date or Social Security number can stall a claim. The state's page sends corrections to a separate Corrections page, and a court order may be needed if you cannot supply required details. Our guide to [fixing a mistake on a death certificate](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-correct-a-mistake-on-a-death-certificate) walks through the general process. Start with the funeral director, who usually filed the original information.

## A quick checklist before you order

- Full name as on the record, city of the death, the exact date, mother's maiden name, and father's name if recorded.
- A copy of ID that shows your current address.
- A document proving your relationship, or proof of appointment as executor.
- A payment method that works for the route you chose.
- A count of copies, with two spares.
