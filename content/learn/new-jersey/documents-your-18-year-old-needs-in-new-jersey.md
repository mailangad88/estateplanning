Dana, 49, in Montclair, has a daughter who turned 18 in June and leaves for a college three hours away in the fall. Dana has always handled Priya's doctor visits, insurance and the portal passwords. On the birthday, none of that changed in Dana's mind. It changed in the eyes of the law.

This page is the New Jersey checklist for that moment: which documents to sign, how New Jersey wants them signed, and what parents still cannot do afterward. The general version of the story is in [what happens if an 18-year-old has a medical emergency with no HIPAA release](/learn/what-if/adult-child-medical-emergency-no-hipaa), and the broader list of papers is in [estate planning at 18](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults). Here we stay on New Jersey forms, as part of our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What can parents no longer do once a child turns 18 in New Jersey?

Parents generally lose the right to consent to treatment, see medical records and manage money for their child. An adult child decides, and the doctor's duty of confidentiality runs to the child. A parent who walks into an emergency room with no paper may be told very little and may be asked to wait for a social worker.

Money works the same way. A parent cannot log in to a university student account, talk to the bank about the child's checking account or sign a lease for the child. Parents on the same cell plan or insurance policy may still get bills, but a bill is not a record.

| Task | Before 18 | After 18, no documents | After 18, with documents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Consent to treatment | Parent | Child, or if unconscious, whoever the hospital's rules allow | Health care representative named by the child |
| Talk to the doctor | Parent | Only with child's permission | Anyone named in a HIPAA authorization |
| Pay bills or deal with the bank | Parent | Child only | Agent under a power of attorney |
| Court involvement | None | Possible guardianship petition | Usually none |

## What is the New Jersey proxy directive and how does it work?

The proxy directive is New Jersey's name for a health care power of attorney. It names a health care representative to make medical decisions if your child cannot, and it can name alternates. The Department of Health offers it as a free PDF form alongside an instructive directive, which is the living will (the Advance Directives for Health Care Act, N.J.S.A. 26:2H-53 and following).

Three signing rules are worth repeating to a young adult, because they trip people up at a kitchen table.

1. The directive must be in writing and signed by your child.
2. It needs either a notary or other authorized official, or two adult witnesses who sign and date it.
3. The representative, and any alternate, cannot be a witness. Dana therefore cannot be one of the witnesses on Priya's form.

The directive takes effect only after a physician decides your child cannot make their own decisions. Until then Priya talks to her own doctors. That is a feature to explain to a teenager who worries you are taking over. For the full rules, including who may serve, see [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives).

One practical point: a college student's friends are not good choices as the only agent, because roommates move and lose touch. Name a parent first and an adult sibling, aunt or uncle as an alternate. Our guide on [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) goes into this choice.

## Why does an 18-year-old also need a HIPAA release?

The proxy directive gives your child's representative the right to review medical records once it takes effect, but a HIPAA authorization covers a different moment: the one before anyone has decided your child lacks capacity. If Priya is in the hospital and awake, a doctor may talk to Dana only if Priya agrees. If Priya is groggy and the hospital is unsure, a signed release removes the doubt.

The state's directive pages do not mention HIPAA, so do not assume the proxy form covers it. A separate authorization names the people who may receive information, what it covers and when it expires or is revoked. Many families have the college sign one on arrival, but the hospital near campus will honor one from home just as well. Details on drafting are in [HIPAA authorizations: why your family may be shut out without one](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization).

## Does a New Jersey 18-year-old need a durable power of attorney?

Usually yes, though it matters less than the medical papers. A durable power of attorney lets an agent handle bank accounts, bills, taxes and similar matters if your child cannot. New Jersey's Revised Durable Power of Attorney Act asks for a writing, a signature and an acknowledgment, and it survives incapacity only if the document includes language showing that intent (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.9 and 8.2).

Most 18-year-olds have little to manage, so a short document with limited powers can fit. Picture Priya hurt in a car accident in March of her sophomore year. Her landlord needs the rent, her phone bill is due and her scholarship office needs a form. Without an agent, Dana would have to ask a court for authority, which is slow and public. With a power of attorney, Dana can pay the rent the same week.

Two cautions. First, gift powers need to be spelled out in the document because the Act does not address them, and most parents of an 18-year-old will want none. Second, your child should understand that the agent owes fiduciary duties and must keep records. For more, see [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) and [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

## What happens if your child never signs anything?

Doctors turn to family, and a parent may be asked, but the law does not promise a parent that role. If no one has clear authority and a dispute or a long incapacity develops, a parent may need to ask the Superior Court for guardianship. That is a court case with a filing, medical evidence and a hearing, and it ends your child's say over key decisions. See [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey), and the plain comparison in [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

Compare that with the signing rules above. A form that takes an hour at a notary replaces a case that can take months.

## A signing checklist for the summer before college

1. Sit down together and explain what each paper does and does not do. Your child decides the agents.
2. Download the proxy directive from the Department of Health. Have your child pick a representative and an alternate.
3. Prepare a HIPAA authorization naming the same people.
4. Prepare a durable power of attorney, with limited powers if you prefer.
5. Sign the proxy directive with two adult witnesses who are not named in it, or in front of a notary. Sign the power of attorney in front of a notary.
6. Scan every signed page. Put copies on your phone, your child's phone and a shared folder.
7. Give paper copies to your child's primary care doctor and, if your child sees campus health, to that office.
8. Note the next review date. A change of agent or a marriage means new papers.

Your child can revoke any of these, and should be told how. The same steps are covered for [a New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/how-to-revoke-a-new-jersey-power-of-attorney).

## What if my child has a disability?

If your child can understand and sign, these same documents can work. If they cannot, the choice may be between guardianship and other tools. New Jersey also has special needs trusts and benefit rules that start to matter at 18. Talk with an attorney before the birthday, not after.
