When someone dies in New Jersey, probate does not begin in a large courthouse with a judge. It begins at a counter in the county Surrogate's office, usually with a stack of paper and a death certificate. The process is more orderly than most families fear, but it has a few steps and one deadline that people miss.

This article follows the process in order. It draws on the New Jersey statutes, the court rules and the instructions that county Surrogates publish. For the bigger picture, start with our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Where does probate happen in New Jersey?

Probate is filed with the Surrogate of the county where the person lived. Middlesex County's Surrogate tells families to use the county on the death certificate. The Surrogate's court and the Superior Court both have power to admit a will and issue letters (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-17), and a resident's will may be probated in either (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-24).

A will probated only in another state has no effect in New Jersey until it is also probated here (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-24). That matters for retirees who kept a home in New Jersey and live in Florida, and for the reverse. Our general article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains the second filing.

## What happens in the first 10 days?

Nothing can be finished, but you can prepare. County Surrogates say the probate and administration process cannot be completed until 10 days after the date of death, and the Middlesex County guide puts it as "probate and the issuance of letters occur only after the 10th day after death." Use the time to do these things:

1. Find the original will. Check the home, a safe deposit box and the lawyer who prepared it. Give the original to the named executor.
2. Order certified death certificates with the raised seal. Funeral directors usually order them.
3. List the next of kin by full name and address, including anyone estranged.
4. If there is no will, list the assets in the decedent's sole name that have no named beneficiary.
5. Bring a photo ID for the person who will serve.

Monmouth County's Surrogate asks for a certified death certificate, the original will with any codicils, and identification. Counties differ on whether you need an appointment, so call first.

## How does the Surrogate admit the will?

The executor, or the executor's attorney, brings the original will to the Surrogate. If the will is self-proving, probate can happen quickly, according to the Morris County Surrogate's guide. If it is not, one of the witnesses must appear or give proof of their signature. A will executed under the signing statute can be probated on the proof of one attesting witness or another person with knowledge of the execution (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-19). To see why a self-proving affidavit matters, read our [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements).

Once the will is admitted, the Surrogate issues letters testamentary to the executor. The statute speaks of granting "letters testamentary or letters of administration with the will annexed" (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-17). Letters are the document banks and title companies ask for. Our article on [letters testamentary and letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) lists who asks for them and why.

On bond, Morris County's guide says a will can provide that an executor serves without bond. An administrator, who is appointed when there is no will, must post a surety bond, set by the Surrogate and usually based on the estate's value.

Court fees are set by the Legislature, according to the Morris County Surrogate. Counties accept different forms of payment. Your county Surrogate's office can confirm what it accepts.

## What if there is no will?

The Surrogate appoints an administrator, who distributes the estate under New Jersey's intestate succession rules, and a letter of administration is issued. If the estate is small, the family may be able to skip this. Our article on the [New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) explains the $50,000 and $20,000 routes. For the order of heirs, see [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey).

## What must the executor do within 60 days?

The executor must mail a notice of probate to all beneficiaries named in the will and all next of kin within 60 days after the will is probated (R. 4:80-6). The notice states that the will was probated, the date and place, the name and address of the executor, and that a copy of the will is available on request.

Three details trip people up:

- **Unknown addresses.** If an address can't be found through reasonable inquiry, the Morris County instructions say to publish the notice in a newspaper of general circulation in the county.
- **Gifts to charity.** If the will leaves property to a charity, the same instructions say to send the notice and a copy of the will to the Attorney General's Charitable Trust Section.
- **Proof of mailing.** The executor files the original notice and a signed proof of mailing with the Surrogate within 10 days of mailing.

Take Ravi, named executor for his aunt's estate. The Surrogate admits the will on March 3. His 60 days end May 2, so he mails notices to his aunt's three nieces and two nephews in the middle of March and files the proof of mailing the next week. A missed notice is the kind of lapse that gives an unhappy relative something to raise with the court.

## What happens after probate?

After the letters are issued, the executor collects assets, pays valid debts, deals with taxes and distributes what is left. Two items are specific to New Jersey.

First, the inheritance tax. The executor must deal with the Division of Taxation if property passes to anyone other than Class A beneficiaries (spouse, children and the like), and the return is due eight months after death. Banks and title companies may require a tax waiver before releasing assets. The details are in our article on [the New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

Second, creditor claims and payment. For how the executor handles debts, our national article on [creditor claims in probate](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) shows the general pattern. Keep a ledger of every payment. The executor's pay and the timeline are covered in [New Jersey executor commissions and the probate timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline).

## When does the Superior Court get involved?

Disagreements leave the Surrogate's counter and enter the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part. The Surrogate also serves as deputy clerk of the Superior Court and keeps the files for contested matters, according to the Morris County guide. The court's model form for starting a probate action by order to show cause cites R. 4:83-1 and R. 4:83-3.

Cases that tend to end up there include:

- a challenge to the will's validity,
- a request to remove an executor,
- a handwritten will, which the Morris County guide says can be admitted only in Superior Court, and
- a dispute over the interpretation of the will.

A caveat is the early-warning tool. Someone who suspects trouble files a caveat with the Surrogate before the executor is appointed, which temporarily blocks the appointment (Morris County Surrogate guide). After the appointment, the route is an application to the court for removal. Caveats are not a casual step, because they can freeze an estate. Our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains when disputes succeed.

## What is the difference between a small and a regular probate?

| | Full probate | Small estate affidavit |
|---|---|---|
| Needs a will probated | Yes | No, intestate only |
| Who is appointed | Executor or administrator | Affiant gets administrator powers |
| Dollar limit | None | $50,000 (spouse) or $20,000 (heirs) |
| Where | Surrogate | Surrogate |

If the estate is small, ask first whether you can skip probate. For the general idea, see [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) and [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).
