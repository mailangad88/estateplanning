You signed a New Jersey will years ago and something has changed. A child was born, a marriage ended, an executor moved away, or you simply want to give the house to someone else. The question is how to make the change so it holds up when a Surrogate reads it after your death.

New Jersey's rules sit in Title 3B of the statutes, mainly N.J.S.A. 3B:3-13 through 3B:3-15 for revoking, reviving and the effect of divorce. This page walks through each route, which ones are safe, and which ones cause trouble. Our general article on [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) covers the common ground, and the [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey) lists the rest of our state material.

## How can a New Jersey will be revoked?

A will, or part of one, is revoked in one of two ways under N.J.S.A. 3B:3-13: by a later will, or by a physical act on the document. Those are the only routes the statute describes for the maker acting alone, and each has conditions.

| Method | What it takes | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Later will that says it revokes | A new will signed with full formalities | Forgetting that the new will must itself be validly signed and witnessed |
| Later will that conflicts | A new will with terms that cannot stand beside the old one | Assuming silence means the old will is gone |
| Burning, tearing, canceling, obliterating, destroying | Done by you with intent to revoke, or by another person in your conscious presence at your direction | Leaving the act to someone else out of the room |
| Codicil | A signed, witnessed amendment | Writing the change by hand on the original |

A physical act counts when it is done to the will itself. The statute says burning, tearing or canceling revokes the will even if no words are touched, so a diagonal line across the first page can do the job. A burned corner counts as much as a burned page.

## What does a later will do to the earlier one?

A later will revokes an earlier will either by saying so or by being inconsistent with it. The easy case is the express one, where the first article of the new will reads that all prior wills and codicils are revoked. It is a sentence worth having in every will.

When the new will is silent, the statute sets two presumptions. If the new will makes a complete disposition of your estate, it is presumed to replace the earlier will. If it covers only part of your estate, it is presumed to supplement the earlier will, and the old one is revoked only where the two conflict. Both presumptions can be overcome only by clear and convincing evidence.

Take Gerald, who signed a will in 2012 leaving everything to his two daughters. In 2021 he signed a short second will naming his nephew as executor and leaving his boat to a friend. It does not mention the first will. Because the second will does not dispose of the whole estate, the presumption is that both wills work together. The nephew serves, the friend gets the boat, and the daughters take the rest under the 2012 will. If Gerald meant to cancel the first will, he needed to say so. If he meant to keep it, the result is right, but the Surrogate and his daughters will have two documents to read together, which is how disputes start.

## How do you change a will with a codicil?

A codicil is a short amendment to an existing will. The 3B:3-15 text speaks of a "duly executed codicil," which means it must be signed and witnessed the same way as a will. Our article on [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) covers who signs and when the self-proving affidavit is added.

A codicil is a reasonable fit for one narrow change, such as swapping an executor. It is a poor fit when the changes are many, when the original has been marked up, or when several codicils already exist. Every added document is another chance for a missing page or a mismatch. For anything beyond a line or two, a new will that expressly revokes the old one is cleaner, and it leaves one document for the executor to hand the Surrogate.

Here is a short sequence that works for most people:

1. Find the original will and any codicils, and read them together.
2. Decide whether the change is one clause or a rewrite.
3. For one clause, have a codicil drafted that names the will by date and states which paragraph it changes.
4. For a rewrite, sign a new will that revokes all earlier wills and codicils.
5. Sign with two witnesses and add a self-proving affidavit.
6. Keep the new original safe. Our guide on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) explains the options.
7. Do not destroy the old will until the new one is signed. A gap in the paper trail helps no one.

## Can a revoked will come back?

Generally no. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:3-15, a revoked will or codicil is not revived unless it is re-executed or a duly executed codicil shows an intent to revive it. The statute then covers a later will that is itself revoked. There are three cases.

- If the later will wholly revoked the earlier one and is then revoked by an act, the earlier will stays revoked unless clear and convincing evidence shows you meant it to take effect as signed.
- If the later will only partly revoked the earlier one and is then revoked by an act, the revoked part of the earlier will is revived unless clear and convincing evidence shows you did not intend that.
- If a later will revoked an earlier one and is then revoked by a still later will, the earlier will stays revoked unless it is revived, and then only to the extent the terms of the latest will show you intended it to take effect.

The practical point is simple. Tearing up a new will you have second thoughts about does not reliably bring back the old one. If you want the old terms, say so in a new document.

## What does divorce do to a New Jersey will?

N.J.S.A. 3B:3-14 provides that, unless the will, a court order or a contract relating to the division of the marital property says otherwise, a divorce or annulment revokes the following:

- Revocable dispositions of property to the former spouse.
- Revocable dispositions to relatives of the former spouse.
- Powers of appointment given to the former spouse or those relatives.
- Fiduciary nominations of the former spouse or those relatives, such as executor or trustee.

Joint tenancies with right of survivorship, and tenancies by the entirety, between the former spouses are converted into tenancies in common. If the two remarry each other, or the divorce is revoked, suspended or nullified, the revoked provisions are restored. A buyer who pays value in good faith without notice is generally protected, and a payor is not liable for a payment made under a governing instrument unless it received written notice of the claimed revocation first.

Two cautions. The rest of the will stays alive, so the shares that used to go to the ex-spouse go wherever the remaining terms and the residuary clause send them, and that may not be where you would choose. And the statute is no substitute for new forms with banks, insurers and plan administrators. Our article on [what happens when an ex-spouse is still the beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) shows how often that goes wrong.

## What about marriage after the will is signed?

A marriage after signing does not revoke the will, but it can give the new spouse a floor. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:5-15, a spouse or domestic partner who married or entered the partnership after the will was signed receives at least the intestate share. There are three exceptions. The will was made in contemplation of the marriage. The will says it survives a later marriage. Or you provided for the spouse outside the will as a substitute for a gift. The surviving spouse's other protections are described in [the New Jersey elective share article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse).

## What if the original will cannot be found?

If you destroy a will on purpose, the revocation is real. If it simply disappears, the question becomes whether it was revoked or lost. That is a proof problem for the executor, not a drafting problem, and our article on [a lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will) goes through it. A signed copy kept with your attorney is worth having.
