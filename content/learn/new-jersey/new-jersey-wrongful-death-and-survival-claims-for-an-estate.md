After a death caused by a car crash, a medical error, a fall at a store or a workplace accident, the family usually expects the lawsuit to belong to whoever is hurting most. New Jersey does not work that way. The claim belongs to the estate's representative, who sues in the estate's name, and the money then goes to a different group of people than the will names. If you are asking who files and who gets paid, those are two separate answers.

This page explains how New Jersey wrongful death and survival claims fit around a probate estate: who must be appointed, how the recovery is divided, what the deadline is, and how it affects the executor's job. It sits in our [New Jersey estate guide](/learn/new-jersey). It does not cover whether you have a good case, which depends on the facts and needs a personal injury lawyer. For the estate side, start with the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process).

## Who can file a wrongful death claim in New Jersey?

Only the representative of the estate, in the representative's name. N.J.S.A. 2A:31-2 requires the action to be brought by an administrator ad prosequendum or the decedent's administrator, or by the executor named in a probated will once qualified, or by an administrator with the will annexed where no executor serves. The people who will receive the money are not the plaintiffs.

If the person died without a will and nobody has opened an estate, the Surrogate can appoint an administrator ad prosequendum under N.J.S.A. 3B:10-11. That appointment is limited to bringing the claim. It can be granted by the Surrogate in the county where the decedent lived, or, for someone who lived out of state, the county where the fatal accident happened. The statute says an administrator ad prosequendum does not have to give a bond. This is useful for a family that has no reason to administer the rest of the estate, for example when the decedent owned little.

A plaintiff who qualified for appointment but had not yet been appointed when the complaint was filed may be treated as the representative, and amended pleadings can relate back to the first filing (2A:31-2(b)). Do not count on that. Get the letters first.

## What is the difference between a wrongful death claim and a survival claim?

They are two claims that cover different losses.

| | Wrongful death | Survival |
|---|---|---|
| What it covers | The survivors' losses from the death, including financial loss | The decedent's own damages from the injury until death, such as pain and medical bills |
| Statute | N.J.S.A. 2A:31-1 and following | N.J.S.A. 2A:15-3 |
| Who brings it | The estate representative | The estate representative |
| Who receives it | Relatives who take intestate personal property, or dependents as a court apportions | The estate, in the usual view |
| Funeral costs | Recoverable | Recoverable |

Section 2A:15-3 lets the representative recover the damages the decedent would have had if living, and in a case caused by another person's wrongful act, also reasonable funeral and burial expenses and damages that accrued during the decedent's lifetime. Under 2A:31-5, a jury may award damages it considers fair and just for the financial loss caused by the death, plus hospital, medical and funeral expenses.

Both claims are filed by the same person, usually in the same complaint. Whether the case is one of each depends on whether the decedent survived the injury for any length of time. A person who died instantly may have little survival value; a person who spent three weeks in intensive care has medical bills and pain and suffering to claim.

## Who receives the money from a wrongful death case?

The recovery is held for the exclusive benefit of the people entitled to take the decedent's intestate personal property (N.J.S.A. 2A:31-4). That means the intestacy formula, not the will. If a surviving spouse and descendants both exist, the statute says they take in equal shares. And where anyone depended on the decedent at the time of death, a judge divides the money without a jury to reach a fair and equitable apportionment, looking at ages, health, educational needs, financial condition and other support.

Here is an example, and the numbers are invented. Luis, 52, dies in a work-zone crash. He leaves a wife, Carmen, an adult son who lives on his own, and a 16-year-old daughter, Ana, who depends on him. His will leaves everything to Carmen. The wrongful death settlement is $900,000 after fees and costs. The will does not control it. Carmen and the children are the intestate takers, and Ana, as a dependent, may be awarded a larger part than a simple equal split, because a judge considers her need. If a settlement is for a minor, the share usually has to be placed under court supervision, so read our page on [when a minor inherits money in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/minor-inherits-money-in-new-jersey-guardian-of-the-property).

For who counts as an intestate taker, see [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey). Domestic and civil union partners are treated in [our partner article](/learn/new-jersey/domestic-partners-and-civil-union-partners-in-new-jersey-estate-law).

## Is a wrongful death settlement part of the probate estate?

The part that compensates the survivors is not distributed under the will, and it is generally outside the reach of the decedent's creditors. The survival part is the decedent's own claim and is, in the usual view, estate property that passes under the will and is open to the estate's debts. Those are conclusions a lawyer should confirm for your case, and the settlement agreement should allocate the money between the two claims. An allocation that ignores the distinction invites a fight with a creditor, or with a beneficiary of the will who wants a share.

Our article on [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) is the general framework for sorting this. The wrongful death share behaves like a non-probate asset with a different map.

## What is the deadline?

Two years from the date of death, under both N.J.S.A. 2A:31-3 and 2A:15-3(b). The one exception in the statutes is for a death caused by murder, aggravated manslaughter or manslaughter where the defendant was convicted, found not guilty by reason of insanity, or adjudicated delinquent. The action may then be brought at any time.

The estate side has its own, slower clock. Opening an estate can take weeks, and an administrator ad prosequendum can be appointed much faster than a full administrator. Do not let a probate dispute over who should serve as executor run the limitations period. Claims against a government entity or a public employee usually carry separate notice requirements, and we have not confirmed them here, so raise the point with a personal injury attorney in the first weeks.

## How does this affect the executor?

Four practical points.

1. **Get letters or a limited appointment early.** Without them, no one can sue or settle.
2. **Keep the wrongful death money out of the regular estate account.** Hold it in a separate account titled for the claim, so it does not blend with money creditors can reach. Our page on [opening an estate bank account](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-bank-account-ein-and-tax-waivers) covers the usual account.
3. **Tell the lawyer about every possible heir.** A missing beneficiary can reopen a settlement.
4. **Track funeral and medical bills.** They may be part of the damages and part of the creditor claims in [paying debts of a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate).

Settlement approval, attorney fees and tax treatment are not covered by the statutes above, so treat any quoted percentage with care and ask for the fee agreement in writing.
