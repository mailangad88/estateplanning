A transfer on death designation is the closest thing to a free pass around probate: you name who gets the asset, you keep it for life, and the person you named collects it without a court file. People hear that and ask if it works for the house. In New Jersey, the answer depends on the asset, and for real estate the honest answer today is "not directly."

This page goes through the four assets people ask about: the house, brokerage accounts, bank accounts and cars. It names what New Jersey law allows, what it does not yet, and what to use in place of a deed. It pairs with the national guides on [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) and [POD and TOD accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts), and with the [New Jersey guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Does New Jersey allow a transfer on death deed?

As of this writing, no New Jersey law creates one. A bill to establish the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act, Senate Bill 3679, was introduced on February 24, 2026. The Senate Community and Urban Affairs Committee reported it with amendments on May 11, 2026. The text we read had not been enacted, and it said the act would take effect 60 days after enactment and apply only to deeds made on or after that date. The bill's status may have moved, so check the Legislature's bill page before you plan around it.

The bill gives a good picture of what a New Jersey deed might look like if it passes:

- The owner must be 18 or older and of sound mind.
- The deed must be recorded with the County Clerk before the owner's death.
- It is effective without payment and without the beneficiary's acceptance during the owner's life.
- It stays revocable, even if the deed says otherwise, by a later recorded instrument.
- If the probate estate cannot cover allowed claims, the estate may pursue the property, and the proceeding must start within 18 months of death.

Do not sign a form you found online that calls itself a New Jersey TOD deed. Until the law is enacted, a recorded document of that kind may do something very different from what you intend. Our attorneys can tell you what your county clerk will record and what it would actually accomplish.

## Can you name a TOD beneficiary on stocks and brokerage accounts?

Yes. New Jersey adopted the Uniform TOD Security Registration Act, N.J.S.A. 3B:30-1 and following. A security, whether evidenced by certificate or by account, is registered in beneficiary form when the registration includes a designation of a beneficiary to take ownership at the death of the owner, or the deaths of all multiple owners (N.J.S.A. 3B:30-5). The Act's other sections deal with how ownership is treated while the owner is alive (N.J.S.A. 3B:30-7, titled "No effect on ownership, cancellation, change permitted") and how the security is re-registered after death (N.J.S.A. 3B:30-8).

In plain terms, you stay the owner. You can sell, borrow against it or change the beneficiary. After you die, the beneficiary shows the broker a death certificate and the firm's paperwork. The account does not pass through the Surrogate's office.

Ask the firm for its TOD registration form, and read it with the same care you give a will, because for that account it controls who receives the asset.

## How do payable on death bank accounts work?

A POD account is a bank account titled in your name with a named person to receive the balance at your death. The bank sets it up, usually on a signature card or short form. I could not find a New Jersey statute on this that I read for this page, so confirm the details with your bank, including how it handles a beneficiary who dies first or a joint owner.

What a POD account does well is small, fast transfers. A beneficiary with a certified death certificate and ID can often collect without court involvement. For smaller estates, our article on the [New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) is the other tool: it applies only when there is no will, while a POD account works whether or not there is one.

Two warnings. A POD designation on a single account sends it to one person, so if you meant to split everything among three children, the accounts must be set up to match. And naming a minor directly can lead to a guardianship problem later, a point we cover in [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Can you put a transfer on death beneficiary on a New Jersey car title?

Yes, since 2023. P.L.2022, c.13, approved May 9, 2022, allows a vehicle to be titled in transfer on death form, with one or more beneficiaries. A trust may be a beneficiary. The Motor Vehicle Commission announced the program on May 18, 2023 and uses a Transfer on Death Beneficiary Designation form.

The rules, as the law states them:

1. The beneficiary has no interest in the vehicle until the owner dies, or until the last surviving owner dies if there are several.
2. The transfer is subject to the rights of all lienholders.
3. A will does not revoke or override the designation, whenever the will was signed.
4. The owner can change the beneficiary at any time, without the beneficiary's consent, by applying for a new certificate of ownership.
5. If no beneficiary survives, the vehicle belongs to the owner's estate.

The Commission says no letters of administration are needed, because the vehicle is not part of the estate. For other vehicles and what to do after a death, see [transferring a car, boat or RV](/learn/property-and-assets/transfer-a-car-boat-or-rv-after-death).

## What should you use for the house instead?

Most families choose between three options, and each has a cost.

| Option | Control while you live | Probate avoided? | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Funded revocable living trust | Full | Yes, if the deed is signed over to the trust | Setup cost and the need to retitle |
| Deed to a child or joint tenant now | Shared or lost | Yes | Exposes the house to your child's creditors and can change tax treatment |
| Will only | Full | No, goes through the Surrogate | Court filing and delay |

For most homeowners who want to avoid probate on the house, a trust is the cleanest answer today, and the signed deed to the trustee does what a TOD deed would have done. Our article on [living trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) explains when that is worth it given the state's relatively simple Surrogate process. The general comparison is in [is a TOD deed better than a trust](/learn/probate/is-a-tod-deed-better-than-a-trust).

Adding a child to the deed today is the shortcut people regret. It makes the child a present owner, which can bring in the child's divorce or creditors, and it can complicate the tax basis. We cover it in [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed).

Consider Frank, 79, a widower in Bergen County with a house, a brokerage account and a car. He names his daughter Lisa as TOD on the account, as POD on his checking, as TOD on the car title, and signs his house into a living trust with Lisa as successor trustee. After he dies, Lisa still needs the death certificate and several phone calls, but nothing needs to be probated except a small leftover item. That is the goal.

## What do these options not solve?

They avoid probate. They do not remove the New Jersey inheritance tax, which depends on who receives the property, and they should not be assumed to shelter a house from Medicaid estate recovery, which can reach beyond the probate estate in some states. If long-term care is a concern, read about [New Jersey Medicaid and nursing home planning](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning) and [estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery) before you retitle anything.
