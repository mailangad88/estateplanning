You have been qualified as executor, the Surrogate has handed you a stack of paper, and every institution you call asks for a "short certificate." The question is how many to buy. Order too few and you make a second trip. Order too many and you pay for pages that expire in a drawer.

This article is narrower than [executor duties in New Jersey, step by step](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step), which covers the whole sequence. Here we focus on the certificate itself: what it proves, how to count, who accepts it and when it goes stale. The wider picture of the filing is in the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process), and the whole state is mapped in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What is a short certificate in New Jersey?

It is a certificate issued by the county Surrogate, showing that the named executor or administrator holds authority over the estate. County guides describe it as the document for one asset at a time. Monmouth County calls it evidence of authority to transfer or sell an asset and says you need one for each. Burlington County lists stock transfers, closing bank accounts, retitling a car and selling real estate as typical uses.

Think of the letters testamentary as the full authority and the short certificate as a copy of that authority you can leave behind. A bank wants to keep something for its file. You do not want to hand over your only proof and then need it at the brokerage the next morning.

For the underlying grant of authority, see [letters testamentary and letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration).

## How many short certificates should you order?

One for each asset in the decedent's sole name that passes under the will, plus a small cushion. Anything with a named beneficiary or a surviving joint owner usually does not need one.

Sort the assets first. Our [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) splits the list. Then count.

| Asset | Needs a short certificate? |
|---|---|
| Checking or savings account in the decedent's name alone | Yes, one per bank |
| Brokerage account, no beneficiary | Yes, one per firm |
| Car titled to the decedent alone | Yes, for the MVC |
| House titled to the decedent alone | Yes, for the closing |
| Joint account with a surviving owner | Usually no |
| Life insurance or IRA with a named beneficiary | Usually no |
| Account with a payable-on-death beneficiary | Usually no |

Example only. Rosa died with a checking account at one bank, a savings account at the same bank, a small brokerage account, a car and a house, all in her name alone. Her life insurance named her daughter. That is four institutions: the bank (one certificate may cover both accounts if the bank agrees), the brokerage, the MVC and the title company. Her daughter orders five, one spare for the bank in case it keeps one and wants another for the second branch.

If the estate has a lot of small accounts, ask each institution whether it will take a copy or a single certificate for several accounts. Policies differ, and a call before the order is cheaper than an extra trip.

## What does a short certificate cost?

Counties set their own schedule, and not every county publishes a price. Monmouth County describes a $5 charge per certificate. Gloucester County says the probate fee for a two-page will includes one short certificate. Because the cost is modest, the real expense of ordering too many is wasted effort and stale dates, not money.

Check the current figure with your own Surrogate before you go. This page does not list every county's fee.

## What do banks, brokers and the MVC actually ask for?

Each wants a slightly different bundle, so read what each one asks for before your visit.

| Who | Typically asks for |
|---|---|
| Bank | Letters or short certificate, death certificate, the estate's EIN, and often its own forms; see [the estate bank account guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-bank-account-ein-and-tax-waivers) |
| Brokerage or transfer agent | Short certificate (sometimes dated within 60 days), death certificate and its own forms |
| Motor Vehicle Commission | Old title signed by the executor, the Surrogate's Short Certificate, driver's license, EIN if the estate keeps the car; see [transferring a car title](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-car-title-after-death-in-new-jersey) |
| Title company | Short certificate, letters and the tax waiver for real estate; see [selling an estate house](/learn/new-jersey/selling-an-estate-house-in-new-jersey) |

The MVC point is one executors miss. An executor with a will brings the Surrogate's Short Certificate; a car cannot be retitled in the first week anyway, because probate does not occur until after the tenth day following death. Bank release can also depend on the inheritance tax waiver, and a short certificate alone does not unlock an account if the bank is waiting on one.

## Why does an old short certificate get rejected?

Because some institutions care about the date. Monmouth County says a certificate is typically good for up to a year, but some institutions accept only one dated within 60 days. A brokerage that sits on a request for ten weeks, or a house that goes under contract six months after probate, can end with a certificate that is technically valid but too old for the person across the table.

The fix is routine. Return to the Surrogate and ask for a current certificate. Do it when the closing date is set, not the day before. If the buyer's title company needs it in hand a week in advance, you want to learn that early.

## What mistakes do executors make with short certificates?

Three come up again and again. The first is ordering before the asset list exists. A family orders twelve on day one, then finds half the accounts have beneficiaries and never needed one. The second is handing over the only certificate to the first bank and being told the brokerage wants an original too. Ask each institution whether it keeps the certificate or returns it, and write the answer down.

The third is assuming the certificate settles everything. It proves you have authority. It does not prove the inheritance tax is handled, and it does not prove the creditor window is closed. A bank can accept your certificate and still hold part of an account until the Division of Taxation issues a waiver, as the [inheritance tax article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) explains. Treat the certificate as one key in a set of three: authority, tax release and a clean death certificate.

Keep your certified [death certificates](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-death-certificates-who-can-order-and-how-many-you-need) and short certificates in one folder, labeled by institution. When a clerk asks for a document you gave away last month, the log tells you where it went.

## When does a short certificate need updating?

Three situations call for a new one.

1. **The date is too old** for the institution, as above.
2. **Your authority has changed.** If an executor resigns or is replaced, or an administrator is appointed in place of a named executor, certificates in the old person's name no longer help. See [what happens when a named executor will not or cannot serve](/learn/new-jersey/when-a-named-new-jersey-executor-will-not-or-cannot-serve).
3. **You find a new asset.** An overlooked account or an unclaimed-property payout may need its own certificate. Our article on [unclaimed property for a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/finding-unclaimed-property-for-a-new-jersey-estate) covers where those hide.

A final tip from the executors we see: keep a log with the asset, the institution, the date you handed over each certificate and whether it was returned. At month nine, you will not remember.
