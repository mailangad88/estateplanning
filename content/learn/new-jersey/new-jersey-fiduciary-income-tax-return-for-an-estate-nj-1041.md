An executor in New Jersey can end up with three different tax returns in the same year, and they are easy to confuse. One covers the person who died. One covers what the heirs receive. The third, the fiduciary income tax return, covers money the estate itself earns while you are settling it.

This article is about that third return, the NJ-1041. It shows who files, when the $10,000 line matters and how the return differs from the inheritance tax return. The rest of the executor's path is in [executor duties in New Jersey, step by step](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step), and the wider state picture is in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Does a New Jersey estate have to file an income tax return?

Sometimes. An estate is a separate taxpayer from the person who died. From the day of death, anything the estate earns, such as interest, dividends, rent or the gain on a sale, is the estate's income. The New Jersey Gross Income Tax Act says an estate or trust is not subject to the tax if its gross income is $10,000 or less (N.J.S.A. 54A:2-4). The Division of Taxation describes the same line: estates and trusts with income of $10,000 or less for the entire year owe no gross income tax.

So the real question is whether the estate crosses $10,000 in a year. Most small estates do not. A bank account holding $120,000 at a modest rate may earn a few thousand dollars in a year, and a house held for a few months produces no income at all until it sells. The ones that cross the line tend to have either rental property, a large brokerage account or a sale of appreciated property after a long administration.

## Who files the NJ-1041?

The executor or administrator files it, because the executor is the person who acts for the estate. You sign as fiduciary and use the estate's own EIN, not the decedent's Social Security number. If you have not yet obtained one, our article on the [estate bank account, EIN and tax waivers](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-bank-account-ein-and-tax-waivers) walks through it, and the federal side is covered in [IRS paperwork for an executor](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810).

If you are co-executors, one return covers the estate. If you hired an accountant for the federal fiduciary return, the same accountant will usually prepare the state return from the same numbers.

## When is the return due?

The Gross Income Tax Act sets a general rule for taxpayers: a calendar-year return is due on or before April 15 after the year ends, and a fiscal-year return is due by the fifteenth day of the fourth month after the fiscal year ends (N.J.S.A. 54A:8-1). Payment is due at the same time as the return. The Division may extend filing or payment times for good cause.

Two cautions. This section is written for taxpayers generally, and the Division's NJ-1041 instructions are the authority for how it applies to an estate, including which tax year an estate may choose. Read the instructions for the year you are filing. And the statute text we cite is from a commercial copy of the code, which says it may not be the most recent version.

## How does the NJ-1041 differ from the inheritance tax return?

They tax different things, at different times, in different places.

| | NJ-1041 fiduciary return | Inheritance tax return (IT-R) |
|---|---|---|
| What is taxed | Income the estate earns after death | What certain heirs receive |
| Who owes it | The estate, from its own income | Beneficiaries, by class of relationship |
| Due | General gross income tax schedule, set by tax year | 8 months after death |
| Late payment | Per the instructions | 10% annual interest on unpaid tax |
| Extension | Per the instructions | Filing only, Form IT-EXT; payment not extended |
| E-filing | Per the instructions | Not available; paper only |

The inheritance tax details come from the Division's filing requirements page. Filing the IT-R, or a waiver such as Form L-8 for accounts or L-9 for real estate, does nothing for the income tax return, and the reverse is also true. Our [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article covers classes, rates and who pays nothing at all.

There is also no New Jersey estate tax any more. The Division says it is no longer imposed for people who died on or after January 1, 2018. Do not let the word "estate" in a tax name mislead you. The NJ-1041 is an income tax on an estate, not an estate tax.

## How does it differ from the final NJ-1040?

The final NJ-1040 reports the decedent's own income up to the date of death. The NJ-1041 starts after death. Different taxpayer, different EIN or Social Security number, different return. The [final income tax return for someone who died](/learn/new-jersey/final-new-jersey-income-tax-return-for-someone-who-died) explains the first, including the refund and who signs. The federal parallel is in our article on [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## A worked example

Example only, with invented numbers. Nadia died in June 2026 with a will naming her son Elias as executor. Her estate held $240,000 in a money market account earning interest and a rental condo. During the first full year of administration, the estate collected about $14,400 in rent after expenses and $6,000 in interest. That is $20,400 of income, above $10,000, so Elias files an NJ-1041 for the year, signed as executor and under the estate's EIN.

Separately, Elias files an inheritance tax return or waiver. Nadia left everything to Elias and his sister, both Class A beneficiaries who owe no inheritance tax, so he uses the waiver forms rather than a full return, and the filing has nothing to do with the rental income. He also files Nadia's final NJ-1040 for the months she lived in 2026. Three returns, three purposes.

If he had sold the condo at a gain in the same year, the gain would raise the estate's income as well, which is a reason to talk to an accountant before the closing.

## What if the estate is near the line?

Ask before you distribute. Executors sometimes close an estate in November and discover a December interest payment pushed the year over the line. Keep a running total of interest, rent and gains from the first month. Include it in the ledger you keep for the beneficiaries. If the estate has a pattern of income, an accountant can tell you whether income passing to a beneficiary changes who reports it, a rule we do not summarize here because it depends on the year's instructions and the estate's facts.

A checklist for the executor:

1. Get the estate's EIN and open the estate account.
2. Track every interest, rent and dividend deposit by date.
3. Add up the year's total each quarter.
4. If it passes $10,000, tell your accountant before year end.
5. Get the current NJ-1041 form and instructions from the Division of Taxation.
6. File the NJ-1041 by the date those instructions give, and keep a copy with the estate records.
