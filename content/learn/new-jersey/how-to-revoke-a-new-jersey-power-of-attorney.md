You signed a power of attorney years ago, and now you want it gone. Maybe the daughter you named in 2018 moved overseas, or the agent is a brother you no longer trust, or you want to replace the old form with a better one. In New Jersey you can do that, as long as you can still understand what you are signing. The harder part is not the revocation. It is making sure everyone who might rely on the old document hears about it.

This page walks through the three legal methods, the replacement question, and a notice plan for banks and the old agent. It is part of our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). For how a power of attorney works day to day, read [New Jersey power of attorney: signing, durability and agent duties](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney).

## How do you revoke a power of attorney in New Jersey?

The Revised Durable Power of Attorney Act says a power of attorney is revoked when any one of three things happens (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.10).

1. All executed originals are physically destroyed.
2. The principal signs and acknowledges a written revocation.
3. The principal delivers a written revocation to the agent.

Each has a weakness. Destroying the originals sounds simple, but many people do not know who holds the originals. The agent may have one, the bank another, and a lawyer a third. A signed and acknowledged revocation, meaning you sign it before a notary, is the cleanest because it leaves a record. Delivery to the agent works, but you must be able to prove delivery, so use a method that gives a receipt or a signature.

Our attorneys usually combine methods: a notarized revocation, delivered to the agent and every institution, and collection of any original that can be found.

## What should a revocation letter say?

It should say who you are, which document you are revoking, and that all authority ends now. Keep it plain. A good letter includes:

- Your full name and address.
- The title and signing date of the power of attorney, and the agent's name.
- A sentence such as "I revoke all authority granted in that document, effective immediately."
- A request that the agent return all originals and copies and stop acting.
- Your signature, the date, and a notary acknowledgment.

Do not guess at statute language. Ask an attorney to draft it if the document was recorded with a county, if real estate is involved, or if you expect a fight. For a recorded document, ask the county clerk or the title company how a revocation is placed on the record.

## Does signing a new power of attorney cancel the old one?

Not by itself. A later power of attorney does not revoke an earlier one unless it says so in plain words. If you are replacing your document, put an express revocation clause in the new one: it should name the earlier document, or say all prior powers of attorney are revoked. Then do the notice work below, because the bank still has the old document in its file.

This is the most common mistake we see. A client signs a fresh power of attorney naming a new agent, files it in the drawer, and never mentions the old one. Two years later the old agent, who still holds a signed original, walks into the old branch with it. The bank sees a valid document and no notice of revocation.

If you want two agents to coexist, say so in the document instead. Our page on [naming more than one agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-name-more-than-one-power-of-attorney-agent) explains how.

## How do you notify banks and the old agent?

Send written notice to every institution and person who might rely on the old document, and keep proof. This matters because a person who deals with the agent in good faith may rely on the power until they receive actual notice of revocation, termination or your death (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.6). Third parties may also rely on a photocopy or certified copy of the signed original (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.11). The document's life depends on who has a copy, not on who holds the original.

| Who to notify | What to send | Keep as proof |
|---|---|---|
| The old agent | Notarized revocation, request to return originals | Certified mail receipt or signed acknowledgment |
| Each bank and credit union | Copy of the revocation, your ID, the new agent's details if any | Dated copy, branch manager's name |
| Brokerage and retirement plan | Same, sent to the account's legal department | Confirmation letter |
| Doctor, hospital or insurer that has a copy | Short letter saying the financial power is revoked | Copy of letter |
| Your attorney, accountant, and any title company | Copy of the revocation | Email confirmation |
| County clerk, if the old power was recorded | Ask what they need for a revocation | Recording receipt |

Call the bank first. Ask who handles powers of attorney, because the branch often cannot do it. Banks usually want the revocation in writing, with your signature, and sometimes in person. Some will ask you to sign their own form. Do not skip it. Tell them to flag your accounts for the old agent's name, and ask in writing that they confirm they have removed the agent.

A realistic example. Ruth, 77, in Ocean County, revokes the 2019 power of attorney naming her son Eric. She signs a notarized revocation, mails it to Eric by certified mail, and takes copies to her bank and her brokerage. The bank confirms in writing three days later. The brokerage takes two weeks. Until it confirms, Ruth keeps a log and checks the account online each week.

## What if the old agent will not cooperate?

You can still revoke, because the agent's consent is not needed. What you cannot count on is a smooth handover. If the agent holds records, ask for them in writing. An agent has duties to keep accurate financial records, and the principal can require an accounting (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.13). If the agent refuses, or you suspect misuse, the Superior Court can order an accounting on application by an heir or next friend. Our article on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) covers warning signs, and [challenging or removing an agent when siblings disagree](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-challenge-or-remove-a-power-of-attorney-agent-when-siblings-disagree) covers the family fight.

If the agent has taken money, freeze the exposure first: notify banks, change online passwords and consider a credit freeze. Our guide to [freezing a parent's credit and putting safeguards on accounts](/learn/elder-care/how-to-freeze-a-parents-credit-and-put-safeguards-on-their-accounts) shows how.

## What if the principal can no longer revoke?

Then a family member cannot revoke on the principal's behalf. Revocation is something the principal does. If the principal has lost the ability to understand, the options are an accounting action, or a guardianship in which the court appoints someone who then deals with the agent. When a guardian or conservator is appointed over the principal's property, the agent answers to that person as well (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.4). The principal's power of attorney can also name whom the principal would want the court to choose as guardian.

For the court route, read [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey). It is slower and more expensive than a revocation, which is why acting while the principal still has capacity matters. A short checklist if you are worried about a parent:

1. Ask the doctor whether the parent can understand a simple document today.
2. If yes, see an attorney within days and revoke or replace.
3. If no, gather bank statements and talk to an attorney about a court accounting or guardianship.

## Do divorce and death end a power of attorney?

Death ends it. Divorce can end an agent's role if the agent was a spouse, depending on the document and state law. Our article on [how divorce affects a New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/how-divorce-affects-a-new-jersey-will-trust-power-of-attorney-and-beneficiary-forms) explains what to update, and [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) lists the other triggers. Do not rely on divorce alone: send the notice anyway.
