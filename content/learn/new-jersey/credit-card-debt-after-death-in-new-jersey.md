The first statement arrives a few weeks after the funeral. It is addressed to your father, shows a balance of $6,400 and has a number to call. Then a collector phones you, not the executor. The question every family asks is whether they now owe it.

For most families in New Jersey the answer is no, with some real exceptions. This page covers who is liable, what the estate pays and in what order, and what collectors can and cannot demand. It is part of our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). The claim process itself has its own page: [creditor claims and paying debts of a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate).

## Who is responsible for a dead person's credit card in New Jersey?

The estate first, and a few people personally. A credit card is a contract between the cardholder and the bank. When the cardholder dies, the balance becomes a claim against what the person left. Relatives who did not sign the agreement are not parties to it, and being an heir does not make you a debtor. Whoever is on the contract is a different matter.

| Person | Usually liable for the card balance? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The estate | Yes, up to its assets | The debt belongs to the cardholder |
| Surviving spouse who is not on the account | Generally no | Not a party to the contract |
| Co-signer or joint account holder | Yes, and possibly for all of it | Signed or applied for the same account |
| Authorized user | Generally no | Allowed to use the card but did not agree to repay |
| Adult child who is an heir | No | Inheriting does not create a debt |
| Executor acting for the estate | Not personally, if acting properly | Pays from estate funds, in the right order |

Two caveats. First, joint and co-signed cards are the main exception, and some people do not know they are on the account. Check the original application. Second, state rules on a spouse's responsibility for a husband's or wife's necessary medical care are a separate subject from card debt, so ask an attorney if the debt is hospital or nursing home bills. Our article on [whether a nursing home can make children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) addresses the children's side.

An executor is not liable for the card by being executor. The risk comes from mistakes, such as paying heirs before creditors or paying in the wrong order, which we cover below.

## What does the estate pay, and where do cards rank?

Last. New Jersey sets a statutory order (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-2) and credit cards sit in the final class, "all other claims," behind funeral expenses, the costs of administration, certain tax and government debts, last-illness medical expenses and judgments. Our creditor claims article has the full table. What it means in practice is simple: when the estate is thin, the card company usually collects little or nothing.

An example, with invented numbers. Maria, 71, dies with $38,000 in her estate: $26,000 from the sale of her condo and the rest in a bank account. Her bills are $9,500 for the funeral, $7,000 for lawyer and court costs, $11,000 for her last illness and $14,000 on two credit cards. The first three classes total $27,500. That leaves $10,500 for cards, which would share it in proportion. The card issuers collect about 75 cents on the dollar and the rest goes unpaid. Her daughter owes nothing.

If Maria's estate had been only $20,000, the cards would receive nothing, and her children would receive nothing either. That is the part families find hard: it is not that heirs pay the card, it is that the card comes out of what the heirs would have received. Our general article on [what happens when an estate owes more than it has](/learn/probate/what-happens-when-an-estate-owes-more-than-it-has) covers shortfalls.

## How and when does a card company collect from the estate?

By sending the executor a written claim signed under oath, within nine months of the date of death (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-4). The executor then has three months to allow, dispute or part-allow it, in writing (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-7). A creditor whose claim is disputed has three months to sue (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-8). A statement, a phone call or a collector's letter is not a sworn claim.

That gives an executor useful leverage. Ask for the sworn claim, and ask for the account history showing the balance, the charges and the last payment. Dispute anything that looks wrong: charges after the date of death, fees and interest that were added since, or a card the decedent never opened. A debt that was already past the statute of limitations when the person died generally cannot be paid, and the executor can waive that defense only when the estate is solvent and all successors agree (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-1).

## What can debt collectors demand from the family?

Less than they often suggest. Collectors are allowed to contact the people who handle the estate. The federal debt collection rule defines "consumer" for its communication limits to include the consumer's spouse and, if the consumer has died, the executor or administrator of the estate, and the commentary treats a personal representative as any person authorized to act for the estate (12 CFR 1006.6(a)). So a collector may speak with the surviving spouse or with you as executor.

What a collector may not do is harass. The rule bars conduct whose natural consequence is to harass, oppress or abuse any person, including repeated calls meant to annoy, threats of violence, obscene language, and calls that do not identify the caller (12 CFR 1006.14). The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau also says a collector cannot threaten you with arrest over an unpaid debt, and that you can tell a collector to stop contacting you, which does not erase the debt but ends the calls.

Practical rules for a family member who is not the executor:

1. Ask the caller who they are, who they represent and for the request in writing.
2. Say that you are not the executor, give the executor's name and stop there.
3. Do not confirm that you will pay, and do not give a bank account or card number.
4. Keep a log of dates, times and what was said.
5. If calls continue after you object, send a written request to stop contact.

The most frequent mistake we see is a relative who, trying to be kind, agrees to a "small payment to settle things." That payment can look like acceptance of the debt. ## What should the executor do about the cards?

Follow a sequence and write each step down.

1. Get certified death certificates and list every card from mail, credit reports and statements. See [New Jersey death certificates](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-death-certificates-who-can-order-and-how-many-you-need).
2. Notify each issuer in writing of the death and ask for a sworn claim. Cancel the cards and stop automatic charges.
3. Open the estate bank account with its own tax number so you are not paying from personal funds. See [the estate bank account, EIN and tax waivers](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-bank-account-ein-and-tax-waivers).
4. Log every claim, the date it came in, and your decision.
5. Pay funeral and administration costs first, hold a reserve for disputed claims, and do not distribute to heirs until the nine-month window is handled. Details are in [executor duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step).

If you are the heir and the executor is slow, see [what New Jersey heirs can do about a slow or silent executor](/learn/new-jersey/what-new-jersey-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-or-silent-executor).

## What about accounts that pass outside the estate?

Joint bank accounts, payable-on-death accounts and beneficiary-designated assets generally pass to the named person without going through the will. A joint account is treated under the Multiple-Party Deposit Account Act, which presumes the surviving owner takes the balance (N.J.S.A. 17:16I-5). Whether a card company can reach those assets is a separate legal question, and the answer depends on how the asset passes. Our article on [whether creditors can take what a beneficiary inherits](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits) explains the usual rules, and [joint bank accounts after a death in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/joint-bank-accounts-after-a-death-in-new-jersey) covers the account rules. Ask an attorney before assuming either way.
