Lena's father left her a traditional IRA with a balance in the low six figures. She lives in Montclair. The custodian has sent a form asking how she wants to take the money, and the accountant has asked one question she cannot answer: how much of each withdrawal does New Jersey tax?

New Jersey does not have a special inherited-account tax. It applies the ordinary retirement income rules, with some quirks that catch people who assume the state follows the federal return. This page uses the Division of Taxation's retirement income guide (GIT-1 and 2, dated January 2026, with amounts for tax year 2025) and ties it to the federal timing rules. For the federal 10-year rule, see [the inherited IRA 10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals). For the state's wider death taxes, see our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## How does New Jersey tax payments from an inherited IRA?

The same way it taxes the original owner. The Division says payments from a pension, annuity or IRA to a survivor or beneficiary follow the regular rules, and they are taxable to the extent they exceed the decedent's previously taxed contributions or the beneficiary's contribution.

The key New Jersey difference is that the state does not allow a deduction for IRA contributions. A federal deduction for an IRA contribution does not reduce New Jersey taxable income. So a traditional IRA built partly from after-tax-in-New-Jersey contributions has an excludable slice. The guide gives the formula: the taxable portion of the account divided by its total value, multiplied by the distribution. Interest, dividends and growth are taxable when withdrawn.

Here is an example with invented numbers. The IRA is worth $200,000, and $50,000 of it is contributions that New Jersey already taxed. So 25% of each withdrawal is excludable and 75% is taxable. Lena takes $40,000 in a year. She reports $30,000 as taxable and $10,000 as not taxable. The custodian's Form 1099-R shows the full $40,000, so the split has to be worked out on the state return, using the Division's Worksheet C. Keep the owner's records of contributions. Without them, you may end up reporting the whole withdrawal as taxable.

## What about a 401(k) or other employer plan?

A 401(k) is usually simpler and more heavily taxed. The Division says contributions made on or after January 1, 1984 were not included in New Jersey income, unless they were above the federal elective deferral limit, so distributions are fully taxable. Older contributions and any above the limit are handled with Worksheet C.

The same guide treats other plans differently.

| Plan | How New Jersey treats a distribution | Source note |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional IRA | Only the earnings and untaxed part; formula in Worksheet C | NJ allows no IRA deduction |
| Roth IRA, qualified | Not reported | Five-year period plus death, age 59 and a half, disability or first-home purchase |
| Roth IRA, nonqualified | Taxable and excludable parts | Taxable part on line 20a |
| 401(k) (post-1983 contributions) | Fully taxable | Direct rollover not taxed until withdrawn |
| 403(b) | Amounts over contributions are taxable | Contributions were taxed when made |
| 457 (government) | Reported on Form 1099-R, treated as a pension | Since 2002 |
| Pension, noncontributory | Fully taxable | You never paid tax on the plan funds |
| Pension, contributory | Contributions recovered by the Three-Year Rule or General Rule | Depends on whether you recover within 36 months |

If the beneficiary is a spouse who rolls the account into their own IRA, the rollover is not reported in New Jersey, and later withdrawals are taxed. [Spousal rollover vs inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira) explains the choice. A non-spouse moving money should do a direct transfer to an inherited IRA, never a check payable to the person.

## Does the pension exclusion help an heir?

Sometimes, and you should not assume it. New Jersey's pension exclusion is for a person who was 62 or older, or disabled under Social Security guidelines, on December 31 and whose total income for the year was $150,000 or less. The Division's examples show maximums of $75,000 for a single filer, $100,000 on a joint return, and a reduction for joint incomes between $100,001 and $150,000. There is also an exclusion that can apply to age-62 filers with little earned income.

The guide does not say how these tests apply to a younger heir receiving a parent's payments. A 55-year-old inheriting a parent's IRA is not 62 and likely does not qualify on age alone. A surviving spouse or partner who is 62 or older may. Have a preparer run the numbers before you build a plan on the exclusion.

## Do I owe New Jersey inheritance tax on the account too?

Possibly, depending on who you are. The state's inheritance tax is separate from income tax. Class A beneficiaries, which includes spouses, children and parents, owe nothing; Class C and Class D beneficiaries pay on what they receive, as explained in [the New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax). The statute lists exemptions for certain pensions, including a qualified plan benefit paid to a surviving spouse or domestic partner. It does not name IRAs or 401(k) plans, so a niece who inherits an IRA from an uncle should not assume it is exempt.

Where the benefit is subject to inheritance tax, the Division's guide treats the value used for inheritance tax as the beneficiary's contribution. That can reduce the income that is later taxed. If it is not subject to inheritance tax, the beneficiary excludes only the decedent's remaining previously taxed contributions. This interaction is easy to miss, so an attorney or CPA should confirm which rule applies.

## How do the federal timing rules fit in?

Federal law decides when the money must come out; New Jersey decides how much of what comes out is taxed. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty the account within 10 years. Spouses, a minor child of the owner, disabled or chronically ill heirs and heirs not more than 10 years younger have other options. Whether you owe yearly withdrawals in years one through nine depends on the owner's age and status at death.

That timing is a tax-planning lever. In a New Jersey household, a withdrawal that lands in the same year as a big bonus is taxed at a higher rate than the same withdrawal in a lean year. Lena could take the money evenly over ten years, or hold off in early years if the owner died before required distributions began. Federal income tax on the same withdrawal is covered in [do beneficiaries pay taxes on what they receive](/learn/beneficiary-designations/do-beneficiaries-pay-taxes-on-what-they-receive).

## What if the account passes to the estate?

If the estate is the beneficiary, payments go to the executor, and the account may have to be emptied faster than if a person had been named. The estate may then file a [New Jersey fiduciary income tax return](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-fiduciary-income-tax-return-for-an-estate-nj-1041) and pass the income to the heirs. Naming a person on the beneficiary form usually avoids that, which is why [the beneficiary designation matters more than the will](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

## What are the first steps for a beneficiary?

1. Get the owner's date of death and the account statements, including the basis records for any after-tax contributions.
2. Tell the custodian you are a beneficiary and ask for the inherited IRA paperwork. Do not take a check in your own name.
3. Decide whether you are a spouse, an eligible designated beneficiary, or a 10-year beneficiary.
4. Ask the plan for any Roth first-contribution year.
5. Plan withdrawals around your other income so you do not push yourself into a higher bracket in one year.
6. Keep Form 1099-R and your worksheet with your New Jersey return.
