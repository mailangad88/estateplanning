Someone has died, the funeral is planned, and now the question comes up that nobody wants to ask. Was there a will, and where is it? In New Jersey the answer matters early, because the Surrogate will not admit a will to probate until the tenth day after death, and an estate without a will follows a different path entirely.

This page is a search plan for the first two weeks: who to call, what the state's Will Registry does and does not do, how safe deposit boxes work, and what to do when all you find is a copy. For the steps after the will is found, see our [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) and the main [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Where should you look first?

Look where an original is most likely to be kept, in this order, and write down each answer as you go.

1. **The person named as executor.** People often tell one child or one friend where the will is. Ask before searching the house.
2. **The drafting attorney.** Many firms keep the signed original in a vault. Find the name in old invoices, email or a checkbook register.
3. **The home.** A fireproof box, a desk drawer, a file marked "insurance" or "taxes," a folder in a filing cabinet. Wills are often tucked in with a deed or a life insurance policy.
4. **The financial advisor, accountant or insurance agent.** They sometimes hold a copy, and a copy tells you which attorney to call.
5. **The bank.** Look for a small key, a box rental notice or an annual box fee on a statement.
6. **The state Will Registry.** Covered next.

Keep a log with the date, the person you asked and what they said. If the estate is later contested, that log shows you made a reasonable search.

## What is the New Jersey Will Registry?

It is a voluntary registry kept by the Office of the Secretary of State under N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2.1, which dates from 2005. A person who makes a will, or the person's attorney, can record the testator's name, the date of the will and enough information to locate the original. It does not store the will.

Two points help you set expectations. Registering costs $10 and so does a search, according to the Department of State's page. And registration is optional, so an empty result tells you only that nobody registered, not that no will exists.

Who can search? The Department of State says searches are limited to interested persons or their representatives, and that only executors or fiduciaries are given the location of the will. If you are a child, spouse or heir, you can ask, but be ready for the answer to go to the named fiduciary rather than to you. The search is made by mailing the state's request form with a check or money order to the Office of the Secretary of State, PO Box 300, Trenton, NJ 08625-0300. Confirm the current form number and fee on the state's site before mailing.

## Can you get into a safe deposit box to look for a will?

Usually only with paperwork. The box belongs to the account holder, and a bank will not hand a key or contents to a relative because the relative says there is a will inside. Banks commonly ask for a death certificate and proof of authority, such as letters issued by the Surrogate. That creates a loop: you need the will to get letters, and you may need letters to open the box. Banks have their own ways around it, so call the branch, say that you are looking for a will, and ask what they require. Practice differs from bank to bank, so treat the branch's answer as the rule.

Two habits help. Do not remove anything except the will without proper authority, and ask for the bank's inventory when the box is opened. Our article on [what happens when a family cannot find a will or open the box](/learn/what-if/family-cannot-find-your-will-or-open-the-safe-deposit-box) covers the practical side in more detail. If you are the one holding the key, the lesson for your own plan is in [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

## What if someone has the will and will not hand it over?

Ask in writing and say the will is needed for probate. If the person stalls, the Superior Court has a specific remedy. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:3-29, the court can order discovery about whether a will of a deceased county resident exists, or where it is, when it has not been offered for probate. It can also require that the document be filed with the county Surrogate for probate. The statute does not name who may apply or set a penalty, so this is a case for an attorney.

A common version is the sibling who lived with a parent and says there is no will, while another sibling remembers signing as a witness. The memory is worth writing down with the date, because a witness can later help prove a will even when the original cannot be located.

## What if you find only a copy?

A photocopy or a scan is a lead, not a will. New Jersey probate works from the original signed document, and the Surrogate's office will ask for it. When only a copy exists, the first question is whether the original still exists, was destroyed on purpose or was simply lost. A will that the maker tore up is revoked. A will that was misplaced is not, but someone has to prove what it said and that it was never revoked (see [changing or revoking a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-new-jersey)).

Consider an example. Rosa dies in Bergen County. Her son Marco finds a signed-looking photocopy of her will in a folder, naming him and his sister as equal heirs. The attorney named on the copy retired years ago. Marco calls the firm, which says it moved its files to a successor who has no original. The next steps are a search of the house, a Will Registry request and a call to the sister. If nothing turns up, Marco will need an attorney to ask the court to admit the copy, with testimony from the witnesses to the signing if they can be found. That is slower and costlier than a routine filing, and the court may not accept it. Nobody should promise a result before the search ends.

For the general rules on lost originals, see our article on [a lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will). For a will that was hidden or destroyed by a person, see [hidden, forged or destroyed wills](/learn/wills/hidden-forged-or-destroyed-wills-what-families-can-do).

## What should you do if nothing turns up?

Keep the search on the record, then decide how to proceed. If nobody can find a will after a good-faith effort, the estate is handled as an intestate one. The county Surrogate appoints an administrator, usually starting with the surviving spouse or partner, and the property goes to heirs by statute. Our guides on [administering an estate without a will](/learn/new-jersey/administering-a-new-jersey-estate-without-a-will) and [who inherits without a will](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) explain how that works.

If a will appears after an administrator is appointed, the administrator's good-faith earlier acts stay valid (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-6). The will then takes over and the executor named in it can seek letters. Tell the Surrogate and the administrator as soon as you learn of it.

## A checklist for the first ten days

- Ask the named executor and the closest family where the will is kept.
- Call the drafting attorney and ask whether an original is in the firm's vault.
- Search the home, desk, filing cabinet and any fireproof box.
- Look for a bank box key, rental notice or fee on a statement, and call that branch.
- Mail a Will Registry request if you are an interested person.
- Order certified death certificates (see [who can order and how many you need](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-death-certificates-who-can-order-and-how-many-you-need)).
- Do not distribute property or sign anything for the estate until you know whether a will exists.
