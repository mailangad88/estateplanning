Most of a New Jersey estate is paperwork that nobody sees. The last piece is the one that decides whether the executor sleeps afterwards: getting each beneficiary to sign a release before the checks go out. It is a short form, and skipping it is one of the few mistakes an executor cannot easily repair.

This article explains the form, the two kinds of accounting, and the court route when families cannot agree. It follows [executor duties in New Jersey, step by step](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step), and the bigger picture is in our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What is a refunding bond and release?

It is a signed agreement between the executor and one beneficiary. The Burlington County Surrogate's guide says it dispenses with a formal accounting, approves the executor's actions and the amount and manner of distribution, and releases the executor from further liability. The beneficiary also promises to refund a proportionate share of any legitimate debts, expenses or taxes found after distribution.

The statute behind the refund promise is N.J.S.A. 3B:23-24. As reproduced in the compiled code, it requires the personal representative to take a refunding bond when paying a devise or distributive share or delivering an instrument of distribution, and to file it with the surrogate of the county where the representative received letters. The Morris County form cites N.J.S.A. 3B:23-24 through 3B:23-27 and says the obligation passes to the beneficiary's heirs, executor or administrator if the beneficiary dies.

Think of it as a trade. The beneficiary gets the money now. The executor gets protection from a later complaint, and the estate gets a way to recover cash if a bill turns up.

## Who signs, and how?

Every heir or beneficiary signs, according to the Morris and Atlantic County Surrogates, including the executor if the executor is also inheriting. The signature is sworn before a notary or an attorney. If the beneficiary is a minor, the minor's guardian signs. If the beneficiary is an incapacitated person, the guardian of the person and property appointed by the Superior Court signs.

That last point surprises families. A cousin with dementia and no guardian cannot sign a valid release, and the estate may have to wait for a court. Our article on [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) explains how that appointment works.

The executor gives the form to each beneficiary and helps fill in the blanks. The dollar figure is the total value of estate assets that person receives: cash, bank accounts, stock, personal property and real estate. Assets that pass outside the estate, such as joint accounts or accounts with another named beneficiary, stay out of the number, say the Morris and Atlantic County Surrogates.

## When does the form get signed, and what does it cost?

Before any distribution. The Morris County Surrogate says it must be completed before a distribution is made and before the beneficiary receives a bequest, and that if it is not completed, an administrator may have to renew a surety bond, which adds expense.

| Item | What counties publish |
|---|---|
| Filing fee | $10 per form up to two pages (Burlington, Morris, Atlantic) |
| Extra pages | $5 for each page beyond two (Atlantic) |
| What you file | The original signed, notarized form for each beneficiary |
| File-stamped copy | Send an original and a copy with a stamped, self-addressed envelope (Morris) |
| Where | The Surrogate of the county that issued letters |

The Burlington County guide adds a point about bonds. Administrators usually post a surety bond, and the surety cannot be cancelled by the insurance agent without proof that the refunding bond and release was filed. Only the Surrogate can release the surety. So in an administration, the signed releases are what end the bond premium.

## What is the difference between informal and formal accounting?

An accounting is the executor's report of what came in, what went out and what is left. New Jersey recognizes two levels.

An informal accounting is a summary, which the Mercer County Surrogate says is how most accountings are done. Beneficiaries look it over, and if they are satisfied they sign the release. Burlington County says an informal accounting may be requested by next of kin or, where there is an executor, by beneficiaries named in the will, and that no request should be made until one year after probate.

A formal accounting is a Superior Court case. Rule 4:87-1(a) says actions to settle the accounts of executors and administrators are brought in the county where the fiduciary was appointed, by complaint in the Chancery Division and an order to show cause under R. 4:83. The order must state the commissions and any attorney's fee requested. Rule 4:87-3 sets the contents, and the Mercer County Surrogate notes a $175 filing fee. A formal accounting also puts the fiduciary's work before a judge, who can hear objections.

Courts can be asked to compel an account. Under R. 4:87-1(b), an interested person may start an action to compel a fiduciary to settle an account, and in suitable cases to require an inventory and appraisal. N.J.S.A. 3B:17-2, as the Mercer County page cites it, lets the court require an account, but generally not before a year has passed unless special cause is shown.

## What if someone will not sign?

Most refusals come from a lack of information, not a fight. A sibling who has not seen a number hesitates to sign a paper saying she received it. Send the informal accounting first, with copies of statements and the sale closing sheet for the house. Answer questions in writing. If a beneficiary still refuses, the executor's choice is to wait or to file a formal accounting and ask the court to approve the account and discharge the executor.

Commissions come up here, because the court looks at them in a formal accounting. The schedule and the court's power to adjust them are in [executor commissions and the probate timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline). For problems on the other side, see [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed). A caveat or contest usually changes the whole picture; that is covered in [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats).

## A short example

Priya is executor for her mother. The estate is a house that sold for cash and two bank accounts, and her brother Dev and sister Anjali are the other two beneficiaries. In month ten she sends each sibling a one-page informal accounting, the closing statement from the sale and the bank statements. Each signs the refunding bond and release before a notary, listing the sum received, and Priya files all three, hers included, with the Surrogate. A utility bill nobody knew about arrives in month fourteen for a few hundred dollars. Because the releases are in place, Priya can ask each sibling for a third of it, and none of them can say she paid out too soon. Without the releases, she would have been the one answering.

## A closing checklist

1. Confirm the tax waivers or tax clearance are in hand and all debts are paid or reserved.
2. Prepare an informal accounting that lists assets, income, expenses and proposed distributions.
3. Send it to each beneficiary with a refunding bond and release.
4. Have each signature notarized, with guardians signing for minors and incapacitated beneficiaries.
5. File the originals with the Surrogate and pay the fee.
6. Distribute. Keep the file stamped copies.
7. If a surety bond exists, ask the Surrogate to release it.
