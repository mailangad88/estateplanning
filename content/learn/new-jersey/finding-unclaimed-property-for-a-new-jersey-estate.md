Most estates hold a few small things nobody knew about: a final paycheck that was never cashed, a utility deposit, a dormant brokerage account, an old insurance refund. When a business cannot find the owner, it eventually reports that money to the state. In New Jersey the holder is the Unclaimed Property Administration, or UPA, a unit of the Treasury.

This article explains how an executor, administrator or heir searches for that money and claims it. It covers the documents the UPA asks for, where a Surrogate's paperwork fits in and how to avoid paying a stranger for something that is free. It is one piece of the broader [executor duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step), and the state overview is in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Who can claim a dead person's unclaimed property?

Only someone with a legal right. The UPA's FAQ says a person can file on property that is not in their own name only in limited cases, such as an estate executor, a holder of a power of attorney or a holder of a letter of guardianship. A power of attorney ends at death, so for a dead owner that means the executor or administrator. The UPA lists the claimant types as heir, owner and legal representative.

Its claim page defines the roles this way.

- An **executor** is the person the Surrogate confirms under a will that nominated them.
- An **administrator** is the person the Surrogate appoints when there was no will.
- **Next of kin** are the blood relatives who inherit when there is no will.
- A **surviving spouse** is the spouse listed as "Surviving Spouse" on the death certificate.

The page applies only to people who lived in New Jersey at death. If your parent lived elsewhere, contact the UPA about the case or look to that state's rules.

## How do you search for the property?

Start online, and search for every name and address the person ever used. The UPA says filing through its search website is the fastest way to get a claim processed. Check the person's maiden name, a married name, a business name and each address they used, because the record lists the "original owner's address as reported."

If the search finds nothing but you still believe the state holds something, use the "Request a Property Search" link. The UPA asks you to allow up to 2 weeks for a response. If no property is located, you receive an email saying so.

A few cautions on timing:

- Property reported late can take time to show up. The FAQ says items reported after the October 31 reporting deadline can take up to 3 months to appear in the search system.
- A letter from a bank or insurer saying your relative's money will be turned over by a certain date is not the state's date. The FAQ warns that dates in due diligence letters from financial institutions are not the state's escheat dates.
- Searching for a person usually comes after you have found the ordinary accounts. Our article on [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) gives the full hunt.

## What documents does the UPA ask for?

The claim form lists the proof for the specific property, and the UPA's claim documentation page lists the common items. For a deceased owner you will usually need these.

| Document | What the UPA says |
|---|---|
| Probate document | From the county Surrogate and dated within one year of the present date |
| Your photo ID | A clear photocopy showing a photo and signature |
| Your Social Security proof | A copy of a document that shows the executor's or administrator's number |
| The deceased owner's Social Security number or FEIN | A copy of a document showing the full number, unless the death certificate already shows it |
| Proof of reported address | A document showing the person lived or did business at the address on the record |
| Death certificate | A photocopy is enough |

The UPA's instructions add a rule that surprises people: do not send documents unless the UPA asks for them through formal correspondence. You submit the claim form, then upload documents through the "Upload Claim Documentation" portal on the UPA website. You can check status with your claim ID.

Two details matter more than they look. The probate document, usually the Surrogate's certificate naming the executor, must be recent. If the estate has been open for two years, order a fresh one. And if the claim involves multiple deceased owners, the UPA wants a death certificate for each, plus a Surrogate's certificate dated within the previous 12 months for the owner who died last. See the [probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) for how to get letters in the first place.

## What if there is no will and the estate is small?

A next of kin letter may stand in for administration. The UPA describes the case this way. The estate has no will, no surviving spouse and a value of $10,000 or less. One next of kin, with written consent from all other next of kin of equal degree, may then file an Affidavit of Next of Kin with the Surrogate instead of opening an administration. That is the UPA's description of its own paperwork. The Surrogate's small estate affidavits have their own limits, which are explained in [the New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit). Check the Surrogate in the county where the person lived. The UPA's claim page lists all 21 counties' contact numbers.

Consider Dana, whose father Walter died with no will, no spouse and a checking account of about $3,000. A search turns up an uncashed $620 insurance refund in the state's records. Dana is Walter's only child. She gets the Affidavit of Next of Kin from the Surrogate, attaches it to the claim and uploads her ID and Walter's Social Security proof. The same route would not work if Walter had a sibling with an equal claim who did not sign.

## What other situations change the paperwork?

The UPA's documentation page covers several cases that come up in estates.

- **Several owners:** every owner must sign the claimant information pages, and ID and Social Security proof are required for each owner.
- **Stock or bonds:** originals are required for stock certificates and bearer bonds. An original statement must show the number of shares.
- **A trust:** the trustee must show current trusteeship under a properly certified trust agreement, plus the owner's Social Security document.
- **An assignment of interest:** this requires the transfer paperwork and a statement about unclaimed funds turned over to New Jersey.
- **A divorce:** a copy of the decree is required so the property is divided according to its terms.
- **A turnover order:** it must state the amount and direct the UPA to pay the creditor.

If an uncashed check is part of the claim, the UPA says a replacement check can be requested only after at least 30 days from the original issue date, and only if the original went to the correct address.

## Should you hire someone to find the property?

Usually not. The UPA states that it never charges a fee to search and claim, and its FAQ says heir finders can charge up to 20% of the property's value. It does not say whether you should use one. The reasoning is simple: the same search and the same forms are open to you at no cost. A finder may help if the property is in another state, or the family cannot be located.

If someone contacts you offering to recover the estate's money for a fee, ask for the claim ID and check it yourself on the UPA's site.

## How long does the state keep the money?

Indefinitely. The FAQ says unclaimed property remains in the Unclaimed Property Trust Fund in perpetuity, and the UPA states that it has returned over $3.0 billion to rightful owners since it began. Interest accrues from the date the property was escheated until the date a return is approved. That does not change the practical rule: the executor's time is limited by the estate, not by the state. File the claim before you close the estate so the money can go through the accounting and be distributed.

If an heir cannot be found, [what to do when an heir cannot be found](/learn/what-if/heir-cannot-be-found-or-no-family-survives) explains the search, and [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) covers the more usual account.

## What should the executor do next?

1. Search the UPA site under every name, business name and address the person used.
2. Request a manual search if the first search turns up nothing.
3. Order a fresh certificate from the Surrogate if yours is more than a year old.
4. Gather ID, Social Security proof and a death certificate copy.
5. File the claim, upload the documents the UPA asks for and note the claim ID.
6. Add anything recovered to the estate inventory before the accounting.
