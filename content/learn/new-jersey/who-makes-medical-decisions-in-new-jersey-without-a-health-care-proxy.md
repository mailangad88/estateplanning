The phone rings at two in the morning. Your father is in the intensive care unit after a stroke, he cannot speak, and a doctor needs someone to say yes or no to a procedure. There is no health care proxy anywhere. Who decides?

In New Jersey the honest answer is that the hospital turns to the family and tries to reach agreement. That works well when one person is obviously in charge and everyone agrees. It works badly when three children live in three states, or a second wife and an adult son want different things. This page is part of our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). It covers the no-document case, what happens when the family disagrees, and when a court has to step in.

## Who decides if there is no proxy in New Jersey?

The people closest to the patient, as far as the care team can tell. New Jersey's Advance Directives for Health Care Act (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-53 and following) is built around the choices you put in writing: a proxy directive naming a health care representative, an instruction directive stating your wishes, or both. We cover them in [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives). When there is no document, the statutes cited on this page do not hand the hospital a ranked list of relatives to follow.

What actually happens is a custom. A spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner is usually asked first, then adult children, then parents and siblings. Doctors prefer one voice. They will ask who the family wants to speak for the patient, and they will try to document that. Hospitals also have their own consent policies, which vary, so ask the nurse manager or social worker what theirs says.

Hospitals are expected to ask about advance directives and to help people complete one. A New Jersey institution must have a way of finding out whether a patient has a directive, must give patients and families information about directives, and must set up procedures to resolve disputes (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-65). The second part matters here. If the family is stuck, the hospital has a process before anyone files in court.

## What happens when the family disagrees?

Treatment decisions slow down, and the hospital brings in its ethics process. Disagreement is the main way a no-proxy case becomes a crisis. Consider Leon, 79, a widower from Bergen County with three adult children. One daughter, a nurse, wants comfort care after a severe stroke. His son wants every intervention. The third daughter lives in Oregon and wants to wait. Leon never signed anything.

The care team keeps treating while it looks for common ground. The hospital's ethics committee or a palliative care team meets with the family. They ask one question over and over: what would Leon say? They look for evidence, such as things he told his doctor, a church bulletin note, a conversation after a friend's funeral. If agreement still does not come, the family may need a guardian, which is the next section.

Four things help in the meantime.

1. Ask the hospital for one family meeting with the doctor, not several separate calls.
2. Pick one relative as the point person, even if only for communication.
3. Write down what the patient actually said, with dates, and share it.
4. Search the house, the safe deposit box and the doctor's office for a directive the patient forgot.

## When is guardianship needed?

When there is no one who can lawfully decide and the family cannot agree or resolve it, or when the patient needs long-term decisions a hospital cannot make by consensus. In New Jersey, a family asks the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part, to find an adult incapacitated and appoint a guardian (N.J.S.A. 3B:12-25). The complaint needs certifications from two physicians, or a physician and a psychologist. The court appoints a lawyer for the adult and holds a hearing (R. 4:86). Our article on [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) lays out the steps, including limited guardianship when the person can still decide some things.

Guardianship is slow and public compared with a proxy, and it costs money. It also gives the court a role in decisions the patient would have wanted private. Families sometimes assume that a hospital will call a judge in an afternoon. That is rare. For a stable patient it usually takes weeks.

Here is how the paths compare.

| Situation | Who decides | How long to resolve |
|---|---|---|
| Signed proxy directive | The named health care representative | Immediately |
| No document, one clear family member, no objection | That family member, by hospital practice | Immediately |
| No document, adult children disagree | Ethics process, then maybe a guardian | Days to weeks, then court time |
| No document, no relatives | Hospital seeks a court-appointed guardian | Weeks or longer |

## What can a patient or family do right now?

If the patient can still understand and communicate, even a little, sign a directive today. Two adult witnesses or a notary make it valid, and the representative cannot be a witness (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-56). A hospital social worker can usually help find forms and witnesses. Our attorneys can attend if the situation is difficult.

If the patient cannot sign, the options are narrower.

- **Look for older documents.** A health care proxy from a surgery years ago, a will with healthcare wishes, or a form held by a primary doctor can still help.
- **Ask about POLST.** A POLST is a medical order, not a directive. If the patient lacks capacity, a representative may sign it following the patient's known wishes or best interests, along with a clinician (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-129 and following). Our article on [the New Jersey POLST form](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-polst-form-who-qualifies-who-signs-and-how-it-works) explains who qualifies.
- **Ask about a HIPAA release.** The patient may have signed a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) that lets named relatives see records, even if there is no proxy.
- **Start the guardianship paperwork early** if the patient will not recover soon. Waiting for a better day costs time.

## What if the patient has no family at all?

Then the hospital, a social worker and sometimes a county adult protective services office look for anyone who knows the patient. Friends, a clergy member or a longtime neighbor can speak about wishes, though they do not have legal power on their own. Guardianship may be the only path. See our article on [being incapacitated with no one to name](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-with-no-family-or-no-one-to-name-as-agent) for planning in that situation.

## Does a divorce or separation change who decides?

Yes, if a spouse was named. A spouse or civil union partner named as health care representative loses that role on divorce, legal separation or dissolution unless the directive says otherwise, and a domestic partner's designation ends with the partnership (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-57). If you are separated but not divorced and there is no document, expect the hospital to ask the spouse anyway, and expect adult children to push back. Our article on [how divorce affects a New Jersey will, trust and power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/how-divorce-affects-a-new-jersey-will-trust-power-of-attorney-and-beneficiary-forms) shows what else to update.

## What should you do so your family never faces this?

Sign a proxy directive, name two backups, and give copies to the people involved and your doctor. Talk through the hard cases, such as ventilators and feeding tubes, out loud with your representative. A sign-in-a-lawyer's-office document is not required: the state says you can complete one yourself. What matters is that it exists and people know where it is. A typical checklist:

1. Choose a representative and two alternates, and ask each if they will serve.
2. Complete the proxy directive, and the instruction directive if you want one.
3. Sign before two adult witnesses or a notary.
4. Add a HIPAA authorization so named relatives can talk to your doctors.
5. Give copies to your doctor, your representative and your family.

Pair it with a financial [power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney), because the person who handles your bills may not be the person who handles your care.
