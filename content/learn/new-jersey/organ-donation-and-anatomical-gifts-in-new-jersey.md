Priya, 34, ticked the donor box at the license office years ago. Her husband has no idea. If she were hurt tomorrow, a transplant coordinator would ask him a question he has never thought about, and he would be answering it while a doctor explained that she was not going to wake up. In New Jersey her registration probably settles the matter, but only if the hospital can find it and her husband believes it.

This page covers how organ and tissue donation works under New Jersey law, how it fits with your will and advance directive, and who decides when you did not register. It sits within our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). For the general version of how donor choices and directives interact, see [organ donation and your advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/organ-donation-and-your-advance-directive).

## How do you become an organ donor in New Jersey?

You can make a gift in any of several ways. New Jersey's Revised Uniform Anatomical Gift Act, N.J.S.A. 26:6-77 and following (enacted in 2008), recognizes these (N.J.S.A. 26:6-81):

| Method | How it works | Catch |
|---|---|---|
| Driver's license or ID | A statement or symbol on the card shows the gift | If the card later expires or is cancelled, the gift is not invalidated |
| Donor registry | You authorize the gift to be listed on a registry | The state's donor program uses the National Donate Life Registry |
| Donor card or signed record | You sign it | Someone has to find it |
| Will | The gift takes effect at death whether or not the will is probated | The will is usually read days later |
| Statement during terminal illness | You tell at least two adults, one of whom is disinterested | Only fits a bedside situation |

The New Jersey Sharing Network's registration page asks for your name, email, address and the last four digits of your Social Security number, and describes the registration as a binding legal document of gift covering all eligible organs and tissue. It also says you can update or remove it and set more detailed preferences at any time through RegisterMe.org.

If you cannot sign because of a disability, another person may sign at your direction, but two adults, at least one of them disinterested, must witness the record and it must state how it was signed.

## Why is a will the wrong place for this decision?

A will can make a gift, and the law says it takes effect at death even if the will is never probated and even if the will is later found invalid (N.J.S.A. 26:6-81). The trouble is practical, not legal. Organs have to be recovered within hours, and a will is typically found, read and taken to the Surrogate after that window has closed. Our article on [how a New Jersey will goes through probate](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) shows the timeline.

Put the choice in the registry and on your license, and use the will for the things only a will can do. Mention it in your directive and tell your family.

## How does a gift fit with your advance directive?

New Jersey law addresses the collision directly. If a patient who is a prospective donor has an advance directive or other record limiting life support that conflicts with making a gift, the rules in N.J.S.A. 26:6-93 apply:

1. A patient with decision-making capacity decides whether to withhold or withdraw treatment, after talking with the attending physician about donation.
2. If the patient lacks capacity, the agent named in the advance directive resolves the conflict. If there is no agent or none is available, another person authorized by law to make health care decisions does so.
3. Everyone works to resolve the conflict quickly and gets information from the procurement organization.
4. Measures needed to keep the part medically suitable are not withheld or withdrawn before the conflict is resolved, unless appropriate end-of-life care requires it.

A directive that says "no machines, ever" can stall the process, even though your agent has the legal power to resolve it. The cleanest fix is to say in the directive what you want. A sentence such as "I want to donate, and I accept short-term ventilator support if needed for that purpose" removes the doubt. Our page on [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives) covers how a directive is signed and changed, and a [POLST](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-polst-form-who-qualifies-who-signs-and-how-it-works) completed with a clinician should reflect your donor choice too.

The statute also lets your agent make a gift while you are alive, unless your directive or another record prohibits it (N.J.S.A. 26:6-80). If you object to your agent making that choice, say so in writing.

## Who decides if you did not register?

If you made no gift and did not refuse, the law lets certain people decide after your death. They act in this order, and a person lower on the list may not act if someone higher is reasonably available (N.J.S.A. 26:6-85):

1. Your agent at the time of death, if the agent could have made the gift.
2. Your spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner.
3. An adult child.
4. Either parent.
5. An adult sibling.
6. Another adult related to you by blood, marriage or adoption, or who showed special care and concern for you.
7. Your guardian of the person at death.
8. Anyone else with authority to dispose of your body, including a hospital administrator if you were a patient.

The gift can be made by a signed document or by an oral statement that is recorded or written down and signed by the person who took it. If several people in one class can act and one objects, the majority of those reasonably available decides. A person higher on the list can amend or revoke the gift before the recovery team knows of the revocation and begins.

That list is different from the one for [funeral and burial decisions](/learn/new-jersey/who-controls-funeral-and-burial-decisions-in-new-jersey), and it is not the same as the order of heirs in [who inherits without a will](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey). Donation authority can sit with someone who has no say over the funeral, which is another reason to name your wishes.

## Can your family override your registration?

Generally not. After you make a gift, others may not make, change or cancel it unless you clearly indicated otherwise (N.J.S.A. 26:6-84). Revoking a gift is not treated as a refusal, and a gift of one organ is not a refusal of the others. The exception is a minor: a parent who is reasonably available may revoke or amend an unemancipated minor's gift after the minor's death.

The medical examiner may be involved if your death falls under that office. The statute requires the examiner to cooperate with the procurement organization and, on request, release your name, contact details and available medical history (N.J.S.A. 26:6-94), but it does not stop the examiner from investigating the death.

## What to do this month

1. Check that your registration is current at the state's registry page and that your license reflects it.
2. Add one sentence about donation and short-term support to your directive.
3. Tell your agent, your alternate and your spouse or partner what you chose, because they will be asked.
4. If you do not want to donate, say that in writing as well, since silence hands the decision to the list above.
5. Write funeral wishes separately. Our article on [planning your own funeral in advance](/learn/basics/plan-your-own-funeral-in-advance) explains how.

Our article on a [healthcare agent's authority after death](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-agent-authority-after-death) explains why donation is one of the few decisions that crosses that line.
