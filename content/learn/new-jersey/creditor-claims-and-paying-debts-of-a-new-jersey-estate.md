A few weeks after a funeral, the mail starts to carry bills addressed to someone who has died. Some are real. Some are wrong. A few are scams. The executor, who may never have run a business or a household budget this large, has to sort them and decide what to pay.

New Jersey has a clear procedure for this in chapter 22 of Title 3B. This article walks through it: how a creditor claims, what the executor must do and by when, how disputes end, and who is paid first if the money is short. The nine-month window is mentioned in our [executor commissions and timeline article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline). Here we cover everything that happens inside it. For the broader state picture, see the [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## How does a creditor make a claim?

By sending the personal representative a written claim, signed under oath, that states the amount and the particulars, within nine months of the date of death (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-4). In New Jersey the personal representative is the executor named in the will or the administrator the Surrogate appoints. The claim goes to that person, not to the court.

The nine months are the creditor's problem, but they are also the executor's protection. If a claim is not presented in time, the executor is not liable to that creditor for anything already paid out or distributed before the claim arrived. That is why careful executors do not rush distributions.

Three small points save trouble:

- Keep a log. Date every claim when it arrives, note whether it is sworn, and record what you decided and when.
- Do not ignore a bill because it looks wrong. Do not let it sit, either, because the three-month decision period described below runs from the day you receive it.
- A claim for a debt that was already barred by the statute of limitations when the person died generally cannot be paid. The executor may waive that defense only if the estate is solvent and all the successors agree (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-1).

## What does the executor do with a claim?

Decide within three months of receiving it. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:22-7 the executor may allow the claim, dispute it, or allow part and dispute part, and must tell the creditor, or the creditor's agent or lawyer, in writing which portions are which. Allowed claims are paid from the estate, up to the assets available.

If the executor disputes a claim, the creditor has three months from notice to sue (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-8). A creditor who does not sue in that time cannot hold the executor liable for amounts already paid out under the estate. While the dispute is live, the executor must hold back enough to cover the claim, plus interest and costs, until the creditor gets a judgment or gives up (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-11).

Here is the sequence in short form.

1. Creditor sends a sworn, written claim within nine months of death.
2. Executor logs it and investigates. Is the debt real, owed by the decedent, and unpaid?
3. Within three months, the executor accepts, rejects or splits it, in writing.
4. If rejected, the creditor has three months to file suit.
5. The executor holds a reserve while any suit is possible or pending.
6. The estate pays what is owed in the statutory order.

## What about claims that arrive late?

They can still be presented while there is something left to pay. If a creditor misses the nine-month deadline, the statute allows a late claim in proper form to be presented at any time before the remaining assets are distributed, so long as the estate has more than enough to pay the claims filed on time (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-10). A creditor who shows up in month eleven may be paid from what is still in the account. A creditor who shows up after the checks have gone to the heirs generally cannot reach the executor for money already distributed.

A debt that is not due yet can also be presented. A liquidated claim payable in the future, such as an installment note, can be submitted for allowance, with a reasonable discount if it carries no interest (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-5).

Consider Marcus, executor for his mother. She owed a dentist $1,800 and a department store card about $2,300. The store's sworn claim arrives in month four. The dentist sends only an invoice in month five. Marcus pays the card after checking the balance, then writes the dentist and asks for a sworn claim. If Marcus pays the invoice in good faith before the sworn claim arrives, the statute lets the court allow the payment later, provided he can show the debt was owed (N.J.S.A. 3B:22-6). It is easier to ask for the sworn claim.

## In what order are debts paid?

When the estate cannot pay everything, N.J.S.A. 3B:22-2 sets the order. Claims in the same class share equally, and no claim moves up because the creditor sued.

| Rank | Class of claim |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reasonable funeral expenses |
| 2 | Costs and expenses of administration |
| 3 | Debts for services of the Office of the Public Guardian for Elderly Adults |
| 4 | Debts and taxes with preference under federal or New Jersey law |
| 5 | Reasonable medical and hospital expenses of the last illness |
| 6 | Judgments against the decedent, ranked by date entered |
| 7 | All other claims, such as credit cards and personal loans |

The practical lesson is the one at the bottom. Credit cards and personal loans come last, so a person with a modest estate and large card debt usually leaves the card companies short. That is not the family's failing. It is how the order works.

Two items sit in the fourth class, and they deserve their own attention. Tax debts to the government have priority rules that an executor should confirm before paying anyone below them. And if the decedent received Medicaid long-term care, the state may file a claim of its own, which our article on [New Jersey estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery) explains. An executor who hands out the checking account before a known Medicaid claim is resolved can be called to account.

## What if the estate cannot pay everything?

Then stop, count and pay in order. A personal representative who pays a lower class while a higher class is unpaid takes the risk of paying twice. Our general article on [what happens when an estate owes more than it has](/learn/probate/what-happens-when-an-estate-owes-more-than-it-has) walks through insolvent estates, and the overview of [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) covers the non-New Jersey basics. If the numbers look tight in week one, speak to an attorney before you pay anything beyond the funeral.

## What the executor should not do

Do not pay the family's own bills from the estate account, and do not pay yourself back for expenses without a receipt. Do not distribute to heirs until the reserve is set, as discussed in [executor duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step). And do not guess at inheritance tax, because the Division of Taxation has its own timetable, described in the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article.
