A grandparent wants to leave $30,000 to a six-year-old. A minor cannot manage that money, and a bank will not hand it to a first-grader. The state has a simple tool for this, the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, and New Jersey has its own version in Title 46, chapter 38A.

This page explains how the New Jersey act works: who owns the property, who runs it, when it ends, and how to name a custodian in a will so the money does not land in a court-supervised account. We compare it with a trust near the end. The common ground is in our article on [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children), and the [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey) links our other state material.

## What is a UTMA custodianship?

It is a way to give property to a minor and have an adult manage it, without a trust document or a court. The act's short title, in N.J.S.A. 46:38A-1, is the New Jersey Uniform Transfers to Minors Act. Property is registered or delivered in the name of a custodian, with wording identifying the child and the act.

Three features define it. The transfer is irrevocable, and the property is vested in the minor, so the giver cannot take it back (46:38A-24). The custodian has the powers and duties the act gives, and neither the child nor the child's legal representative can direct the property except as the act allows. And a single transfer can benefit only one minor with one custodian (46:38A-22). All property one custodian holds for the same child under the act is treated as one custodianship.

The act can hold money, securities, insurance and annuities, real property recorded in the custodian's name, titled property such as vehicles, and other property transferred to an adult or trust company by a written instrument (46:38A-19).

## What age does a New Jersey custodianship end?

The act defines a minor as a person under 21, and an adult as a person who is 21 or older (46:38A-2). That is a New Jersey feature. Many people expect 18.

Under 46:38A-52, the custodian must hand over the property when the minor reaches 21, dies, or reaches the age of majority, depending on how the property arrived:

| How the property was transferred | When it ends |
|---|---|
| A gift (46:38A-8) | At 21, or at an earlier age after 18 if the giver set one |
| A transfer authorized by a will or trust (46:38A-9) | At 21, or at an earlier age after 18 if the will or trust sets one |
| A transfer a personal representative or trustee makes without authority in the will or trust (46:38A-12), or a guardian's transfer (46:38A-13) | At majority under other New Jersey law |
| Any of them | At the child's death |

An age set in the instrument can only be earlier than 21, and it must be after 18. If you want a child to wait until 25, the act will not do that. You need a trust.

## How do you name a custodian in a will?

N.J.S.A. 46:38A-6 lets a person who can choose who receives property that becomes transferable on a future event nominate a custodian for a minor. The permitted instruments are a will, a trust, a deed, an instrument exercising a power of appointment, or a written beneficiary designation for contract rights. Contract designations must be registered with or delivered to the payor or issuer.

The nomination must name the custodian and identify the custodianship, using the wording "as custodian for" the minor's name under the New Jersey Uniform Transfers to Minors Act. You can name one or more backups, to serve in order if the first dies, cannot serve, will not serve or is ineligible. A nominee must be someone who qualifies under 46:38A-19: an adult other than you, or a trust company.

Then the other statutory pieces apply. When a will authorizes the transfer, the personal representative can transfer to the nominated custodian (46:38A-9 and 46:38A-10). If you named no one, or all of your nominees are dead or unable, the executor, or the trustee under a trust, picks a qualified custodian (46:38A-11). And a nominee who does not want the job can decline by written notice to the nominator, the transferor or the transferor's legal representative (46:38A-42).

A sample clause would read something like: "I give $20,000 to my granddaughter Mia Torres, to be held by my son Ben Torres as custodian for Mia Torres under the New Jersey Uniform Transfers to Minors Act. If Ben cannot or will not serve, I name my daughter Nina Torres." Have your attorney confirm the wording and the gift. The same drafting problem shows up in [what happens when a minor inherits with no trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust).

## What does the custodian have to do?

The custodian is a fiduciary with a set of duties. In plain terms:

1. Take control of the property and register or record title where appropriate (46:38A-26).
2. Manage it with the care of a prudent person dealing with someone else's property, using any special skill the custodian has or claimed to have (46:38A-27).
3. Keep it separate from the custodian's own property and titled to show it belongs to the child, as "custodian for [name] under the New Jersey Uniform Transfers to Minors Act" (46:38A-29).
4. Keep a complete account of every transaction, with what is needed for the child's tax returns, open to a parent, the child's legal representative, and the child once 14, at reasonable intervals (46:38A-30).
5. Hand over everything at the end date, with an accounting if a court requires one.

A custodian who receives property from the giver may keep it without liability for doing so (46:38A-27). That helps in the common case of a grandparent giving stock that the family wants to hold.

The custodian may spend custodial property for the child when the custodian thinks it appropriate, with no court approval, even if someone owes the child support or the child has other money (46:38A-32). A parent should take care: using a child's account for expenses the parent owes by law is allowed by the act, but it blurs a line and invites questions when the child turns 21. The custodian may be repaid reasonable expenses (46:38A-35). A custodian other than a giver who served under 46:38A-8 may take reasonable annual compensation (46:38A-36). A custodian does not normally post a bond unless the court orders one on removal (46:38A-37).

## When does a court get involved?

Mostly when something goes wrong, or when a fiduciary acts without the will's permission. A personal representative, trustee or guardian who makes a transfer under 46:38A-12 or 46:38A-13 must judge it to be in the minor's best interest and must not conflict with the documents or law, and if the transfer is worth more than $10,000 a court must authorize it (46:38A-14). That limit is in the section on transfers made without the will's authority, which is one more reason to put the clause in the will.

## UTMA or a trust?

A UTMA is fast and cheap. A trust costs more to set up and gives you control. Compare them:

| Question | UTMA custodianship | Trust |
|---|---|---|
| Age the child gets the money | 21, or an earlier age after 18 | Any age you choose, or in stages |
| Who owns it | The child, from the start | The trust, until you say otherwise |
| Can the giver change it | No | Yes, if revocable at the start |
| Setup | An account or a will clause | A trust document |
| Good for | Modest gifts and mature families | Large amounts, special needs, or an uncertain heir |

Think about a $400,000 life insurance payout to a nineteen-year-old with no direction. If a UTMA is the plan, the child receives everything at 21, no matter what is happening in the child's life. A trust gives the family a way to stage the money. The article on [naming a minor as a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary) explains when a trust is the better answer.
