Someone in your family has died, a will has turned up, and it does not look like the person you knew. Maybe it left the house to the one child who handled the checkbook for the last two years. Maybe it was signed three weeks after a hospital stay. You want to know whether you can do anything, how fast you have to move, and what a court will actually want to see.

New Jersey handles this through two different tools at two different moments. A caveat works before probate. A complaint in the Superior Court works after it. This page explains both, the deadline that catches families off guard, and the standard for undue influence. It sits alongside the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) and our national guide to [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will). Everything here is general information, pending attorney review, and a real dispute needs a lawyer who can see the documents.

## What is a caveat in New Jersey?

A caveat is a short written objection filed with the county Surrogate. It tells the office that the filer wants notice and a chance to be heard before the will is admitted or an executor or administrator is appointed. It does not argue the case. It holds the door open.

The Morris County Surrogate publishes a caveat form with a $25 filing fee, and the form has the filer state that he or she has standing as a relative of the person who died. A matching form lets the filer withdraw the caveat, also for $25, in a signed and notarized statement. After withdrawal the form says probate or administration may go forward in the Surrogate's Court or in the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part, depending on the estate. Other counties have their own versions, and I could not confirm from official sources how long a caveat stays in effect, so ask the Surrogate where you file.

The Rules of Court back the pause. Under R. 4:82, the Surrogate's Court may not act in a matter where a caveat was filed before the court's judgment, unless the Superior Court authorizes it and only as that order directs.

## When does a caveat make sense?

A caveat makes sense when you have a concrete reason to think a will is about to be offered that should not be, and you are not yet an appointed fiduciary. Typical reasons:

- A parent with dementia signed a new will shortly before death, and an older will treated the children equally.
- A caregiver, new spouse or neighbor was added as the main beneficiary.
- You know of a later or earlier will that nobody has produced.

It is not a free move. A caveat can freeze an estate, and an executor who cannot be appointed cannot pay the mortgage or insure a vacant house. Beneficiaries who file one just to pressure the family can end up on the wrong side of a judge's patience. Talk to a lawyer first and file only if you would be ready to follow it with a real challenge.

## What is the deadline to challenge a New Jersey will?

Generally four months from the date the will was probated. R. 4:85-1 lets a person aggrieved by a probate action file a complaint in the Superior Court. In a 2019 appellate case, In the Matter of Miranda (A-1117-18), the challenger did not file within four months after the will was probated and then asked the court to relax the time limit. The court affirmed the trial judge's ruling against that request, and the challenger's argument under R. 4:50-1(f) failed because exceptional circumstances were not shown. That opinion is marked not for publication, so it binds only the parties. It is a fair picture of how seriously the court treats the clock.

The rule has additional provisions, including for people who lived outside New Jersey and for certain fraud claims. I did not confirm that wording from the court's own rule book, so read the current text of R. 4:85-1 on the New Jersey Courts site before relying on it.

| Step | What happens | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Caveat filed | Appointment of executor is held up | County Surrogate |
| Will offered for probate | Surrogate or court admits the will | Surrogate or Superior Court |
| Four-month window runs | Aggrieved person may file a complaint | Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part |
| Hearing | Court decides capacity, execution or influence claims | Superior Court |

The four months run from probate, not from the date you found out. If you lived with the person for years and heard nothing about a probate filing, you may still be out of time. This is why a caveat, filed early, matters: it can help you learn when probate is about to happen. Executors must also mail a notice of probate to beneficiaries and next of kin within 60 days of probate (R. 4:80-6), which is another reason to watch the mail.

## What is the undue influence standard in New Jersey?

Undue influence is mental, moral or physical pressure of a kind and quality that overrides the will-maker's own wishes (In re Estate of Stockdale, 196 N.J. 275, 302-03 (2008), quoting Haynes v. First National State Bank, 87 N.J. 163, 176 (1981)). Two points carry most of the weight.

First, it must be present when the will is signed. A son who pushed his mother hard last year does not taint a will she signed freely this year. Second, the burden is normally on the contestant. Courts have said it "must be clearly established" (Matter of Will of Liebl, 260 N.J. Super. 519, 527 (App. Div. 1992), quoting Gellert v. Livingston, 5 N.J. 65, 71 (1950)). Gellert also recognizes a presumption that the person who made the will was of sound mind.

The presumption that shifts the burden is the part contestants build their case around. If the will benefits someone who stood in a confidential relationship with the testator, and there are additional suspicious circumstances, the burden moves to that person to explain (In re Rittenhouse's Will, 19 N.J. 376, 378-79 (1955), as applied in Stockdale). An appellate court describing Stockdale said the proponent must then overcome the presumption by a preponderance of the evidence.

### What counts as a confidential relationship?

One where the person who made the will, because of weakness or dependence, put trust in a particular beneficiary (In re Hopper, 9 N.J. 280, 282 (1952)). The circumstances must show the parties were not dealing on equal terms (In re Stroming's Will, 12 N.J. Super. 217, 224 (App. Div. 1951)). A daughter who manages her father's bank accounts and medical appointments fits. A daughter who calls on Sundays does not. The court has said that "the mere existence of family ties" does not create one (Vezzetti v. Shields, 22 N.J. Super. 397, 405 (App. Div. 1952)).

### What counts as suspicious circumstances?

Circumstances that "require explanation" (Haynes). A drastic change in who receives the estate is the classic example. The bar is low: in Stockdale the circumstances "need only be slight," although the contestant must still present some evidence of them.

Consider Dolores, 84, who left her house equally to her three children for fifteen years. After a stroke she moves in with her youngest son Tom, who drives her to the lawyer, sits in the room and hears her new will leave him everything. Tom's role and the sudden change are the facts a challenger would put in front of the judge. Tom would then need to show Dolores knew what she was doing and chose it herself, with records such as the drafting lawyer's notes and independent witnesses.

## What else can a challenge allege?

Undue influence is only one theory. A contestant can also claim the will was not signed and witnessed as the statute requires, which our article on [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) covers, or that the maker lacked the mental capacity to make a will. [Fraud, forgery and a later revocation](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) are other grounds. A signed self-proving affidavit makes the execution theory harder, so the claims that survive are usually about capacity or influence.

Check for a [no-contest clause](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) before you start. Whether and how it is enforced in New Jersey is a question to ask your lawyer, because I have not confirmed it from a New Jersey source.

## How do you protect your own will from a challenge?

You cannot make a will contest-proof, but you can make one hard to knock down.

1. Sign with two witnesses and add the self-proving affidavit.
2. If you are treating children unequally, write a short, signed explanation and tell the lawyer why.
3. Meet the lawyer alone, not with the person who benefits.
4. Keep a record of your health at signing, ideally a note from your doctor if capacity could be questioned.
5. Keep your [plan up to date](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) so that changes are explained, not sudden.

A [living trust](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) can be challenged on the same grounds, so it is not a magic shield, but it avoids the Surrogate's file and may keep the details more private.
