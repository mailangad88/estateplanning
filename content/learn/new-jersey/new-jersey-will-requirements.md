Most people assume a will is valid because they typed it, signed it and put it in a drawer. In New Jersey the signature is only half of it. Two other people have to sign too, and what you do at the signing table can decide whether your family spends a month or a year in front of a court.

This article walks through what the statute requires, what the court may forgive, and how to make the will easy to prove. It is part of our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What does a New Jersey will need to be valid?

A New Jersey will must be written, signed and witnessed. The maker must be at least 18 and of sound mind (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-1). The execution statute then asks for three things (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2):

1. The will is in writing.
2. It is signed by the maker, or in the maker's name by another person who signs in the maker's conscious presence.
3. At least two individuals sign it as witnesses, each within a reasonable time after seeing the signing or the maker's acknowledgment.

Nothing in that list mentions a notary, a lawyer, a particular paper or a filing. Those are common practice, not legal requirements.

The "conscious presence" language matters for people who cannot sign. If Walter has a stroke and cannot hold a pen, another person can sign his name, but only while Walter is awake to it and directing it. The witnesses then sign as usual.

## Who should be a witness?

Pick two adults who gain nothing under the will and who will be easy to find years from now. A neighbor, a bank teller or a colleague from the law office works. A child, a spouse or a named friend does not.

The execution section does not list who is disqualified, and we are not going to guess how a New Jersey court treats a witness who also receives a gift. The safe habit is simple: never ask someone who is receiving a gift to sign as a witness. If Dolores names her daughter Priya as executor and sole heir, Priya should not be one of the two signatures.

Witnesses should also be people who could testify if asked. If they have moved overseas or died by the time of probate, the Surrogate needs another way to prove the signing. That is the problem the next section solves.

## What is a self-proving affidavit?

A self-proving affidavit is a sworn statement that lets the Surrogate admit your will without hearing from the witnesses. The statute says a will can be executed, attested and made self-proved at the same time by the maker's acknowledgment and the witnesses' affidavits, all made before an officer authorized to take acknowledgments and proofs (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-4). In practice that officer is a notary.

If the will is not self-proving, one of the witnesses has to appear at the Surrogate's office or otherwise prove their signature. The Morris County Surrogate's guide says that when a will is self-proving, probate can move quickly. Otherwise the clock runs on tracking down a witness.

You do not have to decide at the signing. A will that complied with N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2 can be made self-proved later, by the same acknowledgment and affidavits (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-5). The cost of waiting is that you must find both witnesses again.

Our general article on [self-proving affidavits](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) explains the idea outside New Jersey.

| Step | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Maker signs in front of both witnesses | Matches the "signed by the maker" step in N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2 |
| Both witnesses sign right then | Keeps them within a "reasonable time" |
| Maker and witnesses sign the self-proving affidavit before a notary | Lets the Surrogate admit the will without a witness |
| Original goes somewhere safe, copies to the executor | The Surrogate generally wants the original |

## Can a handwritten will be valid in New Jersey?

Yes, in some cases. A will that does not meet the witnessing rules is valid as a writing intended as a will if the signature and the material portions are in the maker's handwriting, whether or not it was witnessed (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2). This is what lawyers call a holographic will.

"Material portions" is the phrase to notice. A form with a few handwritten blanks is not the same as a letter written entirely in the maker's own hand saying who gets what. Ravi writes out his wishes on a legal pad, dates it and signs the bottom. That has a fair chance. A printed template with his name written in and a signature has less.

Even when a handwritten will is good, the road to probate may be longer. The Morris County Surrogate's guide states that a holographic will can be admitted only in Superior Court, which means a court filing in addition to the Surrogate's office. Fees, delay and the chance of an objection all go up. If you want your family to avoid that, [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) lists the tradeoffs, and a two-witness signing is almost always cheaper in the end.

## What if the document was never properly signed?

A court may still treat it as a will. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:3-3, a document that was not signed and witnessed as the execution section requires is treated as if it had been, but only if the person offering it proves something specific by clear and convincing evidence. They must show the maker intended it to be one of the following:

- a will,
- a full or partial revocation of a will,
- an addition to or alteration of a will, or
- a full or partial revival of a will that was revoked.

That is a high standard. It is more than "probably." In a reported Appellate Division case, In re Estate of Ehrlich, 427 N.J. Super. 64 (2012), an unsigned, unwitnessed typed "Last Will and Testament" was accepted under this section. A bill introduced in the 2024 session would add a requirement that the document be signed by the maker or substantially in the maker's handwriting, with the stated aim of overturning that result for future cases. We have not confirmed whether it became law, so this is one more reason not to rely on the safety net.

The practical lesson: an unsigned draft sitting on a laptop is a fight waiting to happen. Heirs who would take less under it will dispute it. Heirs who would take more will defend it. Our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains how those disputes run.

## How does the court admit a New Jersey will to probate?

The Surrogate's court of the county, or the Superior Court, can admit a will to probate and issue letters (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-17). A resident's will can be probated in either place (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-24). A will executed in compliance with the signing statute can be probated on the proof of one attesting witness or another person with knowledge of the execution (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-19). The details of that process are in our [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) article.

Before anyone gets that far, the will has to be found. Tell your executor where the original is. Our article on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) covers the options, and [lost original wills](/learn/wills/lost-original-will) explains why a copy is a poor substitute.

## What mistakes cause problems?

A few come up again and again:

- **Signing with only one witness.** The statute asks for two.
- **Witnesses who sign weeks later.** The signature has to come within a reasonable time of seeing the signing.
- **Changing a signed will by hand.** Crossing out a name or writing a new gift in the margin is a risky way to change a will. Use a [codicil or a new will](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils).
- **Using an out-of-state form without checking it.** A form built for another state may skip steps New Jersey expects.
- **Forgetting that a will does not control everything.** Retirement accounts and life insurance follow their beneficiary forms, and jointly owned property passes by title. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).

If you die without a valid will, the intestacy statutes decide who inherits. Our article on [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) covers that order.

## What should you do at the signing?

Treat the signing as a short ceremony. Gather the maker, two witnesses and a notary in one room. Have the maker say, aloud, that this is their will. The maker signs. Each witness watches and signs. Then everyone signs the self-proving affidavit before the notary. Keep the original in a fireproof place and give your executor a copy and the location.

If your will was signed years ago, check it against this list now. Wills made in another state may still work, but they are worth a second look after you move. [Moving states with a will](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) explains why.
