Every executor reaches the same fork. The debts are paid, the house is sold, the checks are ready, and one question stands between the executor and a clean finish: how do I prove I did this right? New Jersey gives two answers, and they cost very different amounts of time and money.

Our article on [refunding bonds and releases](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-refunding-bonds-and-releases-when-an-estate-closes) explains the signed form itself. This page steps back to the decision: when to use releases, when to go to the Superior Court, and what the court route actually involves. The wider process is in the [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What are the two ways to close a New Jersey estate?

| | Releases from beneficiaries | Formal court accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | N.J.S.A. 3B:17-1 | N.J.S.A. 3B:17-2; R. 4:87 |
| Who must agree | Every beneficiary, individually | A judge decides; beneficiaries may object |
| Where it happens | Filed with the county Surrogate | Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part |
| Typical use | Family agrees, estate is straightforward | Dispute, unsigned or incapacitated beneficiary, or desire for court approval |
| Executor's protection | Releases from those who signed | A court's approval of the account |
| Cost | Surrogate form fees, a notary | Filing fee plus attorney time and a hearing |

A fiduciary can skip rendering or settling an account if the fiduciary files a release or discharge from a beneficiary who has reached the age of majority and is not incapacitated. The statute, 3B:17-1, says the release must be "executed and acknowledged as provided for deeds of real estate to be recorded." Notary or attorney, same as a deed.

The other road is a court case. Rule 4:87-1(a) says actions to settle the accounts of executors and administrators are brought in the county where the fiduciary was appointed, by complaint in the Chancery Division and an order to show cause. The order must state the commissions and any attorney's fee requested.

## When do releases fit?

Releases work when everyone is an adult, everyone is competent, and nobody is angry. Take Carol, who is executor for her mother's estate. The estate has a house, two bank accounts and a car. Carol and her two brothers all talk weekly. Carol prepares a summary of what came in and what went out, mails it to both brothers, and gets signed releases within a month. Total court cost is the filing fees for the forms. Total drama is none.

Releases also fit when a beneficiary is a charity or an institution with a standard process, as long as someone with authority signs. Our [executor duties guide](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step) shows where releases sit in the closing steps.

Now change one fact. Suppose Carol's youngest brother, Eli, is 17 when their mother's estate is ready to close, because their mother had him late. Eli cannot sign a release as an adult, and a guardian would have to sign for him in the refunding-bond setting. Carol can wait for that appointment, or she can ask the court to approve the account with Eli's interest protected. Which is cheaper depends on how soon Eli turns 18 and whether a guardian is already in place. That is the kind of detail that decides the route, not the size of the estate.

## When does a court accounting fit?

Four situations push executors toward court.

**A beneficiary will not sign.** Maybe he suspects the house sold too cheaply. Maybe he is just stubborn. Either way, the executor cannot rely on his release and cannot fully distribute with confidence.

**A beneficiary cannot sign.** A minor, or an adult who is incapacitated, cannot give a valid release under the statute's wording. The guardian signs for them in the refunding-bond setting, which means waiting on a court appointment. If none is coming, a judge's approval of the account may be the faster route.

**The executor expects a challenge.** If a sibling has already contested the will or accused the executor of favoritism, signed releases are unlikely and a judge's order is worth more than a friendly note. See our piece on [will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats).

**The executor wants certainty.** Some executors, especially professionals or those handling larger estates with complicated assets, prefer a judge's approval over a stack of individual releases.

## What does a formal accounting involve?

The sequence below follows the rule's structure. Details vary by county and by the judge, so an attorney should confirm local practice.

1. **Compile the records.** Statements, receipts, the sale closing documents, tax payments, commission and legal fee figures.
2. **Prepare the account.** It reports what the estate had, what came in, what went out, and what remains, in the form the court rules require (R. 4:87-3 sets contents).
3. **File a verified complaint and order to show cause** in the Superior Court of the county where the executor was appointed, with the filing fee. The Mercer County Surrogate lists $175 for this filing; confirm your county's current amount.
4. **Serve the beneficiaries.** The order sets a return date and gives them notice and a chance to object.
5. **Hear objections, if any.** Without objection, matters often proceed quickly. With objection, the judge may hold a hearing, and may require backup for specific payments.
6. **Obtain the court's order** approving the account and settling the executor's commissions and fees, then distribute.

The Surrogate's own recommendation is to use an attorney, because the matter may lead to further court proceedings.

## Can a beneficiary force an accounting?

Yes, within limits. Under R. 4:87-1(b), an interested person can start an action to compel a fiduciary to settle an account, and in suitable cases to require an inventory and appraisal. Section 3B:17-2 provides that a personal representative generally cannot be required to account until a year after appointment, unless special cause is shown.

A year is not a long wait for an estate with real estate, but it is a useful boundary. A beneficiary who is worried at month three should ask for information first. Burlington County's Surrogate says an informal accounting may be requested by next of kin or, where there is an executor, by beneficiaries named in the will, and that a request should not be made before one year after probate. ## What mistakes cause trouble at the end?

- **Distributing before releases are in.** Once the money is gone, the leverage is gone too.
- **Treating a vague email as an accounting.** A sibling who says "fine, looks good" in a text has not signed a release.
- **Skipping the guardian problem.** A minor beneficiary will need a legally authorized signer.
- **Mixing personal and estate money.** Even careful executors get dragged into court by commingled accounts. See [executor commissions and the timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline).
- **Starting court when a conversation would do.** A formal accounting for a family that would have signed is money lost.
