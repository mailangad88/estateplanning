The funeral usually happens within days of a death. The will may not even be found until after it. That timing is why New Jersey has a specific statute on who gets to decide whether you are buried or cremated, where, and with what service. If you have never named anyone, the law picks for you, and the person it picks may not be the person you would have chosen.

This page covers the New Jersey rule: how to appoint someone, the order of priority when you do not, and where families run into trouble. It sits in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey) because the answer lives in the state's funeral practice law, not in the probate code.

## The short version

- You can choose the person who controls your funeral and disposition. Do it in writing.
- If you do not, the statute ranks your relatives. Your spouse or partner is first.
- The person in control decides burial or cremation and the arrangements. Who pays is a different question.
- The rule is N.J.S.A. 45:27-22, amended by P.L. 2019, c.187, approved July 19, 2019.

## How do you appoint someone to control your funeral?

You have two options under N.J.S.A. 45:27-22, and either one works.

The first is your will. A will can name a person to control the funeral and disposition of your remains. That person does not need to be your executor. The statute also allows them to act before the will is probated, which matters because most wills are not opened until after the funeral.

The second is a written appointment on a form approved by the board that regulates funeral directors. Under the 2019 amendment, the form is signed by you and by at least two witnesses, and it must be notarized. The person you name can also be your executor if you want one person to do both jobs.

If you have signed more than one appointment, the most recently dated valid will or board-approved form controls. That is worth remembering for people who wrote a funeral wish into a will twenty years ago and then signed a form last year naming someone else.

Two limits apply. An officer, partner, owner or employee of a funeral home, cemetery or crematory that is providing services for you cannot be the appointee, unless that person is a relative. And for an active-duty service member, the statute gives control to the person named on the Department of Defense emergency data record, DD Form 93 or its successor.

An appointee can also step aside. The statute lets an appointed person, or anyone in a priority class, relinquish control, and the right then passes down to the next class.

## Who decides if you never named anyone?

If there is no appointment, the statute sets an order. A court can direct something else, but absent that, the order is:

| Rank | Who controls |
|---|---|
| 1 | Surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner |
| 2 | A majority of the surviving adult children |
| 3 | The surviving parent or parents |
| 4 | A majority of the brothers and sisters |
| 5 | Other next of kin, by closeness of relationship |
| 6 | If no relatives are known to be living, written authorization from another person acting for the decedent, which a cemetery may rely on |

Three details change how this works in practice.

**The 72-hour clock.** The highest class has 72 hours from the death to act. If it does not act, or cannot be reached after a reasonable attempt, the right passes to the next class. It also passes if a physician finds a person medically incapable or incapacitated.

**Shared rank means a vote.** When several people hold the same rank, a majority must authorize the arrangements. If both parents hold the right and both are alive and reachable, both must agree. If only one can be reached, that one is enough.

**Some people drop out.** A restraining order against a person in a higher class, or a charge that the person intentionally killed the decedent, moves the right to the next class. A divorce, annulment or dissolution ends a former spouse's or partner's control, unless that person qualifies in some other capacity, such as an "other interested party".

## Why this matters more for some families

Consider Dolores, 71, widowed, with three adult children. Two live nearby and want a traditional burial next to her husband. The third lives in Oregon and has said for years that she would rather be cremated. Without anything in writing, the three children vote and the majority wins. Dolores might have felt strongly about this. Nobody knows, because she never put her wishes in a form that carries weight.

Now consider Marcus, 54, who has lived with his partner Yusuf for fifteen years. They never registered as civil union or domestic partners. Marcus's mother is alive. Under the statutory ranking, with no appointment, Marcus's mother outranks Yusuf, since an unregistered partner has no place on the list. If Marcus's mother disapproves of the relationship, Yusuf may be pushed out of a service for a person he lived with for fifteen years. A short signed form would have prevented it. Our article on [domestic and civil union partners](/learn/new-jersey/domestic-partners-and-civil-union-partners-in-new-jersey-estate-law) explains what registration changes.

Blended families have the same problem. A second spouse ranks first, even when the children from the first marriage expect to decide. If that is a concern, an appointment is the way to settle it while everyone is calm.

## How to put an appointment in place

1. Decide who you trust to carry out your wishes, and ask them. Choose a backup in case the first person cannot act.
2. Get the board-approved form from the state or ask an attorney to prepare the right document. Do not rely on an unapproved template.
3. Sign it in front of at least two witnesses and have it notarized.
4. Give copies to the appointee, the backup and your health care representative. Keep the original where it can be found fast. A safe deposit box is a poor choice, since nobody can open it on a Sunday.
5. Put matching wishes in your will, and re-date and re-sign the form whenever you change your mind, since the most recent one wins.

Your funeral wishes also fit alongside your [health care directive](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives), though the directive deals with treatment while you are alive. The funeral appointment takes effect at death. For the general rule that a will cannot control everything, read [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).

## Does control of the funeral mean control of the money?

No. The person who decides on the arrangements is not automatically the person who owns the estate. If you sign the funeral home contract, you are the person the funeral home can collect from, even if you had no say in the estate. The executor can repay reasonable funeral costs from the estate once appointed, and our article on [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral) covers how that works.

For that reason, many people pair the appointment with a prepaid plan or a small account that the executor can reach quickly. An appointee with no money and no access to the estate can end up financing a service out of pocket for weeks.

## Where the statute stops

The statute decides who has authority. It does not tell a funeral director what is lawful in every case, and it does not govern the estate, the will, or who inherits. A person appointed to control disposition is not the executor unless the will also names them. Estate matters still run through the county Surrogate, as the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) explains.

If your family situation is complicated, such as an estranged parent, a stepfamily or a partner who is not registered, an attorney can help you draft the appointment and confirm that your other documents agree with it.
