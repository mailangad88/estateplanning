Most beneficiaries do not ask for much. They want to know the trust exists, who is in charge of it, what it owns, and when money will arrive. A trustee who answers those questions early rarely sees a lawsuit. One who goes quiet is the usual start of one.

New Jersey's rules are in the Uniform Trust Code, N.J.S.A. 3B:31, enacted by L. 2015, c. 276. This page explains what that code requires a trustee to tell beneficiaries, what you can ask for, and what the code does if a trustee falls short. For general background on trusts here, see our article on [living trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) and the [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What does a New Jersey trustee have to tell beneficiaries?

Section 3B:31-67 is titled "Duty to disclose and discretion to periodically report." Its first rule is that a trustee must keep the qualified beneficiaries reasonably informed about the administration of the trust and about the material facts they need to protect their interests. Its second is that, unless the circumstances make it unreasonable, the trustee must promptly respond to a beneficiary's request for information.

Notice what the words leave out. There is no calendar. The code does not say "every year" or "within 60 days." It says reasonable and prompt, and a judge decides what that means in a given case. A trust with one apartment building and two siblings calls for different updates than a trust holding a stock portfolio for a grandchild.

In practice, "reasonably informed" covers what you would want to know once a trust becomes irrevocable. Think of these four things.


- That the trust exists, who the trustee is and how to reach them.
- What the trust owns and roughly what it is worth.
- What has come in, what has gone out, and what the trustee has been paid.
- When and how distributions will happen, and any decision that affects them.

Our article on the [trustee duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) walks through that list in a general way.

## Who counts as a qualified beneficiary?

The duty runs to qualified beneficiaries, defined in N.J.S.A. 3B:31-3. You are one if, on the date it is measured, you are a distributee or permissible distributee of trust income or principal. You are also one if you would be a distributee or permissible distributee if the interest of the people in the first group ended on that date, or if the trust ended on that date.

That rule sweeps in two groups. The first is the present beneficiary, say a surviving spouse who receives income. The second is the person next in line, say the children who take what remains. A grandchild named as a fifth-in-line fallback usually is not.

Someone who asks the trustee in writing for notice is also entitled to receive notices the code requires for qualified beneficiaries (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-10). That small step is worth taking if you are a contingent beneficiary and want to be kept in the loop.

## What can you ask for?

The code names one thing outright: on a beneficiary's request, the trustee must promptly provide a copy of the trust instrument (3B:31-67(b)). Beyond that, the right is to information material to protecting your interests.

| What to request | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Copy of the trust instrument and amendments | Shows your interest, the trustee's powers and any restrictions |
| Statement of assets and values | Lets you see whether property is being preserved |
| Receipts and disbursements | Shows where money went and what fees were taken |
| Trustee compensation and its source | Lets you test the charge against the trust and the law |
| Plan and timing for distributions | Tells you when to expect money and whether delay is reasonable |

The code also limits how far a trust can go in cutting this off. As summarized from the 2015 act's text, the duty to respond to a request for a copy of the trust instrument or related information cannot be overridden by the trust terms for a qualified beneficiary of an irrevocable trust who is at least 35 years old. For other beneficiaries, a trust that tries to restrict information is a matter for a lawyer to read closely.

## What happens when a trustee sends a report?

Section 3B:31-67(c) lets a trustee who wants the protection of N.J.S.A. 3B:31-74 give beneficiaries a report. It may cover trust property, liabilities, receipts and disbursements, the source and amount of the trustee's compensation, a list of assets, and their market values where feasible.

Here is why trustees send them. Under 3B:31-74, a beneficiary may not sue for breach of trust more than six months after being sent a report that adequately disclosed the potential claim and stated the time allowed for suing. A report is adequate if it gives enough information that the beneficiary knew, or should have inquired about, the claim. When a beneficiary is represented by another person, the report counts as sent when the representative receives it.

That is a short clock, and many beneficiaries miss it because the report arrives as a spreadsheet and a cover letter. Treat every report as a deadline in disguise.

Without an adequate report, the default outer limit is five years after the earliest of three events: the trustee's removal, resignation or death; the end of your interest; or the end of the trust. The five years does not begin to run until you have reached majority and know both that the trust exists and that you are a beneficiary. Fraud or misrepresentation related to a report is not barred.

## What do you do when a trustee will not answer?

Work through it in order. Each step also builds a record a judge will want to see.

1. **Ask in writing.** Email and certified mail, citing your status as a beneficiary. Ask for specific items from the table above, not "everything."
2. **Set a reasonable date.** Thirty days is a fair request for a trust with ordinary assets. Say you will treat silence as a refusal.
3. **Read the instrument.** The trust may require reports, set up an accounting schedule or name an independent person to review the trustee.
4. **Send a short follow-up.** One reminder, calm and dated.
5. **Consult an attorney** before the six-month report deadline or any distribution-proposal deadline passes.
6. **Ask the court.** A proceeding is the last step, not the first.

Our article on [when a trustee does nothing](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-trustee-does-nothing) covers the slow-trustee version of this in more depth.

## What can a New Jersey court do about a trustee who falls short?

Section 3B:31-71 treats a violation of a duty owed to a beneficiary as a breach of trust and lists the court's options. A court may compel the trustee to perform or enjoin a breach. It may require the trustee to make up a loss by paying money or restoring property, and it may order an accounting. It may appoint a special fiduciary to take over the trust property, suspend or remove the trustee, or reduce or deny compensation. It may also void an act, impose a lien or constructive trust, trace and recover wrongly disposed property, or order other relief.

Removal has its own section. N.J.S.A. 3B:31-51 lets the settlor, a co-trustee or a beneficiary ask the court to remove a trustee, and lets the court act on its own. While the request is pending, or instead of removal, the court can order protective relief. Our guide to [how to remove a trustee](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-remove-a-trustee) covers that process in general terms.

When a trust ends, a trustee has a separate duty to distribute. Under 3B:31-70, the trustee must proceed expeditiously, but may keep a reasonable reserve for debts, expenses and taxes. If a proposal for distribution arrives, answer it within the 30 days it states.

## A short example

Priya and her brother Anand are the remainder beneficiaries of their father's trust, with their stepmother Joan as trustee and income beneficiary. A year after the death, neither has seen a statement. Priya sends Joan a letter asking for the trust instrument, a list of assets, and a summary of income and expenses. Joan sends a copy of the instrument and a one-page asset list. Priya asks again about expenses and gets silence for 60 days.

Priya is a qualified beneficiary, so she has the right to a copy of the instrument and to a prompt answer on material facts. The one-page list may or may not be "reasonably informed," and that is the question a judge would weigh. She and Anand now have a dated record, which is exactly what an attorney would ask for first.
