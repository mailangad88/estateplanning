Nobody can find a will. The house is full of paperwork, the bank wants someone with legal authority, and the family is not sure who that is. In New Jersey the answer is a person appointed by the county Surrogate and called the administrator.

This article explains who qualifies, what happens if the first person in line says no, why a bond is required and what the job looks like once the appointment is made. Who actually inherits is a separate question, answered in [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey). For the full state overview, see the [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Who can be the administrator?

The statute sets an order. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:10-2, letters of administration go first to the surviving spouse or domestic partner, if that person will accept. If there is no such person, or the person declines, they go to one or more of the remaining heirs who will accept. If no heir will serve, any other person willing to accept may be appointed.

There is also a time rule. If the decedent left no heirs "justly entitled" to administration, or the heirs do not claim it within 40 days of death, the Superior Court or the Surrogate's court may appoint any fit person who applies. A creditor, a friend or an attorney can end up in the role when a family does not move.

| Priority | Who | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner | If willing to serve |
| 2 | One or more of the other heirs | Often an adult child |
| 3 | Any other person willing to accept | When no one above will serve |
| 4 | Any fit person who applies | If no heirs, or none claim within 40 days of death |

Partners stand where spouses do, which our article on [domestic partners and civil union partners](/learn/new-jersey/domestic-partners-and-civil-union-partners-in-new-jersey-estate-law) explains.

## What if the person with priority does not want to serve?

They can decline, and the decision should be in writing. A spouse who is ill, grieving or simply not suited to paperwork can step aside so a daughter or son can apply. The Surrogate's office controls the form, and counties differ, so call and ask what it wants rather than drafting your own. The statute ranks the people who may serve. It does not spell out the wording of a declination, so treat the county's instructions as the rule.

Declining the job is not giving up your inheritance. A widow who steps aside as administrator still takes her share under the intestacy rules. Refusing the property itself is a different act, a disclaimer, covered in [disclaiming an inheritance in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-new-jersey).

Say Priya's father dies without a will. His wife, Anita, is 81 and does not drive. She has first priority but would rather not manage an estate. She signs the county's written declination, and Priya, an heir, applies. Priya serves, and Anita keeps her share.

## Where and how does the application go?

To the Surrogate of the county where the person lived, or to the Superior Court (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-1 and 3B:10-13). The court has exclusive authority over granting letters. The steps run in a familiar order.

1. Gather a certified death certificate and a list of every heir with address and relationship.
2. Search for a will. A will found now changes everything, as the [probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) article explains, because an executor would replace the need for an administrator.
3. Check the small estate route. If the estate is within the limits in our [small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) article, an affidavit may replace a full appointment.
4. Complete the county's application and have the other heirs sign whatever consents or waivers the county asks for.
5. Arrange the bond (below) and take the oath.
6. Receive letters of administration and order certified copies for the banks. Our general piece on [letters testamentary and letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) lists who asks for them.

Time matters because interested people can ask to be heard. A caveat filed with the Surrogate before an appointment can pause it, a point we describe in [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats).

## Why does an administrator need a bond?

Because the court, not a will, picked the person. New Jersey requires the court or Surrogate to demand a bond when it appoints a fiduciary because no one was named, and for any form of administration (N.J.S.A. 3B:15-1). The bond protects heirs and creditors if the administrator misuses the money. The court sets the amount and conditions, based on the value of the estate and the scope of the administrator's authority.

One exception is worth knowing. When the entire estate passes to a surviving spouse, the statute does not require a bond. The statute also lets a court require a bond later from a fiduciary whose handling of property looks unsafe, on the request of an interested person such as a creditor (N.J.S.A. 3B:15-4).

The bond is not a deposit. It is an insurance-type promise from a surety company, which charges a premium. Ask the Surrogate which sureties it accepts.

## What can the administrator do, and what if a will appears?

The administrator collects the assets, pays debts through the process in our [creditor claims article](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate), deals with the inheritance tax, and distributes according to the intestacy chart. The steps match the [executor's path](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step), with one difference: the administrator follows the statute rather than the decedent's instructions.

If a will turns up after the appointment, the administrator's good-faith acts before learning of it stay valid, and good-faith buyers are protected (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-6). The administrator still answers to the executor for property left unadministered or mishandled. The first general letters carry exclusive authority until changed (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-22), so a mistaken second grant to someone else can be undone by the first person.

For urgent needs before regular letters can issue, the Superior Court can appoint a limited administrator, such as a temporary one (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-12). Ask about it if the delay is costing money.

## What if the heirs disagree about who should serve?

It happens more than families admit. Three adult children all rank the same, and two of them do not trust the third. The statute says "one or more" of the remaining heirs may be appointed, so a family can agree on two co-administrators, but then each added fiduciary means more paperwork and a joint signature on every check. A calmer fix is to let one sibling apply with the other heirs' written consent and a promise to share records.

If the family cannot agree, ask the Surrogate how the county handles competing applications before anyone files in anger. A contested appointment moves toward the Superior Court and costs far more than the disagreement is usually worth. Whoever serves owes the same duty to every heir, including the ones who objected, and keeping a clean ledger from the first week is the best answer to suspicion.
