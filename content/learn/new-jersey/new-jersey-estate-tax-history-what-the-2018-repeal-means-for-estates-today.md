If you remember hearing that New Jersey taxes estates over $675,000, that is an old fact. The New Jersey estate tax is gone for anyone who died on or after January 1, 2018. The Division of Taxation says so on its inheritance and estate tax page, and says no estate tax return is required for those deaths.

That does not mean New Jersey stopped taxing inheritances. Two taxes lived side by side for decades, and only one was repealed. This page explains what the old tax was, what changed, and what an estate looks like today. For the full inheritance tax rules, use our [New Jersey inheritance tax guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax), and for the rest of the state's probate rules, the [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey).

## What was the New Jersey estate tax?

It was a tax on the estate as a whole, owed when the total value of what someone left was above an exemption amount. The Division of Taxation gives the figures this way.

| Date of death | State estate tax exemption |
|---|---|
| On or before December 31, 2016 | Capped at $675,000 |
| January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2017 | $2 million |
| On or after January 1, 2018 | No estate tax imposed |

The size of the old exemption is why the tax mattered. A family home in Bergen or Monmouth County plus a retirement account and a life insurance policy could clear $675,000 without anyone feeling wealthy. The Division notes that life insurance paid to a named beneficiary was exempt from the inheritance tax but still counted in the gross estate for estate tax purposes, so a policy could tip an estate over the line. A New Jersey family in that position could owe estate tax even though federal tax was far away. With the 2017 jump to $2 million and then repeal, that exposure ended.

The difference between the two kinds of tax is explained in [estate tax versus inheritance tax](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-vs-inheritance-tax). In short, an estate tax is figured on the whole estate and paid by it. An inheritance tax is figured on each person's share and paid by the heir.

## What still applies after the repeal?

Three layers, and only one of them is new money for most families.

1. **New Jersey inheritance tax.** Not repealed. Class A heirs (spouse, civil union and domestic partner, children, grandchildren, parents) and Class E charities owe nothing. Class C (siblings and children-in-law) pays 11% to 16% after a $25,000 exemption. Class D, which includes nieces, nephews and friends, pays 15% on the first $700,000 and 16% above that.
2. **Federal estate tax.** The 2026 federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, with $30 million for a married couple using portability. Top rate: 40%. Our [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) article keeps the current figures.
3. **Other states' taxes** if the person owned property elsewhere. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for how that works.

The repeal changed the first layer for exactly one kind of family: those whose estates were large enough to trigger the old estate tax but whose heirs were all in Class A. For them, the inheritance tax was already zero, and the estate tax was the only state bill. Now there is none.

## A worked example for 2026

This is an example, not a prediction. Ruth, a New Jersey widow, dies in 2026 with a house worth $1 million and $2.2 million in investments and bank accounts. Her will leaves $1.5 million to each of her two sons and $200,000 to a niece.

- **New Jersey estate tax:** none. She died after 2017, so there is no estate tax and no estate tax return.
- **Federal estate tax:** none. $3.2 million is far below the $15,000,000 exclusion.
- **New Jersey inheritance tax, sons:** none. Children are Class A.
- **New Jersey inheritance tax, niece:** 15% of $200,000, or $30,000. A niece is Class D.

The total New Jersey bill is $30,000, and it comes entirely from the one gift that is not Class A. Had Ruth died in 2016, the same $3.2 million would have been well over the $675,000 level, so an estate tax return would have been the first problem the executor faced. That is the change in one paragraph.

The planning point follows. For most New Jersey families the question is no longer how big the estate is. It is who is named. A niece, a sibling or a friend can create a tax even when the estate is small. Our [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) gives a first look.

## Does the repeal still matter for anyone?

Yes, in three situations.

**An estate from before 2018 is still open.** Late estates exist, usually because of a dispute, a sale delayed by title problems or an undiscovered asset. The tax rules that apply are those in force at the date of death. For these, the Division directs people to its Inheritance and Estate Tax Service Center at 609-292-5033 or Taxation.NJInheritancetax@treas.nj.gov, and an attorney should confirm the filing history.

**An executor is reading old articles.** Many websites still describe the $675,000 threshold as current. If an estate planner or bank tells you an estate tax return is due for a 2026 death, ask for the source. The rule is that none is due.

**A plan was built around the old tax.** Some older wills and trusts split an estate to use a state exemption, with a "credit shelter" share sized to the $675,000 or $2 million level. If the formula still refers to a state exemption that no longer exists, it may give a result nobody intended, such as a surprisingly small share to a surviving spouse. A review costs less than an argument among heirs. Our article on [whether you can avoid state estate tax by moving](/learn/estate-tax/can-you-avoid-state-estate-tax-by-moving-to-another-state) explains the broader trade-offs.

## What does an executor still file for a New Jersey death?

A return for the inheritance tax, if one is required, and the waivers that let banks and title companies release assets. The Division says the filing is due within eight months of death, and tax paid late accrues interest at 10% a year. The details, including Forms L-8 and L-9, are in the [inheritance tax guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) and in our article on the [estate bank account, EIN and tax waivers](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-bank-account-ein-and-tax-waivers). Marking the eight-month date on the calendar at the outset is worth more than any strategy, and the [executor commissions and probate timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline) article shows where it fits.

Keep the two systems apart in your head. The old state estate tax asked how much the estate was worth. The inheritance tax that remains asks who is receiving it, and the executor answers that by listing every beneficiary, their relationship to the person who died and what each one gets. Gaps in that list, such as a forgotten friend named on a bank account, are where late filings start.

A federal return is separate. Whether one is required, or worth filing to elect portability, is a question for the estate's attorney; see [Form 706](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return).
