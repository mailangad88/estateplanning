A POLST usually enters a family's life through a social worker or a palliative care nurse, not through an estate plan. Someone says, "Has anyone talked to you about a POLST?" and nobody in the room is sure whether that is the same thing as the living will in the drawer.

It is not. This page takes the narrow question: what a New Jersey POLST is, who it is meant for, who actually signs it, and what happens when it meets your other papers. For the full set of directive types, start with our article on [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives), and for the wider picture see the [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What is a POLST in New Jersey?

It is a one-form medical order. The state Department of Health describes it as a standardized form that lets patients state their preferences about life-sustaining treatment, signed by the attending physician, advanced practice nurse or physician assistant. The governing law is the Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment Act, first enacted as P.L. 2011, c. 145, and later amended. The Institute for Quality and Patient Safety is the lead agency the state designated to run the form.

The Act describes the form as voluntary, uniformly colored and uniquely identifiable, and says it is not an advance directive. It also records anatomical gift status, and it is meant to be honored in every care setting. That last feature is the point. A hospital chart note stays in the hospital. A POLST travels with the patient to a nursing home, to hospice, or to the kitchen floor when the ambulance arrives.

## How is it different from an advance directive?

The easiest way to hold the difference is this: an advance directive speaks for you later, and a POLST gives orders now.

| | Advance directive | POLST |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Your instructions and/or choice of a representative | A medical order for your current condition |
| Who completes it | You, with two witnesses or a notary | You or your representative, plus a clinician |
| Names a decision-maker | Yes, in a proxy directive | No |
| Who it speaks to | Your family and your care team, later | Nurses, EMTs and doctors, immediately |
| Best for | Every adult | People with serious or advancing illness |
| Takes effect | After a physician finds you lack capacity | When signed by the clinician |

The Act says a POLST complements an advance directive, or stands in for one when the patient has none. In practice, the directive sets values and picks the person. The POLST turns those values into specifics a paramedic can follow in ninety seconds: whether to attempt CPR, how much intervention, whether to go to the hospital at all.

## Who should consider one?

Mostly people who already know what they are facing. The Act describes the form as recommended for patients with advanced chronic progressive illness, or who otherwise want to define their care preferences. Think of Walter, 81, with heart failure and two recent hospital stays. He knows that he does not want to be put on a ventilator again. A directive that says "no life support if terminal" is too vague for an emergency crew. A POLST says it plainly.

For a healthy adult, the first step is a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) or proxy directive. Adding a POLST in your thirties has little benefit, and it can misstate your wishes if your health changes.

## Who signs it, and what makes it valid?

Three signatures make a form complete under the Act.

1. **The patient, if the patient has decision-making capacity.** The Act defines capacity as the ability to understand the nature, consequences, benefits, risks and alternatives of a health care decision and reach an informed choice.
2. **Or the patient's representative, if the patient lacks capacity.** The representative signs according to the patient's known wishes, or the patient's best interests if those wishes are unknown.
3. **The attending physician, physician assistant or advance practice nurse,** with a signature and date.

A form executed in another state is valid in New Jersey if it meets the requirements of the Act. Nothing in the form requires a notary or two witnesses, which is the big procedural difference from a directive. The clinician conversation does the work a witness would do.

## How do you change or revoke it?

A patient with capacity can change or revoke the form at any time, or ask for different treatment. The change takes effect once the clinician signs the form attesting to it. A clinician can also issue a new order after evaluating you and obtaining informed consent from you or your representative.

Two rules surprise families.

First, if you have a more recent directive that conflicts with the form, the clinician must honor the more recent directive. So if you sign a new proxy directive saying you want everything done and the POLST says otherwise, the newer paper wins and the clinician should update the form.

Second, a representative can modify or revoke the form for a patient who lost capacity only if the patient authorized that on the form, and the representative must first consult the clinician. A daughter holding a health care proxy cannot simply cross out the boxes.

## What if the hospital or family disagrees?

The Act lets institutions settle disputes about capacity or about how the form applies through their own procedures, such as an ethics committee, or through a court. People involved in the care, or an administrator, can seek that resolution. Courts are the last stop. Ethics consultation is usually the faster route.

The Act also says it does not limit a person's constitutional right to refuse treatment, does not remove the duty to provide comfort and relieve pain, and does not require conduct that is contrary to law or accepted medical standards. A POLST that says "comfort-focused treatment" does not mean "no care."

## What happens if a hospital or an ambulance crew does not know about it?

The Act puts the duty on the care team: health care professionals, institutions and emergency providers must treat a patient with a completed form according to its terms, except as the Act otherwise provides. The same law protects those who act in good faith under it, and it says that withholding or withdrawing treatment under a valid form is not homicide, suicide or euthanasia. It also sets consequences for intentionally ignoring a valid form, and for concealing, falsifying or coercing a form.

None of that helps if the crew never sees the paper. A POLST is only as useful as it is findable. Keep the original where responders will look first, give copies to the people named in your directive, and ask your doctor's office to place a copy in your chart. If you move into a facility, bring it to the admission meeting and ask that it be reviewed with the staff.

## How does a POLST fit with the rest of your documents?

Consider the sequence for Gloria, 74, who has COPD.

1. She signs a proxy directive naming her son Daniel and a backup, with two witnesses, at her kitchen table.
2. She talks to her pulmonologist and a palliative care nurse about what a future flare-up should look like.
3. They complete the POLST and the clinician signs it.
4. Gloria keeps the original where responders can find it and gives copies to Daniel, her facility and her primary doctor.
5. When her condition changes, she asks for the form to be reviewed, rather than waiting for the next crisis.
