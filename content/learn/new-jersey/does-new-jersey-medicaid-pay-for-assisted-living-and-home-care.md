Dolores, 81, pays $7,000 a month, give or take, for an assisted living apartment in Bergen County, and her savings will last about two more years. Her daughter has been told by three people that Medicaid "doesn't pay for assisted living," and by two others that it does. They are both partly right.

New Jersey's answer runs through MLTSS, Managed Long Term Services and Supports. This article takes the narrow question of assisted living and home care: how the screening works, who runs your care afterward and what a family should settle before a move. The money limits, look-back and married-couple rules are in our main article on [New Jersey Medicaid and nursing home planning](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning), and the state overview is our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey). The example figures here are invented.

## Does New Jersey Medicaid pay for assisted living?

The state says MLTSS services can be delivered at home, in an assisted living facility, in community residential services or in a nursing home, and it lists assisted living among the MLTSS benefits. So the program can pay for assisted living for someone who qualifies. What the state's overview does not settle is how the monthly charge for the apartment is treated, because that varies with the facility and the plan.

For that reason, treat "does Medicaid pay" as three separate questions: whether you qualify, whether the facility takes Medicaid, and what the resident still owes each month. Get the third answer in writing before a move. It is common for an assisted living contract to say nothing about what happens when private funds end. National background on who pays for each type of setting is in [assisted living, memory care, hospice and respite care](/learn/elder-care/assisted-living-memory-care-and-hospice-who-pays).

## Who qualifies for MLTSS?

Three tests apply to adults 21 and older.

| Test | What the state asks | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Age or disability | 65 or older, or under 65 and determined blind or disabled | Bring proof of age or the disability determination |
| Clinical | Nursing-facility level of care, meaning hands-on help with three or more activities of daily living | Call the Area Agency on Aging for the screening |
| Financial | Monthly income and total liquid assets under the state limits | Gather five years of statements; see the 2026 figures in our MLTSS article |

The financial test is where families get stuck. As of 2026 a single applicant may own $2,000 in countable resources, measured on the first day of each month, and a monthly income above $2,982 requires a Qualified Income Trust. Dolores, with a pension and Social Security above that line, would need one. The state also reviews five years of transfers. [The MLTSS article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning) has the numbers, the penalty divisor and the married-couple rules, and the [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) helps you count back.

## How does the clinical screening work?

You start with a call. The Division of Aging Services says adults 21 and older contact their county Area Agency on Aging (AAA) or Aging and Disability Resource Connection (ADRC) for a clinical screening. A clinician decides whether the person needs a nursing-facility level of care. The county welfare agency separately reviews the money side.

Prepare for the visit the way you would for a doctor's appointment.

1. List diagnoses, medications and recent hospital stays.
2. Write down which of the daily activities the person needs hands-on help with, and how often.
3. Have the person who knows the day-to-day routine in the room, not just the person who visits on Sundays.
4. Bring the power of attorney or health care proxy if the person cannot speak for themselves.

People tend to put the best face on it. A mother who "manages fine" when her daughter visits but cannot get out of the bathtub alone should say so. Overstating helps nobody, but understating can lead to a denial that has to be appealed.

## Who runs your care after you qualify?

A NJ FamilyCare managed care organization, called an MCO, coordinates all services under MLTSS. In practice that means a care manager builds a plan for care at home or in a residence, authorizes the services and arranges providers. The state's page does not describe how the plan assigns hours, so ask the care manager for the approved schedule and for the process to ask for more.

For someone staying home, the question is whether the hours cover real need. A care plan of a few hours of help a day does not replace a person who needs help overnight. Raise that early, with a doctor's note if you have one, instead of discovering it after a fall.

## What should you ask an assisted living facility before signing?

Ask these before the family signs anything.

- Do you accept Medicaid, and specifically NJ FamilyCare MLTSS?
- Is there a private-pay period first, and for how long?
- If the resident's money runs out, what happens to the apartment?
- What monthly amount will the resident or family still owe once Medicaid begins?
- Who is the financially responsible party named in the contract, and does the contract ask a relative to co-sign for payment?

The last question matters more than most families realize. Some admission contracts ask a child to co-sign, and that can create a personal debt. Have a lawyer read the contract before anyone signs as responsible party.

## What does estate planning have to do with it?

Quite a lot, because a Medicaid application is a paperwork project that someone has to carry out. A durable [power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) that lets the agent apply for benefits, access accounts and sign contracts is usually the first document. Without it, a family can face a guardianship proceeding, which takes time and money. Our article on [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) explains that route.

The second issue is what the state can claim afterward. The state's rules include recovery from the estate of a person who received long-term care, which is covered in [New Jersey estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery). Money placed in a Qualified Income Trust may also be subject to recovery after death, according to the state's brochure. Planning around the home and the will should happen before the application, not after.

The third is paying family. If a daughter moves in to help, put a written caregiver agreement in place instead of handing her cash. [Paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) explains how, and the national [Medicaid home care waiver article](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver) shows the choices other states use.

## A realistic timeline

Here is what Dolores's daughter might do.

1. **Month 1.** Call the AAA to schedule the clinical screening. Ask the facility the five questions above.
2. **Month 1 to 2.** Collect five years of bank and pension statements, the life insurance letter and any records of gifts.
3. **Month 2.** File with the county welfare agency. If income is above the line, set up the Qualified Income Trust before the month coverage should start.
4. **Month 3 and after.** Answer every caseworker request quickly, keep resources under the limit on the first of each month, and ask the MCO for the care plan in writing.
