Walt, 79, farms 42 acres of hay and vegetables in Hunterdon County. His property tax bill is a fraction of what the house lots down the road pay. He wants to know what happens to that bill if he dies, and whether his two children will owe a bundle of back taxes if one of them decides to sell. These are the right questions, and they have specific answers in the New Jersey Farmland Assessment Act of 1964, N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.1 and following.

This page covers the New Jersey angle on keeping a farm after the owner dies: how farmland assessment works, what the executor should do about it, how rollback taxes are triggered, and how the farm fits with the inheritance tax and a family plan. For the national view of passing on a farm, including federal valuation rules, read our article on [farm succession planning](/learn/business-owners/farm-succession-planning). ## How does farmland assessment work in New Jersey?

Farmland assessment lets qualifying land be taxed on its value for farming, not its market value (N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.2). The assessor looks only at what the land is worth for agricultural or horticultural use (N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.7). Here is the shape of the test, as the statutes read:

| Requirement | What the statute says |
|---|---|
| Size | At least five acres |
| Use | Actively devoted to agricultural or horticultural use for two successive years before the tax year |
| Income, first five acres | At least $1,000 a year in qualifying sales, payments or imputed income, measured over the two preceding years |
| Income, land above five acres | An average of at least $5.00 per acre per year; woodland and wetland above five acres need $0.50 per acre |
| Woodland | Needs a forest stewardship or woodland management plan, an annual attestation and an annual application |
| Application | Annual, due August 1 of the year before the tax year |

The income minimums are reviewed every three years by the State Farmland Evaluation Committee and can be raised, so check the current numbers with the municipal assessor before you rely on them (N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.5). The county assessor can tell you exactly which of your parcels are enrolled.

## What happens to farmland assessment when the owner dies?

The statute gives the estate some breathing room, and it does not hand heirs a blank check. Three provisions matter.

1. **The deadline stretches.** The assessor may extend the August 1 application deadline to no later than September 1 when the owner is ill or has died (N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.6).
2. **An income shortfall can be excused.** If the owner's death, injury or illness caused the farm to miss the income minimum, the assessor may excuse it once, with a death certificate or physician's certificate (N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.5).
3. **The assessment follows the use of the land.** Rollback taxes attach to a change to a non-farm use. The rollback section does not mention death or inheritance (N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.8), so title passing to heirs is not by itself the event that triggers the tax.

What this means in practice: after Walt dies, his executor should treat the farmland application as a deadline like any other estate tax matter. Find the last application, note the next August 1, ask the assessor how to file in the name of the estate or the heir, and keep the farm operating. If the hay field sits fallow for two years because everyone is grieving, the land may fail the use test, and the lower assessment goes with it.

## What are rollback taxes, and when do they apply?

Rollback taxes are the extra property tax the farm would have paid if it had been assessed at ordinary value. They apply when farmland valued under the Act is put to a non-agricultural or non-horticultural use (N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.8). The bill covers the year of the change and up to the two immediately preceding tax years in which the land was assessed as farmland, so at most three years.

The computation, in the statute's terms: the assessor finds the fair value of the land, applies the county percentage level, subtracts the actual farm assessment to get an additional assessment, and multiplies that by the district's general tax rate. Here is a simplified example. The numbers are invented for illustration, and real values and rates differ by town and year.

| Step | Example figure |
|---|---|
| Fair value of the land | $400,000 |
| County percentage level | 100% |
| Actual farmland assessment | $6,000 |
| Additional assessment | $394,000 |
| Municipal tax rate | 2.5% |
| Rollback for one year | $9,850 |
| Three years | about $29,550 |

Land acquired by the State, a local government unit, a qualifying nonprofit or the Palisades Interstate Park Commission for recreation and conservation is not subject to rollback taxes. The taxes are collected with a lien under the omitted-property procedures (N.J.S.A. 54:4-23.9), which is why a buyer's title company pays attention.

Rollback is the reason a farm sale to a developer costs the family more than the sale price suggests, and the reason heirs who split a farm for houses should price the tax before they sign anything.

## Does farmland assessment affect the New Jersey inheritance tax?

Not directly. Farmland assessment is a local property tax program. The state's inheritance tax is a different tax that depends on who receives property. Spouses, children and charities pay nothing, siblings and others pay at higher rates, and the return is due eight months after death, as described in our [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article. A farm left to a child, then, generally creates no state inheritance tax, while a farm left to a nephew or a friend can.

New Jersey has no estate tax for deaths since 2018, as covered in the history of the [New Jersey estate tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-tax-history-what-the-2018-repeal-means-for-estates-today). Large farms can still reach the federal estate tax. The federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and land inherited at death generally gets a step-up in basis. Our [farm succession planning](/learn/business-owners/farm-succession-planning) article covers special-use valuation and the other federal tools.

## How do you keep the farm in the family?

The tax rules only matter if the family stays together on the land. A plan that works for a New Jersey farm usually answers four questions in writing.

1. **Who farms?** The child who works the land needs a lease or an ownership role, and the others need to accept that.
2. **Who owns?** A trust or LLC can hold the land so no heir can force a sale by partition. Without one, co-owners can end up in a dispute described in our [heirs property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) article.
3. **How are the other children paid?** Equal value is not always equal acres. Life insurance, other assets or a buyout over time can balance the shares.
4. **Who keeps the paperwork?** The annual application, the income records and any woodland plan need an owner who will not miss August 1.

Consider Walt's family. His son Dan farms with him. His daughter Alicia lives in Pennsylvania and would sell tomorrow. Walt puts the land into a trust that leaves Dan a long-term lease at a fair rent and gives Alicia half the trust's value in cash from life insurance. Dan keeps farming, the land stays in qualifying use, and nobody faces a rollback bill. If Alicia had inherited half the land outright, she could have asked a court to divide or sell it.

Land also passes through probate if it is in your name alone. See [New Jersey probate](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) and [living trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) for how a trust avoids that. If the farmhouse is part of the estate, the rules in [inheriting a house in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/inheriting-a-house-in-new-jersey) apply to it, and the house may not be part of the farmland assessment at all, so ask the assessor how your parcel is split.

## A short checklist for the executor of a farm estate

- Find the most recent farmland assessment application and the assessor's contact.
- Note the next August 1 deadline and ask about the September 1 extension.
- Keep farm income records. Sales receipts matter more than ever.
- Do not change the use of any acre until the family has priced a rollback tax.
- Find the leases, equipment titles and operating loans, which belong to the farm business and not just the land.
- Ask a tax professional about the federal rules if the estate is large.
