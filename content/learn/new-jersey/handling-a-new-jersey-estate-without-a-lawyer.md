Plenty of New Jersey executors never hire a lawyer. A parent leaves a house and a few accounts to two children, the will is clear, and the family gets along. If that is your situation, you can do the work yourself, and the county Surrogate's office is built to receive the paperwork. The part people underestimate is everything that happens between the Surrogate's counter and the last check.

This page separates the two. It shows what the Surrogate does for you, what it leaves to you, and the four places where do-it-yourself executors most often stop. For the filing steps themselves, see the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process). For the full to-do list, use [executor duties in New Jersey, step by step](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step).

## Can you handle a New Jersey estate without a lawyer?

Yes, when the estate is simple. A clear will, family members who agree, no business and no property in another state are the signs. County Surrogates publish their own guides for executors, and the steps are ordinary: bring the original will, a certified death certificate and identification, wait until after the tenth day following death, and qualify.

The cases where we tell people not to go it alone are narrower than most expect, and our general article on [whether you need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer) lists them. In New Jersey the usual triggers are:

- A relative who has said the will is not valid, or a caveat on file ([will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats)).
- A person who received Medicaid, which brings a state claim ([estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery)).
- Debts that may exceed the assets ([creditor claims](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate)).
- Real estate in another state ([ancillary probate](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-out-of-state-property-and-ancillary-probate)).
- A beneficiary who is a minor or cannot manage money.

## What does the Surrogate actually do for you?

The Surrogate's office is the filing and records office for probate in each county. For an executor it does the following.

| Task | What you get |
|---|---|
| Reviews and admits the will | Probate of the will, with a witness appearing if the will is not self-proving |
| Qualifies you | Letters testamentary, the document banks ask for |
| Issues short certificates | One proof of authority per asset in the decedent's sole name |
| Sets bond when needed | A surety bond for an administrator; a will can waive it for an executor |
| Takes your proof of mailing | The record that notice of probate went to beneficiaries and next of kin |
| Receives releases | Refunding bonds and releases, for a fee per form |
| Seals small estate affidavits | The raised seal that lets a family skip an administrator in small estates |

Fees differ by county, and each county lists its own schedule on its Surrogate page. Gloucester County, for example, lists $100 for probate of a two-page will with one short certificate included. Check your county before you go.

That is real help. A competent clerk saves you a trip and a rejected form. But notice what the list does not contain.

## What will the Surrogate not do?

The Surrogate does not manage the estate. It does not find the assets, value them, decide what is probate property and what passes by beneficiary designation, choose a tax form or pay a creditor. It does not tell you when it is safe to distribute. It does not mediate between you and a sibling. Those decisions are yours, and so is the exposure if one is wrong.

An executor is a fiduciary, which means the law holds you to a duty of care toward the beneficiaries. A mistake made in good faith is still a mistake. If you pay a beneficiary and then a legitimate bill or tax claim shows up, the money has to come from somewhere. That is why the closing paperwork described in [refunding bonds and releases](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-refunding-bonds-and-releases-when-an-estate-closes) matters so much, and why we raise it first with people who plan to act alone.

## Where do do-it-yourself executors get stuck?

Four places, in the order they usually appear.

### The tax waiver

Banks and the Division of Taxation want proof that the inheritance tax is handled before they release an account in full. A bank may release up to half of an account to the executor before a waiver, but the rest waits. Which form applies depends on who inherits, and real estate always needs a waiver from the Division. The mechanics are in [the estate bank account, EIN and tax waivers](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-bank-account-ein-and-tax-waivers) and [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

Executors who stall here usually guessed at the form. If every beneficiary is a spouse or child, a short affidavit may be all you need. If a niece or a friend is in the will, a full return is required even if no tax ends up due.

### The 60-day notice

The notice of probate goes to beneficiaries and next of kin within 60 days of probate, with proof of mailing filed with the Surrogate. The trouble is the next of kin you did not know about: a half-brother, a child from an earlier marriage. Make the list at the start, not in week eight.

### The unknown or unwilling heir

When an address is missing or a relative will not answer, the whole release process slows. Do not distribute around that person. Ask the Surrogate what proof of notice it accepts, and talk to an attorney about the next step.

### The final releases

Most estates close with each beneficiary signing a refunding bond and release rather than a formal court accounting. Executors forget to ask, or ask after the money is spent. A formal accounting is a court proceeding, and a beneficiary can request one, so a clean ledger from week one is cheap insurance. [Court accountings and informal releases](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-court-accounting-or-informal-releases-for-an-estate) explains the difference.

## A realistic example

Example only. Priya's mother, Anita, died in Camden County with a will leaving everything equally to Priya and her brother Dev. The estate was a house, one checking account, a small brokerage account and a car.

Priya took the original will and a certified death certificate to the Surrogate after the tenth day, qualified, and ordered four short certificates, one each for the house, the bank, the brokerage and the car. She mailed notices to Dev and to two cousins in week three. Because both beneficiaries were children, she used the affidavit route for the bank and the Division's waiver form for the house. She sold the house, paid the funeral bill from the estate account with receipts, waited out the creditor window, and asked Dev to sign a release before the final check. She paid an attorney once, for an hour, to review the release. That is a sound do-it-yourself estate.

## A checklist before you decide

1. Is the will original, signed and clear about who gets what?
2. Do all beneficiaries know and agree?
3. Are there debts, a Medicaid history or a business?
4. Is any property outside New Jersey?
5. Can you give the estate several hours a week for six to twelve months?

Our [executor workload tool](/tools/executor-workload) gives a rough picture of the hours, and the [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) shows what goes through you. If a "no" answer shows up on questions one to four, a consultation is cheap relative to a mistake. You can also hire help for one task. An accountant or attorney can prepare the tax forms while you handle everything else.
