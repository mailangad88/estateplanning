Most families find out about digital assets the hard way. A mother dies, her daughter has the laptop, and nobody knows the password. The email company says it cannot talk to anyone but the account holder. New Jersey has a statute that answers when a company has to talk to someone else.

This article walks through the New Jersey act itself: who it covers, what the executor has to send, what the company can hand over and the deadline that makes it respond. For the general idea behind these laws across the country, see [what RUFADAA is](/learn/digital-assets/rufadaa-explained). Here the focus is what New Jersey's version says. It belongs with the rest of the [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What is the New Jersey law, and where is it?

It is P.L. 2017, c.237, approved September 13, 2017 and codified at N.J.S.A. 3B:14-61.1 through 3B:14-61.18. It took effect on the 90th day after enactment. The act applies to a fiduciary acting under a will or power of attorney signed before or after that date, to a personal representative for someone who died before or after it, to guardians and to trustees. In other words, an old will does not lose its effect.

The act applies if the user lives in New Jersey or lived here at death. Digital assets here means an electronic record in which a person has a right or interest. In practice that reaches email, cloud photos, social media and similar accounts. The act leaves out an employer's digital assets that an employee uses in the ordinary course of business.

The Probate Part of the Chancery Division, which includes the Surrogate's Court, is the court the act refers to.

## What order of instructions does the act follow?

The act sets a ranking, and the account holder's choices come first.

1. **The company's online tool.** Some providers let you name a person to receive your account, in a setting separate from the terms of service. If the tool lets you change or delete that instruction at any time, it overrides a conflicting instruction in your will, trust or power of attorney (section 4a).
2. **Your legal documents.** If you did not use an online tool, or the company offers none, you can allow or prohibit disclosure to a fiduciary in a will, trust, power of attorney or other record (section 4b).
3. **The terms of service.** If you gave no direction, the company's terms of service decide how far access goes (section 5c). A direction you gave overrides a terms-of-service clause that you did not separately and affirmatively agree to (section 4c).

A fiduciary never gets more rights than the account holder had. The act does not let anyone impersonate the user, and a fiduciary stays bound by the terms of service, copyright law and the limits of their role.

## Content or catalogue: what can the executor actually get?

The act splits digital records into two kinds, and the split drives most of the practical outcomes.

| | Content of communications | Catalogue and other assets |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The substance of emails, texts and messages the user sent or received | Who the user wrote to, with dates, times and addresses, plus non-message assets |
| Default | Needs the user's consent or a court direction | Disclosed unless the user prohibited it or the court orders otherwise |
| Executor's extra proof | Evidence of consent, unless the online tool was used | An affidavit that disclosure is reasonably necessary for estate administration, instead of a court finding |

For a deceased user, sections 7 and 8 set out what the personal representative sends: a written request, a death certificate, a certificate of appointment (letters) or a small estate affidavit, and any identifiers the company asks for. Because a small estate affidavit counts, a family that never opened a formal estate can still make a request. Our article on the [small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) explains when that route is available.

Take Gloria, 66, who died with a Gmail account full of tax records and photos. Her will says her executor may access the content of her electronic communications. Her son, the executor, mails the company a written request, her death certificate and his letters, and attaches the will page. That is the package the act describes. Had her will been silent and she had not used any online tool, he might still get the catalogue, but the messages themselves could need a court order.

## What about agents, trustees and guardians?

Each has its own section.

- **Agents under a power of attorney.** Content requires that the power of attorney expressly grant authority over communications content. The agent sends a written request, the document and a certification under penalty of perjury that the power is in effect (section 9). Without express authority, the agent can still seek the catalogue and non-content assets (section 10). See our piece on [power of attorney and digital accounts](/learn/digital-assets/power-of-attorney-and-digital-accounts).
- **Trustees.** If the trustee was the original user of the account, the company must disclose all trust digital assets, including content, unless the court orders otherwise or the trust says otherwise (section 11). For other trustees, content needs a certified trust instrument or a trustee certification that includes consent to disclosure.
- **Guardians.** A court order is required, issued after a hearing (section 14). Our overview of [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) covers the process.

## How long does the company have to respond?

Sixty days. Section 16 requires a custodian to comply with a request within 60 days after it receives the information the act requires. If it does not, the fiduciary or designated recipient can apply to the court for an order, and the court must find that compliance does not violate the federal Stored Communications Act (18 U.S.C. s.2702).

The company chooses the form of help. It can give full access to the account, partial access sufficient for the fiduciary's tasks, or a copy of the assets the user could have accessed on the date of the request (section 6). It need not disclose assets the user deleted. It may charge a reasonable administrative fee. If separating the assets would be unduly burdensome, the company or fiduciary can ask the court for an order, and the court may limit the date range, order all or none, or review the material privately.

A custodian that acts in good faith to comply with the act is immune from liability. A fiduciary acting within scope counts as an authorized user under computer-fraud laws, which matters because logging into someone's account without permission could otherwise raise a legal question.

## What should you do now to make this easier?

The act helps people who set things up. A few practical steps:

1. Use each provider's own tool for naming a person, where one exists. It outranks everything else.
2. Add a short clause to your will, trust and power of attorney that expressly consents to your fiduciary's access to the content of your electronic communications. Without it, content is the hardest thing to get.
3. Keep an inventory of accounts, with no passwords in your will, since a will becomes a public record once probated.
4. Name a person to handle digital matters and tell them. Our article on a [digital executor](/learn/digital-assets/what-is-a-digital-executor) covers the role.
5. Remember that crypto in a wallet you control yourself is not held by a company at all, so this act has no one to send a request to. Our [cryptocurrency inheritance](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance) article covers that gap.
