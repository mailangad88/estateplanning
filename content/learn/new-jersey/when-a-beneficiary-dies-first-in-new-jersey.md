Wills assume the people named will outlive the person who wrote them. Often they do not. A brother named as executor moves to hospice, a daughter dies in her fifties, or two spouses are hurt in the same accident and die a day apart. What the will says in the plain words on the page may no longer decide who gets the property.

New Jersey has specific rules for those cases in Title 3B of the statutes. This page covers anti-lapse in N.J.S.A. 3B:3-35, what happens to failed gifts in 3B:3-36, and the 120-hour survival rule in 3B:3-32. For the national picture, our article on [lapsed gifts and simultaneous death](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death) is a good companion, and the [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey) links our other state articles.

## What is a lapsed gift?

A gift in a will lapses when the person it was meant for is dead at the time it would take effect. The gift cannot go to a dead person, so the question is where it goes instead. New Jersey answers in layers: first an anti-lapse substitute if the statute applies, then the will's own backup language, then the residue, then your heirs.

## What does New Jersey's anti-lapse rule cover?

N.J.S.A. 3B:3-35 steps in when a devisee dies before the will is signed, fails to survive you, or is treated as having predeceased you. If that beneficiary was a grandparent of yours, a stepchild of yours, or a lineal descendant of one of your grandparents, the beneficiary's descendants who survive you by 120 hours take in the beneficiary's place, by representation.

The list matters. A lineal descendant of a grandparent covers your children, grandchildren, siblings, nieces, nephews, cousins and parents. A stepchild is a child of your surviving, deceased or former spouse who is not also your child. The statute does not list a spouse, a friend, a business partner or a charity. A gift to someone outside the list fails if they die first, unless your will provides a backup.

The same section treats a person who would have been a member of a class gift, such as "my nieces and nephews," as a devisee if they would have qualified had they survived you. That helps in the family-class case, whether that person died before or after the will was signed.

| If the beneficiary who died first was your... | Anti-lapse under 3B:3-35? |
|---|---|
| Child, grandchild | Yes, their descendants who survive by 120 hours take their place |
| Sibling, niece, nephew | Yes, they are lineal descendants of a grandparent |
| Stepchild | Yes, with the statute's definition of stepchild |
| Spouse | No, not on the statutory list |
| Friend, neighbor, caregiver | No |
| Charity | No |

Consider Walter, a widower with a will that leaves his estate to his son Danny and his daughter Alice in equal shares. Danny dies in March. Walter dies in October and never changed his will. Danny's two children, who survive Walter by well over 120 hours, take Danny's half between them. Alice keeps her half. If Danny had no children, nothing passes by anti-lapse. Because the gift was a residuary gift to two people, Alice would take Danny's share under the residuary rule covered next.

## What happens when there is no anti-lapse substitute?

The gift fails, and the will's own terms and N.J.S.A. 3B:3-36 decide where it goes. The section gives two rules:

1. A gift that is not residuary and that fails for any reason becomes part of the residue.
2. If the residue was left to two or more people and one of them dies first, the failed share goes to the other residuary beneficiaries in proportion to their interests, unless a contrary intention appears in the will.

Say Priya's will leaves $15,000 to her friend Joan and the residue to her two nephews. Joan dies first. Joan is not on the anti-lapse list, so the $15,000 falls into the residue and the nephews split it. If a nephew had died first leaving children, his children would step in under the anti-lapse rule. Only if no residuary beneficiary can take does the property pass to your heirs, because under N.J.S.A. 3B:5-2 any part of the estate not effectively disposed of by the will goes by intestacy. Our article on [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) explains how those labels work.

## What is the 120-hour survival rule?

N.J.S.A. 3B:3-32 says that, in reading a will, trust or other governing document, a person who cannot be shown by clear and convincing evidence to have survived an event, such as another person's death, by 120 hours is deemed to have died first. That is five days.

The rule keeps property from passing to someone who lived only briefly and then through that person's estate to someone you never meant to benefit. It also works for couples. If a husband and wife die in a crash and no one can prove the survivor outlived the other by 120 hours, each is treated as having died first for purposes of the other's will. The backup gifts in each will take over.

Joint property gets its own rule. For two owners with survivorship rights, such as joint tenants or a married couple holding as tenants by the entirety, if it cannot be shown that one outlived the other by 120 hours, half the property passes as if each had survived. For more than two owners, the property divides equally. Joint accounts are covered by the same definition.

Where the section conflicts with the Uniform Simultaneous Death Law in N.J.S.A. 3B:6-1 and following, the 3B:3-32 rule controls. That older law, in 3B:6-2, treats each person as having survived the other when there is no sufficient evidence of the order of death, and for life insurance 3B:6-5 treats the insured as having outlived the beneficiary on the same footing. Those fallback rules matter less when the 120-hour rule applies.

## Can a will override these rules?

Yes, in the ways that matter. The 120-hour rule does not apply when the document expressly addresses simultaneous deaths, deaths in a common disaster or a required survival period. It also does not apply if clear and convincing evidence shows applying it across several documents would cause an unintended failure or duplication of a gift. And N.J.S.A. 3B:3-36 gives way to a contrary intention in the will.

That is why a well-drafted will names backups. A short checklist:

- Name a contingent beneficiary for every significant gift. Our article on [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) covers the same idea for forms.
- Say what happens if a named beneficiary dies first: does their share go to their children, to the other beneficiaries, or to a charity?
- Choose a survival period. Some couples pick 30 days so that a survivor who lives only a short while does not carry property to the other side of the family.
- Name backup executors and trustees, because a fiduciary can also die first. See [when your named executor cannot serve](/learn/wills/when-your-named-executor-cannot-serve).
- Re-read the wills of both spouses together.

## What about beneficiary forms and trusts?

The rules above are written for wills and similar governing instruments. Beneficiary forms for life insurance and retirement accounts are controlled by the form on file and the plan or contract terms, so read each one. If the primary beneficiary dies first and no contingent is named, a plan may default to the estate, which sends the money through probate.
