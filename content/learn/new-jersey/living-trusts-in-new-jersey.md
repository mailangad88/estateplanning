The question most New Jersey clients ask about trusts is a fair one: "Do I really need it?" Friends in other states have told them probate is a nightmare and that a living trust fixes it. In New Jersey the picture is calmer. Probate happens at the county Surrogate's office, the executor has a clear sequence to follow, and many estates finish without trouble.

So this page does not try to sell you a trust. It explains where a revocable living trust earns its keep in New Jersey, what the state's trust code says about control and creditors, and what a trustee owes beneficiaries once the settlor has died. It is part of our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey) and builds on the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) and the national guide to [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).

## What is New Jersey's Uniform Trust Code?

It is Chapter 31 of Title 3B of the New Jersey statutes, enacted as P.L.2015, c.276 and approved on January 19, 2016. The act took effect 180 days after it was enacted, in 2016.

A revocable trust, in the Code's words, is one that is revocable by the settlor without the consent of the trustee (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-3). The points that matter most:

- **Capacity.** The capacity required to create a revocable trust is the same as that required to make a will (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-42).
- **Revoking or amending.** The settlor may revoke or amend unless the terms expressly make the trust irrevocable, and on revocation the trustee delivers the property to the settlor as directed (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-43).
- **Who the trustee answers to.** While the trust is revocable, the rights of the beneficiaries are subject to the settlor's control, and the trustee's duties are owed to the settlor alone (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-44).
- **Creditors.** During the settlor's lifetime, the property of a revocable trust is subject to claims of the settlor's creditors (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-39).

That last point is the one people miss. A living trust is a way to organize and pass property. It is not asset protection.

## When does a living trust actually help in New Jersey?

When probate or incapacity would cause a real problem that a will cannot solve. These are the situations we see most.

| Situation | Why a trust can help | Will-only alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Real estate in another state | Avoids a second court case there | Ancillary probate in each state |
| Beneficiary is a minor or has special needs | Trustee manages money on your schedule | Court-supervised guardian of the estate or a testamentary trust |
| You may lose capacity | Successor trustee steps in without court | Power of attorney, which banks sometimes resist |
| Blended family | Locks in who gets what after the second spouse | Will, which can be contested more openly |
| Privacy matters | The trust is not filed with the Surrogate | Will becomes part of the probate file |
| Simple estate, one child, beneficiary forms in place | Little gain | Will, power of attorney, advance directive |

A will that was never probated in New Jersey has no effect here until it is, so a Florida snowbird with a New Jersey condo still faces a New Jersey proceeding (see [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate)). A funded trust avoids that step.

Whether privacy matters is personal. The probate file at the Surrogate is generally open to the public, and some families dislike the idea of neighbors reading what each child received. Others do not care.

## Is Surrogate probate simple enough that you can skip a trust?

Often, yes. After the tenth day following death, the Surrogate can admit the will and issue letters. The executor then mails the notice of probate within 60 days (R. 4:80-6). For a small estate with no will, the affidavit routes in the [New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) article can avoid administration altogether. Costs and timing are covered in [probate costs in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/probate-costs-in-new-jersey) and [executor commissions and the probate timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline).

That does not make a trust pointless. It means the argument for one is about incapacity, privacy, out-of-state property and structure, not about escaping New Jersey probate as though it were a disaster. If a salesperson says otherwise, ask what specifically in your situation they are solving. Our article on [whether you need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) works through the same question nationally.

## Does a trust change the New Jersey inheritance tax?

Not on its own. The tax depends on who receives property and how they are related to the person who died, so a trust that leaves a house to a nephew leaves the nephew in the same class. Read the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article for the classes. A trust may help with timing and structure, but it is not a tax elimination device.

## What must the trustee tell the beneficiaries?

The Code's disclosure section is titled "Duty to disclose and discretion to periodically report" and numbered N.J.S.A. 3B:31-67. It says a trustee shall keep the qualified beneficiaries of the trust reasonably informed about the administration of the trust, and shall promptly furnish a copy of the trust instrument on a beneficiary's request. The trustee may also provide beneficiaries with a report of the trust property, which the statute frames as discretionary.

I looked for a fixed deadline such as "within 60 days of the settlor's death" in the New Jersey text I read and did not find one. That does not prove there is none elsewhere in the law, so confirm the current deadline with an attorney. In practice, a careful trustee does the following after a death:

1. Order several certified death certificates and read the trust to see who the beneficiaries are now.
2. Write to each qualified beneficiary, say that the trust is now irrevocable, name the trustee and offer a copy.
3. Get a tax ID number for the trust and open a trust account.
4. Make an inventory with date-of-death values and keep records.
5. Send updates at least annually, and keep the receipts.

Other sections of the Code cover methods and waiver of notice (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-9), and who is treated as a qualified beneficiary when no formal status applies (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-10). A section on limiting actions against a trustee (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-74) means that how and when a report is delivered can affect when a claim must be brought. Read the section itself, or ask a lawyer, before you rely on a report to start or stop any clock.

For the full process, see [trustee duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) and [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).

## What are the common mistakes?

The most frequent one is a trust nobody funded. Fran signs a beautiful trust, then never retitles the house or accounts. When she dies, the Surrogate still has to open a file for the assets in her own name, and the trust sits empty except for what a pour-over will sends into it. [How to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) and [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) explain the deed and account steps. Our companion article on [transfer on death options in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-on-death-options-in-new-jersey) shows the alternatives for accounts and vehicles.

Other mistakes: naming a trustee who cannot handle the work, using a certification of trust that is not signed by every acting trustee (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-81), and assuming the trust solves a Medicaid problem. For long-term care, read [Medicaid and nursing home planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning) first.
