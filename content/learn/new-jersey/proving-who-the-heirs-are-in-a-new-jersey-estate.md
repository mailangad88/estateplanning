The first question a Surrogate's office asks in an intestate case is not about money. It is about people: who survived this person, and how are they related? An executor with a will faces the same question in a quieter form, because the heirs still get notice. Getting the family tree right on day one saves an estate from a wrong distribution.

This page is about proof. It explains how to build the tree, what records back it up, what to do about relatives you cannot find, and what New Jersey law asks of the person in charge. The rules that decide who inherits are on our page about [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey), and the wider process is in the [New Jersey guide](/learn/new-jersey). For the general version in other states, see [how to prove you are an heir](/learn/probate/how-to-prove-you-are-an-heir).

## Who counts as an heir in New Jersey?

It depends on who survived. N.J.S.A. 3B:5-3 gives a surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner a share first. The rest goes under 3B:5-4 in a fixed order: descendants, then parents, then the descendants of the parents (siblings, nieces and nephews), then grandparents, then the descendants of grandparents, and finally stepchildren and their descendants. A relative in an earlier group blocks the later ones. Each person also has to survive the decedent by 120 hours (3B:5-1).

Some details decide real cases:

- **Half-blood relatives** take the same share as whole-blood relatives (3B:5-7).
- **Children born outside marriage** are children for intestacy purposes "regardless of the marital state" of their parents, and parentage can be established under the Parentage Act (3B:5-10).
- **Adopted children** have the rights that the adoption statutes give them (3B:5-9).
- **A child born after death** counts if he or she lives 120 hours or more after birth (3B:5-8).

Our [estate planning guide on domestic partners](/learn/new-jersey/domestic-partners-and-civil-union-partners-in-new-jersey-estate-law) covers the partner side of the first group.

## How do you build the family tree?

On paper, from the decedent outward, with one page per generation. Do not trust a single relative's account. Start by writing every spouse, partner and child, living or dead, then the parents, then siblings. Add the dates and places of each birth, marriage, divorce and death you know, and mark each as proved or unproved.

Here is a worked example with invented names and numbers. Harold dies in Camden County with no will, no spouse, no children and no living parents. He leaves an estate of $300,000. His family: a sister, Ruth, who is alive; a brother, Ed, who died in 2019 leaving two children, Nina and Paul; and a half-brother, Sam, from his father's second marriage, who is alive.

| Person | Relationship | Status | Share of $300,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ruth | Sister | Living | $100,000 |
| Ed's branch (Nina and Paul) | Nephew and niece, by representation | Ed died first | $50,000 each |
| Sam | Half-brother | Living | $100,000 |

Sam takes a full share because half-blood relatives inherit the same share (3B:5-7), and Ed's children split Ed's share. The tree turns a fuzzy family story into numbers. It also shows what proof is needed: Ruth's, Ed's and Harold's birth certificates showing the parents; Ed's death certificate; Nina's and Paul's birth certificates; Sam's birth certificate showing the father; and the father's marriage record for the second marriage. ## What records does the Surrogate want?

Counties publish their own checklists, so ask the Surrogate where the decedent lived before you file. Expect to prepare these items, and expect the details to differ by county:

1. A certified death certificate (see [how many to order](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-death-certificates-who-can-order-and-how-many-you-need)).
2. A list of every heir and next of kin with address and relationship, which is how the administration application starts (see [administering an estate without a will](/learn/new-jersey/administering-a-new-jersey-estate-without-a-will)).
3. Whatever consents, waivers or declinations the county asks the other heirs to sign.
4. The original will, if there is one, and the proof of mailing of the notice of probate, which goes to beneficiaries and next of kin within 60 days of probate under R. 4:80-6.
5. For a very small estate with no will and no spouse, an Affidavit of Next of Kin with written consent from the other next of kin of equal degree, as explained in [our small estate affidavit article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit).

Ask the county whether it wants copies of the certificates filed or only the sworn list. Either way, keep the certificates in your file to back the list up. If someone later challenges the list, or a title company wants proof before insuring a house sale, you will need them.

## What if an heir cannot be found?

You search, and you write down the search. N.J.S.A. 3B:5-5.1 tells a fiduciary of an intestate estate who has reason to believe unidentified or unlocated people may be entitled to a share to investigate. The statute scales the effort: the steps must have a reasonable likelihood of finding the individuals, and their cost must be reasonable compared with the value of the distribution.

A practical search runs in this order.

1. Ask every relative, neighbor and old friend, and look through the decedent's address book, holiday cards and mail.
2. Check obituaries, which often list survivors by name and city.
3. Search public records, social media and the last known address, and write to each address by a method that gives you a receipt.
4. Check the death certificate for the informant's name.
5. If the share is large and the trail is cold, hire a professional heir search firm and keep the invoice.

Our companion article on [an heir who cannot be found or when no family survives](/learn/what-if/heir-cannot-be-found-or-no-family-survives) walks through the end of the road. New Jersey's Unclaimed Property Administration can hold funds for owners nobody can locate, but whether it is the right route for an estate share is a question for an attorney, and our [guide to unclaimed property for an estate](/learn/new-jersey/finding-unclaimed-property-for-a-new-jersey-estate) explains how the agency treats claims.

## Does a will make the heir question go away?

No. If the will leaves everything to one child, the other children are still next of kin and are entitled to the notice of probate described above. The executor's duty is covered in [executor duties in New Jersey step by step](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step). A forgotten half-sibling who gets no notice is the person most likely to complain years later.

The tree also drives the tax. New Jersey's inheritance tax depends on the relationship of the person who receives property, so a sibling or a niece falls in a different class from a child, as our page on the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) explains. Getting the relationships right is therefore a tax fact as well as a family fact.

## Common mistakes our attorneys see

- Listing only the relatives who showed up to the funeral.
- Forgetting a child from an earlier relationship or a child placed for adoption long ago.
- Treating a half-sibling as "not a real heir."
- Assuming a niece takes her late parent's place without checking that the parent really died first.
- Distributing before the list has been checked against the records.
