New Jersey does not run its estates the way the next state over does. The county Surrogate, an official most people have never heard of, handles probate. The state has no estate tax anymore but keeps an inheritance tax that depends on who you are leaving things to. Small estates have their own affidavit shortcuts, and the rules for powers of attorney, health care directives and a spouse's rights each have their own New Jersey answer.

This guide summarizes the New Jersey articles on this site and links each one. It also points to our national guides on probate, wills, estate tax, powers of attorney and trusts, so you can see where New Jersey follows the common pattern. The rules below that carry a citation come from the statute, the court rules or a New Jersey government page. Our attorneys still have to confirm the details, so treat this page as a map and not as advice for your case.

## How does the New Jersey system fit together?

Wills and estates are governed by Title 3B of the New Jersey statutes. Probate runs through the county Surrogate and, when there is a dispute, the Superior Court. The inheritance tax is run by the Division of Taxation. Each office has a different job.

## What makes a will valid in New Jersey?

A New Jersey will must be in writing, signed by the maker (or by someone else in the maker's conscious presence), and signed by at least two witnesses within a reasonable time (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2). The maker must be 18 or older and of sound mind (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-1). The execution statute does not require a notary.

A notary comes in for the self-proving affidavit, which is the maker's acknowledgment and the witnesses' affidavits before an authorized officer (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-4). That affidavit lets the Surrogate admit the will without finding a witness. It can be added after the signing (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-5).

Two safety nets exist, and neither is a plan. A will that was not witnessed is still valid as a writing intended as a will if the signature and material portions are in the maker's handwriting (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-2). And a court can treat a document that missed the signing rules as a will if clear and convincing evidence shows the maker intended it (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-3). Both lead to more court time than a clean two-witness signing.

The full discussion is in [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements). For the rules every state shares, read our [wills guide](/learn/wills), and for how a will stands up to a challenge, see [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## Who inherits if there is no will?

State law decides. When someone dies without a will, the Surrogate appoints an administrator, who distributes the estate under New Jersey's intestate succession rules. The order of heirs, and how it treats a spouse, children, parents and siblings, is covered in [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey).

Intestacy reaches only probate assets. Accounts with a beneficiary, jointly owned property and trust assets pass by their own terms. For the idea in general, see our guide to [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## How does New Jersey probate work?

Probate starts at the Surrogate's office in the county where the person lived. A resident's will can be probated in the Surrogate's court or in the Superior Court (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-24). County Surrogates say probate and the issuance of letters occur only after the tenth day after death.

After that the executor follows a sequence:

1. Bring the original will, a certified death certificate and identification to the Surrogate.
2. Have the will admitted and receive letters testamentary. If the will is not self-proving, a witness may have to appear (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-19).
3. Mail a notice of probate to all beneficiaries and next of kin within 60 days of probate, then file the proof of mailing with the Surrogate (R. 4:80-6).
4. Collect assets, pay valid debts and deal with taxes, including the inheritance tax.
5. Distribute what is left.

A caveat filed with the Surrogate before an executor is appointed can temporarily block the appointment. Contested matters, and handwritten wills in at least some counties, go to the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part. The step-by-step version is in [the New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process). For the national picture, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate) and the article on [letters testamentary](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration).

Executor pay and how long the work takes are covered in [New Jersey executor commissions and the probate timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline). We do not state numbers here because that article owns them.

## Can a small estate skip probate?

Sometimes, when there is no will. A surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner can take the assets of an intestate estate by filing an affidavit with the Surrogate if the total real and personal assets do not exceed $50,000. The first $10,000 is free from the decedent's debts (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-3). If there is no spouse or partner, one heir can do the same for an estate of $20,000 or less, with the written consent of the other heirs (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-4).

Those limits come from a law approved on January 19, 2016. The person who signs takes on an administrator's powers and duties and can be sued and required to account. The estate of someone who left a will goes through probate instead.

Consider Helen, whose husband died without a will with $37,000 in his name alone. She fits the spouse route. If her husband had also had a $40,000 brokerage account in his name, she would not. See [the New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) for the contents of each affidavit, and our [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) to add up the assets.

## What is the New Jersey inheritance tax?

New Jersey taxes what certain heirs receive, based on the relationship to the person who died. Class A pays nothing: spouse, civil union or domestic partner, children, stepchildren, grandchildren, parents and grandparents. Class E, which includes qualified charities and the State, also pays nothing. Class C, mainly siblings and children-in-law, pays nothing on the first $25,000 and 11% to 16% above that. Class D, everyone else, pays 15% on the first $700,000 and 16% above.

The return and the tax are due eight months after death, and unpaid tax accrues interest at 10% a year. There is no extension of time to pay. Banks and title companies may require a tax waiver first. Form L-8 covers bank, stock and brokerage accounts that pass to Class A beneficiaries, and Form L-9 requests a real property waiver when the whole estate is untaxable.

The estate tax is different. The Division of Taxation says no New Jersey estate tax applies to deaths on or after January 1, 2018. The federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most New Jersey families owe no estate tax at all, but the inheritance tax can still reach a friend or a niece.

Ten thousand dollars to a friend costs $1,500 in tax if it is the friend's only inheritance. The examples and the rate tables are in [the New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax). Our [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) gives a first look, and our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) covers the federal side.

## What can a surviving spouse claim?

New Jersey gives a surviving spouse protections beyond what a will says, and a spouse who is left out of a will can have a claim. The details, including how the elective share works, what it reaches and how it is measured, are in [New Jersey elective share for a surviving spouse](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse). For inheritance tax purposes, a spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner is in Class A and pays no New Jersey inheritance tax on what they receive.

## What do powers of attorney and advance directives do?

They let someone act for you when you cannot. A power of attorney covers money and property. An advance directive covers health care decisions. Most adults need both, and they follow different signing rules.

[New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) explains the property document: what the agent can do, when it starts and how banks treat it. [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives) explains the health care documents: naming a health care representative and writing down your wishes.

Without a power of attorney, a family may have to ask a court to appoint a guardian, which is public, slow and costly. Our national guides on [powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) and [health care directives](/learn/healthcare-directives) cover the common ground, and [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains why the signed documents are the better path.

## What about trusts and avoiding probate?

A living trust can keep assets out of probate, but only if the assets are actually moved into it. Beneficiary designations, joint ownership and transfer on death deeds can also keep assets out of the Surrogate's office. None of these changes the inheritance tax picture on its own, since the tax follows the relationship of the person who receives the property. Our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) and the article on [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) cover the options. We have not written New Jersey-specific articles on trusts or transfer on death deeds, so ask an attorney how the local rules apply to your property.

## What should you do next?

Work through these in order, and stop where your situation is covered.

1. List your assets and note how each is titled and who is named on each beneficiary form.
2. Decide who the heirs would be today under the New Jersey rules, and who you want them to be.
3. Check that your will, if you have one, was signed with two witnesses and has a self-proving affidavit.
4. Put a power of attorney and an advance directive in place while you have capacity.
5. If you want to leave something to a friend, a niece or a nephew, look at the inheritance tax classes.
6. If you are in a second marriage, ask how your spouse's rights fit with the rest of your plan.
7. Review everything after a move, a marriage, a divorce or a death in the family.

If a death has just happened, find the original will, order certified death certificates and call the county Surrogate. Mark the 60-day notice and the eight-month tax date on a calendar.
