Most families first learn about advance directives in a hospital hallway, when a doctor asks who speaks for a parent who cannot speak for himself. A signed directive answers that question ahead of time. It tells the care team who decides and what you would want.

In New Jersey these documents are governed by the Advance Directives for Health Care Act, N.J.S.A. 26:2H-53 and following. This page explains the two kinds of directive, how to sign one so it holds, who can serve as your representative, how to change it, and how a POLST differs. For the wider picture, see our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What are the two kinds of New Jersey advance directive?

The state Department of Health describes two types, and you can have either or both.

**The proxy directive** is a durable power of attorney for health care. You appoint a health care representative to make medical decisions for you when you cannot, whether the problem is temporary, such as after an accident, or permanent. The representative follows the choices you would have made. If they cannot tell what you would want, they decide based on your best interest.

**The instruction directive** is often called a living will. It says which situations you would or would not want life-sustaining treatment, and it can describe your values and general preferences. It guides the people around you when your directive does not cover a situation exactly.

The two work well together. The proxy picks the person, and the instruction directive gives that person something to hold on to. For help comparing the labels, see [advance directive versus living will versus healthcare proxy](/learn/healthcare-directives/advance-directive-vs-living-will-vs-healthcare-proxy).

## How do you sign a New Jersey advance directive?

It must be in writing and signed by you. Your signature must then be either witnessed by two adults or acknowledged before a notary public or another authorized official, such as a New Jersey attorney or judge. A notary is optional. If you use one, you do not need witnesses. Execution requirements sit in N.J.S.A. 26:2H-56.

The state's own forms add detail on witnesses.

- Each witness must be 18 or older.
- Each witness confirms that you signed knowingly and voluntarily, that you appear to be of sound mind, and that you are free of duress or undue influence.
- The person you name as health care representative or alternate cannot be a witness.

A short signing checklist:

1. Pick your representative and up to two alternates, and ask each if they will serve.
2. Complete the proxy directive, the instruction directive, or both.
3. Sign and date in front of two adult witnesses, or in front of a notary.
4. Give copies to your representative, alternates, doctor and family.
5. Ask your doctor to place the directive in your permanent medical record.

## Who can be your health care representative?

You can name a competent adult: a spouse, civil union partner, domestic partner, adult child, parent, other relative, friend, religious or spiritual advisor, or anyone else you trust (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-58). You may name alternates in order of priority. If your first choice is unavailable, unable, unwilling or disqualified, the next one steps in, and the first can resume later if practical.

A few rules to know before you choose.

- **Institution limit.** Staff of a facility where you are a patient or resident generally cannot serve unless they are close family.
- **Spouse designations.** A spouse or civil union partner designation ends on divorce, legal separation or dissolution, unless your directive says otherwise (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-57). Domestic partner designations end when the partnership ends.
- **Limits.** The statute expects you to state any limits on your representative's authority, including what applies if you are pregnant.
- **Consultation.** You can tell your representative to consult certain people, such as alternates or family.
- **The right to decline.** A person you appoint may decline to serve (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-61).

## When does a directive take effect?

Only after a physician determines that you cannot understand your diagnosis, your treatment options, or their likely benefits and harms. Until then you make your own choices. The state forms say the same thing: they take effect "in the event I become unable to make my own health care decisions," as determined by the physician with primary responsibility for your care. For more, see [when a healthcare directive takes effect](/learn/healthcare-directives/when-does-a-healthcare-directive-take-effect).

## How do you change or revoke a directive?

You can revoke a directive, either part or both, by telling your representative, your physician, another health care professional or another reliable witness, or by an act that shows you mean to revoke it, or by signing a new directive (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-57). A reaffirmation or change must follow the same signing rules as the original, so sign a fresh copy with two new witnesses or a notary.

Revoking does not have to be formal, but it should be clear. Tell the doctor, tell the family, and collect old copies. See [how to change or revoke an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive).

## What is a POLST in New Jersey?

POLST stands for Physician/Practitioner Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment. It is not an advance directive. It is an actual medical order, completed with a physician or advance practice nurse, that becomes part of your medical record and is meant to be honored across settings, including at home, in a facility, or at the scene of an emergency. The state describes it as a tool for people facing serious illness or planning end-of-life care.

The POLST Act (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-129 and following, as amended in 2019) sets the rules. A form is complete and valid when it reflects the patient's preferences, is signed voluntarily by the patient with capacity, or by the patient's representative following the patient's known wishes or best interests, and is signed and dated by the attending physician, physician assistant or advance practice nurse. A patient with capacity can modify or revoke it at any time. A representative can change it only if the form authorized that and only after consulting the clinician.

A POLST complements a directive. It does not appoint an agent, so you may still need a proxy directive. Our article on [DNR orders and POLST](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst) shows how they fit together.

## What should your directive say about feeding tubes and CPR?

The state's proxy form asks you to initial one of two statements about artificially provided fluids and nutrition, such as a feeding tube or IV infusion. One says your representative may direct that they be withheld or withdrawn. The other says your representative lacks that authority and that they should be provided to preserve life, to the extent medically appropriate. The instruction directive form goes further, with choices about CPR and about conditions such as terminal illness or permanent unconsciousness.

These are the questions families fight about, so answer them on paper. Take the form home, read it aloud with your representative, and talk about the hard cases. A directive that is signed but never discussed leaves your representative guessing at the worst moment. If a mix of religious beliefs and medical standards matters to you, the instruction form has a separate line for objecting to the whole brain death standard. Add personal notes in the space the form provides, because a few sentences in your own words often help more than any checkbox.

## What happens without a directive?

Doctors turn to family, and relatives may disagree or may not know your wishes. If no one has authority and a dispute arises, a court case may follow.
