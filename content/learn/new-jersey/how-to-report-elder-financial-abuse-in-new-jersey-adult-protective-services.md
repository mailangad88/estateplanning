If you think someone is draining an older relative's accounts, the first call in New Jersey is to Adult Protective Services, known as APS. It runs through a local office in each county, not through one central desk. This page lists those offices, explains what happens after you call, and covers what a family can sign now so the problem is harder to start. It is one of our [New Jersey estate planning guides](/learn/new-jersey).

For the general warning signs and the common scams, see our national article on [protecting older adults from financial exploitation](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse). This page is about the New Jersey process.

## Who does New Jersey Adult Protective Services help?

APS helps adults who are 18 or older, live in the community, and are being abused, neglected or exploited. The State's fact sheet adds a second test: the adult cannot make, communicate or carry out decisions about their own well-being because of a physical or mental illness, disability or deficiency. Community means a private home or any setting that no state department licenses or regulates.

That second test surprises people. A sharp 84-year-old who is being talked into bad loans by a grandson may not fit it, even though the grandson is plainly taking advantage. APS still takes the call and decides. The State's own guidance is that you should report even if you are unsure. If the person is in immediate danger, call 911 or the local police first.

## What counts as financial exploitation?

The State's "Signs of Abuse" sheet describes financial exploitation as the misuse or mishandling of an adult's property, possessions or assets, including use without consent or through deception, coercion or manipulation. It lists these warning signs.

- Large withdrawals, or ATM and credit card use the person does not recognize.
- Missing checks or bank statements.
- Changed names or addresses on accounts.
- Purchases the person did not make or approve.
- New or forged signatures.
- Missing belongings.
- Changes to a financial power of attorney.
- Changed ownership of bank accounts or property deeds.

The last two matter more than families expect. A deed that quietly moves a house into a caregiver's name, or a new power of attorney signed by a parent with dementia, is a classic pattern. Our article on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) covers what an agent may not do.

## What should you say when you call?

Have these facts ready. You can still call without them.

1. The adult's name, address, and age or date of birth if you know it.
2. What you saw, with dates: the missing money, the new name on an account, the unpaid bills.
3. Who is involved and their relationship to the adult.
4. Any danger in the home, such as an unsafe person, no food or untreated illness.
5. Whether the adult would talk to a social worker, and whether they have a diagnosis such as dementia.

Write the dates down before you call. Memory goes soft quickly in family disputes, and your notes may later help a bank, an attorney or a court.

## What happens after you report?

The State describes the investigation as a thorough assessment of a vulnerable adult who is experiencing maltreatment. In order:

1. **Face-to-face meeting.** A trained APS social worker must meet the adult within 72 business hours of receiving the report.
2. **Private interview.** The social worker interviews the adult alone and assesses their decision-making ability.
3. **Other interviews.** People or agencies who know the situation may be interviewed.
4. **Finding.** The social worker reports to the APS Supervisor, who decides whether the adult is experiencing abuse, neglect or exploitation.
5. **Services.** APS recommends services and tries to connect the adult with them, using the least restrictive measures and keeping the adult at home where possible.

What APS cannot do matters just as much. Services are voluntary unless a court orders otherwise. APS cannot force help on an adult who has capacity and says no, cannot remove someone from their home without consent or a court order, and adults keep all their civil and constitutional rights unless a court has restricted them. Everything about the report is confidential.

Here is the part we see most. Dolores, 79, lives alone with early memory loss. Her nephew Paul "helps" with her bills and has been added to her checking account. A neighbor calls APS after seeing Paul carry out boxes. The social worker may confirm exploitation, but APS does not return the money. Dolores or someone acting for her would need the bank, the police, or a lawyer to pursue that. A report starts protection. It is not a recovery.

## What do you do besides calling APS?

Do the practical things the same day:

- Tell the bank or brokerage you suspect exploitation. Ask what they can freeze or flag, and read [how to freeze a parent's credit and add safeguards](/learn/elder-care/how-to-freeze-a-parents-credit-and-put-safeguards-on-their-accounts).
- File a police report if money or property is gone.
- Keep copies of statements, deeds and any new documents.
- Ask an attorney whether a signed power of attorney exists, whether it can be revoked, and whether the person still has capacity to sign a new one.

If a power of attorney exists and the agent is the problem, New Jersey law is on your side in one respect: the agent is a fiduciary and must keep accurate records (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.13). If there is no document, or the adult can no longer sign one, the remaining route may be a court case, described in our guide to [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey).

## How can a power of attorney or trust prevent exploitation?

Neither one stops a determined thief. Both make theft easier to spot and harder to excuse, which is most of what planning can do. A few drafting choices help.

| Safeguard | What it does | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Name a backup agent | Someone other than the first agent can step in if trust breaks down | Power of attorney |
| Say plainly whether gifts are allowed | The New Jersey Act does not address gifts, so silence invites arguments | Power of attorney |
| Require records and copies to a second person | The agent already must keep records; a named reviewer makes it real | Power of attorney |
| Split roles | One person pays bills, another oversees | Power of attorney or trust |
| Use a trustee and a successor trustee | Assets sit under written duties to inform beneficiaries | Living trust |
| Keep the original safe and limit copies | Fewer chances for a new document to appear | Both |

Our articles on [limiting an agent's powers and adding safeguards](/learn/power-of-attorney/limiting-an-agents-powers-and-adding-safeguards-in-a-power-of-attorney) and [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) explain the signing rules. For a trust, the New Jersey Uniform Trust Code requires a trustee to keep qualified beneficiaries reasonably informed, and we explain it in [trustee duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-trustee-duties-to-beneficiaries-under-the-uniform-trust-code) and [living trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey).

If you are the parent and you still have capacity, choose the agent who will let the bank call your daughter when something looks odd, not the one who resents questions. If you are the adult child, an uncomfortable conversation now is cheaper than a court file later.
