New Jersey is one of a handful of states with an inheritance tax. It is easy to confuse with an estate tax, and in New Jersey the two used to exist side by side. The estate tax is gone for deaths since 2018. The inheritance tax remains, and it can reach families who thought the state tax had been repealed.

The rule is simple to state. What you owe depends on how you were related to the person who died. This article covers who falls into which class, what the rates are, how the tax waivers work and what the executor has to do and when. It sits inside our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What is the difference between the estate tax and the inheritance tax?

An estate tax is paid by the estate, based on its total size. An inheritance tax is paid by each person who receives property, based on who they are. New Jersey had both until recently. The Division of Taxation says that for deaths on or after January 1, 2018, no New Jersey estate tax applies and no estate tax return is required.

For a death in 2017 the estate tax applied to estates over $2 million, and for earlier deaths from 2002 through 2016 it applied above $675,000 (Division of Taxation). Those years only matter if you are closing out an older estate.

The inheritance tax never went away. Our general article on [estate tax versus inheritance tax](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-vs-inheritance-tax) explains the difference, and [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) shows how other states compare. The federal estate tax is a separate system, with a 2026 exclusion of $15,000,000 per person; see [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

## Who is in each class?

The Division of Taxation sorts beneficiaries into classes by relationship.

| Class | Who | Tax |
|---|---|---|
| A | Spouse, civil union partner, domestic partner, children (including adopted and mutually acknowledged), stepchildren, grandchildren and further descendants, parents, grandparents | None |
| C | Brothers and sisters, spouses or civil union partners of the decedent's children | 11% to 16% after $25,000 |
| D | Anyone not in A, C or E, such as nieces, nephews, friends and unmarried partners without a registered status | 15% or 16% |
| E | Qualified charities, religious, educational and medical institutions, nonprofit benevolent or scientific organizations, the State of New Jersey and its political subdivisions | None |

Some details matter. Step-grandchildren are not in Class A. Civil union partners count as Class A for relationships formed after February 19, 2007, and domestic partners for relationships formed after July 10, 2004, as the Division's class chart lists them. There is no Class B; the Division says it was eliminated in 1963.

The consequence is easy to miss. A daughter-in-law is Class C. A longtime friend is Class D. Lina, 71, wants to leave $100,000 to the neighbor who drove her to appointments for a decade. That gift is taxable in Class D.

## What are the rates?

The tax is figured per beneficiary, on what that person receives. The Division publishes these rates:

| Class | Amount received | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| A | Any | No tax |
| C | First $25,000 | No tax |
| C | Next $1,075,000 | 11% |
| C | Next $300,000 | 13% |
| C | Next $300,000 | 14% |
| C | Over $1,700,000 | 16% |
| D | First $700,000 | 15% |
| D | Over $700,000 | 16% |

Run two examples. A sister receives $200,000. The first $25,000 is free, and 11% of the remaining $175,000 is $19,250. A friend receives $100,000 and owes 15%, which is $15,000. A niece who receives $800,000 pays 15% on $700,000 ($105,000) and 16% on $100,000 ($16,000), a total of $121,000.

The Division's booklet adds that no inheritance tax applies to transfers to a beneficiary whose total from the decedent is under $500. Check the current instructions before relying on that.

## What property is taxed, and what is not?

The rules follow the nature of the asset. According to the Division's general information booklet:

- Life insurance paid to a named beneficiary is exempt from inheritance tax.
- Real property held by spouses or civil union partners as tenants by the entirety need not be reported in the estate of the first to die.
- Payments from New Jersey public retirement systems are among those the booklet lists as exempt.
- Vehicles, household goods, accrued wages and mortgages do not need a waiver but must be reported.

For a resident, the return covers New Jersey real and tangible property and all intangible property such as bank accounts and stock. For a non-resident, it covers New Jersey real and tangible property. A non-resident decedent who owns a New Jersey vacation house leaves a New Jersey filing even though the person never lived here.

Our article on [beneficiary designations and life insurance](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) shows why who is named matters for tax as well as for control.

## When is the return due?

The executor, administrator or heir-at-law must file the return, if one is required, within eight months of death, and any tax due must be paid in that same eight months. Unpaid tax accrues interest at 10% a year. The Division says there is no extension of time to pay. Form IT-EXT extends only the filing deadline, and returns are filed on paper.

A return is required if tax is due or if property passes to anyone other than Class A beneficiaries (Division of Taxation general information booklet). Resident decedents use Form IT-R. Non-residents use Form IT-NR.

This is a hard deadline sitting inside a slow process. The executor is appointed by the Surrogate after the tenth day, and the first weeks go to paperwork. Read the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) and mark the eight-month date on the same calendar as the 60-day notice to heirs.

## What are the tax waivers, and what do L-8 and L-9 do?

A tax waiver is the Division's permission for a bank, broker or title company to release assets. Without one, institutions may freeze an account or refuse a deed. Two forms handle the simplest cases.

1. **Form L-8** is a self-executing waiver affidavit for a resident decedent. The Division's executor guide says it is used only when no inheritance or estate tax is due. It covers bank accounts, stocks, bonds and brokerage accounts passing to Class A beneficiaries, and it cannot be used for real estate. It is sent to the financial institution, not to the Division. The form itself tells filers not to send it to the Division.
2. **Form L-9** requests a real property tax waiver for a resident decedent when the entire estate is untaxable. It goes to the Division's Inheritance and Estate Tax Branch. A separate version, L-9 NR, is used for non-residents.

When there is any taxable beneficiary, the executor files the full return and the Division issues waivers after it reviews the return. Real estate transfers always need a waiver, the executor guide says.

Take Marcus, who dies with a checking account and a house, leaving both to his two adult daughters. The executor sends Form L-8 to the bank and files Form L-9 for the house. No tax is due, and the filing is mostly paperwork. If Marcus had left $60,000 of that to a friend, the executor would file the full return.

## How can you plan around the tax?

Planning starts with the class chart. A few ideas that attorneys discuss, none of which fits everyone:

- **Name beneficiaries deliberately.** Life insurance paid to a named beneficiary is exempt, so it can be a clean way to leave something to a friend or a niece.
- **Leave to charity.** Class E gifts are exempt.
- **Review unmarried partner plans.** A partner who has no registered status is Class D, so the gap between a registered partnership and none is large.
- **Think about timing.** The tax is figured on the date-of-death value, so gifts made during life are a conversation to have with an attorney, not a rule of thumb.

We have not covered lifetime gifts, because the Division sources we reviewed do not say how they interact with the tax. Ask before you act.
