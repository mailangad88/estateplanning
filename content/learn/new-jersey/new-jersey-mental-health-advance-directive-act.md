Marcus lives in Camden County and has bipolar disorder. Twice, a manic episode has ended in the emergency room, and twice he was given a medication that made things worse. The one that works for him went unmentioned because nobody asked. He wants his sister to speak for him next time, and he wants his preferences on paper before then.

New Jersey has a law for exactly that: the Advance Directives for Mental Health Care Act, found in the state's health statutes starting at N.J.S.A. 26:2H-102. This article covers how the Act works in New Jersey. For the general idea and the national picture, see our [mental health advance directive overview](/learn/healthcare-directives/mental-health-advance-directive), and for the everyday directive see [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives). Our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey) shows where this fits among the other documents.

## What is the New Jersey Advance Directives for Mental Health Care Act?

It is a 2005 law (P.L. 2005, c. 233) that lets an adult with capacity write down mental health treatment choices for a time when they cannot make them. The Legislature's own findings say people living with mental illness needed a statute of their own because their civil rights and due process protections were often compromised, and because doctors and lawyers did not always understand their particular concerns.

The Act recognizes two kinds of document, and you can use either or both:

- A **proxy directive** names a mental health care representative, plus alternates.
- An **instruction directive** states your wishes about treatment.


## What can a mental health directive cover?

An instruction directive can state a general philosophy of care or get specific. The statute lists examples: preferred providers or facilities, consent to admission for a set number of days, refusal of particular treatments such as certain medications, preferred medications, and your preferences for crisis intervention. It also allows other instructions.

Here is how that might look for Marcus.

| Topic | What Marcus writes |
|---|---|
| Medication | Prefers the mood stabilizer he took for six years; refuses the antipsychotic that left him unable to work |
| Facility | Names the hospital whose staff know him; asks not to be transferred to a facility outside his county unless no bed exists |
| Crisis response | Asks that his sister be called first, and that police not be the first contact if a clinician can come |
| Admission | Consents to a stay of up to ten days; anything longer goes back to his representative |
| People to consult | His sister must talk with his psychiatrist before agreeing to any new medication |

A video or audio recording may supplement the written directive, but not replace the signed paper.

## Who can be a mental health care representative?

Any adult with the mental capacity to make these decisions: a family member, friend, spiritual adviser or anyone else you choose (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-107). You can name alternates in priority order, direct the representative to consult particular people, and limit the representative's authority.

Three rules matter in practice.

1. **Facility staff are mostly excluded.** Staff of a psychiatric facility where you are a patient or resident generally cannot serve, unless they are related to you by blood, marriage, domestic partnership, civil union or adoption.
2. **Spouse designations can lapse.** Unless your directive says otherwise, naming a spouse is revoked by divorce or legal separation, and a domestic partner or civil union partner designation ends when the partnership or union ends (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-106). Name an alternate.
3. **Nobody has to say yes.** A person you name can decline to serve (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-110). Ask before you write the name down. Our piece on [who should be your health care agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent) covers how to choose.

## Can my representative consent to a psychiatric admission?

Only if you said so, and only if you initialed it. If you authorize your representative to consent to admission to a psychiatric facility, each paragraph granting that power must be separately initialed when you sign and the witness signs (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-107). That is a deliberate speed bump, and a good one.

Even then, the professional cannot simply admit you on the representative's say-so. Before admitting, the professional must review your psychiatric history, diagnosis, need for care and stated wishes, and determine in writing that inpatient care is needed and cannot be provided in a less restrictive setting (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-112). If you want your representative to have this power, say it in plain words and consider setting the limits Marcus did, such as a number of days.

## How do you sign a New Jersey mental health directive?

You sign and date it, or direct someone to sign for you, in front of at least one adult witness who attests that you are of sound mind and free from duress and undue influence (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-105). This is lighter than the general New Jersey directive, which calls for two witnesses or a notary. Do not assume the two rules are the same, and follow whichever law your document is written under.

Who can witness is restricted.

- Your representative cannot be a witness.
- The responsible mental health care professional treating you at the time cannot be a witness.
- A person cannot be the only witness if they are related to you by blood, marriage or adoption, are your domestic partner, share your home, or are entitled to part of your estate.
- The same applies to someone who runs or works for a rooming house, boarding house or residential health care facility where you live.

If you want extra assurance, use more than one unrelated witness. It costs nothing.

## When does the directive take effect?

It becomes operative when it is transmitted to your responsible mental health care professional or the psychiatric facility and you are determined to lack capacity for a particular decision. You can also name an earlier date in the document (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-108). Transmitted is the key word. A directive in a drawer helps nobody, so give copies to your psychiatrist, your preferred hospital and your representative.

Capacity is judged one decision at a time, not once for all purposes. Under N.J.S.A. 26:2H-109:

1. Your responsible mental health care professional makes the first determination, in writing, stating the nature, cause, extent and likely duration of the incapacity.
2. One or more other mental health care professionals must confirm it, also in writing.
3. You and your representative must be told about the finding and that each of you has the right to contest it.
4. Your representative cannot make this determination.

The finding counts only for carrying out the directive. It is not a finding of incapacity for any other purpose, so it does not create a guardianship. If you regain capacity for a decision, the decision is yours again, and the representative may stay on in an advisory role unless you object (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-112). Treatment decisions also cannot be carried out until there has been a reasonable opportunity to establish a reliable diagnosis and prognosis (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-108).

## Can a doctor ignore a mental health directive?

Only on narrow grounds. A responsible professional may depart from your instructions, or from your representative's decision, only if following them would (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-110, 26:2H-113):

- violate the accepted standard of care for your condition,
- require a treatment the professional cannot provide,
- violate a court order or a statute, or
- endanger the life or health of you or someone else.

The Act also leaves existing court orders and commitment laws in place, and if a court order or another statute knocks out part of your directive, the rest stays valid (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-116). In plain terms: the directive guides treatment and carries real weight, but it does not override a commitment order.

If you and your representative disagree with the professional, the facility's dispute process or a court can decide (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-115). Vague wishes are the easiest to set aside, so be specific.

## How is it different from a general health care proxy?

The general directive deals with medical care and end-of-life choices, and it takes effect when a physician finds you cannot decide. The mental health directive has its own capacity test, a confirming opinion, a separate signing rule, and a special initialing requirement for admission.

| Question | General New Jersey directive | Mental health directive |
|---|---|---|
| Governing law | N.J.S.A. 26:2H-53 and following | N.J.S.A. 26:2H-102 and following |
| Signing | Two adult witnesses or a notary | At least one adult witness |
| Who decides you lack capacity | Physician with primary responsibility | Responsible mental health care professional, confirmed by another |
| Psychiatric admission | Not the focus | Representative needs express, initialed authority |
| Typical use | Life support, CPR, feeding tubes | Medications, facilities, crisis response |

Most people with mental health concerns should have both, plus a [power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) for money and a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so the people you trust can talk to your providers. The two directives should agree with each other. A general proxy naming your brother and a mental health proxy naming your sister will confuse everyone in a crisis, so name the same person or explain the split in both documents.

## What happens without one?

Without a directive, family members and providers decide, and the decision-maker may not know your history. If no one can legally act, a court may appoint a guardian, which is a heavier, slower and more public step. Our article on [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) explains that process, and [who makes medical decisions without a health care proxy](/learn/new-jersey/who-makes-medical-decisions-in-new-jersey-without-a-health-care-proxy) covers the default rules for ordinary health care. If a guardian has already been appointed, your representative keeps authority over mental health decisions unless a court order says otherwise (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-110).

Professionals must ask about directives and chart them, and facilities must have policies for routinely asking patients (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-111, 26:2H-114). Do not rely on that. Hand the document over yourself and ask that it be attached to your record.

## How do you change or cancel one?

You can reaffirm, change, revoke or suspend a directive at any time, subject to the inpatient rule below. Reaffirming or changing it follows the same signing rules as the original. Revoking can be done by telling your representative, your professional or another reliable witness, orally or in writing, or by any act showing you mean it, and signing a later directive also revokes the earlier one (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-105, 26:2H-106). There is a rule for patients in a psychiatric facility: they can modify, revoke or suspend by notifying the representative or professional, unless the responsible professional finds they lack capacity for that decision. For the general steps, see our guide to [changing or revoking an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive).

Review the document each year, and after any change in medication, diagnosis or the people involved.
