People often ask two things at the same meeting. What can an executor be paid, and when will this be over? In New Jersey the first question has a clear statutory answer. The second depends on the clocks the law sets, the Division of Taxation, and how orderly the paperwork is.

This page gives the commission schedule with worked examples, then the dates that shape a timeline. It sits within our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). For the steps themselves, see the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process).

## What commissions can a New Jersey executor take?

Commissions are set by statute, in two parts: one for estate property, called corpus, and one for income the estate earns.

| Component | Rate | Statute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corpus, first $200,000 | 5% | N.J.S.A. 3B:18-14 |
| Corpus, over $200,000 up to $1,000,000 | 3.5% | N.J.S.A. 3B:18-14 |
| Corpus, over $1,000,000 | 2% | N.J.S.A. 3B:18-14 |
| Each additional fiduciary | 1% of all corpus, capped so no one gets more than a sole fiduciary | N.J.S.A. 3B:18-14 |
| Income | 6% on all income | N.J.S.A. 3B:18-13 |

The section says commissions "may be taken," which makes the schedule the normal fee rather than a ceiling that needs a judge's blessing. The Legislature amended 3B:18-14 for that reason: a fee computed under the schedule is the ordinary one, and a court steps in only if a beneficiary objects.

### Worked examples

**A $500,000 estate.** Marcus is the sole executor. Five percent of the first $200,000 is $10,000. Then 3.5% of the next $300,000 is $10,500. His corpus commission is $20,500. If the estate also earns $10,000 of interest and dividends while it is open, income commission at 6% adds $600.

**A $1,500,000 estate.** Five percent of $200,000 is $10,000. Then 3.5% of $800,000 is $28,000. Finally 2% of the last $500,000 is $10,000. The corpus commission is $48,000.

Real accountings are rarely this clean. What counts as corpus, and how real estate is valued, are questions for the executor's attorney. The point of the arithmetic is to show that for most estates commissions run to a few percent of the whole, not the large slice some families fear.

## Should the executor take the commission?

Often the answer is no, and that surprises people. The executor chooses. Many executors who are also heirs skip the fee, since commissions are paid out of the estate and are income to the person who receives them, while an inheritance generally is not. An accountant should look at this case by case. A professional executor, or a relative doing months of work for siblings who live far away, may reasonably want to be paid.

Fairness helps. If one child will run the estate for a year, say in the will or in a family meeting whether commissions will be taken. Surprises create fights. Our article on [what an executor may and may not take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate) covers reimbursements and gray areas, and [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) lists the work the fee is supposed to cover.

### When can a court cut the commission?

Only on application by an adversely affected beneficiary. That person must show that the services were materially deficient, or that the executor's pains, trouble and risk were substantially less than usually required (N.J.S.A. 3B:18-14). An estate with many beneficiaries should expect questions if the executor did little and still takes the full schedule.

## How long does a New Jersey estate take?

There is no single statutory deadline for finishing. What the law sets are checkpoints. Together they put a rough floor under most estates.

| Date or period | What happens | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Death | Nine-month creditor clock starts | N.J.S.A. 3B:22-4 |
| Within weeks | Will offered for probate, executor qualifies at the county Surrogate | See our [probate guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) |
| 8 months | Inheritance Tax return due and any tax payable, where a return is required | NJ Division of Taxation |
| 9 months | Creditors' window to present claims closes | N.J.S.A. 3B:22-4 |
| After return is filed | Division processing takes "at least several months," longer if audited | NJ Division of Taxation guide |

### What do those dates mean in practice?

A simple estate with a will, one house, a few accounts and no tax due may close in under a year, because the nine-month window is the longest fixed wait. An estate where tax is due, real estate must be sold, or a beneficiary is contesting things will run longer. The Division of Taxation notes that processing a filed return takes at least several months, that audits add several months more, and that returns are handled in the order received.

Tax waivers also gate the work. Banks may release part of an account early. The Division's guide says financial institutions may release up to 50% of funds on hand before a waiver is received, but only to the executor, administrator or a joint owner. Real estate transfers need a waiver from the Division. Plan for the paperwork by reading [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

## How long do creditors have to make a claim?

Nine months from the date of death. The Legislature lengthened the period from six to nine months in 2004 and removed the old requirement to publish a notice to creditors. So a family can count the window straight from the date on the death certificate.

That date drives executor behavior. An executor who pays beneficiaries in month three has no certainty that a medical bill, a credit card or a tax claim will not arrive in month eight. Executors who hold back a reserve until the window closes sleep better. Our guide to the [probate creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) explains the general approach, and [New Jersey's small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) covers a lighter path for modest estates.

## What slows an estate down?

Most delays have ordinary causes. A house must be cleaned out, appraised and sold. A bank wants a specific form. A brother in another state does not answer email. A relative decides to contest the will, which stops distribution until the dispute is resolved. Missing paperwork is the commonest cause: an executor who cannot find account statements spends weeks writing letters to banks.

You can speed things up with a short routine. Order several certified death certificates at the start. Open an estate account early and run every payment through it. Keep a running list of assets, debts and dates. File the tax paperwork the day you have the numbers, because the Division handles returns in the order received. And tell beneficiaries what to expect, because silence breeds suspicion. Our [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) article has a fuller checklist.

## What tax dates apply?

- **Inheritance Tax.** Where a return is required, it is due within eight months of death, and the tax is due in the same period. Unpaid tax accrues interest at 10% a year. You may ask for more time to file with Form IT-EXT, but not to pay.
- **Estate Tax.** For deaths on or after January 1, 2018, no New Jersey Estate Tax return is required.
- **Federal.** The federal exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families owe no federal estate tax.
