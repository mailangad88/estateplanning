Maybe you are the person named in your mother's will and you cannot imagine doing the job. Maybe you are a beneficiary and the executor has been silent for six months. Either way the same question comes up: what happens in New Jersey when the executor in the will will not, or cannot, act?

The answer depends on timing. Before the executor qualifies, the executor can say no. After qualifying, the exits are narrower. We lay out each stage here, and link the neighboring pages for the details. The sequence for an executor who does serve is in [executor duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step), and the state overview is in the [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Can a named executor say no?

Yes. Naming someone in a will does not draft them. Before the Surrogate issues letters, the person can decline, and the decision should be in writing on whatever form the county wants. The statutes we cite here do not set out the wording, so call the Surrogate's office for its current paperwork rather than writing your own letter.

Declining usually costs nothing. If you are also a beneficiary, you still take your gift. Refusing the job and refusing the property are two separate acts, and the second is a disclaimer, which we cover in [disclaiming an inheritance in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-new-jersey). Decline early and decline cleanly, because the family cannot move until someone is appointed.

Consider Helen, who is named executor for her uncle. She lives in Oregon, works long hours and has never met his lawyer. She declines in writing within the first few weeks. The will names her cousin Dev as the backup, and he applies.

## Who takes over if the named person does not serve?

In this order:

1. **The backup named in the will.** A good will names one or two. This is the cleanest outcome, and the reason our article on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) tells people to name successors.
2. **An administrator with the will annexed.** When no named executor will act, the Surrogate's court or the Superior Court grants letters of administration with the will annexed (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-17). The administrator carries out the will, because the decedent's wishes still control (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-16).
3. **A substitute, if an executor drops out partway.** If the sole executor, or the last remaining one, dies or is removed or discharged after starting, the court appoints a fit person as substituted administrator to finish the work (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-15 and 3B:10-17).

The statutes cited here do not rank who gets first claim on a with-the-will-annexed appointment, so ask the Surrogate how the county decides. For the without-a-will version, see [administering a New Jersey estate without a will](/learn/new-jersey/administering-a-new-jersey-estate-without-a-will).

## Does the replacement need a bond?

Usually yes. The bond statute requires the court or Surrogate to demand a bond when the appointee replaces a person the will named, when a substitute or additional fiduciary is appointed, and for any form of administration (N.J.S.A. 3B:15-1). The original executor could be excused from bond by the will, but the replacement was not chosen by the person who wrote it. The amount is set by the court based on the estate and the fiduciary's authority.

| Situation | What replaces the executor | Bond |
|---|---|---|
| Named executor declines, will names a backup | The backup, who qualifies as executor | Depends on the will's bond waiver |
| Nobody named will serve | Administrator with the will annexed | Generally required |
| Sole executor dies mid-estate | Substituted administrator | Generally required |
| Executor is removed or discharged | Substituted administrator | Generally required |

## What if the executor already qualified and wants out?

A person who has been appointed and then finds they cannot do it has a narrow door. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:10-30.1, a personal representative who is unwilling or unable to continue may ask the Surrogate for a discharge if every party in interest consents. The paper must include notarized consents from every party, a statement that the discharge will not impair the rights of parties or creditors, and an application from the proposed successor. A final account is generally required too, unless every party waives it in writing, and an unpaid creditor can still ask the Superior Court for an accounting. The executor can alternatively file a discharge action in the Superior Court.

Three consequences follow. The executor is discharged and letters are revoked. All money and assets go to the successor. And the executor gets no statutory commissions for the time served. The sureties on the bond are not released until a final judgment on the account, unless the account was waived.

A single holdout beneficiary can therefore keep a tired executor in place, which is a reason to talk to the family before accepting the role.

## What can heirs do about an executor who will not act?

First ask in writing for an update, and keep a copy. Many silent executors are overwhelmed, not dishonest, and a polite request for an inventory and a timetable often restarts things. Our article on [what to do when an executor is not doing the job](/learn/probate/executor-not-doing-their-job) lists practical steps.

If that fails, the court has power. N.J.S.A. 3B:14-21 lets it remove a fiduciary in six situations.

- Ignoring a court order to file an inventory, render an account or post security.
- Ignoring any other court order.
- Embezzling, wasting or misusing estate property.
- No longer living or keeping an office in New Jersey, and neglecting the estate.
- Being incapacitated from conducting business.
- Neglecting duties as a co-fiduciary, or refusing to act jointly, so the estate is held up.

The statute does not say who may apply, so confirm that with an attorney before filing. Removal is a court case, and the judge wants specifics: dates, missing accounts and unanswered letters. Our general article on [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) explains how courts view these requests, and a caveat may be the quicker tool if the appointment has not yet happened, as described in [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats).

## A note on timing and the other people involved

Do not let the question sit while the estate bleeds. A house with no one authorized to act can go without insurance, the lawn, the heat and the mortgage payment, and a vacant property is the first thing to go wrong. If the delay is causing a loss, say so to the Surrogate, because the court can appoint a limited administrator for an emergency (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-12).

Tell the other beneficiaries what is happening, too. A short, honest email that says the executor has declined, the backup has been asked and a date for the next step saves a great deal of suspicion. Silence is what turns a paperwork problem into a family fight. If a will contest is also brewing, read [New Jersey will contests and caveats](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats) before anyone files anything. And if the reason the executor cannot serve is illness or age, a [power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) for the executor's own affairs is worth a conversation.
