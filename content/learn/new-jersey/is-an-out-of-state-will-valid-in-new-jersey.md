You moved to New Jersey with a will signed years ago in Ohio, Florida or Illinois. It sits in a folder, and you wonder if it still counts. The short answer is that it very likely does, and that the better question is whether it still fits your life here.

This page covers the statute that protects an out-of-state will, what the Surrogate looks for at probate, and a checklist of what to update after a move. For a general version that is not about New Jersey, see our article on [wills when moving states or living abroad](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad). The rest of the state's rules are in the [New Jersey guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Does New Jersey accept a will signed in another state?

Yes, in most cases, because of N.J.S.A. 3B:3-9. A written will is validly executed if it meets any one of three tests. It complies with New Jersey's own statutes (3B:3-2 or 3B:3-3). Or it complies with the law of the place where it was signed. Or it complies with the law of a place where the testator was domiciled, had an abode or was a national. The domicile test applies at signing or at death.

Notice the shape of the rule. A will made in Pennsylvania by a Pennsylvania resident follows Pennsylvania's law, and that is enough. The same will is not rejected because the maker now lives in Morris County. The other side of the coin also holds: a will signed in New Jersey for a person who later moves away is generally accepted elsewhere, a point our wills pillar and the [moved-states scenario](/learn/what-if/moved-states-plan-not-updated) explain.

## What does the Surrogate need at probate?

The same thing it needs for any will: the original, a death certificate, and proof that the will was properly signed. A resident's will is probated in the Surrogate's court of the county or in the Superior Court (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-24), and a will probated only in another state has no effect here until it is probated in New Jersey. Our [probate process article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) walks through the steps.

The wrinkle is the affidavit. A New Jersey self-proving will uses an acknowledgment by the maker and affidavits by the witnesses (3B:3-4). If your Ohio will has an Ohio-style affidavit, the Surrogate may accept it or may call for a witness to prove the signing. That is a county and attorney question, and it is exactly the sort of delay a quick re-signing avoids. For the basics on signing, see [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) and [what a self-proving affidavit does](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained).

Real estate you kept in your old state is another matter, covered below.

## What should you update after moving to New Jersey?

Think of the will as valid but possibly mismatched. Here is the list we use.

| Item | Why it may need a change in New Jersey |
|---|---|
| Executor | The person you named may live far away or may have died. Ask whether they can serve here and whether a local backup would help. |
| Guardian for children | The guardian you named years ago may have moved. New Jersey's rules for naming one are in [our guardian naming article](/learn/new-jersey/naming-a-guardian-for-your-children-in-new-jersey). |
| Spouse or partner | The elective share (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-1) gives a surviving spouse or partner a one-third claim to the augmented estate regardless of the will. See [New Jersey elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse). |
| Beneficiaries | Gifts to siblings, nieces, friends and others may carry New Jersey inheritance tax, which does not apply to spouses, children, parents and grandchildren. See [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax). |
| Property in the old state | A house or land kept there may need a second probate. See [out-of-state property and ancillary probate](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-out-of-state-property-and-ancillary-probate). |
| Power of attorney and advance directive | Local banks and hospitals are more comfortable with New Jersey forms. See [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) and [advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives). |
| Beneficiary forms | IRAs, life insurance and work plans pass outside the will. Check each one with the [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit). |

The elective share row is the one that surprises people from states with different spousal rules. If your children from a first marriage were meant to receive most of your estate, a will written for a different state may leave your current spouse with more rights here than you intended. Describing the result in the will, or in a marital agreement, is a job for an attorney.

## A realistic example

Teresa moved from Florida to Bergen County in 2025 after her retirement. Her will, signed in Florida in 2018, leaves her house and savings to her two children and names her brother in Tampa as executor. She also owns a small condominium in Florida.

The will is valid in New Jersey. But her brother is 79 and lives 1,000 miles from the Bergen County Surrogate, her new husband has an elective share claim she never planned for, and the condo needs its own filing in Florida. Teresa visits an attorney, who adds a New Jersey backup executor, rechecks the family picture, replaces the old affidavit with a New Jersey one, and moves the condo into a trust so that no second probate is required. None of that involved re-writing a lawful will from scratch.

## When should you update the will?

Within a few months of becoming a New Jersey resident, and then at the usual life events. The rule of thumb in our article on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) applies here: marriage, divorce, a birth, a death in the family, a major sale or a new business. A divorce is worth a special warning because New Jersey automatically revokes gifts and roles for a former spouse unless the documents say otherwise (N.J.S.A. 3B:3-14). See [how divorce affects a New Jersey will](/learn/new-jersey/how-divorce-affects-a-new-jersey-will-trust-power-of-attorney-and-beneficiary-forms).

To change a will, you sign a codicil or a new will with the same formalities. Do not mark up the old one. Our page on [changing or revoking a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-new-jersey) covers what works.

## Should you make a new will or just a codicil?

For a will that is short and still mostly right, a codicil can fix a name. After a move, a new will is usually cleaner, because it can also swap the affidavit, adjust for the spousal rights and drop references to your old state's courts and statutes. A new will that says it revokes all earlier wills ends the question. Keep the old original until the new one is signed, then follow your attorney's advice on what to do with it.
