Marcus opened his late father's closet in Toms River and found a shotgun, a revolver and a box of ammunition. He has no permit of any kind. His brother wants the revolver. A cousin says the police must be called. Everyone is guessing.

If you are asking whether you can inherit a gun in New Jersey, the short answer is yes, and without the purchase permit that a buyer would need. The harder questions are who qualifies to hold it, what the executor does in the meantime, and what to do when the right heir cannot own a gun. This page covers those, with the federal basics left to our general article on [firearms in estate planning](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning). The state overview is our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## The short version

- An heir or legatee does not need a permit to purchase or a firearms purchaser identification card to receive the gun. The statute is N.J.S.A. 2C:58-3(j).
- That exemption does not make a disqualified person eligible. Disqualified heirs have a 180-day route to sell.
- The executor locks up the guns, lists them and waits. Nobody takes a gun home on the day of the funeral.
- Buying ammunition for an inherited handgun is a separate step with its own card or permit rule.

## Do you need a permit to inherit a gun in New Jersey?

No, not to receive it as an heir or legatee. New Jersey's gun law requires a buyer of a handgun to hold a permit to purchase, which is good for 90 days from issue, and a buyer of a rifle or shotgun to hold a firearms purchaser identification card and show it to the seller. Subsection (j) of the same section carves out gifts at death. A permit or card is "not required" when the gun passes to an heir or legatee, whether by testamentary bequest or under the intestacy laws. The heir must still comply with the rest of the chapter.

Two limits matter. The first is that a will leaving the gun to a friend works the same way, since a legatee is whoever the will names. The second is that the exemption is for the transfer. Marcus can take the shotgun without a card. If he later wants to buy a second one, he needs the card.

### What does it cost to get a card or permit anyway?

The statute sets $25 for a handgun purchase permit and $50 for a firearms purchaser identification card, and it requires the police chief or the Superintendent to act within 30 days on a complete application from a New Jersey resident (45 days for a nonresident). Fees and processing change, so confirm with the State Police firearms unit or your local department before you apply.

## Who cannot receive a gun in New Jersey?

Anyone the statute disqualifies. The list in subsection (c) is long. It includes certain criminal convictions and confinement or involuntary commitment for a mental disorder. It also covers a physical defect or substance use disorder that would make handling a gun unsafe, and age: under 18 for a card, under 21 for a handgun permit. Restraining orders, extreme risk protective orders, fugitive status and a terrorist watchlist listing are on it too. An adult child with a past conviction, a minor grandchild and an heir under a protective order are the common examples in estates.

For the minor, the practical answer is to hold the gun until adulthood or to sell it. For the adult who is disqualified, subsection (j) gives a way out. The heir may keep ownership for the purpose of sale for up to 180 days, longer if the chief law enforcement officer of the municipality or the Superintendent approves, and the gun stays in that officer's custody throughout. The sale proceeds then belong to the heir, which is a cleaner outcome than a quiet handoff to a relative.

## What should the executor do with the guns meanwhile?

Treat them as the most dangerous and most legally exposed items in the house. The executor's job is to protect the estate's property, and a firearm is also a public safety problem. Follow this order.

1. **Do not move or hand over anything.** The unlawful-possession statute (N.J.S.A. 2C:39-6) lists many exemptions, from hunters to dealers to law enforcement, and none of them mentions an executor or administrator. Do not assume your letters from the Surrogate protect you if you carry guns around in a car.
2. **Secure the house.** Check where each gun is. If there are children or visitors in the home, lock the guns in a safe or a locked cabinet that the executor controls, and keep the key.
3. **Make a list.** Write down make, model, caliber and serial number for each gun, and photograph each one. Add ammunition, holsters, scopes and gun cases. This list goes in the inventory you will file later with the estate.
4. **Find paperwork.** Look for purchase receipts, old permits and cards, and any will language that leaves the guns to a named person.
5. **Check each heir's eligibility.** Ask the intended recipient plainly whether anything in subsection (c) applies. If it does, go to the 180-day sale route above.
6. **Call the local police for anything that worries you.** An unloaded gun in a locked safe is not an emergency. A loaded gun in a vacant house, or a collection that nobody can account for, is. Call the non-emergency line first and ask how they want to take custody.
7. **Value them.** Guns are tangible personal property and need a fair market value for the inventory. A licensed dealer or appraiser can value a collection. Ordinary stepped-up basis rules apply to sales; see [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

Executors who have worked through the rest of the estate will recognize the pattern. [Executor duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step) walks through letters, inventory and distribution, and firearms are one more item on the inventory. They are just the item you cannot hand out casually.

## How does a handgun heir get ammunition?

Handgun ammunition has its own rule. N.J.S.A. 2C:58-3.3 requires a buyer or recipient of handgun ammunition to hold a valid firearms purchaser identification card or a handgun permit, with photo ID, and a violation is a fourth-degree crime. So Marcus, who inherits the revolver with no permit, can take the revolver but should not assume the box of cartridges came with it. Ask an attorney or the State Police before taking handgun ammunition from the house. Ammunition for rifles and shotguns alone is not covered by that section.

## What about antiques, collections and federally regulated guns?

Federal law adds a layer. Items regulated under the National Firearms Act, such as machine guns, suppressors and short-barreled rifles, need federal approval to transfer, even to an heir. The same is true of any heir who is a federally prohibited person. If the collection includes anything of that kind, stop and get legal advice before anyone touches it. A mistaken handoff can create a legal problem for the person who receives the item, however innocent the intent.

Antique firearms are treated differently under the permit statute, which exempts some antiques from the card requirement for a purchase. Whether a particular gun is an antique in the legal sense is a technical question, so do not decide that from the date stamped on the barrel.

## What if the heir lives in another state?

Then the other state's law governs what the heir can hold at home, and federal rules apply to moving a gun across state lines, so nobody should simply mail or drive one over on the strength of an inheritance. The New Jersey exemption removes the New Jersey permit requirement, nothing more. A nonresident heir should check their own state and federal rules first, and in many cases the better answer is to sell through a licensed dealer and send the money. Our article on [property in two states](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-out-of-state-property-and-ancillary-probate) covers the court side for other types of assets.

## Can you plan ahead so heirs are not stuck?

Yes, and it is worth doing if you own more than a couple of guns. State in your will who gets each gun or that the executor may sell them through a licensed dealer. Name a backup. List serial numbers on a separate schedule that your executor can find. If a likely heir is under 21, has a record or is out of state, say what happens then.
