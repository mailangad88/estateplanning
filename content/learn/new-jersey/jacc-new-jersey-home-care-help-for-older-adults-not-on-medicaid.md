Eleanor, 78, lives alone in Morris County. Her Social Security and a small pension bring in about $3,900 a month, and she has roughly $35,000 saved. She needs help bathing and getting in and out of her chair, and a friend told her to "just go on Medicaid." She cannot. Her income is over the Medicaid line and her savings are over the $2,000 resource limit.

New Jersey has a program for people in exactly her spot, and most families have never heard of it. It is called Jersey Assistance for Community Caregiving, or JACC. This article covers JACC and how it sits beside Medicaid. The Medicaid side is in our articles on [New Jersey Medicaid and nursing home planning](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning) and [Medicaid for assisted living and home care](/learn/new-jersey/does-new-jersey-medicaid-pay-for-assisted-living-and-home-care), and the wider picture is in our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). Figures here come from the state's 2026 program pages and may change, so confirm them when you apply.

## What is JACC?

JACC is a state-funded program that helps older adults stay in their own homes instead of moving to a nursing home. It is run through the Division of Aging Services. It is not Medicaid, and the state describes it as serving people who are not enrolled in Medicaid programs such as NJ FamilyCare or Medicaid MLTSS.

Every participant gets care management and a plan of care. The plan can draw on homecare, adult day health and social adult day care, home-delivered meals, chore services, a personal emergency response system, respite care and transportation. It can also pay for home modifications for accessibility and for medical equipment and supplies.

You may also be able to hire and direct your own providers, called participant-employed providers. What you actually receive depends on an assessment of your needs, your care plan, and service and funding availability. The services are not an entitlement, and a plan that looks generous on paper may be trimmed to fit the funding.

## Who qualifies for JACC?

You qualify if you meet every test in this table.

| Test | What the state lists |
|---|---|
| Age | 60 or older |
| Residency and status | New Jersey resident; U.S. citizen or qualified alien |
| Where you live | In the community, not in a facility |
| Clinical need | Nursing-facility level of care, meaning help with at least three activities of daily living such as bathing, toileting, dressing, transfers and getting around |
| Monthly income | Not more than 365% of the federal poverty level, listed for 2026 as $4,855 for one person and $6,582 for a married couple |
| Countable assets | $40,000 or less for one person, $60,000 or less for a couple |
| Other programs | Not enrolled in the Traumatic Brain Injury Fund, the Alzheimer's Adult Day Services Program, the Statewide Respite Care Program or the Personal Assistance Service Program |

Compare those to the Medicaid numbers. Under MLTSS a single applicant is limited to $2,000 in countable resources and $2,982 in monthly income, or needs a Qualified Income Trust. JACC lets in people with a decent pension and a modest nest egg, which is exactly the person Medicaid turns away.

Eleanor's income of $3,900 is under $4,855 and her savings of $35,000 are under $40,000, so on those two tests she looks eligible. This is an example with invented numbers. Whether she gets approved still depends on the clinical assessment and on whether funds are available.

## How much does JACC cost?

The program caps services at a monthly dollar amount per participant, plus care management, and you may be required to pay a co-pay. The state says the co-pay is based on your income applied to a sliding scale. The state does not publish the co-pay percentages, and its monthly cap figure has differed between program pages, so ask your Area Agency on Aging for the current cap and your own co-pay before you plan around either one.

A cap matters more than people expect. If your care plan needs round-the-clock help, a capped program covers a slice of it, not the whole bill. Eleanor might use JACC for a few mornings of home care, meals and an emergency pendant, and pay privately for the rest. Think of JACC as a supplement that stretches your savings, not a full replacement for paid care. For what full-time care costs, see our figures on [what a nursing home costs per month](/learn/elder-care/how-much-does-a-nursing-home-cost-per-month).

## How do you apply for JACC?

Call your county Area Agency on Aging (AAA) or Aging and Disability Resource Connection (ADRC) at 1-877-222-3737. The steps usually run like this.

1. Call and say plainly that you are asking about JACC and about screening for other programs.
2. Expect a clinical assessment of the activities you need help with. Describe a bad day, not a good one.
3. Gather proof of age, residency, income and assets.
4. Ask whether there is a waiting list, since service depends on funding.
5. If approved, meet with the care manager and review the written plan, the hours and the co-pay before services start.

Bring someone who knows the daily routine. If the older person cannot handle the paperwork, a [power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) that covers benefits applications saves a great deal of friction.

## What other New Jersey home help exists for people who are not on Medicaid?

The state runs more than one program, and they do not stack automatically. The Statewide Respite Care Program is aimed at the unpaid family caregiver. It requires that the person receive daily care or supervision from an unpaid caregiver age 18 or older, with functional impairments certified by a licensed medical provider. For 2026 the state lists limits of $2,982 in monthly gross income and $40,000 in liquid assets for one person, and the cost share runs on a sliding scale from 0% to 25% of the cost of services. It excludes people in MLTSS or JACC, although people in JACC may switch to it.

Notice the trade-off. Respite has a lower income ceiling, and JACC has a higher ceiling but a firm nursing-home level-of-care test. If a family caregiver is the main support, ask the AAA to look at both. Our article on [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) explains how to document family caregiving so it is not later mistaken for a gift.

Private options sit alongside these programs. [Long-term care insurance](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance), if you have a policy, may pay for home care that no program covers.

## How does JACC fit with Medicaid planning?

JACC is a bridge, not a plan. Used well, it keeps you at home and slows the drain on savings while you decide what comes next. The mistakes our attorneys see are these:

- **Treating JACC as the Medicaid plan.** JACC does not pay for nursing home care. If needs rise past what home care can cover, you will probably need MLTSS, and that brings the $2,000 resource test, the Qualified Income Trust question and the five-year look-back.
- **Giving money away to get under $40,000.** The look-back applies to Medicaid, and gifts made now can still create a penalty period later. Our article on the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains it, and the [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) helps you count.
- **Spending down without a plan.** Paying a daughter back "for all she has done" looks like a transfer to a caseworker unless there is a written agreement and a fair rate.
- **Waiting for a crisis.** If the person is on JACC and then falls and needs a facility, the move to Medicaid happens fast, with little time to arrange anything.

JACC serves people who are not in Medicaid programs, so the two do not run together. Use the JACC period to get a power of attorney and a health care directive signed, gather five years of statements, and talk to an attorney about whether the home or other assets need attention. [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and [estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery) both belong in that conversation, because a house that is exempt for eligibility can still be reached after death.
