A child under 18 may be about to receive money from an estate, a life insurance policy or a lawsuit. If nobody set up a trust or custodian first, the money cannot just be handed over. A bank will not release a large sum to a parent on request. The Surrogate's Court in the county where the child lives usually appoints a guardian of the property, and that guardian must turn all of it over at 18.

This page walks through what that appointment involves, when you can skip it, what the Surrogate's investment fund does, and what you can do before the child turns 18. We kept it narrow. Choosing a guardian to raise a child is a different job, covered in [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey), and the voluntary custodian route has its own page on [New Jersey UTMA accounts](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors). Our [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey) links the rest of the state guide.

## Who manages money a minor inherits in New Jersey?

There are four possibilities, and which one applies depends on what the person who died set up.

| Situation | Who manages the money | Court involved |
|---|---|---|
| The will or beneficiary form names a UTMA custodian | The custodian | No |
| The will creates a trust for the child | The trustee | No, unless there is a dispute |
| Nothing is set up and the amount is small | A parent or other listed adult, under N.J.S.A. 3B:12-6 | No |
| Nothing is set up and the amount is larger | A guardian of the property appointed by the Surrogate | Yes |

The fourth row is the one families do not expect. A parent can be the guardian of the property, and often is, but the parent has to be appointed, post a bond and answer to the court. The authority comes from the Surrogate, not from being the child's mother or father.

## When can you skip the guardian?

New Jersey allows a person who owes money or property to a minor to pay it to certain adults without a guardian, as long as it does not exceed $5,000 per year (N.J.S.A. 3B:12-6). The statute lists the minor, if married, a parent or parents, a custodian with whom the minor lives, a guardian of the person, and a federally insured savings account in the child's sole name, with written notice to the child. The adult who receives it must apply it to the child's support, maintenance or education, and the statute says any excess sums are preserved for the child's future support (3B:12-9).

Say a great-aunt leaves her grandniece Priya, age 9, a $3,000 savings account. The estate can pay it to Priya's mother. For $85,000 from a life insurance policy that named Priya directly, the insurer will want letters of guardianship. The cutoff matters most for the executor, who cannot close the estate by writing a check to an adult who has no authority to take it.

## How does a Surrogate appoint a guardian of a minor's property?

Under N.J.S.A. 3B:12-12, the surrogate's court of the county where the minor lives has the power to appoint guardians for minors, with the same authority as the Superior Court. If the child lives outside New Jersey, the county where the child has property can step into that role.

In practice, someone files a request with the Surrogate, usually a parent, and the court appoints a guardian and sets a bond. New Jersey requires a bond for a guardian of a minor, with an exception for guardians a parent named in a will (N.J.S.A. 3B:15-1; the bond condition for guardians is in 3B:15-7). The bond condition is that the guardian will manage the estate as well as possible and keep an accurate record. The bond costs money. Ask the Surrogate's office what forms and fees your county uses, since they differ by county.

Once appointed, the guardian holds legal title as trustee for the child (3B:12-38) and spends under 3B:12-43 through 3B:12-46: for the child's support, education and benefit, thinking about the child's other resources and the family's standard of living. A guardian can also reimburse a parent who has already paid for something the guardian could have paid (3B:12-47).

## What is the Surrogate's Intermingled Trust Fund?

County Surrogates in New Jersey hold court-ordered money for minors in a pooled account commonly called the Intermingled Trust Fund, usually in connection with settlements of a child's injury claim. You will not find it on most estate checklists, and it applies mostly to money a judge orders deposited. Two statutes show the idea. The first lets the court direct the surrogate to invest a minor's funds, and says the money can be released only by court order (N.J.S.A. 3B:15-17). The second says that when a minor's estate is a judgment recovered in a New Jersey court and the surrogate holds the funds, they are paid to the child at 18 unless a court finds the person incapacitated (3B:15-17.1).

Rules for the fund, interest and release forms are set by court order and county practice. Call the Surrogate in the county that ordered the deposit before you assume anything about timing or withdrawals.

## What happens when the child turns 18?

The guardian must deliver the property to the child. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:12-54, when a minor who has not been found incapacitated turns 18, the guardian, after settling prior claims and expenses, promptly transfers all funds and property to the former ward. Section 3B:12-9 says the same for property a payer delivered to a parent.

That is the part that surprises families. Eighteen is the end of guardianship, not 21, and a UTMA runs longer. New Jersey defines a minor under its UTMA as someone under 21, and a custodianship for gifts and will transfers ends at 21 unless an earlier age after 18 was set. So the same $85,000 policy payout reaches the child at 18 through a guardianship and at 21 through a UTMA custodian, and either way the child gets all of it in a lump sum. A trust is the only route that can pay later.

Take Devon, 17, who inherits $140,000 from his father, who died without a will. Devon's mother was appointed guardian of the property. At 18, she transfers the whole account to Devon, who may use the money for a truck, tuition or something the family regrets.

## Can a court set up a trust for a child who inherits without a will?

Sometimes. Section 3B:12-54.1 lets a parent, guardian or other person with standing ask the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part, to approve a trust when a beneficiary under 18 takes from an intestate estate as a child or descendant. The trustees may spend for health, support, maintenance and education, including college and graduate school. The beneficiary may request principal in stages: one-third at 25, half of the remaining balance at 30, and the rest at 35, though the court may set different ages. The statute sets no minimum or maximum amount, and the court weighs the amount involved, other resources and tax consequences. The court keeps jurisdiction and can modify the trust.

If your family is in that situation, tell the lawyer before the Surrogate appoints a guardian, since the guardian route and the trust route lead to very different results. Our article on [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) explains how children come to inherit by default.

## What to do if a minor is about to inherit

1. Find out the amount and the source: estate, beneficiary form, or settlement. Beneficiary forms bypass the will, so a form naming a child directly cannot be fixed in the will.
2. Check the documents for a UTMA custodian or a trust.
3. If neither exists and the sum is over $5,000 a year, ask the Surrogate in the child's county about a guardian of the property.
4. Ask an attorney whether a court-approved trust is available.
5. If the child has a disability, stop and plan first. Our article on [special needs planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey) covers trusts that protect benefits.
6. Calendar the child's 18th birthday. The guardianship ends then.

The fix is cheaper before death. A will that creates a trust, or one that nominates a custodian with backups, avoids the Surrogate's oversight.
