A bank will not open an estate account for a daughter who says she is handling Dad's affairs. It wants three things: the estate's tax ID number, proof the person died, and proof from the county Surrogate that you have authority. This page walks through the order in which an executor gets those, what the bank usually asks, and how New Jersey's inheritance tax waivers decide when the decedent's own accounts can be released.

We assume a probate estate with an executor or administrator. For a very small estate with no probate, use the [small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) route. The overall sequence is in [executor duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step), and the [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey) lists the rest.

## What do you need to open an estate bank account in New Jersey?

Banks differ, but the list is short.

1. **The Surrogate's proof of authority.** For a will, that is letters testamentary; for an estate with no will, letters of administration. Counties also issue short certificates, which the Surrogate gives as proof of authority over a single asset. Burlington County says short certificates are needed to close bank accounts, and Monmouth says you need one for each such asset, so ask for several.
2. **The estate's EIN.** More below.
3. **A certified death certificate.** Banks often keep the copy, so budget extras.
4. **Your own photo ID.**
5. **A copy of the will, if the bank asks.** Some want it; many accept the letters alone.

The account title usually reads something like "Estate of Maria Santos, Joan Santos, Executor". Ask for a checking account with check-writing, not a savings account you cannot pay from.

## How do you get an EIN for the estate?

Apply on the IRS website. The IRS says the application is free and that estates are among the entities that require an EIN. The form asks for a responsible party, the person in charge of the estate, with that person's Social Security or taxpayer ID number and signature, and the IRS says a nominee cannot apply on the estate's behalf. You can also fax or mail Form SS-4. The IRS page says a faxed application with a fax number returns the EIN in about 4 business days, and a mailed one takes about 4 weeks. Online is the quickest.

Do not pay a website to do it. The number never has a fee at the IRS. For the rest of the IRS paperwork, including Form 56 and the tax year choice, see [IRS paperwork for an executor](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810).

## Why does the estate need its own account?

Because commingling is the mistake that gets executors in trouble. An estate account gives you a clean record of every dollar in and out, which you will need for the [informal release or court accounting](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-court-accounting-or-informal-releases-for-an-estate) and for the beneficiaries. It also protects you. If you pay the funeral home from your own card and reimburse yourself later, the estate account shows the date and the amount.

Deposit into it: the proceeds of the decedent's sole-name accounts, refunds, final paychecks, and sale proceeds. Pay from it: administration costs, valid debts, and eventually distributions. The order for paying claims is in [creditor claims in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate).

Example, with invented numbers. Tomas dies owning a checking account of $18,400 and a savings account of $41,000, both in his name only, and a joint account with his daughter. The executor, his son Ben, opens the estate account, deposits the $59,400 from the two sole-name accounts, and leaves the joint account alone. The joint account passes to the daughter by survivorship and is not in the estate account at all. See [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) for how joint and payable-on-death accounts work.

## How do tax waivers affect the decedent's accounts?

A bank holding a New Jersey resident's accounts may refuse to release them until it has the Division of Taxation's permission. The Division calls that permission a tax waiver. Our page on the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) has the class chart and rates. The account-specific part is this:

| Situation | What the bank usually needs |
|---|---|
| Everything passes to children or other Class A beneficiaries and no tax is due | Form L-8, a self-executing affidavit the executor gives to the bank |
| Any taxable beneficiary (a sibling, niece, friend) | The full inheritance tax return, then waivers the Division issues after review |
| A real estate transfer | A waiver always; Form L-8 cannot be used for real estate |

The Division's executor guide says Form L-8 is used only when no inheritance or estate tax is due, applies to bank accounts, stocks, bonds and brokerage accounts for Class A beneficiaries, and is not sent to the Division. Ask the bank which form it wants and when. Some institutions accept the L-8 at the start, some after letters are issued, and a few have their own waiver policy. Do not assume the form is a substitute for the return when someone outside Class A is a beneficiary.

The return and any tax are due within eight months of death. An account frozen for three months because nobody sent the L-8 is a common and avoidable delay.

## When can you open the account?

Not on the day of death. The Surrogate appoints the executor after the tenth day, as the [probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) explains, so the first week or two goes to the death certificates and the will. You can get the EIN only once you are the person in charge, so plan it for the same afternoon you leave the Surrogate's office with letters. Between the death and that appointment, the family can still cover a funeral bill personally and keep the receipts, which the page on [paying for a funeral from a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/paying-for-a-funeral-from-a-new-jersey-estate) covers.

## What mistakes do executors make with estate accounts?

- **Using the decedent's Social Security number.** The estate is its own taxpayer. Interest reported under the wrong number creates mismatched notices.
- **Paying beneficiaries first.** Wait for the claim window, as the creditor article explains.
- **Cash withdrawals.** Pay by check or transfer so there is a trail.
- **Closing the decedent's account too early.** Automatic deposits may still arrive, and a returned deposit complicates things. Leave it open until the last benefit payment is settled, then close.
- **Letting an account sit.** A year-long estate with $90,000 in a no-interest account is money lost to inflation. Ask for an interest-bearing account.

A Social Security payment that covers the month of death must be returned, as described in [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death). Do not let the bank send them back for you without telling you.

The general steps that apply in any state are in [how to open an estate bank account](/learn/probate/how-to-open-an-estate-bank-account).
