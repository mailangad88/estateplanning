A father dies. His daughter learns that a trust, signed eight months earlier, now leaves the house to one sibling, and the trustee has already asked everyone to sign receipts. She wants to know whether the trust can be challenged, who is allowed to ask, and how fast. In New Jersey the clock can be much shorter than most people expect.

## What is the deadline to contest a trust in New Jersey?

It is the earlier of two dates. Under N.J.S.A. 3B:31-45, a person may start a court proceeding to contest the validity of a trust that was revocable at the settlor's death within the earlier of three years after the settlor died, or four months after the trustee sends the person a copy of the trust instrument and a notice. For someone who is not a New Jersey resident, the second period is six months.

The notice has content requirements. The statute says it must state the existence of the trust, the trustee's name and address, and the time allowed for commencing a proceeding. A letter that merely says "you are a beneficiary" does not start the short clock.

| Situation | Deadline under 3B:31-45 |
|---|---|
| Trustee has not sent the notice and copy | Three years after the settlor's death |
| Trustee sent the notice and copy to a New Jersey resident | Four months after sending, if that is earlier than three years |
| Same, to a nonresident | Six months after sending |
| Trust was irrevocable at death | Section does not set this period. Ask an attorney which one applies |

Compare that to a will, where the Rules of Court generally give four months from probate. A trust gets a similar short window only if the trustee does the paperwork right. Many do not, which leaves the three-year outer limit.

## Who can contest a trust?

A person with a financial stake in the outcome. In practice that means someone who would receive more if the trust failed: a child left out or cut back, a beneficiary of an earlier trust or will, or a spouse. A friend with no interest has no case.

New Jersey's trust chapter ties the right to petition in several places to being a trustee or beneficiary. For example, a proceeding to approve or disapprove a modification may be commenced by a trustee or beneficiary (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-26). The Uniform Trust Code does not spell out a standing test for validity contests, so a court applies general principles. If you are not named in either version of the document, ask an attorney whether you would inherit if the trust failed. That is the question a judge will ask.

Consider Denise and her brother Carl. Their mother's old will split everything equally. A trust signed last year gives Carl the rental property. Denise would take half of that property if the trust were set aside and the old will controlled, so she has a stake. Carl's wife, who was never named in either document, does not.

## What are the grounds to contest a trust?

Three grounds carry most cases.

- **Lack of capacity.** For a revocable trust, the capacity needed to create, amend or revoke is the same as for a will (N.J.S.A. 3B:31-42). That is a fairly low bar, so a diagnosis alone rarely wins. What the person understood on the signing day is what matters.
- **Fraud, duress or undue influence.** A trust is void to the extent its creation was induced by any of these (3B:31-23). The will-contest reasoning about confidential relationships and suspicious circumstances is explained in our [will contest article](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats), and courts use similar ideas for trusts, though you should confirm with a lawyer how they apply to your facts.
- **A trust that was never properly created.** The code lists requirements for creation: capacity, intent, a definite beneficiary and duties for the trustee (3B:31-19). A document missing one of these can fail.

Mistake is a different kind of claim. A court may reform a trust to match the settlor's probable intent, but only on clear and convincing proof of a mistake (3B:31-31). That is not a contest of validity. It asks the court to fix wording. The changes available to a trust that is valid are described in [changing an irrevocable trust in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/changing-an-irrevocable-trust-in-new-jersey).

## How does contesting a trust differ from a will caveat?

A caveat is filed with the county Surrogate to stop an executor or administrator being appointed until the filer is heard. It works because probate runs through the Surrogate. A funded trust usually does not. There is no Surrogate file to block, and the trustee can start distributing without any court order.

So the pause button is built differently. For a trust, it is a written notice to the trustee that you may contest. Under 3B:31-45(b), after the settlor dies the trustee can distribute under the trust terms without liability. There are two exceptions. One is a contest the trustee already knows is pending. The other is a written warning from a possible contestant, followed by notice of a proceeding filed within 90 days after the warning was sent. The result is a trap. The letter helps only if a case follows promptly.

| | Will | Living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Where it is held | County Surrogate's file | Usually no court file |
| Pause tool | Caveat filed with the Surrogate | Written notice to the trustee under 3B:31-45 |
| Short deadline | Generally four months from probate (R. 4:85-1) | Four months after the trustee's notice, or three years |
| If the challenge wins | Earlier will controls, or intestacy | Distributions must be returned (3B:31-45(c)) |

Do not skip the will side. A pour-over will still has to be probated, and a contest of the trust does not stop the four-month period that runs against the will.

## What should you do if you think a trust is invalid?

1. Write down the date of death and find out whether you received a notice from the trustee. If you did, note the date it was sent.
2. Ask the trustee in writing for a copy of the trust instrument and amendments. A trustee must provide a copy of the instrument to a beneficiary on request (3B:31-67).
3. Gather evidence: medical records, the drafting lawyer's name, the dates of each amendment and who was in the room.
4. Send a short letter to the trustee saying you may contest and asking the trustee to hold distributions.
5. See an attorney quickly, and file within the 90 days if you want that letter to protect you.
6. Check for a no-contest clause before filing. How such a clause works in New Jersey is a question for your lawyer. Our general article on [no-contest clauses](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) explains the idea.

## What happens if the trust is set aside?

Distributions already made must be returned. Section 3B:31-45(c) says a beneficiary of a trust later found invalid has to return any distribution received. That is a strong reason for a trustee to wait out a threatened contest, and a reason for beneficiaries to avoid spending money they receive early.

If you would rather prevent the fight than win it, a [living trust](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) signed with a lawyer who meets the settlor alone, and who keeps notes of the conversation, is a sound way to cut the risk. A short explanation of an unequal split also helps.
