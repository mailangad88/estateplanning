Parents of a child with a disability tend to plan in two time frames. There is the next appointment, and there is the day they are no longer around. New Jersey adds its own agencies, forms and ages to both, and families lose benefits mostly by missing one of them, not by doing something dramatic.

This page maps the New Jersey pieces. It rests on pages published by the state's Department of Human Services and its Medicaid agency. For the national rules, start with our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs). The wider state picture is in our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## How do you qualify for DDD services?

You apply to the Division of Developmental Disabilities (DDD) for a determination of eligibility. The Division says an applicant must meet the functional criteria for a developmental disability, be a U.S. citizen and New Jersey resident, and be eligible for or enrolled in Medicaid. The functional test asks for documentation of a chronic physical or intellectual disability that began before age 22, is expected to last for life, and substantially limits at least three life areas. The examples the Division gives include self-care, language, learning, mobility, self-direction, independent living and economic self-sufficiency.

Age matters twice. You must be 18 to apply and 21 to receive DDD services. For the years between, the Division points to school-based services and the NJ Children's System of Care. Its decision can take up to 60 days from a complete application, and missing papers extend that.

The Division uses a short application if the person was already found eligible through DDD or PerformCare and a full application otherwise. Applications go by email, mail or hand delivery to the county Community Services Office.

Two programs fund adult supports, the Supports Program and the Community Care Program. The Division says a person can enroll in only one, must be at least 21, must qualify for Medicaid and must meet the functional standards in Division Circular #3. The Community Care Program adds a waiting list or an imminent-risk finding and a need for institutional level of care. Medicaid for an SSI recipient is automatic according to the state, and a person who loses SSI because of Social Security on a parent's record may qualify as a disabled adult child.

## What is NJ ABLE?

NJ ABLE is the state's tax-advantaged savings account for people with disabilities, run by the Division of Disability Services. The state's page gives the eligibility test as a disability that began before age 46, plus one of three things: eligibility for SSI or SSDI because of disability, blindness under the Social Security Act, or a similarly severe disability supported by a physician's written diagnosis. A power of attorney or court-appointed financial representative can open the account for the person.

Anyone can contribute, and the owner keeps control. Interest is not taxed, and withdrawals do not need proof at the time of qualified expenses, though the owner should keep records in case the IRS or Social Security asks. A person gets one account, with an exception for rollovers from another ABLE program. Contribution caps change over time, so read the current figure on the state's page rather than relying on an old brochure. Our [ABLE accounts article](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) covers how they work nationally.

Two numbers are worth remembering. A balance of $100,000 or less is excluded from the SSI resource limit. And at death, the state Medicaid agency is a creditor entitled to recover what remains, up to the Medicaid paid for the owner after the account was opened. So an ABLE account is a good place for the money the person will spend, and a poor place for a large legacy.

## How do special needs trusts work in New Jersey?

A special needs trust holds money for a person with a disability without making that money count as theirs. The type matters. The New Jersey Medicaid agency's page describes the trust funded with the individual's own assets, often called first-party or (d)(4)(A). Its requirements include that the trust be irrevocable, be for the sole benefit of the beneficiary, and be funded before age 65 by a person who meets the Social Security definition of disability. At the beneficiary's death New Jersey Medicaid is repaid up to the total Medicaid paid during the lifetime, before funeral costs. The trustee sends annual accountings to the eligibility agency and to the Medicaid agency's Beneficiary Administrative Action Unit, and gives 45 days' notice before any expenditure over $5,000 or one that would substantially deplete the principal.

That is a lot of oversight, and it is the price of using the person's own money. A trust funded by parents, grandparents or siblings from their own assets is a different animal, usually called a third-party trust. It is the common vehicle for an inheritance; the payback rule described above attaches to the person's own money, not to a relative's gift. The state page does not discuss third-party trusts, so confirm the terms with an attorney. Our article on [first-party versus third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) compares them, and [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child or sibling](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling) shows how to word the will.

The habit to build is simple. Tell every relative not to leave money to the person directly, a will naming the trust instead. A $40,000 bequest outright to Marcus, who receives SSI, can put his benefits at risk. The same bequest to a trust does not.

## What should you decide before the 18th birthday?

At 18 a person is a legal adult in New Jersey, and parents lose the automatic right to decide. The Department of Human Services describes guardianship as a court process in Superior Court and "a solution of last resort." It can be general or limited to areas such as medical, financial or residential decisions. The Bureau of Guardianship Services can seek guardianship over daily life and care decisions, but the department says a private attorney is needed to obtain guardianship over both the person and their property. Only the court can change an order later.

The alternatives the state lists are supported decision-making, which has no New Jersey statute, and advance directives, either a proxy directive or an instruction directive. A signed durable power of attorney is another route, and our article on [guardianship alternatives for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) compares them. How the New Jersey court case actually runs, including the recent change that lets a family start earlier for a young adult, is in [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey). See also [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives).

## A checklist for this year

1. Gather medical and school records that show the disability began before 22, and the date.
2. Apply to DDD at 18 so the file is ready for 21.
3. Confirm Medicaid or SSI status after the 18th birthday, when parents' income stops counting.
4. Decide whether an NJ ABLE account fits the person's spending.
5. Write or update your will so any inheritance goes to a trust, not outright.
6. Name a successor trustee and write a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent).
7. Choose guardianship or an alternative and sign the documents.
