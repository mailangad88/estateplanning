Within a day or two of a death, someone has to sign a funeral home contract. It is rarely the executor, because the executor often is not appointed yet. So the bill lands on whichever family member is standing at the desk, and that person wants to know whether the estate will pay them back.

In New Jersey it usually does, and reasonable funeral costs get paid first. This page is about the money. Who gets to decide on burial or cremation is a separate question that [New Jersey's funeral control statute](/learn/new-jersey/who-controls-funeral-and-burial-decisions-in-new-jersey) answers. The general rules in other states are in our national article on [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral). The [New Jersey pillar](/learn/new-jersey) links the rest of the state material.

## Who pays for a funeral in New Jersey?

The person who signs the contract. A funeral home can collect from the signer, whether or not the decedent left money. Being the person with legal control over the funeral does not make you responsible for the bill, but signing does. Read the contract line by line before you sign it, and ask for the itemized price list.

Here is the usual sequence:

1. A family member signs and pays, often on a credit card.
2. The family keeps every invoice and receipt.
3. The Surrogate appoints an executor or administrator.
4. The executor opens an estate account (see [how to open a New Jersey estate bank account](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-bank-account-ein-and-tax-waivers)).
5. The executor reimburses the family member from the estate, on the receipts.

Between steps 1 and 5, weeks or months can pass. If a will exists, the tenth day after death is the earliest the Surrogate appoints the executor, and probate can run longer when there is a dispute. Plan the cash.

## What is the funeral expense priority in a New Jersey estate?

N.J.S.A. 3B:22-2 sets the order of payment when estate property cannot cover every claim. Funeral expenses are first.

| Priority | Class of claim |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reasonable funeral expenses |
| 2 | Costs and expenses of administering the estate |
| 3 | Debts for services of the Office of the Public Guardian for Elderly Adults |
| 4 | Debts and taxes with preference under federal or New Jersey law |
| 5 | Final-illness medical and hospital expenses, including compensation of persons attending the decedent |
| 6 | Judgments against the decedent, by date of entry |
| 7 | All other claims |

That puts the family member's reimbursement ahead of the hospital, the credit card and, in an insolvent estate, ahead of the executor's own costs. The statute says "reasonable," and the page we reviewed sets no dollar limit. An executor deciding whether to reimburse a $22,000 bill for a modest estate should be ready to explain it. Our article on [creditor claims and paying debts of a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate) covers the nine-month claim window and the rest of the order. Estates that owe more than they own are handled in [what happens when an estate owes more than it has](/learn/probate/what-happens-when-an-estate-owes-more-than-it-has).

## How do you get reimbursed from the estate?

You ask the executor, in writing, with the contract and proof of payment. A reasonable executor will pay it once the estate account has money, usually without waiting for the creditor claim period to end, because funeral expenses come first. The executor should note the payment in the estate records.

Take Ruth, 61, whose husband Walt dies without a will. Ruth charges $11,800 on a card for a burial. The Surrogate appoints her administrator three weeks later. She opens an estate account, deposits Walt's $34,000 sole-name checking balance, and writes herself a check for $11,800 with the funeral invoice attached. The numbers are an example only. If Ruth had not been appointed, her adult son could not have written that check, which is why the order of appointment matters.

A family member who is not the executor asks the executor for repayment. If the executor refuses, the person can present a claim like any creditor. The 9-month presentment window is in the creditor article, so do not wait.

## Can the family use the deceased person's money before an executor is appointed?

Not as of right. The decedent's sole-name accounts are restricted once the bank hears of the death, and only the person with letters can handle them. The estate account guide explains what the bank requires. Joint accounts with a survivor, payable-on-death accounts and life insurance with named beneficiaries pass outside the estate, so the survivor or beneficiary can use that money. The beneficiary of a $10,000 policy has no duty to pay the funeral, but families often agree to.

Some other sources of money:

- **Social Security.** A $255 one-time death payment goes to a qualifying spouse or child, as our article on [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) explains. It will not cover much.
- **Veterans' burial benefits.** These depend on service and eligibility, so ask the funeral director to check.
- **Prepaid plan.** A pre-need contract from the funeral home can cover the services. Ask the home for the paperwork and who the plan names as the payee.
- **A lawsuit.** Where another party caused the death, funeral and burial costs can be part of the recovery. See [wrongful death and survival claims](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-wrongful-death-and-survival-claims-for-an-estate), and note it does not help you pay in the first month.

## What if the person who controls the funeral cannot afford it?

Control and cost sit with different people more often than families expect. Under N.J.S.A. 45:27-22, the person the decedent appointed, or the statutory first in line, decides on the arrangements. If that person has no money, they can choose a simpler plan. A direct cremation or a simple burial costs far less than a full service, and the funeral home must give an itemized price list on request. Say plainly what the budget is before you sign. If the family cannot agree, our page on [who decides when the family disagrees](/learn/after-a-death/who-decides-funeral-arrangements-when-family-disagrees) helps.

When an estate has nothing and no relative can pay, ask the county about assistance before you sign anything, since county rules differ and we have not confirmed what New Jersey counties offer.

## How do you plan ahead so your family does not carry this?

Three options, each with a catch. A prepaid funeral plan fixes the price but ties you to a provider. A payable-on-death bank account with your executor or a trusted person on it gives immediate access, but check how it is titled. A short note in your file with the funeral appointment form tells your family who is in charge and who pays. Our attorneys often pair the funeral appointment with the will so both name the same person.
