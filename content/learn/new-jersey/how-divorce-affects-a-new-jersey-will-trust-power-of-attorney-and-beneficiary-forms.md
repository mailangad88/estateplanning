If you are divorcing in New Jersey, your estate plan is probably the last thing on the list. The house, the support terms and the children come first. But the documents you signed as a married person are still in force, and some of them point straight at the spouse you are leaving.

This page takes the whole-plan view: what the divorce statute fixes by itself, and what it leaves for you to change. The will has its own deep treatment in [changing or revoking a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-new-jersey), so here we spend most of the space on the documents people forget. For the general life-stage guide, see [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce). The state overview is in our [New Jersey guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## What does divorce change automatically in New Jersey?

N.J.S.A. 3B:3-14 does four things when a divorce or annulment is final, unless the instrument, a court order or a contract about the marital property says otherwise.

- It revokes revocable gifts to the former spouse and to the former spouse's relatives who are not also your relatives.
- It revokes powers of appointment in their favor.
- It revokes their nomination as executor, trustee or other fiduciary, and treats them as if they died before the divorce.
- It converts joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety between the two of you into a tenancy in common.

If you remarry each other, or the divorce is set aside, the revoked parts come back. And a payor, such as a bank or insurer, that pays under the document is not liable under this section unless it received written notice of the claimed revocation before it paid.

That last point is the catch. The statute works on the document. It does not fix the company's records.

## What does it leave alone?

Almost everything outside the will. The table shows where each document stands and what you should do.

| Document | Does divorce fix it? | What you should do |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Gifts and roles for the ex are revoked; the rest stays | Sign a new will so the leftover terms say what you want |
| Revocable trust | Treat as unfixed | Amend or restate; change trustee and beneficiaries |
| Financial power of attorney | Treat as unfixed until revoked | Sign a written revocation; deliver it to the agent and the banks |
| Health care directive | A spouse designation ends on divorce unless the directive says otherwise (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-57) | Name a new representative and a backup |
| Life insurance | Pays the person on the insurer's records | File a new beneficiary form |
| IRA, 401(k), pension | Many plans follow their own paperwork | Update each beneficiary form; check the plan rules |
| Joint bank accounts | Not changed by the statute | Close or retitle; separate the money by agreement |
| House | Entirety title becomes tenancy in common | Follow the settlement; record the new deed |

Because of the notice rule, one habit pays off. After judgment, send each bank, insurer and plan administrator a short letter with a copy of the judgment and the new form, and keep the confirmation. The payor's liability turns on written notice received at its home or principal address, so an email to a branch employee or a phone call proves little. A certified-mail receipt does.

The statute's wording reaches a "governing instrument." Whether a particular beneficiary form or revocable trust falls inside that phrase is a legal question a family should not have to litigate. Fix the paper.

## What about a trust?

If you have a [revocable living trust](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey), read it for three things. Is your spouse the trustee or successor trustee? Is your spouse a beneficiary, either of the whole trust or of a marital share? And is your spouse the person who can sign for you if you become incapacitated? Amend the trust or restate it. Because it is revocable, you can do this alone, but a joint trust that both spouses signed may have its own rules about changing it, so read the amendment clause first. The revocable-trust rules are explained in [how to change or revoke a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-change-or-revoke-a-living-trust).

One more thing that surprises couples. Assets that were meant to move into the trust but never did still pass under the will. A divorce is a good time to check [whether the trust is actually funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded).

## What about the power of attorney and health care forms?

Treat a financial power of attorney that names your spouse as live until you revoke it. A bank that never hears about the divorce may keep honoring the old document, and between filing and judgment your spouse is still your spouse. Our article on [whether divorce cancels a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-divorce-cancel-a-power-of-attorney) covers this, and the [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) page explains the form itself.

The health care side is clearer. A spouse's designation in an advance directive ends on divorce, legal separation or dissolution unless the directive says otherwise. Domestic partner designations end when the partnership ends. See [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives). The practical risk is the opposite one: you may be left with no agent. Sign a new form naming someone you would call at 2 a.m.

## What should you do while the divorce is pending?

Before judgment, the old documents all work as written. A few things are worth doing now, in this order.

1. **Ask your divorce attorney what is restricted.** Some courts and agreements limit changes to insurance and retirement beneficiaries while a case is open. Do not assume you are free to change anything.
2. **Sign a new will** that names a new executor and guardian for any minor children. A will can be changed without anyone's consent.
3. **Revoke the old power of attorney** in writing and sign a new one.
4. **Replace the health care directive.**
5. **List every beneficiary form** and flag the ones that need your attorney's sign-off.
6. **After judgment,** file the new forms, send copies of the judgment to the institutions, and record any deed.

The spouse's position changes in a few specific ways once papers are filed. A person who has filed a divorce or dissolution complaint that has not been dismissed has no elective share, and the intestacy statute excludes that person as a surviving spouse. Those rules matter if one of you dies before the judgment, and our article on [death during divorce](/learn/what-if/death-during-divorce) walks through it. For the right order of operations, [estate planning during separation or a pending divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-during-separation-or-a-pending-divorce) is the companion guide.

## What is a common mistake after a New Jersey divorce?

Leaving the ex-spouse as beneficiary on a work retirement plan or old insurance policy because the will "took care of it." Marisol, 52, divorced in 2019 and signed a new will leaving everything to her two sons. Her 401(k) beneficiary form, filled out at a job she took in 2008, still listed her ex-husband. The will could not change that. The plan paid the person it had on file, and the sons had to litigate. Check the form, not just the will. The pattern is described in [what if your ex-spouse is still the beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).
