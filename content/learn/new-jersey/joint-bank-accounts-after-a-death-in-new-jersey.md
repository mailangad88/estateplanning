A parent dies, and the checking account has two names on it. The surviving name is you, or a brother, or a spouse. Who owns the money? And does the bank need a court order, a tax form or the executor before it will let go?

New Jersey answers the first question in a specific statute, the Multiple-Party Deposit Account Act (N.J.S.A. 17:16I-1 and following). The second question is a tax and paperwork problem. This page deals with both. The general rules for co-owned property, including houses, are in [joint property and tenancy by the entirety in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/joint-property-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety-in-new-jersey), and the wider guide is our [New Jersey estate planning and probate overview](/learn/new-jersey).

## Who owns a joint bank account when one owner dies in New Jersey?

The survivor does. N.J.S.A. 17:16I-5(a) says that sums remaining on deposit at the death of a party to a joint account belong to the surviving party or parties, unless there is clear and convincing evidence of a different intention when the account was created. If three people share an account and one dies, survivorship continues among the other two.

That is why a joint account is not part of the probate estate. It never passes through the executor, and it never needs letters from the Surrogate. Our general article on [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) shows how this fits with single-name accounts.

## Can a will change who gets a joint account?

No. Subsection (e) of the same section covers survivorship rights created by the account terms, a trust account designation or a payable-on-death designation. Those "cannot be changed by will." A father who leaves "my bank accounts to my three children equally" in his will, while his checking account is joint with one daughter, has not changed anything about that account. She takes it.

The statute also says the form of the account controls at death (N.J.S.A. 17:16I-6). A party can change the form during life by signed written notice to the bank, but the notice has to be received while that party is alive and not withdrawn. A deathbed instruction to a nephew does not count.

| Account type | Who owns it during life | Who gets it at death |
|---|---|---|
| Joint account | The parties, in proportion to net contributions (17:16I-4) | The surviving party or parties (17:16I-5) |
| Payable-on-death (POD) | The original payee, not the POD payee | The surviving POD payee |
| Trust account (Totten-style) | The trustee | The surviving named beneficiaries |
| Account in one name only | That person | The estate, under the will or intestacy |

The POD row matters for the Medicaid and creditor questions that families ask. A POD payee has no ownership during the owner's life. The [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) article covers how to set one up.

## Who owns the money while both owners are alive?

Whoever put it in, unless the account terms or other clear evidence say otherwise. N.J.S.A. 17:16I-4(a) says a joint account belongs during the lifetime of all the parties in proportion to their net contributions. If no one can prove contributions, the account belongs in equal shares to parties with a present right to withdraw. The section does not limit a court's power to divide an account in a divorce case.

This is a split that surprises families. Survivorship decides who gets the balance at death. Net contributions decide who owned the money before.

Here is an example with invented numbers. Eleanor, 86, adds her son Paul to her savings account to help with bills. She deposited all $90,000 herself, and Paul never added a dollar. While she lives, the money is hers. If she dies, Paul takes the $90,000 by survivorship. His sister Karen may argue Eleanor only meant a convenience and wanted the money split in three. To win, Karen would need clear and convincing evidence of that intent at the time the account was created, and a mother's vague remark years later is thin. If Eleanor had a lawyer's letter or an account agreement that says "convenience only," Karen's case would be much stronger.

## What should you do before you add a name to an account?

Ask what you want to happen at death, then pick the form that does it.

1. **Want the other person to receive it at death and have no access now?** A POD designation does that.
2. **Want a helper to pay bills but heirs to split the balance?** A power of attorney is usually a better fit than a joint name. See the [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) guide.
3. **Want one person to own it outright after you die?** Joint with survivorship or POD both work. Tell the other children.
4. **Want the money to follow a will or trust?** Keep it in your name, or title it in the trust.

For the choice between joint titles and beneficiary forms, [joint ownership versus beneficiary designation](/learn/beneficiary-designations/joint-ownership-vs-beneficiary-designation) compares them in detail.

## How does New Jersey inheritance tax treat a joint account?

A joint account avoids probate. It does not avoid the inheritance tax return. The Division of Taxation's general information booklet says that for a resident decedent the return covers all intangible personal property, including bank accounts, and that the full amount on deposit at the date of death must be listed. The booklet does not give a separate rule for what share of a joint account is taxed on a survivor who is not Class A, so that is a question for the executor's attorney.

What the survivor owes depends on the class. A spouse, civil union partner, domestic partner, child, grandchild or parent is Class A and pays nothing. A sibling is Class C and pays 11% to 16% after the first $25,000. A friend or niece is Class D and pays 15% to 16%. All of this is in [the New Jersey inheritance tax guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

Banks care about the paperwork before the tax. The Division describes a blanket waiver: for a resident decedent's joint account, a bank may release up to 50% of the balance to the survivor without a waiver (N.J.A.C. 18:26-11.16). The rest may stay held until a waiver is issued. For a Class A survivor, Form L-8 can unlock bank accounts without sending anything to the Division, as explained in our article on [the estate bank account, EIN and tax waivers](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-bank-account-ein-and-tax-waivers).

Take Lucia, 60, whose husband dies with a joint checking account of $44,000. She is Class A and has the account by survivorship. She gives the bank a death certificate and her Form L-8 and the account is released. A brother in the same position is Class C, so the bank may release only up to half under the blanket waiver and wait for the executor on the rest.

## What can the executor do about a joint account the family disputes?

The executor does not control the account but may still have to report it. If heirs believe a joint owner took more than their share before the death, or that an account was never meant to be survivorship, the dispute is a court matter, and the court looks at net contributions and the evidence of intent. It is slow and expensive. Our guide for heirs facing a [slow or silent executor](/learn/new-jersey/what-new-jersey-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-or-silent-executor) covers the nearby steps. The simplest cure is before death: a short signed note stating what an account is for.
