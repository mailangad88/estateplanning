Helen is 61 and Walter is 63. Each has grown children and a house. They want to marry, and each wants their own children to inherit what they brought into the marriage. In New Jersey, a will alone cannot do that, because a surviving spouse can override a will and claim one-third of the augmented estate. A prenuptial agreement is the usual answer.

It sits inside our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey). This article covers what a New Jersey prenup can do about inheritance, what must be disclosed for it to hold up and how to line it up with a will or trust. The right being waived is explained in [the New Jersey elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse), and the general national treatment is in [prenups, postnups and your estate plan](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).

## Can a prenup waive the elective share in New Jersey?

Yes. The elective share can be waived, wholly or partly, before or after marriage, through a written contract or waiver signed by the person giving it up, and the waiver must follow fair disclosure (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-10). The statute adds a default rule: unless the document says otherwise, a broad waiver of "all rights" in the other person's property also waives the elective share, any intestate inheritance and gifts under a will made before the waiver.

That default cuts both ways. A broadly worded clause may do more than a couple intended. If Walter and Helen want Helen to keep the right to live in the house, the agreement should say so, because "all rights" language may remove it.

The premarital agreement statute is separate. It says parties may contract about their property rights, how property is distributed on death, creating a will or trust to carry out the agreement and ownership of life insurance and its death benefit (N.J.S.A. 37:2-34). It also lets them change or end spousal support obligations.

## What must a New Jersey prenup contain to be valid?

Three formalities, from N.J.S.A. 37:2-33. The agreement must be in writing. It must include an attached statement of assets. And it must be signed by both parties. It is enforceable without consideration. The agreement takes effect on marriage or on establishing a civil union (37:2-32).

| Requirement | Source |
|---|---|
| Written agreement | N.J.S.A. 37:2-33 |
| Attached statement of assets | N.J.S.A. 37:2-33 |
| Signed by both people | N.J.S.A. 37:2-33 |
| Fair disclosure, for an elective share waiver | N.J.S.A. 3B:8-10 |
| Effective upon marriage or civil union | N.J.S.A. 37:2-32 |

A statement of assets is more than a courtesy. Attach real numbers: the house and its mortgage, retirement accounts, business interests, accounts and debts. A vague list gives the other side a disclosure argument later.

## What has to be disclosed?

Full and fair disclosure of the other person's earnings, property and financial obligations. That phrase appears in the enforcement section, which lists what a challenger can use. An agreement is not enforceable if the person attacking it proves, by clear and convincing evidence, that they signed involuntarily. It is also unenforceable if it was unconscionable when signed because that person, before signing:

1. was not given full and fair disclosure of the other person's earnings, property and financial obligations;
2. did not voluntarily and expressly waive disclosure in writing;
3. did not have, or reasonably could not have had, adequate knowledge of those finances; and
4. did not consult independent legal counsel and did not voluntarily and expressly waive that right in writing.

The burden is on the person alleging the agreement is unenforceable, and the court decides unconscionability as a matter of law (37:2-38). The statute lists these four circumstances, and an agreement is not unconscionable unless they apply.

Two practical points follow. First, each person should have their own lawyer. If one person goes without, a written waiver of the right to counsel is on the list for a reason. Second, a person who knows everything about the other's finances is on firm ground even if the paperwork is thin, but you cannot count on that. Put the disclosure in writing.

## What can a prenup not do?

It cannot, on its own, change the beneficiary on a retirement plan or a life insurance policy. The beneficiary form on file with the plan or insurer controls the payout, and a federal retirement plan may require its own spousal waiver signed after the marriage. Our article on [why a prenup cannot waive a 401(k) by itself](/learn/blended-families/can-a-prenup-be-changed-after-marriage) explains that.

It also does not decide who gets the rest. A prenup that waives the elective share does not say who inherits Walter's house. His will or trust does. Without one, the intestacy rules apply, and a waiver of intestate rights could leave them in surprising places. See [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey).

## How does a prenup fit with your will or trust?

The prenup sets limits. The will or trust fills in the plan. Four steps keep them consistent.

1. **Sign the estate plan after the agreement, not before.** A will signed earlier may not reflect the waiver, and a spouse's rights can come from documents you forgot.
2. **Make the promises match.** If the prenup says Helen gets a life estate or a fixed payment, the will or trust should carry it out, because the agreement can state that the parties will make a will or trust to implement it (37:2-34).
3. **Fix the beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death accounts follow their forms, not the will. Start by listing every one of them.
4. **Consider a living trust for the promise.** A [New Jersey living trust](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) can hold the house for a spouse for life, then pass it to the children. That honors both sides.

Consider Helen's side. Her house is worth far more than Walter's. The couple agrees that on her death Walter can stay for up to five years, then the house goes to her children. They put that in the prenup, and Helen's attorney drafts a trust to carry it out. If the trust says otherwise, a dispute between the agreement and the trust is the likely result.

## Can the agreement be changed or challenged later?

After the marriage, it can be changed or ended only by a written agreement signed by both spouses, and no consideration is needed (N.J.S.A. 37:2-37). A spouse cannot cancel it alone. A challenge after death is allowed, but the burden stays on the challenger, and the grounds are those above. Our article on [changing a prenup after marriage](/learn/blended-families/can-a-prenup-be-changed-after-marriage) goes through the practical side.
