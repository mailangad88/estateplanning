Many New Jersey families assume that if someone dies without a will, the spouse simply gets everything. Sometimes that is true. Often it is not, and the gap between what people expect and what the statute says is where family arguments start.

This page walks through the New Jersey default rules: what a spouse or domestic partner receives, who comes next, and who is left out. For the rest of the state's rules, start with our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). For the general idea that applies everywhere, read about [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## What law decides who inherits in New Jersey?

Two sections of the Probate Code do most of the work. N.J.S.A. 3B:5-3 sets the share of the surviving spouse or domestic partner. N.J.S.A. 3B:5-4 sends everything that does not go to the partner to relatives in a fixed order. A person who dies this way is [intestate](/glossary/intestate), and the statutes apply only to the part of the estate that passes through probate. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for what stays outside.

The partner's share depends on three questions. Did the person leave children? Are all of those children also children of the surviving partner? Does the partner have children from somewhere else? The answers sort families into four cases.

## How much does a surviving spouse or domestic partner receive?

The statute says "spouse or domestic partner," and the same rules cover a partner in a civil union. Here are the four cases from N.J.S.A. 3B:5-3.

| Family situation | What the spouse or partner takes |
| --- | --- |
| No children and no parent of the decedent survive | The entire intestate estate |
| Every surviving child is also the partner's child, and the partner has no other children | The entire intestate estate |
| No children survive, but a parent of the decedent does | The first 25%, but not less than $50,000 and not more than $200,000, plus three-fourths of the balance |
| Some child of the decedent is not the partner's child, or the partner has children who are not the decedent's | The first 25%, but not less than $50,000 and not more than $200,000, plus one-half of the balance |

The floor and the ceiling matter more than people expect. On a small estate the floor lifts the spouse's cut. On a large estate the ceiling stops the 25 percent slice from growing, so the fraction of the balance carries the rest.

### Three examples with real numbers

**Spouse and a parent, no children.** Carlos dies with a $400,000 estate, a wife named Dana, and a living mother. Twenty-five percent is $100,000, which sits between the floor and the ceiling. The balance is $300,000, and three-fourths of that is $225,000. Dana receives $325,000. Carlos's mother receives $75,000.

**Blended family.** Priya dies with $400,000, a husband named Luis, and two children from her first marriage. Some of her children are not Luis's, so the one-half rule applies. Luis takes $100,000 plus half of the $300,000 balance, or $250,000. The children share $150,000.

**A small estate.** Wen dies with a $100,000 estate, a husband, and a surviving mother. Twenty-five percent would be $25,000, but the floor is $50,000. The balance is $50,000, and three-fourths of that is $37,500. Her husband receives $87,500, and her mother receives $12,500.

## Who inherits after the spouse or partner?

Whatever does not go to the surviving partner passes under N.J.S.A. 3B:5-4, in this order. A closer group shuts out the groups below it.

1. **Descendants**, by representation. Children come first. If a child has died, that child's own children share the parent's portion.
2. **Parents**, equally if both survive, or the one who survives, when there are no descendants.
3. **Descendants of the parents**, meaning brothers and sisters and their children, when there is no descendant and no parent.
4. **Grandparents.** Half goes to the paternal side and half to the maternal side. If one side has no one, the other side takes it all.
5. **Descendants of grandparents**, such as aunts, uncles and cousins. They take equally if they are the same degree of kinship, and by representation if they are not.
6. **Stepchildren**, or their descendants, if no one in the earlier groups survives.

Notice where stepchildren fall. They are on the list, but at the very bottom. A stepchild who helped raise the decedent, lived in the house and managed the finances still loses to a distant cousin. That is a strong reason for [blended families](/learn/blended-families) to put wishes in writing. Our article on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance) covers the options.

## Who gets nothing under the intestacy rules?

Several groups people think of as family are not covered.

- **Unmarried partners.** A couple that lived together for twenty years but never registered a domestic partnership or civil union has no share under the sections described above. If this is your situation, see [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples).
- **A spouse with a pending divorce.** Under N.J.S.A. 3B:5-3(d), a person who has filed a complaint for divorce, dissolution of a civil union, termination of a domestic partnership, or divorce from bed and board, and has not had it dismissed, is not treated as a surviving spouse or partner.
- **Friends, caregivers and charities.** None of them inherit without a will or a beneficiary designation.

A spouse who ends up with a small share or none at all has another option, covered in [New Jersey's elective share for a surviving spouse](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse). It applies when a will leaves a spouse too little, and it is a separate right from intestacy.

## What does intestacy not cover?

The default rules reach only probate property. Life insurance, a 401(k) or IRA with a named beneficiary, a joint bank account, and anything held in a living trust go to the named person or the surviving owner. A house titled in the decedent's name alone, a bank account with no beneficiary, and a car in one name fall under the statute. Our [New Jersey probate guide](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) explains how those assets move, and the [small estate affidavit page](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) covers a shortcut for modest estates.

## What does a family do after someone dies without a will?

Start by looking for a will. Check the home, a safe deposit box, and the lawyer or accountant the person used. If none turns up, the family applies to the county Surrogate to be named administrator, usually a spouse or adult child. The administrator collects assets, pays debts and taxes, and distributes the rest by the chart above. State inheritance tax can apply to some heirs, so read our guide to [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) before anyone receives money.

## How do you avoid these rules?

Sign a will. It lets you choose who receives what, name an executor, and, if you have young children, name a guardian. See [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) so that yours is signed properly. If you want to treat children unequally or leave something to a person the statute ignores, a will is the only tool that works on its own.
