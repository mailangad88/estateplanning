If you are writing a will in New Jersey, the first practical question is who will carry it out. If you were named in someone else's will, the question is whether you are allowed to take the job, and whether living in Florida or Colorado gets in the way. In both cases the real answer is shorter than people fear: a capable adult can serve, wherever they live, with a few extra steps for an out-of-state executor.

This page is part of our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). It covers age and capacity, out-of-state executors, the bond question and how to name backups. For what happens once someone says no, read [when a named New Jersey executor will not or cannot serve](/learn/new-jersey/when-a-named-new-jersey-executor-will-not-or-cannot-serve). For the job itself, see [executor duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step).

## Who is allowed to be an executor in New Jersey?

A person you trust, who is a competent adult, and who is willing. New Jersey gives you wide freedom to choose, and the statutes cited on this page do not hand out a short list of approved relatives. The executor can be your spouse, an adult child, a sibling, a friend, a lawyer or a bank. The same person can be the main beneficiary, which is common when a spouse inherits everything.

The limits show up in the removal statute. A court may remove a fiduciary who is "incapacitated for the transaction of business," who embezzles or wastes estate property, or who ignores court orders (N.J.S.A. 3B:14-21). Those are the traits to screen for on the front end. Pick someone who answers the phone, keeps papers in order and can stay calm with grieving relatives.

### How old does an executor have to be?

Choose an adult, and ask the Surrogate's office in the county that will handle the estate to confirm its age and capacity requirements before you rely on a young adult. A 19-year-old niece may be fully capable. A 17-year-old cannot practically sign the oath, the bond or the estate bank paperwork. If you are leaning toward a young adult, name an older backup so the estate does not stall.

### Can a beneficiary serve?

Yes, and it is very common. It is also where most family arguments start, because the executor decides what is sold, when and for how much while holding a share of the result. Our article on [an executor who is also a beneficiary](/learn/probate/executor-who-is-also-a-beneficiary) covers the conflicts. The fix is the same as for any fiduciary: keep receipts, report to the other beneficiaries, and do not pay yourself without a record.

## Can an executor live outside New Jersey?

Yes, with two extra steps. Anyone appointed as executor in New Jersey, including a person who lives elsewhere, must file a written power of attorney with the Surrogate or court clerk who made the appointment. The paper lists the executor's address and names the Surrogate and successors as the person who may receive legal papers for the estate. Service on the Surrogate counts as service on the executor (N.J.S.A. 3B:14-47). The second step is the bond, below.

Distance also has practical costs. A house in Monmouth County does not mow itself, and a Surrogate expects forms returned on time. An executor in Arizona needs someone local: a trusted cousin, a property manager or an attorney. If the executor later leaves the state and neglects the estate, that can itself be a ground for removal (N.J.S.A. 3B:14-21).

Here is a quick comparison of two executors for the same estate.

| | Dana, lives in Cherry Hill | Marcus, lives in Phoenix |
|---|---|---|
| Files power of attorney naming Surrogate | Yes | Yes |
| Bond when the will is silent | Depends on the will | Yes, unless the will waives it |
| Visits the house, bank, Surrogate | Same day | Needs a local helper |
| Risk if the estate drags | Low | Higher, because problems go unseen |

Marcus is not a bad choice. He is a choice that needs a plan, and the plan costs a little more.

## Does an executor in New Jersey need a bond?

Not if your will says no, in most cases. New Jersey's bond statute makes the court or Surrogate demand a bond when there is no will naming a fiduciary, when a replacement is appointed for the person your will named, and for any form of administration, with an exception for a surviving spouse who takes the entire estate. It also requires a bond when letters go to a nonresident executor, unless the will says no security is required (N.J.S.A. 3B:15-1). That waiver sentence is cheap to write and worth including.

There is one carve-out to ask about. Where a beneficiary has a developmental disability and the person who wrote the will identified that, or the applicant knows of it, the statute can require a bond despite a waiver, with exceptions that include a family member within the third degree and a small estate (N.J.S.A. 3B:15-1). If you have a child with special needs, raise this in the first meeting. Our page on [special needs planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey) is the place to start.

The amount is not a fixed figure. The court or Surrogate sets it based on the estate and the executor's authority, and the cost is charged by the surety company that issues the bond. Ask the county office what it will accept before you apply. Bonds and releases come up again at the end of an estate, which we cover in [refunding bonds and releases when a New Jersey estate closes](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-refunding-bonds-and-releases-when-an-estate-closes).

## How should you name backup executors?

Name at least one, in order, and say what each needs to do. A will that names only one person fails the day that person dies, declines or is too sick to act. Then a court-appointed administrator with the will annexed steps in, usually bonded and not someone you chose.

A workable backup clause does four things.

1. Names a first backup and, if you can, a second.
2. States that the backup serves if the first person dies, declines, resigns or cannot act.
3. Repeats the bond waiver for each backup.
4. Says whether co-executors must act jointly or may act alone.

Co-executors are a trade-off. They share the work and keep an eye on each other. They also need two signatures, two schedules and two opinions. When one of two leaves, the survivor can continue without a new appointment as long as at least one remains qualified (N.J.S.A. 3B:14-1). If your children get along poorly, one executor with a clear reporting duty usually works better than two who split the work.

A practical example. Helen, a widow in Morris County, names her daughter Priya, who lives in Seattle, as executor and her son Anil, who lives ten minutes away, as the first backup. Helen's will waives bond for both. When Helen dies, Priya accepts but hires a local attorney for the house. If Priya had declined, Anil could apply the same week. Nothing has to wait for a judge.

## What do you ask the person before naming them?

Ask them in person, and ask the hard questions. These questions work.

- Are you willing to do this for a year or more?
- Are you comfortable dealing with banks, a county office and our family at the same time?
- Do you live nearby, and if not, will you hire local help?
- Can you accept the commission rules or waive them? See [New Jersey executor commissions and probate timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline).
- Will you read the will and the letters I leave, and call my lawyer early?

People say yes to a general request and no to the details. Better to learn it now. Our broader article on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) covers the personal side of the decision, and the [probate process in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) shows what the person will actually face.

### A common mistake

The most common mistake our attorneys see is naming the oldest child by default, without asking, and without a backup. The oldest child lives far away, has a demanding job and quietly dreads the role. Four years later, the will is stale and the estate stalls. Choose for fit, not for order of birth, and explain your choice in a short letter to the others.
