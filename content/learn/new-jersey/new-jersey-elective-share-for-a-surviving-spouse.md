A New Jersey will can leave a husband, wife or partner almost nothing. The law does not let that stand without limits. A surviving spouse or partner has the right to override the will and claim a fixed fraction of the estate. That right is called the elective share.

This page covers who qualifies, how big the share is, what property is counted, how and when to claim, and what ends the right. It is a New Jersey companion to the [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). For the general idea across states, see our article on the [spouse elective share and disinheriting a spouse](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse).

## What is the elective share in New Jersey?

When a married person, civil union partner or domestic partner dies domiciled in New Jersey, the survivor "has a right of election to take an elective share of one-third of the augmented estate" (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-1). Domiciled means New Jersey was the decedent's permanent home. The [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) works whether or not there is a will. It matters most when a will leaves a spouse little, or leaves everything to children from a first marriage.

The Legislature widened the section in a 2024 law (P.L. 2023, c.238, approved January 8, 2024), which names civil union partners and domestic partners along with spouses. Older articles you may find online still describe the right as limited to spouses.

## Who qualifies and who does not?

A surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner qualifies if the decedent was domiciled in New Jersey at death. Two things cut the right off.

- **A pending divorce.** The right does not apply if either person had filed a complaint for divorce, dissolution of a civil union, termination of a domestic partnership, or divorce from bed and board, and it was not dismissed. The statute ties this to the same definition used for intestacy in N.J.S.A. 3B:5-3(d).
- **A waiver.** Rights of election may be waived, wholly or partly, before or after marriage (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-10). A premarital agreement that gives up "all rights" in the other's estate is typically read as a waiver of the elective share too, unless the agreement says otherwise. If a prenuptial agreement exists, read it before you assume the right is alive. See [prenuptial agreements and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).

Unmarried partners who never registered have no elective share.

## What property counts as the augmented estate?

The augmented estate is wider than the probate estate. The statute starts with the estate, reduced by funeral and administration expenses and enforceable claims, and adds certain transfers the decedent made during the marriage or partnership to someone other than the survivor for less than full value (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-3). The added categories are:

1. Transfers made after May 28, 1980 in which the decedent kept possession, enjoyment or the right to income.
2. Transfers where the decedent kept a power to revoke, consume, invade or dispose of the principal for their own benefit.
3. Property the decedent held with another person with a right of survivorship.
4. Transfers made within two years of death, to the extent that gifts to any one person in either of those years exceeded $3,000.

The idea is simple. A person should not be able to shrink the survivor's share by moving assets into a revocable trust or joint accounts just before death. Whether a particular account, policy or retirement plan lands inside the calculation is a detailed question. Have an attorney run the numbers rather than guessing.

**A worked example.** Tom dies with a will that leaves everything to his two adult children from a first marriage. His wife, Rosa, gets nothing. Say the augmented estate, after funeral costs, administration costs and claims, comes to $600,000. Rosa can elect one-third, which is $200,000. The rest passes as the will directs, adjusted for the amount Rosa takes. How the election is paid, and which transferees must contribute, is covered in the later sections of the same chapter (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-18 and 3B:8-19).

## How do you claim it, and what is the deadline?

The survivor claims the share by filing a complaint in the Superior Court within six months after a personal representative is appointed (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-12). Before the six months run out, the court may extend the time for good cause, with notice to interested people. After the period closes, the safe assumption is that the right is gone.

Here is how it usually unfolds.

1. A will is probated, or an administrator is appointed, and the county Surrogate issues letters. The six-month clock starts with the appointment.
2. The survivor, or the survivor's attorney, files the complaint in Superior Court.
3. The survivor gives notice of the hearing to the executor, heirs and others affected (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-13).
4. The court decides the amount and orders how the share is satisfied.

Count the days from the date of appointment, not from the date of death. A survivor who is grieving, or who has not seen the will, can lose the right simply by waiting. If you are in that position, read our guide to the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) so you know when appointment happens.

## Can the right be lost for other reasons?

Yes. The right is personal. It may be exercised only during the survivor's lifetime, so heirs cannot claim it later (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-11). A guardian for an incapacitated survivor needs a court order first.

A survivor who elects can still change course. The demand may be withdrawn any time before final judgment (N.J.S.A. 3B:8-14). Because the election can shift the whole division of the estate, other beneficiaries often respond with their own filings.

## How does this fit with intestacy and a will?

Without a will, the survivor already receives a statutory share, and that share is often larger than one-third. Our article on [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) shows the numbers. The elective share is a floor that matters mostly when a will leaves too little. If you are writing a will and want your spouse to receive less than the law allows, you will need a signed waiver, and both spouses should have their own lawyers look at it. A will that meets [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements) does not by itself defeat the election.

## Why do families get surprised by the elective share?

Most surprises come from a plan that looks complete on paper. Say Helen and Walter marry late in life, each with grown children. Walter signs a will that leaves his house and savings to his children and says nothing about Helen, because he assumes she will be fine on her own pension. When he dies, the children expect to inherit on the terms of the will. Helen, or her lawyer, can instead ask the Superior Court for one-third of the augmented estate. The children learn this only when the complaint arrives.

Two lessons follow. First, a will alone cannot disinherit a spouse in New Jersey. A written waiver, ideally signed with the advice of separate counsel, is how couples in second marriages protect children from earlier relationships. Second, moving assets into a trust or a joint account does not necessarily put them out of reach, because the augmented estate looks back at several kinds of transfers. Our article on [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) covers the tools that do work.

## What should a spouse do right now?

- Get the dates. Find out whether and when a personal representative was appointed.
- Collect records of accounts, trusts and transfers from the past two years.
- Look for any premarital or postnuptial agreement.
- Talk to an attorney before the six months pass.
