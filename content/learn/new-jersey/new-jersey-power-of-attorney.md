A power of attorney is the document your family will reach for first if you are in an accident or develop dementia. It lets someone you trust pay your bills, deal with banks and sign papers for you when you cannot. If you do not have one, the alternative may be a court case for guardianship.

New Jersey governs these documents in the Revised Durable Power of Attorney Act, N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.1 and following. This page explains what the Act says about signing, durability, the agent's job, gifts and revocation. It is a New Jersey companion to the [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey). The Act covers financial powers. For medical decisions, see [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives).

## What does the New Jersey Act actually say?

The Act, passed in 2000 (P.L.2000, c.109), uses slightly old vocabulary. The person who signs is the principal. The person chosen to act is called the attorney-in-fact, and we call them the agent here. The agent can be one or more individuals or a qualified bank (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.2).

A power of attorney is a written instrument that authorizes the agent to act for the principal. It is durable if it includes language showing it should survive disability or incapacity. Unless the document defines it differently, a principal is under a disability when they cannot manage their property and affairs effectively.

## How do you sign a valid New Jersey power of attorney?

The Act asks for three things: it must be in writing, signed and acknowledged (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.9). Acknowledgment is a formal statement before an authorized officer, such as a notary public, that you signed willingly. The Act cites the state's acknowledgment statute, R.S. 46:14-2.1.

A sensible signing looks like this.

1. Decide who your agent and your successor agents will be.
2. Choose the powers. A broad grant is common, but you can limit it.
3. Put in the durability language so the document survives incapacity.
4. Sign in front of a notary and take the signed original with you.
5. Give copies to the agent and to your financial institutions.

The formality section does not require witnesses. Banks, though, are often cautious about unfamiliar documents, so check what they expect. Our article on [whether a power of attorney needs a notary](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-a-power-of-attorney-need-to-be-notarized) covers the practical side.

## When does the power start, and does it survive incapacity?

Durability is the point of this document. Without the right language, a power of attorney can fail the moment you lose capacity, which is when you need it. With it, acts the agent takes while the power is in effect bind you and your successors as if you were competent (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.3). Unless the document sets an end date, the power remains exercisable despite the passage of time.

Whether the power starts today or only on a future event is a drafting choice. A power that starts at signing is simpler and avoids arguments about proof of incapacity. A power that waits for a trigger is called a [springing power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney), and it often slows banks down. Think hard about which one fits your family.

If a court later appoints a guardian or conservator over your property, your agent answers to that person as well as to you. You can use the document to nominate whom you would want the court to choose (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.4).

## What can a New Jersey agent do?

That depends on what the document grants. A common form gives broad authority over bank accounts, bills, investments, taxes, insurance and real estate. The Act itself stays at the level of structure: it deals with how the authority begins, who may rely on it and how the agent is held to account. It does not give a menu of powers.

A few structural rules matter in real life.

- **Several agents.** The document may let co-agents act separately or require them to act together. If it says nothing, they must act jointly. Unless the document says otherwise, the remaining agents may exercise all the authority after one dies, resigns or becomes disabled (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.7).
- **Successor agents.** The document may name successors who act only after the first agent dies, resigns or becomes disabled.
- **Delegation.** The agent may hand powers to others only if the document expressly allows it (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.8).
- **Real estate.** County recording offices have their own practices, so read [power of attorney and real estate](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate) before a sale.

## Can an agent make gifts?

The Act I read does not address gifts. It neither authorizes them nor sets a limit. That silence is the hazard. An agent who starts giving away your money, even to your own children, will be asked what in the document allowed it. Most attorneys therefore handle gifts in the document itself.

If you want gifting powers, say so clearly and set boundaries: who may receive gifts, a yearly dollar ceiling, and whether the agent may make gifts to himself or herself. For context, the federal gift tax annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, which many people use as a reference figure. If you do not want gifts, say that too. Our article on [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) covers more limits. A worry about large transfers also touches [long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid), where gifts can affect eligibility.

## What duties does the agent owe?

The agent is a fiduciary. Under N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.13 the agent owes a duty to the principal, and to a guardian if one has been appointed, to act within the granted powers and solely for the principal's benefit. The agent must keep accurate financial records. The principal, a guardian or conservator, or the personal representative may require an accounting, and the Superior Court can order one on the application of an heir or next friend.

Pay is up to you. The document, or a separate agreement, can say whether the agent is paid and how. Without direction, a court can award reasonable compensation (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.12). For a plain-language list of the job, see [what a power of attorney agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do). If you fear misuse, read about [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

## How do you revoke a New Jersey power of attorney?

Under N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.10, a power of attorney is revoked when any of these happens.

1. All executed originals are physically destroyed.
2. The principal signs and acknowledges a written revocation.
3. The principal delivers a written revocation to the agent.

A later power of attorney does not cancel an earlier one unless it says so in plain words. If you are replacing your document, put an express revocation clause in the new one and tell the banks. Third parties may rely on the old authority until they receive actual notice of revocation, termination or your death (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.6), so notice matters as much as the paperwork. See [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) for the other triggers.

## Does the power of attorney help after death?

No, it is not a substitute for a will. It exists to manage your affairs while you live. The Act protects an agent who acts in good faith without knowledge of the principal's death, but it does not extend the agent's role. After death, your executor takes over. For how those roles differ, see [power of attorney versus executor](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-executor).
