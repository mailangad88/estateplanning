It is month seven. Your father's will was probated, you were named as a beneficiary, and the executor, your brother-in-law, has not called. You do not know whether the house is listed, whether the credit card is paid, or whether anyone has opened an estate account. Every email gets "working on it."

This page is for that stage. It explains what New Jersey law gives you at each level, from a polite letter to a Superior Court filing, and when the law says to wait. For the executor's side of the work, see [executor duties in New Jersey, step by step](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step), and for the general picture our [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Is the executor actually late?

Often not yet. New Jersey law sets a one-year marker. N.J.S.A. 3B:17-2 lets an executor or administrator settle accounts through the Superior Court, or lets the court require it, but says the representative generally cannot be required to account until after one year from appointment, unless special cause is shown. Our article on [executor commissions and the probate timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline) explains why a year is common for estates with a house or an inheritance tax filing.

So the first question is not "why is this so slow?" It is "what has to happen, and what is blocking it?" A sale pending on the house, an unresolved creditor claim and a tax waiver still waiting for the state are ordinary reasons. Silence is not. A good executor can tell you where things stand even when nothing has moved.

## What is the first step if the executor will not talk?

Write a letter. Date it, send it by email and by regular mail, keep a copy, and ask for facts rather than complaints.

1. State your relationship: beneficiary under the will of the named decedent.
2. Ask for the date letters were issued and the county.
3. Ask for a list of the estate's assets and their approximate value.
4. Ask what debts, taxes and expenses are outstanding.
5. Ask for the expected timeline for distribution.
6. Give a reasonable response date, such as 30 days.

Call before you write if you can stand to. A conversation costs nothing, and sometimes the executor is simply overwhelmed. The most common cause of silence is an executor who has never done this before and is afraid of saying the wrong thing. Our article on [what to do when an executor is not doing their job](/learn/probate/executor-not-doing-their-job) has more on that stage.

## What can you get without the executor?

More than most heirs realize, because probate is a public county process.

- **The will.** Once probated, it is on file with the Surrogate of the county.
- **The case record.** The Surrogate can tell you who was appointed as executor or administrator and when, which fixes your one-year clock.
- **Bond status.** If an administrator posted a surety bond, the Burlington County Surrogate notes that only the Surrogate can release the surety once the refunding bonds and releases are filed.
- **An informal accounting request.** Burlington County's Surrogate says an informal accounting may be requested by next of kin or, where there is an executor, by beneficiaries named in the will, and that no request should be made until one year after probate.

Our article on [finding unclaimed property for a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/finding-unclaimed-property-for-a-new-jersey-estate) is worth a look if you suspect assets are sitting unnoticed.

## When is it time to see an attorney?

When your letter has gone unanswered for the period you gave, or when you see signs that point beyond mere slowness.

| What you see | What it may signal | Likely step |
|---|---|---|
| No reply for months, no contact | Overwhelmed or avoiding | Written request, then attorney letter |
| House sits empty, bills unpaid | Neglect | Attorney letter citing duties |
| Executor refuses to share any numbers | Possible problem with the account | Informal accounting request, then court after one year |
| Executor lives out of state and ignores the estate | Neglect of administration | Consider a removal petition |
| Estate money used for the executor's own expenses | Misuse of estate property | Prompt consultation |
| A release arrives with no figures | Pressure to sign blind | Do not sign; ask for the account |

An attorney letter does two things at once. It tells the executor that someone is watching, and it states which duties are being questioned. For many estates, that ends the stalling.

## How do you force an accounting in court?

Rule 4:87-1(b) lets an interested person start an action to compel a fiduciary to settle an account and, in suitable cases, to require an inventory and appraisal. The executor then has to produce an account, and beneficiaries can object. Our article on [court accountings versus informal releases](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-court-accounting-or-informal-releases-for-an-estate) walks through that process and its costs.

Timing is the main constraint. Under 3B:17-2, an account generally cannot be compelled before a year has passed since appointment, unless special cause is shown. Special cause might be signs of waste, an executor who has left the state or a pattern of ignoring requests. A judge decides, so describe facts, not feelings.

## When can the court remove an executor?

N.J.S.A. 3B:14-21 lists the grounds. The court may remove a fiduciary who:

- Ignores a court order to file an inventory, file an account or post security, after notice and within the time set.
- Disobeys any other court order made under its authority, after notice.
- Misuses estate property through theft, waste or misapplication, or otherwise betrays the trust placed in the fiduciary.
- No longer lives or keeps an office in New Jersey and neglects the estate.
- Becomes unable to conduct business.
- Is one of several co-fiduciaries who fail to perform duties or act jointly, hindering proper settlement.

Look at what the list does not say. Delay by itself is not on it. Delay that follows a court order, or that comes with neglect by an executor who has left the state, is. That is why heirs are usually advised to compel an account first: if the executor ignores that order, removal becomes available on a clear ground.

If a fiduciary is removed or discharged, 3B:14-22 says that does not release the fiduciary or the sureties from liability for estate property received or for neglect, default, wrongdoing or breach of trust during service. Our general article on [whether an executor can be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) covers what happens afterward, and the [refunding bonds and releases](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-refunding-bonds-and-releases-when-an-estate-closes) page explains why you should not sign a release early.

## A short example

Leila's mother died in January. The will named Leila's brother Marcus as executor. By September Leila had received nothing, and no one would say whether the house had been listed. She sent a letter with six questions and a 30-day date. Marcus answered two. In November she asked the Surrogate for the date of appointment and found it was February, so the first anniversary was the following February. She and a lawyer decided to wait for that date while sending a second, firmer letter. In February the lawyer filed to compel an account. Marcus produced one within weeks.

Leila never sued anyone for being slow. She asked for an account, which is the claim the law supports.
