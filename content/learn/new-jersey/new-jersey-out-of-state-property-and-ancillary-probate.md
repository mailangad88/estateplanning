People retire to Florida and keep the shore house. Others grew up in New Jersey, moved to Arizona and still own their parents' old home in Bergen County. Either way, one person ends up with property in two states, and when that person dies, two sets of courts and two tax offices take an interest.

This page follows both directions: a New Jersey resident who owns land elsewhere, and a non-New Jersey resident who owns land here. Our national article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) gives the general idea. Here we stay with what New Jersey offices publish. The home base for the rest is the [New Jersey estate planning guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Why does property in another state need a second proceeding?

Real estate is governed by the state where it sits. A New Jersey Surrogate's letters mean something in New Jersey and not much on a Florida deed. So the executor appointed here usually has to ask a court in the other state for ancillary authority, using certified copies of the New Jersey record. That second case follows the other state's rules on cost, notice and timing, and some states have short procedures for out-of-state executors while others do not.

The New Jersey side of the story is the same in reverse, as the Mercer County Surrogate describes it. Ancillary probate is typically used when the decedent owned real estate in New Jersey, titled tangible property registered here, or livestock, oil, gas or mineral rights tied to real estate. It differs from the main case in one respect that matters: the New Jersey court is not deciding who gets everything. It is only giving someone authority over what is in New Jersey.

## How do you open ancillary probate with a New Jersey Surrogate?

The applicant must be the person appointed executor in the home-state proceeding, according to the Mercer County Surrogate. The Surrogate asks for three things:

1. An exemplified copy of the will and the probate proceedings from the home state, meaning a copy certified under the court's seal.
2. A certified copy of the death certificate.
3. A copy of the deed to the New Jersey real estate, or proof of the personal property located in the county.

File in the county where the property is. Burlington County adds a caution from the other direction: the Surrogate may not act when a nonresident's will is offered and the decedent has no assets in that county. When there was no will, the equivalent is ancillary administration by a court-appointed administrator, with an exemplified copy of the administration proceedings.

New Jersey also has a statute for fiduciaries appointed elsewhere. N.J.S. 3B:14-29 says that once the requirements of N.J.S. 3B:14-28 are met, a fiduciary who is domiciled outside New Jersey may exercise, as to assets in this State, the powers a New Jersey fiduciary would have, and may sue or be sued here. Whether your situation fits that route or needs full ancillary letters is a question for a New Jersey attorney, since it turns on what you are trying to do with the property.

The Mercer page does not describe fees, bond or timing, so ask the Surrogate of the county where the property is. The general probate steps in the county, including letters and the notice to heirs, are in the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process).

## What New Jersey tax applies to a nonresident's property?

The inheritance tax, and only the inheritance tax. The Division of Taxation says that for a nonresident decedent, the tax applies only to real and tangible personal property located in New Jersey, while a resident's tax also reaches all intangible property. It also states there is no New Jersey estate tax for nonresident decedents.

What counts as taxable New Jersey property for a nonresident:

| Asset | Taxed? |
|---|---|
| New Jersey house, land or condo | Yes |
| Cars, boats, furniture, jewelry or art located in New Jersey | Yes |
| Bank accounts, stocks, bonds, mortgages | No, but used in the rate calculation |
| Stock in a corporation that owns New Jersey real estate | No, treated as intangible |
| Partnership interest (bona fide) | No, treated as intangible |
| Real estate outside New Jersey | No |
| New Jersey home held as tenants by the entirety with a surviving spouse | No return or waiver needed |

Two forms matter. Form IT-NR is the return, required unless every beneficiary, inside or outside New Jersey, is in Class A. Form L-9 NR is the real property waiver request, usable when every beneficiary of the entire estate is Class A or Class E. The tax depends on the decedent's residence, not the beneficiaries'. The return and any tax are due eight months after death, interest on late tax is 10% a year, and the tax is a lien on the New Jersey real property for 15 years unless paid sooner or secured by an acceptable bond.

The return offers several ways to compute tax. The simplified method applies 15% to the total of New Jersey real estate, tangible property and certain transfers. The ratio methods compute tax on the whole estate at resident rates and then multiply by the share that is New Jersey property. Either way, you attach the death certificate, letters, the will, the first two pages of the last federal return and the deed, among other items listed on the form. The details of rates and classes are in the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article.

Consider Joan, who lived in Naples, Florida and owned a Long Beach Island cottage worth $900,000 in her name alone, plus $300,000 in Florida accounts. Her daughters inherit everything. Because both are children, every beneficiary is Class A, so the estate files Form L-9 NR for a waiver and no inheritance tax return. Without that waiver the title company will not close a sale. If instead Joan's will left the cottage to her niece, the estate would file Form IT-NR and owe tax on a share of the cottage.

## What happens to the New Jersey resident who owns property elsewhere?

New Jersey's own inheritance tax stays out of it. The Division says a resident's tax applies to real and tangible property in New Jersey plus intangibles, which leaves out real estate in another state. The other state, though, may have its own estate or inheritance tax, and many do. Check the rules where the property sits.

The practical cost is the second proceeding. It means a second lawyer, a second filing fee and usually a few more months. A revocable trust that actually holds the out-of-state deed avoids it, because the trustee already has authority and no court appoints anyone. Our article on [living trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) covers whether a trust is worth it, and [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) lists the other ways to take a property out of the court process. For beneficiary-based options, see [transfer on death options in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-on-death-options-in-new-jersey); whether such an option exists for the out-of-state parcel depends on that state.

A cross-border estate also invites a question about taxes at the federal level. The exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families owe no federal estate tax regardless of how many states hold their property. See our [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## What should you do if you own property in two states?

Start with the deeds. A list of every parcel, how each is titled and who is named on the title is the main document.

- Check whether any parcel is held by tenants by the entirety or with a survivor, which usually passes outside probate.
- Ask an attorney in the other state what it costs to open a proceeding there, and whether a trust avoids it.
- Update your will so it works in both places.
- Keep a certified copy of the will with your records so the executor has what the Surrogate wants.
- Review everything when you move. Our [plan finder](/plan-finder) shows which documents apply.
