When someone who worked for a New Jersey school district, a municipality or the State dies, the family often finds out there is more than one pension-related payment, and that each one follows its own paperwork. Some of it comes from the pension fund and some from group life insurance. Almost none of it is controlled by the will.

This article walks through how the Division of Pensions and Benefits, known as NJDPB, handles death claims for the State-administered systems, how the beneficiary designation works and what goes wrong when it is out of date. The general principle that a form beats a will is explained in [claiming life insurance, pension and VA survivor benefits](/learn/after-a-death/claiming-life-insurance-pension-and-va-survivor-benefits). The state overview is in our [New Jersey estate planning and probate guide](/learn/new-jersey).

## Which systems does this cover?

The Division administers PERS (Public Employees' Retirement System), TPAF (Teachers' Pension and Annuity Fund), PFRS (Police and Firemen's Retirement System), SPRS (State Police Retirement System) and JRS (Judicial Retirement System), plus the Workers' Compensation Judges part of PERS. Each has separate laws. The NJDPB fact sheet on death claims says its first letter to the family specifies the exact amounts payable.

## Who do you notify, and what happens next?

Call the Division at (609) 292-7524 or use its online contact form. Give the member's full name, Social Security, pension membership or retirement number, the date of death, and the name, mailing address and phone number of the person handling affairs.

The Division's fact sheet is firm on one point: do not submit the claim to Prudential, which provides the group insurance. The State Treasurer administers and pays death claims directly.

Then the sequence is this.

1. The Division reviews the account and sends the named beneficiaries a letter with claim forms and instructions.
2. Each beneficiary returns the completed forms with a certified death certificate.
3. Any uncashed pension checks sent to the deceased retiree go back to the Division too.
4. If the claim is approved, the Treasurer pays the beneficiary directly.

For the group life coverage, the PERS group life summary says approved claims are paid within 60 days of proof of death and denials are sent within 45 days. It also says payment is normally a lump sum, and that installments of at least $25 can be arranged only if the beneficiary is a natural person.

For a general list of the other places to notify, see [who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies).

## What does the family receive if the member was still working?

Usually group life insurance, plus either a return of contributions or a survivor's pension, depending on the system. The fact sheet sets this out.

| System | Death while active, per the Division |
|---|---|
| PERS and TPAF | Group life insurance plus a return of the member's contributions |
| PFRS, SPRS and JRS | Group life insurance plus a survivor's pension for a surviving spouse, civil union partner or same-sex domestic partner |
| Workers' Compensation Judges part of PERS | Survivor's pension for a spouse or partner of an active member |

The employer has a job here. It must submit the Certification of Service and Final Salary, Death Claim (form P-29), before benefits can be calculated.

Group life is a multiple of pay. According to the Treasurer's PERS group life summary, active members have basic coverage of 1½ times final compensation, and optional contributory coverage of another 1½ times is available to active members. Service retirees carry 3/16 of final compensation. Final compensation is the pay on which annuity contributions are based during the last year of creditable service. The multiples differ by class and by system, so take the numbers for your own plan from the Division's letter.

A few details trip families up.

- A member who left public service and was waiting on a deferred retirement has no group life. If they die before the deferred retirement is payable, the PERS guidebook says the last named beneficiary receives a return of the pension contributions and no life insurance.
- An unpaid pension loan comes off the top. The guidebook says the outstanding balance is deducted from any benefits paid to the beneficiaries.
- If the member was eligible to retire when they died, the guidebook says the beneficiary may be able to choose between the active death benefit and the retired optional settlement.

## What does the family receive if the member had retired?

It depends on the option chosen at retirement. Group life continues after retirement only if the member had it while active and retired with at least 10 years of pension credit, or retired on a disability retirement, according to the fact sheet.

The PERS guidebook describes the main choices. Under the Maximum Option, pension payments stop at death, and any undistributed contributions with interest go to the beneficiary, along with retirement checks due and payable. Options A through D pay a monthly allowance to the beneficiary for that person's lifetime, and if the beneficiary dies first the allowance rises to the Maximum Option amount. Once retirement is due and payable, the guidebook says the beneficiary under those options cannot be changed.

Take Linda, a retired teacher who chose a survivor option naming her husband Paul. If Paul dies first, the guidebook says the allowance increases to the Maximum Option amount, so what is left for her children after her death depends on the option and the contributions that remain. The group life benefit is a separate matter that follows its own designation. Two different decisions, two different forms.

The Division's retiree designation form gives a second hint. It has one part for the group life benefit and another for remaining pension benefits, each with up to three primary and three contingent beneficiaries.

## How do you name or change a beneficiary?

For PERS, TPAF, PFRS and SPRS, online. The Division no longer accepts paper designation forms for active or retired members of those four systems. Members register for the Member Benefits Online System (MBOS) through the pensions website. Members of JRS, the ABP and the DCRP cannot use MBOS and must use a paper form for their system.

The rules the Division lists:

- Online designations take effect immediately.
- The Division may investigate changes made shortly before death.
- If anyone other than the member uses MBOS, the designation is invalid. The last acceptable designation, or the estate, becomes the designation of record.
- Beneficiaries must be named specifically. "My children" is not accepted. A person, an organization, the estate or a trust can be named.
- Without percentages, benefits are split equally. Exact dollar amounts require a paper form.
- A power of attorney can change a beneficiary only if the document specifically grants that right. To name the agent personally, the document must say so. See [whether a power of attorney can change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary).
- If a court order is on file, the member cannot view or change designations in MBOS and must use a paper form.

Contingent beneficiaries matter because the group life and pension amounts both go to whoever is on file, and the member may have outlived the first choice. [Contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) explains why a second name belongs on every form.

## What about trusts, minors and the estate?

Each needs care. The Division's fact sheet lists these limits.

- Formal trusts, set up by documents filed with a county court, need the trust's name and date of incorporation, and the trustee is asked for a copy at death.
- Testamentary trusts, created by a will, must be probated, and the Surrogate must issue the trustee documents.
- Special needs trusts may be designated in limited circumstances.
- Informal trust agreements are not recognized.
- If the estate is named, a court-ordered surrogate certificate is required when the member dies.
- A beneficiary without a Social Security number must complete federal Form W-8BEN, and the benefit is subject to 30 percent federal withholding. No payment is issued until the form arrives.

For a child, the Division withholds payment until age 18 or until a court-appointed guardian of the property receives it. Naming a trust for the child avoids a guardianship. The same issue arises with any account, as in [naming a minor or incapacitated person as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary).

If a divorce is part of the story, the fact sheet says a new designation replaces all previous ones and the Division can deny a change that violates a court order. After a divorced member's death it may ask for the final judgment and the property settlement agreement before paying.

## Is a pension survivor benefit taxed in New Jersey?

The inheritance tax has its own rules. Our article on [domestic partners and civil union partners](/learn/new-jersey/domestic-partners-and-civil-union-partners-in-new-jersey-estate-law) notes that pension, annuity and retirement payments from a qualified employer plan are exempt when paid to a surviving spouse or domestic partner (N.J.S.A. 54:34-4), and the broader class rules are in [the New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax). Federal income tax on any payment is a separate question for a tax adviser.

## What should you do this month?

If you are a member, log in to MBOS and read what is on file. Check every name against your current wishes, add contingent beneficiaries, and check the retiree group life amounts. If you are a family member, gather the death certificate and the member's pension number before you call. Keep a log of whom you spoke to.
