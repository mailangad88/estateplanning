Parents of adults with disabilities often hear two opposite messages. One is that their child needs a guardian for everything. The other is that the child is an adult and can decide for themselves. The truth sits in between, and it varies by decision.

This page covers three that families ask about most: making a will, signing a power of attorney, and getting married. For each, it explains what the law asks and what to do when the person needs support.

## What does "capacity" mean?

Capacity is the legal ability to understand a particular decision and its consequences. It is not a label that attaches to a diagnosis. A person with Down syndrome may be able to choose a healthcare agent and still need help with a mortgage. A person with a mental illness may have clear periods and cloudy ones. Courts and attorneys ask the same question each time: did this person, on this day, understand this act?

That is why a guardianship that removes all rights is a heavy tool, and why courts and advocates now push for less restrictive options. Our page on [guardianship alternatives for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) lays out the choices, including supported decision-making.

| Decision | What the person must understand | How high the bar is |
|---|---|---|
| Making a will | That this is a will, in general what they own, who their family is, who gets what | Low |
| Signing a power of attorney | What powers the agent has, who the agent is, that the agent can act for them | Moderate |
| Healthcare directive | Who will make medical decisions and what kind of care they want or refuse | Moderate |
| Getting married | That marriage is a legal relationship, and who the partner is | Low, set by state law |
| Signing a contract or lease | The terms, obligations and consequences | Higher |

## Can an adult with a disability make a will?

Yes, if they meet testamentary capacity, which in most states is a lower standard than contract capacity. Our [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) entry gives the definition. The person needs to know they are making a will, have a general idea of what they own, know who their closest relatives are, and be able to connect those pieces into a plan. Complex asset schedules are not required.

Why bother? Because a person with a disability can own things: a car, a bank account, an ABLE account, personal items, perhaps a settlement. With no will, state law decides who gets them, and it may send everything to a parent or relative the person would not have chosen. For a person who receives benefits, a will can also name a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) as the receiver of money passing to someone else with a disability.

Practical steps that protect a will from challenge:

1. Meet with the attorney alone for a part of the visit, so the person's wishes are theirs.
2. Keep notes of what the person said in their own words.
3. Sign when the person is at their best: well rested, medicated as usual, in a quiet room.
4. Consider a letter from a treating doctor who has seen the person recently.
5. Use two witnesses who have no stake and a [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained).

Denise's brother Kyle, 36, has an intellectual disability. He lives in supported housing and owns a small ABLE account and a collection of model trains. He wanted his trains to go to his friend Sam and the rest to his sister. His attorney met with him twice, wrote a one-page will in plain language, and had Kyle explain it back in his own words. If anyone challenges it, the file will show he understood.

## Can an adult with a disability sign a power of attorney?

Often, yes. The standard is practical: does the person understand that they are choosing someone to handle their affairs, and who that person is? Many adults with intellectual or developmental disabilities can say, "I want my sister to help me with bills and doctor stuff." That can be enough, and it is far less intrusive than guardianship.

There are two documents to consider. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) covers money and property. A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) covers medical choices. Some families add a HIPAA release so the person's parents can still talk to providers.

Two cautions. First, a power of attorney is revocable while the person has capacity, so a person can cancel it, sometimes at a bad moment. Second, banks and agencies sometimes resist forms signed by a person with a known disability. A short letter from a doctor and an attorney's cover note reduce that friction. If the person cannot understand and sign, see [getting a power of attorney when someone cannot sign](/learn/power-of-attorney/getting-power-of-attorney-when-someone-cannot-sign).

## Can an adult with a disability get married?

Yes. In nearly every state, a person with a disability has the right to marry, and a guardianship does not automatically take it away. Some states require court approval if the person is under a guardianship, and a few require the guardian's consent. Check your state's rules.

The legal test for marriage is generally low: understanding that marriage is a relationship with legal consequences, and knowing who the partner is. The practical question is more often money.

### How does marriage affect SSI?

Social Security treats married partners as an economic unit. It counts the spouse's income and resources when figuring SSI, and the benefit for a couple is computed differently than for two single people. People who are each on SSI often see a smaller combined payment than they received as individuals. If one spouse has a job or savings, the other's SSI can drop significantly or end.

This is sometimes called the marriage penalty, and it is a reason some couples choose to live together without marrying, or to marry anyway and accept the change. There is no right answer. What matters is that the couple knows the numbers before the wedding. Contact Social Security, or a benefits counselor, and ask for an estimate in writing where you can.

### What about Medicaid and parental benefits?

Medicaid often follows SSI, so a change in one can affect the other, and in many states there are protections that let people keep Medicaid after losing SSI. Disabled adult child benefits on a parent's record generally end on marriage, with exceptions. The federal site at [ssa.gov](https://www.ssa.gov) has the current rules.

The trust matters too. A properly written third-party special needs trust can continue after the beneficiary marries, and the trustee can support both people without the beneficiary having a right to the money. The spouse's own assets may still be counted. Our page on [SSI and Medicaid rules for these trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) explains what a trustee can pay.

## What if someone doubts the person's capacity?

Relatives sometimes raise doubts after a will or a marriage. A few habits blunt later challenges:

- Do not rush. If the person has a bad week, postpone.
- Document the process: who attended, what was said, the doctor's observation.
- Avoid having a person who benefits sit in the room while the document is explained.
- Keep the document consistent with the person's longstanding wishes.

Capacity can also change. A will signed now is still valid if the person later loses capacity, so it is wise to sign while they can. The same is true for powers of attorney: once capacity is gone, they cannot be signed, and the family may be left with guardianship.

## How state law changes this

States set the capacity tests, the witness rules for wills, whether a guardian can consent to marriage, and how courts review supported decision-making agreements. Many states now have statutes recognizing supported decision-making. See your [state estate planning guide](/estate-planning/florida) for local starting points.
