Ruth and Alan signed a special needs trust for their son Caleb eight years ago. Caleb, 24, receives SSI and Medicaid. Since then, their family has changed. Alan's brother, the successor trustee, has moved overseas. Their daughter now seems a better choice. They also want the leftover money to go to Caleb's cousins instead of a charity they chose years ago. They ask whether they can just change it.

The answer to "can a special needs trust be changed or revoked" depends on three things: who put the money in, what the document says, and whether the trust has already become irrevocable. This page explains each. For the basics of how these trusts work, see our [pillar on special needs planning](/learn/special-needs) and the overview of the [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).

## Can a parent revoke or amend a special needs trust?

A parent can usually amend or revoke a third-party special needs trust while the parent is alive, if the document reserved that right. A third-party trust holds money that came from someone other than the beneficiary: parents, grandparents, relatives. Because the money is the parents' own, many attorneys draft it as revocable so the parents keep flexibility. At the first death, or at the death of the surviving parent, depending on the design, it becomes irrevocable.

So Ruth and Alan, both alive, can probably sign an amendment. They can name a new successor trustee, change who receives the remainder, and update the distribution standard. They cannot change the fact that the trust exists to supplement benefits, because that would defeat its purpose.

Check three things in the document:

1. Is there a clause that says "revocable" or "may be amended by the Grantors"?
2. Does it require both parents to sign, or may one act alone?
3. Does it name who can amend after a death, such as a trust protector?

If the document is silent, treat it as locked and use one of the repair methods below.

## When is a special needs trust impossible to revoke?

A first-party special needs trust is irrevocable by its nature. It holds the beneficiary's own money, such as a settlement, an inheritance left outright or back pay, usually under the federal rule called (d)(4)(A). The trust is exempt from counting as the beneficiary's resource precisely because the beneficiary cannot take the money back. A trust that could be revoked would be counted, and the benefits would stop. The same is true for a pooled trust run by a nonprofit.

A first-party trust also carries a payback clause: when the beneficiary dies, the state is repaid for Medicaid it provided before anything goes to family. Our article on the [payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) explains how that works and what it takes from the remainder.

| Type of trust | Can the creator revoke it? | Who can change it after it locks |
|---|---|---|
| Third-party, created by parents | Often yes, while alive | Trust protector, court, or decanting |
| Third-party, created at death in a will | Not applicable; it starts irrevocable | Trust protector or court |
| First-party (d)(4)(A) | No | Court, with state input |
| Pooled trust (d)(4)(C) | No | The nonprofit's rules and a court |

## What are the ways to fix a trust that is already locked?

You still have options when the trust is irrevocable. They fall into four groups, and each has a cost.

- **A trust protector.** Some well-drafted trusts name an independent person who can amend administrative terms, change the trustee, move the trust to another state or fix a clause that conflicts with benefit rules. Our guide to [trust protectors and decanting](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-trust-protector-and-decanting) explains the role.
- **Decanting.** Many states have a statute that lets a trustee pour the assets into a new trust with updated terms. The conditions differ by state. Decanting can be a clean way to correct a drafting error, though it usually cannot change who the beneficiaries are.
- **A nonjudicial agreement.** Where state law permits it, adult beneficiaries, the trustee and sometimes a representative for the disabled beneficiary can sign an agreement that changes administrative terms without a court.
- **A court petition.** A judge can modify or reform a trust when circumstances have changed or a mistake is shown. It takes time and attorneys' fees, and it becomes part of the court record. In a trust for a person with a disability, the court often wants to be sure the beneficiary's benefits are protected.

The lighter the tool, the less it costs. If a trust protector exists, start there.

## What can go wrong if you revoke it?

Revoking an existing trust can cost the beneficiary public benefits. Suppose Ruth and Alan revoke the trust and take the money back. The funds return to them, which is allowed. But if they then pass the money to Caleb directly, even as a gift, SSI would count it as his resource and could stop his benefits when it pushes him over the limit, which is $2,000 for an individual. The article on [what counts as income and resources for SSI](/learn/special-needs/what-counts-as-income-and-resources-for-ssi) lists the line.

The same logic applies to emptying the trust for a large purchase. Spending on the beneficiary's behalf is normal, but a big transfer to the beneficiary, or to a person who might hand it over, is not. See [what special needs trust funds can pay for](/learn/special-needs/what-can-special-needs-trust-funds-pay-for) before the trustee writes a large check.

Another trap is the order of events. If a parent dies while the trust is still unsigned, or if the amendment was never finalized, the trust may lock in the older version. Sign amendments with the same formality as the original: written, dated, signed and notarized.

## Which changes are routine, and which need a lawyer?

Some updates are easy and some are not.

Usually simple, during the parents' lifetimes:

- Naming a new successor trustee or adding a co-trustee.
- Updating addresses and contact details for beneficiaries.
- Changing the remainder beneficiaries, if the trust is a third-party trust.
- Adding a trust protector.

Usually needs legal review:

- Any change to a first-party or pooled trust.
- Moving the trust to another state.
- Changing the distribution standard.
- Fixing a clause that requires cash payments to the beneficiary.
- Adding an asset the trust was not designed to hold, such as a business or house, covered in our article on [special needs trusts and houses](/learn/special-needs/can-a-special-needs-trust-own-a-house).

## What does this look like in New Jersey and Illinois?

Both states permit amendments, nonjudicial settlements and court modifications under their own trust codes, and both give effect to a trust protector when the document creates one. The specifics differ on decanting and on what the court needs to see. See our local guides to [special needs planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey) and [special needs trust rules in Illinois](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-rules-in-illinois) for the points that matter at the benefits level. State agencies run Medicaid, so a trust that was acceptable in one state needs a second look when the beneficiary moves.
