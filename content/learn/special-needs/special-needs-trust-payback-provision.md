Families sometimes find out about the payback provision at the worst moment: in the weeks after a son or sister has died, when the trustee opens a letter from the state Medicaid agency. A payback is not a penalty, and it is not a surprise if you plan for it. It is the price federal law attaches to one kind of trust.

This article explains what it requires, what can be paid before the state, and how families keep it from reaching money that was never the beneficiary's. The broader context is in our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## What is a payback provision?

A payback provision is a clause in a trust that directs the trustee, at the beneficiary's death, to repay the state for Medicaid assistance paid on the beneficiary's behalf before any remaining money goes to other people. Federal law requires it for first-party special needs trusts, sometimes called (d)(4)(A) trusts, and it applies to many pooled trusts as well.

Why does the law ask for this? Because the money in a first-party trust belonged to the beneficiary. Federal rules let that money sit outside the SSI and Medicaid asset count, so that the person can keep benefits. In exchange, the public program that supported them during life is repaid from the leftover. The trade is deliberate.

The clause does not reduce what the trustee can spend during the beneficiary's life. A trustee can pay for therapy, equipment, travel, housing and other support at any time, and the more the trust spends well during life, the less sits there to be claimed later.

## Which trusts have a payback and which do not?

The test is whose money funded the trust.

| Trust | Funded by | Medicaid payback? | Who gets the remainder? |
|---|---|---|---|
| First-party stand-alone ((d)(4)(A)) | The beneficiary's own money | Yes, required | State first, then named relatives |
| Pooled, first-party account ((d)(4)(C)) | The beneficiary's own money | Usually, or the nonprofit retains a share, per the trust terms | Nonprofit and state per the trust |
| Pooled, third-party account | Family money | Often no, but the nonprofit may keep some | Varies by program |
| Third-party stand-alone | Parents, grandparents, friends | No | Anyone the creator names |

If you are weighing the types, our article on [first-party and third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) and our piece on [pooled trusts](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts) cover the choices in more depth.

## How does the payback work in practice?

When the beneficiary dies, the trustee generally follows this order of work.

1. **Secure the trust.** Stop discretionary payments, collect assets, and keep the account open until debts are known.
2. **Notify the state.** The trustee gives the state Medicaid agency notice of the death. In many states the agency has its own form and deadline, so ask early.
3. **Request an itemized claim.** The state tells the trustee what Medicaid paid. Review it for obvious mistakes, such as payments before the trust existed or services that were not Medicaid.
4. **Pay permitted expenses.** The trust document and state policy decide what can come ahead of or alongside the claim. Common candidates are the trust's income taxes, the trustee's reasonable fees, legal costs and some funeral costs.
5. **Pay the state.** The claim is paid out of trust assets. The trust is not usually liable for more than it holds.
6. **Distribute the rest.** Any balance goes to the remainder beneficiaries named in the document, or follows the trust's default.
7. **Account and close.** File the final trust tax return and give a final accounting to those entitled to one. [Trust administration after a death](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) lists the general steps.

### What can be paid before the state?

This is where a well-drafted document earns its fee. Federal law lets some payments come first. States differ in how they read that. Typical items include taxes owed by the trust, fees of an attorney or accountant for the trust, and the beneficiary's funeral and burial expenses, though a few states are narrower. Paying a funeral bill from the trust is often less painful than leaving a family to find the cash. A document that is silent on this can force the trustee to fight for each item.

## How do you keep family money out of the payback?

By keeping the sources separate from the beginning. Three habits do most of the work.

- **Create a third-party trust early.** Parents who can fund a third-party trust through their will or living trust should, so the family has a place to send money that never touches the first-party rules.
- **Tell relatives the trust's name.** A grandmother who writes "to my granddaughter Priya" leaves the money to Priya, outright. A grandmother who writes "to the Priya Raman Supplemental Needs Trust" does not. Give family a sample sentence and the trust's date.
- **Route accounts through the trust.** Name the trust, not the person, as beneficiary on life insurance and retirement accounts. Our article on [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) explains the extra steps for retirement plans.

If an outright gift has already landed in the beneficiary's name, the options narrow. A first-party or pooled trust may be the only route, and it comes with payback. That is a reason to catch the gift before it is paid, which often means a disclaimer or a quick call to the estate's lawyer.

## Is payback ever worth accepting?

Yes, often. A person who wins a large settlement after an injury can use a first-party trust for decades of care, equipment and quality-of-life spending, and the law is clear that the trust is for the person's benefit first. If the alternative is losing SSI and Medicaid, the payback is a cost worth carrying. The goal is to spend wisely during life, not to preserve a balance for the state.

Some families go further and design the trust to be used up: buying an accessible home or vehicle, prepaying certain services allowed under the rules, and funding the person's own ABLE account within the annual limit. Any of those moves should be reviewed first, because a purchase that looks like a shortcut around the payback can be challenged. An [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) has its own state claim at death, so it is not a way around the rule.

## Example: the Brandt family

Teresa Brandt receives a $150,000 settlement for a car accident that left her son Luke, 29, with a brain injury. Luke is on SSI and Medicaid. Their attorney sets up a first-party trust with Teresa as trustee and a successor trustee named. Over fifteen years the trust buys a lift, pays for rehabilitation and home modifications, and covers a supported-living shortfall. When Luke dies at 44, $31,000 remains. The trustee pays the trust's final tax bill and attorney fee, then the funeral home invoice, which the document allowed. The state then files a claim for Medicaid costs that exceeds the balance, and the trustee pays what is left. No relative receives a remainder. Luke's aunt, meanwhile, had left $40,000 to a separate third-party trust, and that trust passes to Luke's cousins as the aunt wished.
