Denise and Paul have two children. Their son Eli is 19 and has autism. He gets SSI and Medicaid and will probably need support for life. They have a $400,000 house, a retirement account and a $250,000 term life insurance policy through Paul's job. The beneficiary form says "Denise," and behind her it says "children, equally."

That form is the problem this page is about. If both parents died tomorrow, half of the policy would go straight to Eli, and his benefits would be in trouble. Life insurance is one of the most practical ways to fund a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust), because it creates a large sum of money exactly when the parents are gone. It only works if the paperwork is right. This is part of our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## How do you use life insurance to fund a special needs trust?

You set up a third-party special needs trust, then make the trust the beneficiary of the policy. When the insured parent dies, the insurer pays the trustee, not Eli. The trustee holds the money under the trust's rules and pays for things that supplement benefits, such as a phone, therapies, travel with a caregiver and housing costs the trust is allowed to cover. Our page on [what the trust can pay for](/learn/special-needs/what-can-special-needs-trust-funds-pay-for) lists them.

A third-party trust is funded with the parents' money, not Eli's own. That is why it does not need a Medicaid payback at Eli's death, and what is left can go to a sibling or charity. The difference is covered in [first-party vs third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust).

The steps look like this:

1. Sign the trust first. A policy cannot name a trust that does not exist yet, so the trust comes before the beneficiary form.
2. Get the exact name and date of the trust from your attorney, such as "Trustee of the Eli Parker Supplemental Needs Trust dated March 3, 2026."
3. Complete the insurer's own beneficiary change form. A line in the will does not change a policy.
4. Name a contingent beneficiary, which is usually the same trust or a back-up trust.
5. Keep a copy of the confirmation from the insurer.

## Why not just name the child?

A payment to a person with a disability is income in the month it arrives and a resource after that, and SSI and Medicaid both have strict limits. A $125,000 death benefit paid to Eli would end his SSI for the month and leave him over the resource limit until he spent the money down. Medicaid eligibility, which is often more valuable than the cash check, follows the same path in most states. Our article on [what counts as income and resources for SSI](/learn/special-needs/what-counts-as-income-and-resources-for-ssi) shows the rules.

Naming the child also creates a practical problem: an adult with a cognitive disability may not be able to manage a lump sum. Insurers do not decide whether someone has capacity, so they will pay out, and then the family may need a guardianship to get control of the money. The same mistake happens with a will that leaves the child a share, which is why we tell parents to review every route, from the will to the retirement account. See [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling).

## Who should own the policy?

Not the child. Ownership of a policy with cash value is a countable resource, and a child on SSI who owns a whole life policy worth more than a small amount can lose benefits. The practical choices are:

| Owner | How it works | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| A parent | The parent owns and pays the policy | Simple and cheap | Included in the parent's estate at death |
| The special needs trust | The trust applies for the policy and pays premiums from parents' gifts | Keeping the policy out of the parent's name | Premium gifts must arrive on time; the trustee must pay |
| An irrevocable life insurance trust | A separate trust owns the policy | Large estates and estate tax | Extra document and annual steps, see [ILITs](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) |
| A sibling | A brother or sister owns the policy on a parent's life | Rarely wise | The sibling's creditors, divorce or death can disturb it |

For most families, parent ownership with the special needs trust as beneficiary is the right answer. The estate tax issue matters less than people think, since the federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. In Illinois, where the state taxes estates above $4 million, a family with a large policy may want a closer look, and the page on the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) explains it. New Jersey has no estate tax, though the [New Jersey special needs planning guide](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey) covers its own benefits issues.

## Term, whole life or a second-to-die policy?

The right type depends on how long the need lasts. Eli will probably need support for 50 or 60 years after his parents are gone, so a policy that expires does not fit.

- **Term insurance** is the cheapest for a young family and is appropriate if the policy covers a gap, such as the years before savings catch up. It ends at a set age and then pays nothing, often just when the parents' health has made new coverage hard or costly to get.
- **Whole life or universal life** lasts for life if the premiums are paid. It costs more, and the cash value is a resource of the parents, not of the child.
- **Second-to-die (survivorship) insurance** covers both parents and pays at the second death. Premiums are lower than two separate permanent policies, and the payout arrives when the child loses both caregivers. The weakness is that nothing is paid at the first death, which can hurt if one parent's income matters.

One family approach is a layered plan: keep the employer's term policy while the kids are young, add a permanent or survivorship policy in the parents' 40s or 50s, and review the amount every five years. How much to buy depends on the lifetime cost of support, which our article on [how much to put in a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/how-much-to-put-in-a-special-needs-trust) works through.

## What happens to the money after the child dies?

In a third-party trust, you decide. The trust says who receives the remainder, and it can be siblings, nieces and nephews or a charity. No Medicaid payback is required, because the money never belonged to the child. A policy bought with the child's own money, by contrast, belongs in a first-party trust with a payback clause, and that is a very different arrangement. Read the [payback provision article](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) if a settlement or inheritance is involved.

## Common mistakes with life insurance and special needs planning

- The beneficiary form says "my children, equally." Parents think the trust handles it, but the insurer follows the form.
- Naming a trust that was never signed.
- Naming the estate, which brings the money into probate and exposes it to creditors.
- Forgetting the employer policy. Group coverage often has its own form, and it is tied to the job, so it can end if the parent leaves.
- Buying more policy than the trust needs while leaving the trustee no instructions. A [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) lists the policy, the agent's name and the premium due dates.
- Choosing a trustee without telling them there is a policy. See [who should be trustee](/learn/special-needs/who-should-be-trustee-of-a-special-needs-trust).
