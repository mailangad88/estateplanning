Parents rarely begin with the legal definition of disability. They begin with a child: a seven-year-old with autism, a nineteen-year-old with Down syndrome, a son whose depression keeps getting worse, a daughter with ADHD who cannot hold on to money. The question is the same in every case. Does my child need a [special needs](/learn/special-needs) trust, or some other kind of trust?

The answer does not follow the diagnosis. It follows two things: what the person can do and what they receive or will receive from the government. This article explains how Social Security thinks about disability, and then walks through the planning that tends to fit different situations.

## How does Social Security define disability?

For an adult, Social Security defines disability as the inability to do substantial gainful activity because of a medically determinable impairment that has lasted or is expected to last at least 12 months, or to result in death. That is a work test. The agency asks what the person can do given their condition, age, education and work history.

For a child under 18, the question is different: does the condition cause marked and severe limits in functioning, such as in learning, communicating, or caring for themselves? When a child turns 18, Social Security applies the adult standard and reviews the case again, which surprises many parents.

Three consequences follow.

- A diagnosis does not decide the outcome. Two adults with the same diagnosis can get opposite answers.
- Evidence matters more than labels. Medical records, school records and statements from people who see the person daily carry weight.
- A person can fall in and out of eligibility. Some conditions improve or relapse, and the planning has to survive both.

The rules for opening a trust track that standard, but not in every case. See [first-party vs third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) for the structural difference.

## When does a person need to meet the definition?

It depends on whose money is in the trust.

| Trust | Funded with | Must the beneficiary meet the SSA definition? | Payback to the state? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Third-party special needs trust | Parents' or relatives' money | No | No |
| First-party (d)(4)(A) trust | The person's own money, such as a settlement | Yes, and usually under 65 at creation | Yes |
| Pooled (d)(4)(C) trust | The person's own money, in a nonprofit's pooled account | Yes | Often, depending on the trust |

The takeaway for parents is that a third-party trust is the flexible tool. You do not have to prove anything to leave your own money to a trust for your child. A first-party trust is a rescue tool for money that already belongs to the person. We cover the pooled version in [pooled special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts).

## How do you plan for autism, Down syndrome or cerebral palsy?

These conditions usually affect a person's whole life, though the range is wide. Some adults with autism hold jobs and live on their own. Some adults with Down syndrome work part time and live in supported housing. Some with cerebral palsy have normal cognition and need physical help only.

That range is the reason to avoid planning by label. A better approach is to describe the child's likely needs in three areas: money, housing and decisions.

1. **Money.** Many of these adults will receive SSI and Medicaid for years, so a third-party [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) is the usual main vehicle.
2. **Housing and care.** Decide who will help coordinate services and housing, and write it down in a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent).
3. **Decisions.** Review the alternatives before the child turns 18, including supported decision-making and powers of attorney, covered in [guardianship alternatives for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children).

Sasha and Tom, whose son Miguel has Down syndrome, set up a third-party trust, named Miguel's aunt as trustee with a professional co-trustee, and left their life insurance to the trust. They kept Miguel's name off every account. At 22 he gets SSI and Medicaid, a small job, and a trust that pays for dental work, a phone and a yearly trip. That is a typical, workable plan.

## What about ADHD or a learning disability?

Most children with ADHD, dyslexia or a learning disability will never receive SSI, and many manage work and independent adult life well. A special needs trust is usually the wrong tool. It adds restrictions designed for a benefit the child may never use.

A flexible discretionary trust is a better fit. It lets the trustee pay for coaching, education and housing, hold back money in difficult years, and release more as the child matures. The trustee follows a clear standard, as described in [trust distribution standards](/learn/trusts/trust-distribution-standards-hems-and-discretion). If you worry about a future change, you can add a provision that lets the trustee treat the trust as a supplemental needs trust if the child ever qualifies for benefits.

## How do you plan for mental illness or addiction?

These are the hardest cases, because the person's capacity and needs can change from year to year. A son with schizophrenia may be stable for a decade, then need intensive help. A daughter with bipolar disorder may work for years, then be unable to. A person in recovery may be fine, or may relapse.

A few design points help.

- **Discretion over rules.** A trustee with discretion can adjust to the year, rather than a trust that distributes on a schedule.
- **No outright inheritance.** A lump sum can disappear in a bad stretch or end benefits.
- **A trustee who is willing to say no.** A family member who cannot refuse a request is a poor choice. Our piece on [who should be trustee of a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/who-should-be-trustee-of-a-special-needs-trust) covers the choice.
- **Coordination with a mental health directive.** The person may want to name an agent for treatment decisions. See [mental health advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/mental-health-advance-directive).
- **Addiction protections.** A spendthrift clause and a trustee who pays vendors directly are standard. See [when a beneficiary is struggling with addiction](/learn/what-if/beneficiary-bankrupt-in-prison-divorcing-or-struggling-with-addiction).

## What if my child is not disabled but cannot manage money?

This is more common than people admit. An adult child may have a gambling problem, a controlling spouse, a history of financial trouble, or simply no interest in managing a large sum. None of this makes them disabled in the legal sense, and a special needs trust is not available or appropriate.

The answer is an ordinary spendthrift or discretionary trust. It protects the money from creditors and from a divorce, and it lets the trustee pay for housing, health care and education directly. Our guide to [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) goes into the details.

## Should you leave a child with a disability out of your will?

Usually not, even though parents are sometimes told to do it. In most states, a parent may disinherit an adult child, and doing so protects the child's means-tested benefits in the short run. But it also leaves the child without the money you wanted them to have.

The better route is to leave the child's share to a third-party special needs trust. The child stays in the plan, the trustee supplements benefits instead of replacing them, and nothing counts against eligibility. For the mechanics, see [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child or sibling](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling).

## What should you do next?

1. Collect the child's diagnosis letters, school evaluations and benefit notices in one folder.
2. Ask yourself whether benefits are likely in the next ten years. If yes or maybe, plan for a third-party special needs trust.
3. Choose a trustee and a backup, and write your wishes in a letter of intent.
4. Review beneficiary designations on life insurance and retirement accounts so none of them name the child directly.
5. Meet with an attorney well before the child turns 18.
