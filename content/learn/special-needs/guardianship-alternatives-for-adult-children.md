On a young person's 18th birthday, something changes that many parents do not expect. The law now sees an adult, even if that adult needs help with medicine, money or safety. The pediatrician may stop talking to you. The school may stop sharing records. A bank may refuse to talk to you about an account.

This guide explains what changes at 18, the main options for keeping your child supported, how guardianship works and when it may be right, and what to do about SSI and Medicaid. The goal is to match the amount of legal help to the person's actual needs, no more and no less.

## What changes legally at 18?

Parents' automatic authority ends and the young adult gains full legal rights. That includes consenting to or refusing medical care, signing contracts, controlling bank accounts, voting, and making decisions about school and living arrangements. Privacy rules also apply: HIPAA and FERPA protect an adult's health and school records, and providers need the adult's permission, or a legal order, to share them.

For many young people with disabilities, this is fine with some support. For others, it creates real risk, such as an adult who cannot evaluate a medical decision or is vulnerable to exploitation. The task for families is to decide which category their loved one falls into, and to avoid a gap in the first weeks after the birthday.

### What happens with special education?

Under federal law, special education rights under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act transfer to the student at the age of majority in most states, and the school must notify the student and parents in advance. The student can then make decisions about their Individualized Education Program. A student who wants parent involvement can sign a consent or give a power of attorney for education decisions, and some states allow a guardian or an "educational representative." Ask the school what your state does, ideally at the IEP meeting when the student is 16.

## What are the options?

Think of the options as a ladder from least to most restrictive.

| Option | What it does | Court needed? | Rights the person keeps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Informal support | Family helps without legal papers | No | All, but third parties may not cooperate |
| Releases and consents (HIPAA, FERPA) | Lets providers and schools share information | No | All |
| Representative payee | Manages Social Security benefits | No (SSA appoints) | All other rights |
| Supported decision-making | Trusted people help the adult understand and decide | Usually no | All decision rights |
| Power of attorney | Names an agent for financial decisions | No | Rights remain; can revoke if able |
| Healthcare proxy | Names an agent for medical decisions | No | Rights remain; can revoke if able |
| Joint bank account or ABLE account | Shared access to money | No | Varies |
| Limited guardianship | Court gives authority in specific areas | Yes | Rights outside those areas |
| Full guardianship | Court gives authority over most decisions | Yes | Few |

### Can a young adult with a disability sign a power of attorney?

Often yes. Signing a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) or healthcare proxy requires that the person understands what they are signing, who they are choosing, and the effect, not that they could manage a complex financial portfolio. Many people with intellectual or developmental disabilities can meet that test, especially with a patient explanation. An attorney can often tell after a meeting whether the person is able to sign. If the person is able, these documents let parents act while the young adult keeps their rights. See [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [HIPAA authorizations](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) for details.

### What is supported decision-making?

It is an approach in which the person chooses trusted people, such as parents, siblings or friends, to help them gather information, understand options, and communicate decisions, but the person makes the decision. A growing number of states have laws recognizing a written supported decision-making agreement, and some require that third parties like doctors accept it. States without a law may still honor the practice informally. It fits many young adults who need help but do not need someone else deciding for them.

### What does a representative payee do?

A representative payee is a person or organization that the Social Security Administration appoints to receive and manage benefits for someone who cannot manage them. The payee must use the money for the person's needs and report to SSA. It is not the same as guardianship. Many parents become payee for an adult child without ever going to court.

## What is guardianship, and when is it appropriate?

Guardianship, called conservatorship in some states, is a court process that appoints someone to make decisions for an adult found unable to make some or all decisions safely. The person appointed is a [guardian](/glossary/guardian). In some states, guardianship covers personal decisions and conservatorship covers money, while other states use one term for both. See [conservatorship](/glossary/conservatorship) for how the terms vary.

Guardianship may be appropriate when:

- The person cannot understand or communicate choices about medical care, housing or safety even with support
- The person cannot sign a power of attorney or healthcare proxy
- The person is at serious risk of harm or exploitation
- A third party, like a hospital or bank, refuses to work with any alternative and the need is pressing

The court generally requires a petition, notice to the person and relatives, a medical or psychological evaluation, an attorney or guardian ad litem for the person, and a hearing. Costs run from a few thousand to well over ten thousand dollars depending on the state and whether anyone objects, and the guardian usually files reports every year.

### What are the downsides?

Guardianship removes legal rights, which can be demoralizing and, if it is broader than necessary, harmful to the person's development and dignity. It requires annual reporting, can be expensive, and can create family conflict over who serves. A guardian may also need court permission to make some decisions. Federal and state policy increasingly favors limited guardianship and less restrictive alternatives, and many states require the court to find that alternatives would not work.

### What is limited guardianship?

Limited guardianship gives a guardian authority only over areas where the person needs help, such as medical consent, while the person retains the right to decide where to work or whom to see. If your state offers it, and it matches your child's needs, it is often the more respectful and more defensible choice.

## What happens to SSI and Medicaid at 18?

SSI changes in ways that often help. For a child under 18, the Social Security Administration counts part of the parents' income and resources, which is called deeming. At 18, deeming stops, and only the young adult's own income and resources count. The SSI resource limit for an individual is $2,000, as of 2026. A young adult whose family earned too much for a child's SSI can become eligible as an adult.

At 18, the Social Security Administration also re-evaluates disability under the adult standard, which can be stricter. Gather medical records, school records and evaluations before the birthday. If SSI is denied or stopped, there are appeal rights with short deadlines, so respond quickly.

For Medicaid, most states connect it with SSI, but pathways differ, and some states offer waiver programs with waiting lists. Apply early. If the young adult will inherit or receive money, make sure it goes into a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) or an [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts), not directly to the person.

## What should you do, step by step?

1. At 16 or 17, ask the school for transition planning and talk to your state's disability agencies about services and waiver waiting lists.
2. Gather documents: diagnoses, evaluations, IEPs, medical records and a list of providers.
3. Think honestly about the person's decision-making ability across areas: health, money, living arrangements, relationships.
4. Choose the least restrictive tools that fit, and involve the young adult in choosing them.
5. Have an attorney prepare releases, a healthcare proxy, power of attorney and, if appropriate, a supported decision-making agreement, to be signed on or just after the 18th birthday.
6. Apply to become representative payee, and re-apply for SSI as an adult.
7. If guardianship is needed, start the petition a few months before 18, since it takes time.
8. Write a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) to guide future decision-makers.

### Example: Jordan

Jordan, 17, has an intellectual disability and attends a high school transition program. He knows his medications, cooks simple meals, and can say who he trusts. His parents ask an attorney to meet him. After the meeting, Jordan signs a healthcare proxy naming his mother, a HIPAA release, and a power of attorney for his father. He chooses his older sister and a family friend for a supported decision-making agreement. His parents apply as representative payee, and Jordan keeps his legal rights. Another family in the same program petitions for limited guardianship for their daughter, who cannot communicate choices about her health, and the court tailors it to medical decisions.

## How do these choices relate to the rest of the plan?

A person's decision-making supports may change over time, and so can the plan. Parents should also name their own successors, such as a sibling who will step in when they are gone, and put them in writing.
