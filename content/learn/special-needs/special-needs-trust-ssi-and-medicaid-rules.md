You may already know that a special needs trust protects benefits. What trips up trustees is the day-to-day question: if I pay this bill from the trust, does it count? The answer depends on two different tests that families often blur together, and on whether the benefit is SSI or Medicaid.

This article takes the tests one at a time, then walks through the common payments. For the basics of why the trust exists, read [special needs trusts and benefits](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) first. The wider picture is in our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs). Dollar thresholds change from year to year, so check the current figures with the Social Security Administration at ssa.gov or with an attorney.

## How do SSI and Medicaid look at a trust?

SSI asks two separate questions. The first is about stock: does the person own assets above the resource limit? The second is about flow: did the person receive income this month, and what kind?

A trust that the beneficiary cannot revoke, cannot direct, and cannot force the trustee to pay is generally not counted as the beneficiary's resource. That is the entire point of the discretionary language. The second question is separate. Even when the trust is safe as a resource, what comes out of it can still be treated as income, or as support that reduces the check.

Medicaid works along parallel lines but is run by each state. In most states, a person on SSI gets Medicaid through related eligibility, so the SSI analysis often decides both. Waiver programs for home and community-based services, and people on Medicaid who do not receive SSI, can be judged under state rules that differ in detail. Our [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) article describes how the program is structured for older adults, which is a different track.

## Which trust payments count as income?

Think in four buckets.

| Type of payment | Usually treated as | Effect on SSI |
|---|---|---|
| Cash or a gift card given to the beneficiary | Unearned income | Reduces the check, after a small general exclusion |
| Trustee pays a provider or store for a non-shelter item (therapy, phone, laptop, clothing, a class) | Not income | None, in most cases |
| Trustee pays rent, mortgage, property tax, utilities or similar shelter costs | In-kind support and maintenance | Reduces the check by a portion, not the full payment |
| Trustee pays for food | Food is not currently counted for this purpose | None under current rules |

Social Security changed the food rule in 2024, so older articles and older advice may be wrong on this. Rules in this area have moved before and can again, so treat the table as a starting point and ask before relying on it.

### Why cash is the riskiest payment

Cash is income in the month it arrives. If the beneficiary does not spend it that month, what is left can also become a countable resource at the start of the next month. A $300 cash payment for a birthday can cost more than $300 in benefits if it is handled loosely. Having the trustee buy the item or pay the vendor avoids the issue altogether.

### What about shelter?

Shelter is the one category where families really have a choice. If the trust pays the beneficiary's rent, SSI is reduced by a portion of the federal benefit rate. The reduction is capped and is smaller than the rent the trust is covering. Many trustees decide it is worth it. A young adult who is stable in an apartment has a better life than one who is fully benefit-compliant and crowded into a relative's spare room.

There is another route: the beneficiary pays rent from SSI and other income, while the trust covers everything else. Some families also hold the home in the trust. Either way, the choice should be made on purpose, with the cost in view. Whether a trust can own a home is a common follow-up, and the general answer is yes, with careful paperwork about property taxes and upkeep.

## What does the trustee need to do each month?

Good habits protect a trust more than any clause. Use this routine.

1. Keep a ledger of every payment with the date, payee, amount and reason.
2. Pay vendors directly. Avoid reimbursing the beneficiary or a relative unless the trust language and your attorney allow it.
3. Keep receipts. Photograph them the same day.
4. Report changes. The beneficiary or the representative payee reports income, living arrangement and other changes that Social Security asks about.
5. Keep the trust's bank account separate. Never mix it with the trustee's own funds or with the beneficiary's SSI account.
6. Review annually. Many trustees send a short accounting to the beneficiary's advocate or to a successor trustee, which is good practice even where not required.

A first-party trust has extra duties because of the state payback described in our article on the [payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision). A third-party trust has fewer, but the same ledger discipline applies. More on the trustee role generally appears in [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).

## Does the trust affect Medicaid differently?

Sometimes. Four differences come up regularly.

- **Waivers.** Home and community-based waiver programs have their own financial tests and sometimes their own review of trust terms. A trust that works for SSI may need a tweak for the waiver.
- **Language requirements.** Some states want specific statements in a first-party trust, such as a payback clause in a particular form.
- **Look-back.** A parent's transfer into a trust solely for a disabled child is generally treated favorably under Medicaid transfer rules, but the details matter. See the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).
- **Estate recovery.** Third-party trusts are generally outside Medicaid estate recovery, while first-party trusts carry the payback.

If your family has more than one program in play, ask the attorney to name each one and confirm how the trust is handled for each.

## Example: the Okafor family

Ngozi Okafor is trustee of a third-party trust for her brother Emeka, 44, who receives SSI and Medicaid waiver services. In March the trust pays $180 to his speech therapist, $60 for a data plan, and $1,100 toward a used wheelchair-accessible van conversion. None of it affects his check. In April Emeka asks for $200 in cash for a concert. Ngozi buys the tickets directly for $85 instead. In June, the landlord raises the rent and Ngozi decides the trust will cover the increase. She writes a note explaining why, tells the Social Security office about the shelter support, and Emeka's SSI drops by a portion for those months. She has the note, the receipts and the reporting letter in one binder.

None of her choices were exotic. They were each a decision made with the cost known, and written down.

## What should you do before the first payment?

Bring the trust document, the most recent Social Security and Medicaid notices, and a list of the beneficiary's monthly costs to an attorney, who can map each recurring cost to the bucket above. Pair this with an [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) if you want a place for small everyday spending that does not need trustee approval each time. Finally, write down the family's preferences in a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent), so future trustees can follow the pattern you set.
