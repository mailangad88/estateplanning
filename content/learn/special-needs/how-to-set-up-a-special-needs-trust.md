You have probably been told for years that your son or daughter needs a special needs trust. Maybe a teacher said it, or a social worker, or a friend. The part nobody explained is what actually happens: who you meet, what you decide, what you sign, and what you do afterward so the thing works.

This article walks through the process for a third-party trust, the kind parents create with their own money. If the money belongs to your child, for example from a lawsuit, you need a different structure; our article on [first-party and third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) explains how to tell. For the big picture, see our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## Can you set up a trust for an adult child?

Yes, and age does not limit it. A parent can create a third-party special needs trust when the child is four, forty or sixty. The child does not sign and does not need to understand the document. The parent, called the grantor, creates it and the trustee manages it. Many families wait until the child turns 18 because that is when benefits begin, but there is no benefit to waiting. If you have a child with a disability who is already an adult and receives SSI, you can set up the trust now and route your estate plan to it.

## What do you decide before you see a lawyer?

Gathering your thoughts first saves time and money. Bring answers, or at least drafts of answers, to the following.

1. **Who will be trustee, and who comes next?** Two or three names in order. Consider whether to pair a relative with a professional co-trustee. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).
2. **What are your goals for the money?** Housing, therapies, activities, a care manager, travel, a funeral. Say it plainly. This becomes the guidance section.
3. **Who receives what is left?** Siblings, nieces, a charity. There is no payback for a third-party trust, so the choice is yours.
4. **What does your child receive today?** SSI, SSDI, Medicaid, a waiver, housing assistance. Bring the award letters.
5. **What assets will fund it?** Life insurance, retirement accounts, a house, savings.
6. **Who is the guardian or decision-maker?** Trust, guardianship and powers of attorney are separate documents. See [guardianship alternatives](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children).

## How do you set it up, step by step?

<!-- visual: timeline id=making-a-plan -->

1. **Meet with an attorney.** Expect questions about your child's benefits, your assets, your other children and your family dynamics. Ask for the scope and fee in writing.
2. **Choose the structure.** A stand-alone trust created now, a testamentary trust inside your will, or a living trust with a special needs sub-trust. Each has tradeoffs for cost, flexibility and whether grandparents can contribute during your life.
3. **Review the draft.** Read the trustee powers, the distribution standard, the housing provisions, the remainder clause and the amendment rules. Ask what each clause does in plain words.
4. **Sign with the formalities your state requires.** Usually a notary and sometimes witnesses. Keep the original in a safe place and tell the trustee where.
5. **Obtain a tax ID and open an account when needed.** A trust that is revocable while you live may use your number. An irrevocable one generally needs its own.
6. **Fund the trust.** This is the step that matters most. See the next section.
7. **Tell the family.** Share the trust's exact name with grandparents, aunts and uncles. Share the trustee's name and the location of the letter of intent.
8. **Review on a schedule.** Every few years, and after any change in benefits, assets or family.

### What does funding mean?

Funding is moving assets so the trust receives them. A signed trust with nothing in it protects no one. Our article on [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) covers the mechanics. For a special needs trust, funding usually means three things: naming the trust as beneficiary of life insurance, naming it on retirement accounts using language an attorney has reviewed, and writing your will or living trust so that your child's share goes to the trust rather than to the child. Keep a list of every account and the form you changed.

## What are the common mistakes?

| Mistake | What goes wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Naming the child on a policy or retirement account | Money passes outright and can end benefits | Name the trust on the form |
| Using a generic online form | Language may not match state Medicaid rules or SSA policy | Have an attorney review or draft |
| Choosing a trustee with no backup | A court may have to appoint one | Name two or three successors |
| Forgetting to tell relatives | Grandma leaves money outright | Share the trust's name and a sample clause |
| Never funding the trust | The trust sits empty | Make a funding checklist and check it off |
| Mixing the child's own money into a third-party trust | Part of the trust may become subject to payback | Keep the child's money in a separate account or trust |

## Do you need a lawyer?

No statute requires one, and nothing stops you from signing a form. But the question is not whether you can, it is what you risk. A special needs trust sits where trust law, federal benefits rules and your state's Medicaid practice overlap. Small drafting choices, such as whether the trustee has sole discretion, how housing is treated, or whether the remainder clause is consistent with the rest of your plan, can decide whether your child keeps coverage. A form can look right and fail the first time someone at an agency reads it.

An attorney also catches what a form cannot: that your retirement account needs special language, that a grandparent's will names the child directly, or that your existing living trust leaves the child's share outright. Ask whether the attorney regularly drafts these trusts, how they keep up with changes in benefits rules, and what the fee covers. Our guide on [choosing an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) lists useful questions.

### What does it cost?

It varies by state, by complexity and by whether the trust is part of a larger plan. A stand-alone trust costs more than a clause in a will, but less than fixing a mistake later. Because fees differ, we do not quote a figure here; see [how much estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost) for the factors that move the price. Ask what is included: the trust, the will, the beneficiary form help and a funding checklist.

## Example: the Lindqvist family

Anders and Karin Lindqvist have a 26-year-old daughter, Elsa, who has cerebral palsy and receives SSI and a waiver program. Their will, written years ago, leaves everything to Elsa and her brother Nils equally. After a conversation with an attorney they realize that Elsa's half would pass outright.

They revise the plan. A third-party special needs trust becomes the recipient of Elsa's share, with Nils and a corporate trustee as co-trustees. Anders's life insurance and 401(k) now name the trust, with the retirement language checked. A short letter is shared with Elsa's grandmother, whose will had named Elsa directly, and she updates it. Karin writes a letter of intent and puts it with the trust. The whole project, from first meeting to the last form, takes about ten weeks.

A letter like Karin's is described in our [letter of intent guide](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent). It is the part of the plan no document can replace.
