Families who love someone with a disability plan for a longer future than most. The questions are specific. Who will help my daughter manage her medications when I cannot? How can my son have a bit of savings without losing Medicaid? What do we do when she turns 18? Can her brother be in charge, and should he be?

This guide gives you the whole map. It explains the benefits rules that shape every decision, then walks through the main tools: special needs trusts, ABLE accounts, decision-making supports and a letter of intent. Each section links to a detailed article. The rules described are federal rules as of 2026. States add their own details, and benefits rules change, so use this as a way to ask better questions rather than as a final answer.

## Why does special needs planning exist?

Special needs planning exists because the main public benefits for people with disabilities are means-tested, so an ordinary inheritance can cost more in lost benefits than it is worth. Two programs drive most of the planning.

**Supplemental Security Income (SSI)** is a federal monthly cash payment for people with disabilities, or who are blind, with limited income and resources. In 2026 the federal benefit is under $1,000 a month for an individual, which is not enough for housing and care on its own. To qualify, a person generally can have no more than $2,000 in countable resources (savings, investments, extra property), or $3,000 for a married couple. Some things do not count, such as a primary residence, one vehicle, and personal belongings.

**Medicaid** pays for health care and, in many states, for long-term supports such as personal care, supported employment and day programs, often through home- and community-based services waivers. In most states, a person who receives SSI also qualifies for Medicaid, although some states handle it differently. Medicaid often matters more than the cash benefit, because the services it funds are expensive or unavailable elsewhere.

**Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI)** is different. It is based on a work record, and has no resource limit. Adults with disabilities that began in childhood may receive "childhood disability benefits," also called Disabled Adult Child benefits, on a parent's record after the parent retires, becomes disabled or dies. Those benefits can carry Medicare and, in some cases, protections for Medicaid. They are worth asking about, and they are a reason to speak with a benefits advisor as well as a lawyer.

The planning problem follows. If a relative leaves $50,000 to a person on SSI, the person owns it, benefits stop until the money is spent below the limit, and Medicaid may stop with it. The family loses a program that may be worth many times that amount.

## What is a special needs trust?

A special needs trust holds money for the person with a disability, managed by a trustee who has discretion over payments, so that the assets are not counted as the person's own. The trust supplements public benefits and pays for things they do not cover, such as therapies, transportation, technology, travel and recreation, or a care manager.

There are two main types.

| | Third-party trust | First-party (self-settled) trust |
|---|---|---|
| Funded with | Parents' and family's money | The person's own money (settlement, back pay, direct inheritance) |
| Medicaid payback at death | No | Yes |
| Age limit | None | Generally under 65 at funding |
| Who decides what remains | The creator | Medicaid first, then others |

Parents' estate plans almost always use a third-party trust, which can also receive gifts and inheritances from grandparents and others. Our article on [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) explains how they work, what a trustee can pay for and how housing payments can reduce SSI. The comparison in [first-party vs. third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) covers the Medicaid payback, the under-65 rule and pooled trusts run by nonprofits.

Two cautions deserve emphasis. First, name the trust, not the person, as beneficiary of life insurance, retirement accounts and wills. Second, tell relatives. A grandparent who writes "to my grandson" in a will can cause the problem the trust was built to prevent.

## What is an ABLE account, and how does it fit?

An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings account for a person whose disability began before a certain age, and it lets the person save and spend without the money counting for SSI, up to $100,000. Earnings grow tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified disability expenses, including housing, transportation, education, health and assistive technology, are tax-free.

Eligibility is expanding. Until the end of 2025 the disability must have begun before age 26. Starting January 1, 2026, the cutoff is before age 46, which brings in many adults with conditions that began in their late twenties, thirties or early forties. Contributions are capped each year at the gift tax annual exclusion, which is $19,000 in 2026, and states cap total balances.

ABLE accounts are easy to use, with debit cards and online access, but a state can claim funds for Medicaid after death, and the annual cap limits size. They work best alongside a trust: the trust holds the larger sums, and the trustee moves part of it each year into the ABLE account for day-to-day spending. Our article [ABLE accounts: how they work alongside a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) covers eligibility, limits, payback and strategies for combining the two.

## What happens when a child with a disability turns 18?

At 18, parents lose automatic legal authority, and the young adult becomes the decision-maker unless a court or document says otherwise. Doctors, schools and banks may refuse to share information or accept a parent's instructions. This surprises many families.

The options run from less to more restrictive:

- HIPAA and school releases, so providers can talk with parents
- A representative payee for Social Security benefits
- Supported decision-making, in which trusted people help the adult decide
- A power of attorney and healthcare proxy signed by the adult, if they understand what they sign
- Limited guardianship, where the court gives authority only in areas of need
- Full guardianship, which removes most decision-making rights

Guardianship is a serious step that removes rights and is generally meant for situations where less restrictive options will not work. Many states require courts to consider alternatives first. The right choice depends on the person, and the person should be part of the decision to the extent possible.

SSI also changes at 18. Parents' income and resources stop counting, and the Social Security Administration reviews disability under the adult standard. Families often need to reapply, and appeals have short deadlines. Our article on [turning 18 with a disability](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) walks through each tool, the court process and a step-by-step timeline. For the general comparison of agent roles and court roles, see [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship), and our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## Why write a letter of intent?

A letter of intent is a non-binding document in which parents describe the person's history, routines, health needs, preferences and hopes, so that future caregivers can continue the life you built. It is often the single most useful document for a trustee or guardian who did not grow up in the household.

A good letter includes an emergency summary, daily routines and communication cues, medical and provider details, living arrangements and hopes for the future, relationships that matter, and notes on benefits and finances. It should be reviewed yearly and shared with the trustee, guardian or supporter and a backup. The article [writing a letter of intent for a loved one with a disability](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) provides a section-by-section outline.

## Who should take on which role?

Special needs plans often fail not because the documents are wrong but because the people are not in place. Consider each role separately:

| Role | What it covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Trustee | Manages the trust and pays for needs | Needs money and benefits knowledge; consider a co-trustee or professional |
| Successor trustee | Takes over | Name at least one |
| Guardian, supporter or agent | Helps with personal decisions | Can be different from the trustee |
| Representative payee | Manages Social Security benefits | Accountable to SSA |
| Advocate or care manager | Coordinates services | Can be paid from the trust |
| Letter-of-intent holder | Keeps the letter updated | Often a sibling |

Siblings often step in. If you plan to rely on a sibling, talk to them early, and be honest about their own lives. Many siblings want to be involved but do not want to be the trustee, the guardian and the caregiver. A professional or nonprofit trustee can handle money while the sibling stays a loving presence. For a deeper look at splitting roles, see [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee).

## How do other estate planning documents change?

Your will, life insurance, retirement accounts and powers of attorney all need a special needs lens.

1. **Wills and trusts.** Add or create the special needs trust, and name a trustee and backup. Do not leave outright shares to a beneficiary with benefits.
2. **Beneficiary designations.** Name the trust on life insurance and retirement accounts. For retirement accounts, a disabled beneficiary may get more favorable payout options, but the trust must be drafted correctly. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).
3. **Siblings' plans.** Siblings should know not to leave money directly to their brother or sister with a disability, and should have their own documents consistent with the family plan.
4. **Parents' incapacity.** A durable power of attorney and healthcare directives protect the family if a parent becomes ill. For parents who may need long-term care themselves, [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) is a separate subject with different rules.
5. **Equal versus fair.** Families often leave siblings unequal shares so the trust gets more, or ask siblings to contribute care. Say clearly what you intend, and consider a plan for what remains.

Our general guides to [trusts](/learn/trusts) and [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) cover the basics that a special needs trust builds on.

## What does a sensible timeline look like?

- **At diagnosis or early childhood:** create a third-party special needs trust, name guardians for minor children, and review life insurance. See [naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship) for parents.
- **Ages 14 to 16:** begin transition planning with the school, learn about waiver waiting lists, and start a letter of intent.
- **Age 17:** assemble medical and school records, decide on decision-making tools, and consult an attorney.
- **Age 18:** sign the documents or file for guardianship, apply as representative payee, and have SSI re-evaluated.
- **Adulthood:** open an ABLE account, update the letter yearly, review the trust every few years, and coordinate with siblings.
- **Parents' later years:** update trustees, move toward the plan you have described, and make sure the next generation knows the details.

## What mistakes should families avoid?

- Leaving money directly to a person who receives SSI or Medicaid
- Naming the person as beneficiary on insurance or retirement accounts
- Using a general trust that is not drafted for benefits
- Assuming guardianship is required at 18, or assuming it is never needed
- Using terms in documents that suggest the person is incapable of any decision
- Failing to name successors for the trustee and decision-makers
- Mixing first-party and third-party money in a single trust
- Not writing anything down about daily care

## How do you talk about it, and with whom?

Include the person with a disability in the conversation as much as they are able. Many adults with disabilities have clear opinions about where they want to live and whom they trust. Their voice should shape the plan, and a plan that respects their wishes is more likely to work. Talk with siblings, grandparents and relatives about their roles, and with a benefits counselor about your state's rules.
