If you live in Illinois and want to leave money to a child or relative with a disability, the question is not whether Illinois has a special trust law. It does not have a standalone one. The rules that decide whether your family member keeps SSI and Medicaid are federal, and Illinois supplies the courts, the trust code and the agencies that apply them.

This page explains what that means in practice, what is specific to Illinois, and what to do first. If you are new to the topic, read our overview of [special needs planning](/learn/special-needs) and then come back.

## What are the special needs trust rules in Illinois?

An Illinois special needs trust is an ordinary trust with language that keeps the money from counting as the beneficiary's own. The benefit rules that matter are federal: SSI allows a single person no more than $2,000 in countable resources, and Medicaid has similar asset tests for disability coverage. Money held in a properly written trust that the beneficiary cannot demand is not counted. Our article on [special needs trust rules for SSI and Medicaid](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) covers that test.

What Illinois adds is local: the Illinois Trust Code governs how the trustee must act, the circuit court of the county handles guardianship, and the Department of Human Services and the Department of Healthcare and Family Services run the benefits. A trust that is correct under federal law but ignores how those offices work can still go wrong. For example, a trustee who pays a landlord directly without telling the caseworker may trigger an SSI reduction no one saw coming.

## Which type of trust applies to an Illinois family?

| Trust | Who funds it | Medicaid payback? | Typical Illinois use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Third-party special needs trust | Parents, grandparents, other family | No | Inheritance, life insurance, gifts |
| First-party (self-settled) trust | The person's own money | Yes, at death | Settlement, back pay, an inheritance left outright |
| Pooled trust | The person's own money, held by a nonprofit | Usually yes, with the rest kept by the nonprofit | Smaller sums, no family trustee |

For a parent's estate plan, the third-party trust is the one that matters. It sits in the will or a living trust, receives the share that would have gone to the child, and leaves what remains to siblings or others after the child dies. Our comparison of [first-party and third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) explains why mixing them is the most common drafting mistake.

## How does the Illinois ABLE account fit in?

The Illinois ABLE program is run through the Illinois State Treasurer and lets a person whose disability began before age 46 save in an account that SSI generally does not count up to $100,000. Anyone can contribute, up to the annual gift tax exclusion, which is $19,000 in 2026. Money can be spent on a broad range of disability-related expenses, including housing.

An ABLE account is not a substitute for a trust. It is small, it belongs to the person, and Illinois Medicaid can claim against what remains at death. Most families use both: the trust holds the large sum, and the trustee moves modest amounts into the ABLE account for day-to-day bills. Our guide to [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) covers the contribution limits.

## What does Illinois DHS offer, and why does registration matter?

The Division of Developmental Disabilities at DHS funds home and community services for adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities, through Medicaid waivers. Demand exceeds funding, which is why Illinois uses the PUNS list. A family registers a person on PUNS, updates it once a year, and records the services the person needs now or expects to need later.

Parents often assume that being on the list is something to do after a crisis. It is the opposite. Register while the parents are healthy, because an emergency category can move someone up, but only if there is a record. See our article on [housing and Medicaid waivers](/learn/special-needs/housing-and-medicaid-waivers-for-adults-with-disabilities) for how the funded services work.

## Can the trust pay for food, rent or a phone?

It can pay for anything the trustee judges helpful, but SSI treats some payments as income. Under the current rules, a payment for shelter, such as rent or a mortgage, is in-kind support and reduces the monthly SSI check by up to about one-third of the federal benefit rate. Food no longer reduces it. A trustee who pays for a phone, therapy, a gym membership, a van, a computer or travel does not trigger any reduction, because those are not food or shelter. Many Illinois families still pay the rent from the trust, accepting the reduction because stable housing is worth more than the lost dollars. Our article on [what special needs trust funds can pay for](/learn/special-needs/what-can-special-needs-trust-funds-pay-for) goes line by line.

## What happens to the money left in the trust?

It depends on whose money it was. In a third-party trust there is no payback. The trust document names who receives what remains, which can be siblings, a charity or the beneficiary's own children. In a first-party or most pooled trusts, Illinois can claim reimbursement for Medicaid spent during the beneficiary's life before anyone else is paid. Read about [the payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) and about [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) for the claim itself.

## An example

Tomas and Elena, both 64, live in Naperville. Their son Mateo, 30, has autism and receives SSI. They have about $900,000 in a house, retirement accounts and life insurance. Their old will leaves everything equally to Mateo and his sister Lucia.

If Tomas and Elena both died today, Mateo's half would go to him directly. He would exceed the $2,000 limit, lose SSI, and likely lose Medicaid-funded services until he spent down. A revised plan puts Mateo's share into a third-party special needs trust with Lucia as successor trustee, names the trust as beneficiary of part of the life insurance, and leaves the remainder to Lucia's children after Mateo dies. They also register Mateo on PUNS and open an Illinois ABLE account.

## What to do first: a short checklist

1. Confirm what benefits Mateo, or your family member, receives now: SSI, SSDI, Medicaid, DHS services.
2. List every asset that could pass to the person, including accounts with beneficiary forms.
3. Decide who will be trustee, and who will be guardian if one is needed.
4. Draft the trust and update the will, the living trust and every beneficiary form to point to it. [Naming a benefits recipient directly](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-minor-incapacitated-or-benefits-recipient-as-beneficiary) is the error that undoes the plan.
5. Write a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) so the next caregiver knows routines, doctors and preferences.
6. Register on PUNS and review it each year.

## How does this relate to guardianship in Illinois?

A special needs trust does not require guardianship, and an adult with capacity makes decisions for themselves. If a court appoints a guardian, the Probate Act allows the order to be limited to what the person cannot do. The guardian and the trustee are different jobs, as our article on [guardian versus trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) explains. Read [Illinois guardianship](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) for local steps and [guardianship alternatives for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) if you would rather avoid a court.
