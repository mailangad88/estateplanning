Most parents of an adult with a disability carry one question around for years: who will do this when I can't? Often the honest answer is "probably her brother," followed by a silence, because the brother has never been asked and the parents are not sure what they would ask.

This page, part of our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs), is about that conversation and the paperwork behind it. It covers who can be guardian, how to ask a sibling, how the roles can be divided, and what happens to benefits at a parent's death. If your own situation is the other side of it, because you are the brother or sister, the same steps work in reverse.

## Who actually decides what happens when a parent dies?

A court does, if the adult needs a guardian. Nobody inherits guardianship, and there is no automatic role for a sibling. If a parent dies and no plan exists, a relative or agency has to file a petition, and a judge picks someone. Until then, the person may be cared for informally by whoever is closest, with no legal authority to sign medical consents or deal with Social Security.

That is the gap to close. You can close it in layers:

- **Personal decisions:** a healthcare power of attorney, if the person can sign one, or a guardian of the person.
- **Money:** a third-party special needs trust with a named trustee, plus a representative payee for benefits.
- **Day-to-day life:** a letter of intent that tells the next person what a good day looks like.

Our page on [alternatives to guardianship for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) explains the less restrictive options, and many families use them first.

## Can you name a guardian in your will?

You can name who you would like, and it matters, but it is a request, not a command. Judges generally give a parent's written preference real weight. The court also looks at the adult's own wishes, the nominee's fitness, and the adult's best interests. Our article on [whether a named guardian holds up in court](/learn/guardianship/does-a-guardian-named-in-a-will-hold-up-in-court) covers the court's thinking, though it is written mainly for minors.

Practical drafting points:

1. Name a first choice and at least one backup.
2. Say why. A paragraph about the sibling's history with your child helps a judge.
3. Ask for the least restrictive arrangement that works, such as limited guardianship over medical decisions only.
4. Keep the guardian separate from the money. Our [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) article explains why splitting them is common.
5. Consider a standby guardian designation for the period when you are alive but sick or hospitalized. See [temporary and standby guardians](/learn/guardianship/temporary-and-standby-guardians).

## How do you ask a brother or sister?

Ask early, ask directly, and make saying no easy. Parents often assume the answer. Then the sibling, who has a spouse, kids and a job in another city, learns of the assumption at a funeral.

A conversation that works usually has these parts:

- Start with the picture, not the request. Explain what a typical week looks like for your child now.
- Describe the jobs as separate. Visiting weekly is a different commitment from being legal guardian, and different again from managing a trust.
- Say what help will be there: a funded trust, a paid case manager, a care coordinator, a lawyer on call.
- Ask what the sibling is willing to do, rather than whether they will do everything.
- Give them time. Put a follow-up on the calendar.
- Write down the outcome, and revisit it every year or two.

Marcus, 71, did this with his daughters. Lena, 44, lives forty minutes from her brother Daniel, 41, who has cerebral palsy and lives in a group home. Marcus assumed Lena would be guardian. When he asked, she said she would gladly handle medical decisions and visit, but she did not want to manage the money. His other daughter, Amy, a CPA, agreed to be co-trustee with a bank. The family drafted a plan to match what each could actually do. A year earlier it would have been a rough assumption.

## Should a sibling be guardian, trustee and caregiver all at once?

Usually not. It concentrates responsibility in one person and puts them in awkward positions, since a guardian often has to ask the trustee for money for the person they are caring for. One sibling can also feel they are living the disabled person's life.

| Role | What it covers | Who often fills it |
|---|---|---|
| Guardian or health care agent | Medical, residential and personal decisions | A sibling or close relative |
| Trustee | Spending trust money and keeping records | A sibling with a professional co-trustee, or a trust company |
| Representative payee | Receives benefits and spends them on needs | A family member or agency |
| Day-to-day support | Visits, outings, advocacy at meetings | Siblings, friends, paid staff |
| Backup for each role | Steps in if the first person cannot serve | Another family member or a professional |

If no sibling is right, a professional guardian, a nonprofit pooled program or a trust company can take roles. Many families pair a sibling with a professional so the sibling can be a sibling.

## What changes for benefits when a parent dies?

Three things are worth knowing before it happens.

First, **SSI continues, but the amount can change.** If your child lived with you and shared household costs, Social Security may have reduced the check because of that support. After a parent dies, a review can change the amount in either direction. Our [SSI and Medicaid rules page](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) explains in-kind support and why trust payments for housing need care.

Second, **a disabled adult child may qualify for benefits on the parent's Social Security record.** If the disability began before age 22, and the parent was insured, a retirement, disability or death of that parent can open a "disabled adult child" benefit. It can be larger than SSI and is not based on the child's assets in the same way. It can also change Medicaid. In many cases a person who loses SSI because they begin to receive this benefit can keep Medicaid, but state rules and paperwork matter. You can read the federal explanation at [ssa.gov](https://www.ssa.gov).

Third, **the person may have been receiving help you did not count.** A parent might have been paying for utilities, driving to appointments, or supervising medication. Make a list of everything you currently do for your child. That list becomes the staffing plan for a trustee or guardian.

## What should be on paper before you meet the lawyer?

1. The name, birthdate and diagnoses of the person, and who currently provides care.
2. A list of benefits: SSI, SSDI, Medicaid or waiver services, housing assistance.
3. Your own assets: house, retirement accounts, life insurance, bank accounts.
4. Names of the people you are considering for each role, with a first and second choice.
5. A rough draft of the letter of intent. See [writing a letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent).
6. Doctor, case manager and school or day program contacts.

## What if there is no sibling or the siblings disagree?

Plan for it openly. Pooled trusts and nonprofit guardians exist in many places, and many states have public guardian offices as a last resort. Medicaid waiver programs often fund a support coordinator, though some states have long waiting lists, so ask now rather than later.

If siblings disagree, the best protection is structure. A trust that names a corporate co-trustee, a guardian who reports to the court, and a letter that states the parent's wishes in the parent's voice make it harder for family conflict to land on the person with a disability. Our page on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) covers how to pick one when relatives may not agree.
