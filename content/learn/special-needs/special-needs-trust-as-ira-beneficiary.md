Retirement accounts are often the largest asset a parent has, and they are the one asset that does not pass through a will or a trust. It goes to whoever is named on the custodian's form. If that person is your son Michael, who receives SSI and Medicaid, the account can arrive in his name and put his benefits at risk within weeks.

This page covers how a special needs trust can be named instead, what changes about the payout rules when the beneficiary is disabled, and the choice every trustee of one of these trusts has to make each year.

## The short version

- Name a third-party special needs trust, not the person, on every IRA, 401(k), 403(b) and annuity form.
- Do not move the retirement account into the trust while you are alive. Keep it in your name.
- A trust that qualifies for the disabled-beneficiary rules can stretch payouts over the beneficiary's life expectancy.
- The trustee will choose each year between keeping the withdrawal in the trust and paying it out. Have your attorney draft for both.
- Check the form, not just the trust. The custodian only follows what is written on its own paperwork.

## Why not just name my child?

Naming a person on SSI directly means the inherited account becomes theirs. Under SSI rules, an account the person can withdraw from generally counts as a resource, and the limit is low enough that even a modest inherited IRA can exceed it. Our page on [what to do when an SSI recipient is left money](/learn/special-needs/ssi-recipient-inheritance-gift-or-settlement-what-to-do) walks through that fallout.

There is also a tax reason to be careful. An inherited IRA taxed at the beneficiary's income level may be fine for someone with little income. But the benefit loss usually outweighs any tax savings. A trust solves the benefits problem because the beneficiary cannot demand the money, so it is not counted as theirs.

## When can an IRA be left to a trust at all?

Any trust can be named on a beneficiary form, but the tax result depends on whether the trust is a "see-through" or "look-through" trust. In plain terms, the IRS has to be able to see who the people behind the trust are. The trust generally needs to be valid under state law, irrevocable at the owner's death, have identifiable beneficiaries, and give the custodian a copy of the trust or a list of beneficiaries by the deadline set in the rules. Our article on [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) covers the general mechanics.

A special needs trust is already irrevocable after you die, so it usually fits. The drafting question is the payout period.

## What does the disability rule change?

Since the SECURE Act, most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited retirement account within 10 years. Disabled and chronically ill beneficiaries are an exception. They are "eligible designated beneficiaries" and can take distributions over their own life expectancy, which keeps more money growing and lowers each year's tax bill.

The tax code uses a Social Security style definition: the person must be unable to engage in substantial gainful activity because of a medical condition expected to last a long time or to result in death. Someone on SSI because of disability is usually in good shape here, but the person who is disabled, working part time, and not on benefits may not be. Proof matters. Custodians ask for documentation, and the rules set a deadline after the year of death to provide it. Your attorney will confirm the current date.

A trust can also use this exception, but only if it is drafted for it. Broadly, the trust has to be for the disabled person's benefit during their life, with no one else able to receive the retirement money while they are alive. Remainder beneficiaries, such as your other children, can take what is left after the disabled beneficiary dies. Trusts that do this are sometimes called applicable multi-beneficiary trusts. A standard special needs trust with a payback to a state, as covered in [our payback provision page](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision), is a different thing: that is usually used for the person's own money, not a parent's retirement account.

| Beneficiary on the form | Payout period | Benefits risk | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Child on SSI, named directly | Life expectancy if disabled | High | Money becomes the child's resource |
| Trust that does not meet the see-through rules | Can be a very short period | Low | Tax cost can be steep |
| Third-party special needs trust drafted for the disability rule | Life expectancy | Low | The usual goal |
| Pooled trust account | Depends on the pool and the rules | Low | Ask the pool before naming it |

## Conduit or accumulation: the trustee's yearly decision

Once the account is inherited, the trustee takes required minimum distributions each year. Then comes the choice.

In a **conduit** design, the trustee passes each year's withdrawal straight to the beneficiary. That is simple for taxes, since the beneficiary reports the income. But the money lands in Michael's hands, and cash from this source can count as income or a resource for SSI. It also defeats much of the point of having a discretionary trust.

In an **accumulation** design, the trustee takes the withdrawal into the trust and keeps it there, paying only for things the beneficiary needs that do not reduce benefits. This protects eligibility. The tradeoff is tax, since trust income is taxed at high rates after a small amount. For some families a qualified disability trust election can soften this, as described in [how a special needs trust is taxed](/learn/special-needs/how-a-special-needs-trust-is-taxed).

For a beneficiary on means-tested benefits, accumulation is the common design. Our attorneys often draft it to let the trustee choose year by year, because the right answer changes with the beneficiary's benefits, income and the account's size. Our general explanation of [the two designs](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) is a useful companion.

## An example

Denise, 66, has an IRA and a daughter, Priya, 34, who has Down syndrome and receives SSI and Medicaid waiver services. Her son Tomas, 40, is healthy and has his own retirement savings. Denise's old beneficiary form splits the IRA between the two children. If she dies with that form in place, half the account goes to Priya outright.

Instead, Denise signs a third-party special needs trust for Priya that allows the trustee to hold retirement withdrawals inside the trust. She changes the IRA form so that Priya's share goes to the trustee of that trust, and Tomas's share goes to Tomas. She names Tomas as trustee, and a trust company as successor. Each year after her death, Tomas takes the required withdrawal, deposits it in the trust account, and pays for things like Priya's dental work, a tablet and a membership at the community pool. He does not hand her cash. When Priya dies, whatever is left goes to Tomas's children, as the trust says.

Tomas also keeps records and files the trust's tax return. That is real work, and it is why the choice of trustee matters. See [who should be trustee of a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/who-should-be-trustee-of-a-special-needs-trust).

## How do you set this up?

1. List every account with a beneficiary form: IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s, pensions, annuities and life insurance. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you sort them.
2. Decide the split between children. Equal is not always fair. See [how much to put in a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/how-much-to-put-in-a-special-needs-trust).
3. Have an attorney draft or update the third-party trust with retirement-account language: payout period, conduit or accumulation flexibility, and who can receive the account after the beneficiary dies.
4. Get the trust signed, and its name and date exactly right. Custodians match what the form says to what the trust says.
5. Complete each custodian's own beneficiary form naming the trust as primary or contingent. Keep the confirmation.
6. If you are married, ask whether your spouse's consent or rights affect the plan. Many employer plans give a spouse automatic rights unless the spouse signs a waiver. Our [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) article explains.
7. Give the trustee the trust, the forms and the custodian contacts, and tell them where the documents are.

## What goes wrong most often?

The commonest mistake is a mismatch. The family spends months on the trust, then leaves the old form naming the child, or names "my special needs trust" without the date or full legal name. The form usually controls. It beats what your will or trust says, as our explanation of [which document controls](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form) shows.

Other problems:

- **Naming a first-party or pooled trust by mistake.** A first-party trust is for the beneficiary's own money and usually carries a payback to the state. Using it for a parent's IRA can add a state payback the family never needed. See [first-party vs. third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust).
- **Naming the trust as the only beneficiary with no backup.** If the trust ends, or the trustee cannot serve, there should be a second choice on the form.
- **Forgetting the spouse.** A surviving spouse can often roll the account over and name the trust later, which sometimes keeps more tax deferral. Whether that is smart depends on the family. Our page on [spousal rollover vs. inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira) describes it.
- **Overlooking the custodian's rules.** Some custodians will not accept a trust that cannot be read, or will want a certification. Ask early.

## How does state law change this?

The tax rules are federal and apply everywhere. What varies is how states treat the trust itself: whether a state taxes trust income, how the state Medicaid agency treats a third-party trust, and how state court procedures treat trustees. If you live in a state with its own income tax, ask how it taxes accumulated trust income. Our [state estate planning guides](/estate-planning/new-jersey), such as New Jersey's, show how one state handles it, and the [plan finder](/plan-finder) can point you to yours.
