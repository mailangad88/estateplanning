Parents of a child with a disability often ask the same question in different words: how do we leave money to our son or daughter without ruining the benefits they need? The usual answer is a special needs trust, sometimes called a supplemental needs trust.

This guide explains how benefits rules create the problem, how the trust solves it, what it can and cannot pay for, how it is set up, and what trustees need to watch. The rules summarized here are federal rules as of 2026. States add their own details, and the stakes are high enough that a plan should be reviewed by an attorney who focuses on this area.

## Why can an inheritance cause someone to lose benefits?

Because SSI and Medicaid are means-tested, meaning eligibility depends on limited income and resources. Supplemental Security Income (SSI) is a federal cash benefit for people with disabilities who have little income and few assets. In most states, SSI eligibility also opens the door to Medicaid, and Medicaid may pay for health care, personal care, and home- and community-based services that are hard to get any other way.

For SSI, an individual's countable resources are generally limited to $2,000, and a married couple's to $3,000. Cash, bank accounts, investments, and most other property count. Some things do not count, including the person's primary home, one vehicle, personal belongings, and certain burial funds. These numbers have not been raised for many years, so even a modest gift can push someone over the line.

If a grandparent leaves $25,000 to a grandchild who receives SSI, the grandchild now owns the money. The Social Security Administration counts it. Benefits stop until the person "spends down" below the limit, and spending down on the wrong things can cause further penalties. In addition to losing SSI, the person may lose the Medicaid that rides on it. The result for a family relying on that Medicaid can be far worse than the money's value.

## How does a special needs trust solve this?

A special needs trust holds the money so it is not the beneficiary's. The key feature is the trustee's discretion. The trust says that the trustee, not the beneficiary, decides whether and when to make payments, and that the beneficiary cannot force a distribution or use the money for support. Because the beneficiary cannot access or direct the assets, the Social Security Administration generally does not count them as a resource.

The trustee spends money to improve the beneficiary's life in ways that benefits do not. Think of it as a supplement: a second layer of support on top of public benefits.

### What can the trust pay for?

A trust can usually pay for almost anything that improves quality of life, if paid correctly and directly to a provider where possible. Typical uses:

- Therapies and treatments Medicaid does not cover
- Dental, vision and hearing care
- A computer, tablet or communication device
- Education, classes and job coaching
- Recreation, hobbies, vacations, and a companion to travel with
- Personal care attendants beyond hours Medicaid funds
- A care manager or advocate
- Transportation, including a vehicle held in the trust
- Furniture and household items
- Legal and accounting fees for the trust

### What should a trustee be careful about?

Not all spending is equal. Two issues come up most often.

**Housing and food.** Under SSI rules, help with shelter costs, such as rent, mortgage payments, property taxes and utilities, is treated as "in-kind support and maintenance." It can reduce the monthly SSI payment, usually by up to about one-third of the federal benefit rate. Since September 2024, food is no longer counted for this purpose, but shelter still is. Many trustees decide that a reduction in SSI is worth it to provide stable housing. The decision should be made deliberately, not by accident.

**Cash.** Money handed to the beneficiary is income and can reduce SSI dollar for dollar after a small exclusion, and unspent cash becomes a countable resource. Paying a provider or buying an item is usually safer than giving cash.

A trustee who understands these rules, documents every payment, and reports required changes to the Social Security Administration protects the beneficiary. A trustee who writes checks casually may not.

## What are the types of special needs trusts?

There are two main types, and which one you use depends on whose money it is.

| | Third-party trust | First-party (self-settled) trust |
|---|---|---|
| Funded with | Money from parents, grandparents, other relatives or friends | The beneficiary's own money, such as a lawsuit settlement or back benefits |
| Medicaid payback at death | No | Yes, repays the state Medicaid program (some pooled trusts differ) |
| Age limit | None | Beneficiary generally must be under 65 when the trust is created |
| Remainder at death | Goes to whomever the creator names | First repays Medicaid, then to others |
| Typical use | Estate planning by family | Settlements, inheritances received outright, back pay |

The most common estate planning vehicle for families is the third-party trust, which has no payback requirement and can leave what remains to siblings or charities. First-party trusts follow special federal rules and require a Medicaid payback when the beneficiary dies. Our article [first-party vs. third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) explains each in detail, including pooled trusts.

## Where should the trust be created and funded?

There are two common ways to set it up in an estate plan.

1. A stand-alone third-party trust, created during your lifetime. It can receive gifts, life insurance proceeds, and money from other family members, and it can be revocable until your death if you want flexibility.
2. A testamentary special needs trust written into your will. It is created at your death and goes through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate), but costs less to set up.

Many families prefer a stand-alone trust so that grandparents and others have a place to leave money, and so the trustee can start acting immediately if parents die. Either way, name the trust as the beneficiary of life insurance and retirement accounts, not the person with a disability. [Naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) requires some care, especially with retirement accounts, which need to be structured so that the trust can use more favorable payout rules for a disabled beneficiary.

Tell relatives. A grandmother who writes "to my grandson Josh" in her will can create a first-party problem for him by accident. Share the trust's name and a sample paragraph so relatives can write "to the Josh Smith Supplemental Needs Trust" instead.

## Who should be the trustee?

The trustee has a heavy job. They invest, make distributions, understand SSI and Medicaid rules, keep records, file tax returns and stay engaged with the beneficiary's life. Look for honesty, reliability and willingness to learn, and always name successors. Options include:

- A family member, often paired with a professional co-trustee
- A corporate trustee or a nonprofit that serves people with disabilities
- A trusted friend or sibling with a clear letter of guidance

The beneficiary should generally not serve as trustee, because that could give them control of the assets. A trust can also name a trust protector or an advisory committee to replace a trustee and keep the trust current as laws change. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) for the broader decision.

### What should go in the trust document?

A well-drafted trust includes:

1. A statement of purpose, that the trust is to supplement, not supplant, public benefits
2. Sole discretion language for the trustee
3. A clear prohibition on the beneficiary's ability to revoke or demand distributions
4. Guidance on housing, caregivers and quality of life
5. Provisions letting the trustee adapt to changes in the law
6. A remainder clause naming who receives leftover assets
7. A successor trustee line

Pairing the trust with a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) gives the trustee a picture of who your child is, which a legal document cannot.

## How does the trust work with other tools?

A special needs trust is rarely the only tool. [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) offer tax-advantaged savings for qualified disability expenses and can be used for day-to-day spending while the trust handles bigger items. When the child reaches adulthood, families also have decisions about [guardianship and its alternatives](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children). For the wider picture, see our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

### Example: the Nguyen family

David and Linh Nguyen have a 17-year-old daughter, Mai, who has Down syndrome. They expect she will receive SSI and Medicaid waiver services as an adult. They create a third-party special needs trust, name Linh's sister as trustee with a professional co-trustee, name the trust as beneficiary of their life insurance, and tell grandparents to use it. When Mai's grandmother dies, her will leaves $60,000 to the trust, and Mai keeps her benefits. The trustee pays for Mai's day program transportation, therapy and a tablet, and the trust pays the property taxes on a home owned by the trust, accepting a small SSI reduction for stable housing.
