Special needs trusts are usually discussed in terms of children. But the rules that decide who needs one, and who can use one, hit older adults too. A 68-year-old widow with early dementia receives a settlement. A husband in a nursing home inherits from his sister. A mother of a disabled son wonders whether her own memory loss changes what she can set up.

This article is about the over-65 version of the question. It explains where the age cutoff comes from, how a pooled trust helps, what a trust can and cannot do for a person with dementia, and how to handle a spouse on Medicaid. For the foundation, start with our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## Where does the age 65 rule come from?

The federal Medicaid statute lets a person with a disability keep their own money in a trust without counting it, but only if the trust meets certain conditions. The stand-alone version, known as a (d)(4)(A) trust, is available only for a person who is under 65. The trust must be established by a parent, grandparent, legal guardian or court, or by the individual if they have capacity under current law, and it must include a payback to the state at death.

The age limit is about the person, not the family. The rule limits how a 66-year-old can shelter *their own* assets. Money that never belonged to the beneficiary sits in a different category, as our article on [first-party and third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) explains.

| Whose money | Type of trust | Age rule |
|---|---|---|
| The beneficiary's own (settlement, back pay, inheritance already received) | Stand-alone (d)(4)(A) | Beneficiary generally under 65 when created and funded |
| The beneficiary's own | Pooled (d)(4)(C) | No federal age limit; some states penalize deposits after 65 |
| A parent, spouse, sibling or friend | Third-party | None |
| A spouse's estate left in a will | Testamentary third-party trust | None |

Older Medicaid applicants are treated differently in another way. Federal law exempts certain transfers to a trust for a disabled person under age 65, but the exemption stops at 65. Our article on [Medicaid transfer exceptions for a disabled child](/learn/special-needs/medicaid-transfer-exceptions-for-a-disabled-child) covers the version that still works for families with a child.

## What can a person over 65 do with their own money?

Three realistic paths exist, and each depends on your state.

1. **Pooled trust.** A nonprofit pooled trust may accept an older beneficiary. The federal statute does not fix an age ceiling, but a state Medicaid agency may treat a deposit made after 65 as a transfer for less than fair value, which triggers a penalty period before nursing home coverage begins. Some states do not apply a penalty if the trust provides for the person's benefit. Others do. The state's policy manual and the trust's own attorney should answer that before any deposit. Our guide to [pooled special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts) describes the fees and payback.
2. **Ordinary spend-down.** Spend the money on needs: care, a home repair, a vehicle, an irrevocable funeral. Our explanation of the [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down) lists the usual choices.
3. **Hold it as a resource and pay privately.** For a person who is not yet on Medicaid, this is simply the first step toward eligibility.

Which one fits depends on the size of the money and the person's situation. A $25,000 settlement for a woman on SSI and Medicaid may be best placed in a pooled account if her state permits. A $400,000 sale of a house when the owner is about to enter a nursing home is a Medicaid planning problem, not a special needs trust problem. Our [Medicaid planning overview](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) handles that case.

## Does a person with dementia qualify as disabled?

Often yes, but the proof matters. For Medicaid purposes the person must meet the Social Security definition of disability, meaning a medically determinable condition that prevents substantial work and is expected to last at least a year or end in death. For a person over retirement age, a state Medicaid agency makes that determination for these purposes. Advanced dementia with a physician's assessment usually meets it. Early memory loss may not, and the state will ask for records.

That distinction decides whether a trust protects benefits at all. Without the disability finding, there is nothing for a special needs trust to hold on to. Our article on [who qualifies as disabled and planning by diagnosis](/learn/special-needs/who-qualifies-as-disabled-and-planning-by-diagnosis) lists the evidence that tends to matter.

Capacity is a separate question. A person with moderate dementia may not be able to sign a trust, which changes who can set one up. A guardian or conservator can act with court approval in many states, and an existing power of attorney sometimes grants authority to create or fund a trust. Many forms do not. See our guide on [creating a trust when someone has dementia or only a power of attorney](/learn/trusts/creating-a-trust-with-dementia-or-a-power-of-attorney).

## How do you leave money to a spouse who needs Medicaid?

Do not leave it outright. If Harold, 80, is in a nursing home on Medicaid and his wife Iris dies with a will that gives everything to him, the inheritance becomes his money. It counts as a resource, benefits can stop until it is spent down, and in many states he may have to reach for an elective share if she left him nothing.

A third-party special needs trust, sometimes called a supplemental needs trust, solves this. Iris's will creates a trust for Harold's benefit and names a trustee. The trustee pays for things Medicaid does not: a private room supplement if the facility allows it, dental work, a wheelchair van, haircuts, visits from family, a personal aide. Because the money was never Harold's, there is no payback to the state at his death, and the remainder goes to whomever Iris chose, often their children.

A few cautions:

- **Timing.** The trust must be in place at the first spouse's death. If Iris dies without a will, state law gives the share to Harold outright.
- **Elective share.** Some states let a surviving spouse claim a statutory share of the estate, and a spouse on Medicaid who does not claim it may be treated as having made a transfer. State rules differ, so ask before relying on the will alone. Our article on the [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) explains the basics.
- **Beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts and life insurance go where the form says, whatever the will says. A form naming Harold directly defeats the trust. Check every form.
- **Community spouse.** The healthy spouse has special protections while both are alive, covered in [what happens to Medicaid when the healthy spouse dies first](/learn/elder-care/what-happens-to-medicaid-when-the-healthy-spouse-dies-first).

## What about the older adult who is a caregiver for a disabled child?

This is the opposite case, and it is common. A mother, 74, cares for a son, 45, who has cerebral palsy and receives SSI and Medicaid. The age rule for first-party trusts does not stop her from creating a third-party trust for him. What worries her is her own health: what if she goes into care first?

The answer is to separate the pieces. Her will and beneficiary forms should leave her son's share to a trust. A durable power of attorney lets an agent handle her money if she cannot. A health care directive covers her medical wishes. A letter of intent explains the son's routines for whoever steps in. A mother who plans for her own Medicaid and her son's eligibility at once needs a plan with both in view. Our guide to [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child or sibling](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling) is the place to begin.

## What should you do first?

1. **Find out whose money it is.** The answer decides everything: the beneficiary's own, or someone else's.
2. **Confirm the person's age at the time the money would go into any trust.** Do not wait. A birthday can close a door.
3. **Collect proof of disability.** Medical records, a physician letter, SSI or SSDI award letters, any prior state disability determination.
4. **Check capacity.** If the person cannot sign, find out whether a power of attorney or guardian can act.
5. **Ask the state.** Illinois and New Jersey each have their own Medicaid rules on trusts and transfers. Our guide to [special needs planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey) is a starting point, and an attorney can explain Illinois practice.
6. **Do not deposit or gift until you have the answer.** Moving money is easy and unwinding it is hard.
