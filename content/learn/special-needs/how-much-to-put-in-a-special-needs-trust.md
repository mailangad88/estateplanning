Parents ask us for a number. They want to know if $50,000 is enough, if a million is too much, or what most families do. We understand the instinct, and we have to answer with some honesty: no figure is right for every family, and anyone who quotes one without knowing your child is guessing.

What we can do is show you how the number is built. This page walks through the pieces: what the trust will have to pay for, how long it has to last, what it costs to run, and where families find the money. For the overall plan, see our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## What is the minimum, and what is the maximum?

There is no minimum under the law. A third-party trust can be created with a small starting amount and then built through your will, a retirement account beneficiary form, or life insurance. There is also no cap on how much a third-party trust may hold. Money you or other relatives put in is not counted as the beneficiary's resource, which is the whole reason it works.

First-party trusts are different. They are funded with the person's own money, such as a settlement or an inheritance that was left to them by mistake, and they carry a payback claim for Medicaid at death. The amount is whatever the person actually received. Our guide to [first-party and third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) explains why that difference matters.

So the real limits are practical. How much do you own? What does the trust cost to keep going? And what does your child need?

## What drives the number?

| Driver | Why it changes the target |
|---|---|
| Your child's age | A longer life means more years to fund |
| Level of support | More daily support, more cost beyond what benefits pay |
| Housing plan | Living at home, in a group home, or in supported housing carry very different costs |
| What benefits provide | State programs and waiver services vary widely and have waiting lists in many places |
| Siblings and relatives | A sibling who will help reduces paid care; one who cannot increases it |
| Trustee choice | A professional trustee costs money; a family trustee costs less but needs help |
| Investment return and inflation | Both work over decades |
| Your own assets | Your retirement and home may need to cover you first |

## How do you estimate what your child will need?

Take it in steps.

1. **List what benefits cover and do not.** Ask a case manager or service coordinator what the state program pays for. Be honest about waiting lists and cuts.
2. **Price the gap, per year.** Housing support, a car, therapies, equipment, an aide for outings, a gym membership, vacations, the phone plan, and a small cushion for emergencies.
3. **Add professional costs.** A trustee fee, tax preparation, an annual accounting and occasional legal advice.
4. **Pick a time horizon.** Use your child's current age and a lifespan that is not pessimistic. If your child is 25 and may live into their 80s, the trust may need to last half a century after you are gone.
5. **Adjust for inflation and investment return.** This is where a planner earns a fee. A small difference in return compounds over decades.
6. **Subtract what is already available.** An ABLE account, a sibling's help, a pension from a parent, or survivor benefits on your record.

What you get is a range, not an answer. That range is more useful than a hunch.

## Where does the money come from?

For most families, the trust is not funded from savings alone. The common sources are:

- **Life insurance.** A permanent or second-to-die policy owned by a trust (see our article on the [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust)) can create a large trust at a modest annual cost. Name the special needs trust, not the child, as beneficiary. See [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).
- **Retirement accounts.** An IRA or 401(k) can pass to the trust if the beneficiary form and the trust language line up. A trust for a disabled beneficiary often qualifies for better payout rules than other trusts, but the drafting is technical, so have an attorney check it.
- **The house.** Some families leave the house to the trust. See [whether a trust can own a house](/learn/special-needs/can-a-special-needs-trust-own-a-house).
- **Savings and investments.** Whatever is left after your own needs.
- **Gifts from relatives.** Grandparents and aunts can name the trust in their wills.

## What if there is not much to leave?

A smaller sum can still help. Three options come up often.

A [pooled special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts), run by a nonprofit, accepts smaller deposits and handles the administration for a fee. An [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) can hold savings that the person can spend on housing and daily costs. And a modest third-party trust with a family trustee costs very little to run if the family keeps it simple.

Do not skip the document just because the number is small. A modest inheritance given directly to a person on benefits can cost them eligibility. In a trust, the same money can pay for a laptop, dental care and trips for years.

## What does it cost to maintain?

Ongoing costs include a trustee fee if a professional serves, tax return preparation, investment fees, bank fees and occasional legal advice. A family trustee who does not charge still has real work to do. Build an annual cost line into your estimate. If the trust is too small to carry those costs, a pooled trust is usually the better home.

## What is the cost of not funding it?

If nothing is set up, the child may inherit outright, lose benefits until the money is spent down, and then have to start again. Or a court may appoint someone to manage it. Neither is cheap. See [what happens when a disabled heir inherits directly](/learn/what-if/disabled-heir-inherits-directly) for the typical sequence.

## A worked example

Sam and Priya, both 58, have a son, Dev, 24, who has Down syndrome. He will receive SSI and likely a Medicaid waiver. They estimate benefits cover basic housing support and health care, but that Dev will need money for an aide on outings, transport, recreation, and a safety cushion. They add a trustee fee and tax costs, and consider a lifespan into his seventies.

They run the numbers with their attorney and a financial planner, and find that savings alone will not close the gap. They buy a second-to-die life insurance policy owned by an irrevocable trust, name Dev's special needs trust as beneficiary, and divide the rest of their estate between Dev's trust and his sister, Nisha. They review the plan every few years. The numbers changed twice, once when Dev's housing plan changed, and once when a state program closed its waiting list.

## What should you ask a planner or attorney?

- What assumptions are in your estimate, and can I see them?
- How is the trustee paid, and what will the fee be on the amount I have in mind?
- Does the trust allow the trustee to spend principal, or only income?
- What happens if the money runs out early?
- How often should the number be revisited?
