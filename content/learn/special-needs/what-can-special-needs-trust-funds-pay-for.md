When a family funds a special needs trust, the first practical question is usually the same: what can the trustee actually spend it on? People hear that the trust "cannot be used for food or rent" or that it "can only pay for extras," and then wonder whether a phone, a vacation or a dog counts as an extra. It is more flexible than that, and less forgiving in a few places.

This page walks through the categories trustees ask about. The rules behind it are in [how a special needs trust affects SSI and Medicaid](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules), and the basics of the document are in [special needs trusts and benefits](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust). The wider plan lives in our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs). Current thresholds belong to Social Security, so confirm figures at ssa.gov.

## The short version

- A special needs trust supplements benefits. It improves life beyond what SSI and Medicaid already provide.
- Most spending is fine when the trustee pays a vendor, not the beneficiary.
- Cash and shelter are the two areas that can reduce SSI.
- Keep a ledger and receipts. The trustee may be asked to prove where money went.
- When in doubt, ask before buying, not after.

## What is the test for any purchase?

Ask three questions in order.

1. Does the trust document allow it? Some documents list purposes. Many give the trustee discretion to spend for the beneficiary's "supplemental needs."
2. Does it benefit the beneficiary, and only the beneficiary? A family vacation where the beneficiary tags along is different from a trip for the beneficiary with a companion.
3. How will Social Security see it? Is it cash, shelter, or something that is neither?

Medicaid has its own view, and states differ. A person on a waiver program may have an additional rule about what the waiver already covers.

## What can a special needs trust pay for?

| Category | Usually allowed? | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Medical and dental costs Medicaid does not cover | Yes | Pay the provider directly |
| Therapies, equipment, hearing aids, glasses | Yes | Keep the prescription or recommendation |
| Phone, internet, tablet, computer | Yes | Pay the provider, and put the account in the trust's name if possible |
| Education, tutoring, vocational classes | Yes | Pay the school directly |
| Travel and vacations | Yes | Include the cost of a companion if needed |
| Entertainment, hobbies, memberships | Yes | Buy the item or pay the vendor |
| Pet food, vet and care | Yes | Treat as a benefit to the beneficiary |
| A vehicle | Usually yes | Title and use must be clear |
| Funeral and burial | Usually yes | Check the document and plan ahead |
| Caregiver or family member pay | Yes, if reasonable | Written agreement and records |
| Housing: rent, mortgage, taxes, utilities | Allowed, but can reduce SSI | Weigh against housing stability |
| Cash given to the beneficiary | Allowed, but counts as income | Avoid when possible |

### Health and therapy

This is the plainest use. Dental work that a state Medicaid program does not cover, private therapy, a second opinion, an evaluation, a dietitian, a gym or swim program prescribed by a doctor. The trustee can pay the bills as they arrive, and pay in advance for a course of treatment where the contract allows.

### Technology, phone and internet

Many trustees overlook these. A laptop, a tablet with communication software, a phone plan and home internet are all ordinary supplemental needs. A phone plan is not food or shelter, so it is generally treated like any other vendor payment.

### Travel and recreation

A trip to see a sibling, a vacation, a concert, a season pass, a visit to a family wedding. These are among the most appreciated uses of a trust. Pay for the airline and hotel yourself, as trustee, and include the airfare for a companion who must go with the beneficiary. Gift cards given to the beneficiary are cash-like, so avoid them.

### Pets

A dog or cat can be a major source of comfort for a person with a disability. A trust may pay for food, vet bills, boarding and supplies. A service animal is a medical expense in many settings. If a pet will outlast the beneficiary, a separate [pet trust](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) can provide for its care.

### Vehicles

A reliable car is often the difference between a job and no job. The trust can buy the car, add accessibility equipment, and pay insurance and repair bills. A lot of families title the vehicle in the trust's name, because it keeps ownership clear and avoids mixed-use questions. If a family member drives it for their own errands, expect a question.

### Caregivers and family

The trust may pay a professional caregiver, a respite worker, or a relative for real services at a fair rate. Keep a simple written agreement with duties, hours and pay, plus time sheets. Pay that is clearly below market is easy to defend. A large, vague payment to a sibling who is also the trustee is not, and it can look like self-dealing. Our guide to [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) covers the same issue in the context of aging parents.

### Funeral and final costs

Many trusts allow the trustee to pay for the beneficiary's funeral and burial. Some first-party trusts set these costs aside ahead of the state's payback claim. The [payback provision article](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) explains the order of payment.

## What should be handled with care?

Two areas deserve more thought than the rest.

**Cash.** Money handed to the beneficiary is income. A gift card works much the same way. If the beneficiary wants spending money, the trustee can arrange an allowance through a prepaid card with limits or, better, go shopping together and pay at the register. Some families use an [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) as the spending account, funded by the trust, because the account can pay for housing and everyday costs within its own rules.

**Shelter.** Rent, a mortgage, property tax and utilities can count as in-kind support, which lowers the SSI check by a portion. That is not always a reason to refuse. A trustee who pays the rent so a young adult can live independently is making a trade, and the trade is often a good one. Our article on [owning a house in a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/can-a-special-needs-trust-own-a-house) covers that decision in detail.

## What if a trustee wants to pay for something unusual?

1. Check the trust document for a stated purpose or a limit.
2. Ask whether the item is cash, shelter or neither.
3. Confirm with the benefit worker or the family's attorney if the choice affects SSI or a Medicaid waiver.
4. Pay the vendor directly and keep the invoice.
5. Record the reason in the ledger in a sentence or two.

## A worked example

Rosa, 54, is the trustee of a third-party trust for her brother Victor, 49, who has schizophrenia and lives in a supported apartment. In one year the trust pays for a new laptop, a dental implant that Medicaid did not cover, a week at a lake cabin with a support worker, vet bills for Victor's cat, and a used car so he can get to a part-time job. It also covers the time of a respite worker for two weekends. Rosa does not give Victor cash, and she does not pay his rent, which he covers from his benefits and earnings. The trust spends about the same each year, keeps a ledger, and files what it must.

Rosa could have chosen to pay the rent, too, and accepted the SSI reduction. She talked it through with an attorney and decided against it. Either choice was reasonable.

## How does state law change this?

Medicaid waiver rules, what a state requires in a first-party trust, and how a state treats certain purchases vary. Ask a local attorney how your state treats vehicles, caregivers and payback priorities. Our state guides, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) and [Ohio](/estate-planning/ohio), outline state basics.
