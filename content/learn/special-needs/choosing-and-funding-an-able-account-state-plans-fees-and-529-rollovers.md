Tanya and Roger have decided to open an ABLE account for their daughter Priya, 19. Then they hit a wall. Their own state has a plan. So do forty other states, and the websites all say they are the right one. Priya's grandmother wants to send money. A 529 account for college sits half-used. And a special needs trust is on the way.

Our main article on [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) explains what the account is, who qualifies and how it affects SSI and Medicaid. This article covers the practical sequel: which plan to pick, what the fees really are, how money gets in, and how an ABLE account fits next to a trust. Start with our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs) if you are new to the topic.

## Do you have to use your own state's ABLE plan?

Generally no. ABLE accounts are created by states, but most plans let residents of any state enroll. A few restrict enrollment to their own residents or give residents better terms. Many states also contract with a shared program, so the same underlying plan may appear under several state names.

A person may have only one ABLE account in general, with a narrow exception for a rollover between accounts, so choose with care. Moving to another plan later is possible, through a program-to-program rollover, but it creates paperwork and sometimes a fee. Treat the choice as a decision that will last, not a trial.

Your state's own plan can still be the right choice. Some states let residents deduct contributions from state income tax, and some give a matching deposit or extra consumer protection. Whether Illinois or New Jersey does is worth confirming on the current plan documents, since states change these terms. Our article on [special needs planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey) is a place to start for that state.

## What should you compare between plans?

Look at six things, in this order.

| What to compare | Why it matters | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Annual account fee | Charged whatever the balance | A flat yearly fee, sometimes waived for online statements or residents |
| Asset-based fee | A percentage of the balance, taken from investment returns | Low for a small account is good; check how it scales |
| Investment menu | Money sits in funds, a savings option, or both | An FDIC-insured savings choice for spending money and a fund option for long-term money |
| Debit card and bill pay | How the owner spends independently | Card fees, replacement fees, ATM charges, and whether bills can be paid online |
| State tax benefit | May lower your state tax bill | Usually only for that state's taxpayers; confirm the rules |
| Contribution tools | How family adds money | A gift link or code, automatic transfers, and ease of tracking |

Run the numbers for the balance you expect. A flat $30 annual fee is a large share of a $2,000 account and trivial for $60,000. A small asset-based fee works the other way. If the account will mostly be a spending account that gets topped up monthly, the savings option and debit card matter more than the investment menu.

Read the plan disclosure statement, not just the marketing page. The disclosure lists every fee, the state's rules for non-residents and what happens if the plan is changed or closed.

## Who can contribute, and how much?

Anyone can put money into an ABLE account, including the owner. The combined total from all contributors cannot exceed the annual limit, which equals the federal gift tax annual exclusion: $19,000 per year in 2026. A gift of that size is not reportable. If you want the details, our article on [gift taxes and the annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax) puts it in context.

Because the limit is shared, families do best with a simple rule. Tanya and Roger might give each relative a dollar target for the year and a way to send it, and keep one person responsible for tracking the total. Many plans warn contributors when a deposit would put the account over the limit and return the excess.

**Working owners.** A beneficiary who earns wages may contribute more than the annual limit, up to a cap tied to the federal poverty level for a single person, under the ABLE to Work provision. It applies only if the person's employer is not also contributing to a retirement plan for them. Check the plan for how it asks for proof of earnings. See the main ABLE article for the full description and for the age-of-onset change to 46.

**The $100,000 line.** SSI counts an ABLE balance as a resource only above $100,000. Over that, SSI payments can be suspended until the balance drops, though Medicaid generally continues. Contributions should be planned with that ceiling in view.

## How does a 529 rollover work?

A family can currently move money from a 529 college savings account into an ABLE account if the ABLE owner is the 529 beneficiary or a member of that beneficiary's family. The rollover counts toward the annual ABLE limit. It cannot exceed $19,000 in a year, including any other contributions.

Say Priya has $38,000 left in a 529 that her grandfather opened. She will not use the money for college. Moving it takes two years if no one else contributes: $19,000 in each of two calendar years. Rolling more than the annual limit in one year leaves the excess in the 529, which can cause tax trouble if it is withdrawn instead of rolled.

Steps to follow:

1. Confirm that the 529 and the ABLE account are for the same person, or for family members as the law defines them.
2. Ask the 529 plan administrator for its ABLE rollover form. Some require that the transfer be sent directly from the plan to the ABLE plan.
3. Ask the ABLE plan how it records the deposit and how it tracks the annual limit.
4. Keep the confirmation and the year-end statement for tax filing.
5. Confirm the current federal rule before you act, since Congress has adjusted ABLE provisions several times and plan rules can lag behind.

If the 529 owner is a grandparent, talk first. The rollover is a taxable-event risk if done wrong, and a grandparent's account often has its own estate planning role.

## How should an ABLE account work with a special needs trust?

They do different jobs, and many families use both. A trust can hold large sums and pay for things that cost more than an ABLE account's annual limit. An ABLE account is the owner's own spending account, with a debit card and no trustee approval for each purchase.

Here is a workable pattern. The trust pays large or complex costs, such as rent supplements, therapy, a van or an attorney's fee. The trustee also moves a set monthly amount into the ABLE account, for groceries, transit, a phone plan and small purchases the beneficiary controls. The trust document needs to allow it, and the trustee should say in writing why the transfer helps the beneficiary. Our article on [what a special needs trust can pay for](/learn/special-needs/what-can-special-needs-trust-funds-pay-for) lists the rules.

One caution sits at the end. At the owner's death, a state can file a claim against the remaining ABLE balance for Medicaid it paid, much as a first-party trust has a payback. Our explanation of the [special needs trust payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) describes the rule for trusts. For parents, that is a reason to keep large long-term savings in a third-party trust, which has no payback, and to use the ABLE account for day-to-day money.

## What can the money buy, and how do you stay safe?

Qualified disability expenses include housing, education, transportation, health care, employment support, assistive technology, legal fees, funeral expenses and basic living costs. The list is broad. Keep receipts, because the account owner can be asked to show that the withdrawals were qualified. Spending on a non-qualified item can be taxed and penalized, and it can count as income for benefits.

Rent and utilities are the usual question. Money paid from an ABLE account for housing is a qualified expense and generally does not trigger the SSI reduction for in-kind support, a point worth checking against the SSA's current rules in our article on [what counts as income and resources for SSI](/learn/special-needs/what-counts-as-income-and-resources-for-ssi).

## A checklist for opening the account

1. Confirm the owner's eligibility, including the age of onset and a signed disability certification.
2. List two or three plans and compare fees on the balance you expect.
3. Choose one owner, one account and one person who will keep the records.
4. Decide who will contribute and what the yearly target is.
5. Check whether a 529 or a trust will fund the account.
6. Open the account, set up the debit card and name a successor if the plan allows.
7. Keep every receipt for qualified spending.
8. Review once a year: the fees, the balance and the cap.
