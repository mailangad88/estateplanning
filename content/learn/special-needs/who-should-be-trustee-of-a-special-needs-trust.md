A special needs trust is only as good as the person running it. The documents can be perfect, but if the trustee pays for the wrong thing, lets a bill go unpaid, or never learns how Social Security counts income, a lifetime of planning slips away in a year or two.

The question families get stuck on is who. Parents want to do it themselves. They also know they will not be here forever. This page walks through the options, what each one is good and bad at, and how to build a chain so there is always someone in charge.

## The short version

- The trustee has real power: they decide what gets paid, and a wrong payment can cost benefits.
- A parent is a fine first trustee. A successor is what protects your child once you are gone.
- Family members bring knowledge of the person. Professionals bring record-keeping, tax filing and continuity.
- A co-trustee arrangement, family plus professional, covers both.
- The beneficiary cannot be trustee of their own trust.

## What does a special needs trustee actually do?

More than a typical trustee. A trustee of an ordinary family trust invests the assets and pays the beneficiary on a schedule. A special needs trustee has discretion over every payment and has to know which payments are safe.

In practice it means:

1. Paying vendors and providers directly, not handing cash to the beneficiary.
2. Knowing which purchases affect SSI or Medicaid. Our page on [what the trust can pay for](/learn/special-needs/what-can-special-needs-trust-funds-pay-for) is a trustee's starting list.
3. Keeping receipts and a ledger so benefit agencies can be shown what was spent and why.
4. Filing the trust's tax returns, as explained in our article on [how a special needs trust is taxed](/learn/special-needs/how-a-special-needs-trust-is-taxed).
5. Investing prudently for a horizon that may be 50 years.
6. Staying in touch with the beneficiary, the caregivers, and the care team.

It is a fiduciary job, and a trustee who fails can be held personally responsible. Our general guide to [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) covers qualities that apply to every trust. This page adds what is particular to benefits.

## Can a parent be the trustee?

Yes, and most do the job at the start. Parents know the child, they usually are the ones paying for things, and they have the most at stake. The trouble is the calendar. A parent in good health at 55 may be 80 when the child is 45, and a decline in memory can show up in the trust's books before the family notices.

Take Nancy, 61, and her son Jordan, 29, who has Down syndrome and receives SSI. Nancy is trustee of a third-party trust her late husband set up. She is organized, keeps a binder of receipts, and knows the case worker by name. That works. But the trust does not name a successor, so if Nancy had a stroke tomorrow, someone would have to ask a court to appoint one. The fix is easy: name two successors in order, and review the choice every few years.

A parent should also resist mixing the trust with their own finances. A trust account in the parent's name, a pattern of paying the family's groceries from it, or loose records give an agency reason to question the arrangement.

## Can a sibling be the trustee?

A sibling is often the natural next step, and often the most loving. They know the person, share the history, and will probably still be around in 30 years. They are also a person with a job, a spouse, children and a mortgage.

Before naming a sibling, have a straight conversation:

- Are they willing, or just not able to say no?
- Do they live close enough to see the beneficiary regularly?
- Is their spouse supportive? A spouse who resents the work makes it harder.
- Will they be a remainder beneficiary of the trust? If so, they have a conflict, because every dollar they spend now is a dollar they do not inherit later. Naming a co-trustee or a trust protector reduces that conflict.

Siblings often do best with help: an accountant for the tax returns, an attorney for the occasional question, and sometimes a corporate co-trustee. Our article on [co-trustees](/learn/trusts/co-trustees-and-changing-a-trustee) explains how shared authority works.

## When is a bank or professional trustee a good idea?

A bank, trust company or professional fiduciary offers continuity. It does not get sick, move away or forget. It files the returns, keeps the books and can serve for decades. For a large trust, or one with no suitable family trustee, that stability is worth the cost.

The cost is real. Corporate trustees charge ongoing fees, and many set minimums that exclude smaller trusts. They also tend to be cautious, which can mean slow responses to requests that need judgment, like a move to a new apartment. Ask any candidate how many special needs trusts they run, whether a person you can call handles your file, and how they handle benefits reporting. The answer should be specific.

For smaller amounts, a nonprofit [pooled trust](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts) does much of the same job at lower cost.

## Can the beneficiary be their own trustee?

No, not in a way that works. A special needs trust relies on the trustee having discretion and the beneficiary having no right to demand payment. If the beneficiary is trustee and can pay themselves, Social Security treats the assets as theirs and counts them. A beneficiary can have a voice, though. They can tell the trustee what they need, attend meetings, and sit on an advisory group of family and friends. Many trusts say the trustee must consult them.

## Who should be successor trustee and trust protector?

Name at least two successors, in order, and state what happens if all of them are gone. A common chain is a parent first, then an adult sibling, then a corporate trustee. Write down a way to appoint a new one without going to court, such as the right of a majority of adult family members, or a named trust protector, to choose.

A trust protector is a person the trust gives limited powers: to remove and replace a trustee, to change technical terms when the law changes, or to move the trust to another state. They do not manage the money. Benefit rules change, and a trust drafted today will be interpreted decades from now, so this role is worth having. The protector should be independent, ideally not the person who is also the remainder beneficiary.

| Option | Strength | Weakness | Often paired with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parent | Knows the person, low cost | Age and health | Sibling as successor |
| Sibling | Lasting relationship | Time, conflicts, spouse | Professional for taxes |
| Friend | Neutral, trusted | May not stay involved | Written guidance |
| Bank or trust company | Continuity, records | Fees, minimums, distance | Family co-trustee |
| Pooled trust nonprofit | Experienced staff, low cost | Less personal control | Family advocate |

## How to prepare whoever you choose

Give your trustee more than a document. A [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) tells them about doctors, routines, preferences and what makes a good day. Give them contact information for the benefits counselor, the care team and the lawyer who drafted the trust. A short written policy on common requests, like travel or a gym membership, helps them decide consistently.

Review the choice every few years, or after a birth, death, move or divorce in the family. If you are still deciding on the structure of the trust, read how to [set up a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/how-to-set-up-a-special-needs-trust). The full picture is in our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).
