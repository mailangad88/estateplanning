Parents of an adult child with a disability often tell us the same thing: the house is the one asset I know my child will need. They want to know whether the family home can sit inside a special needs trust, or whether a trust can buy a place for their son or daughter to live. The short answer is yes. The longer answer is about how it is titled, who pays the bills, and what Social Security does with the housing costs.

This page covers the house questions only. For what the trust is and why it protects benefits, start with [special needs trusts and benefits](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust), and for the broader plan see the [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## The short version

- A special needs trust can own real estate if the document lets the trustee buy, hold and sell it.
- The deed names the trustee, "as trustee of the Ruiz Supplemental Needs Trust," not the person with the disability.
- The house is not counted as the beneficiary's resource when the trust is drafted and run properly.
- Housing costs the trust pays can reduce SSI by a portion. That reduction is often worth accepting.
- The trust needs cash, not just a house. Property tax and a roof repair do not wait.

## When the answer is yes

A special needs trust can hold a house in three common ways.

**You leave it at your death.** Suppose Carol, 71, owns a three-bedroom house and has a son, Daniel, 38, who has an intellectual disability and gets SSI and Medicaid waiver services. Her will, or her revocable trust, leaves the house to the trustee of Daniel's third-party trust. After Carol dies, the trustee holds the house, and Daniel can keep living there. Nothing is counted as Daniel's, because he does not own it.

**You deed it in while you are alive.** Some parents transfer the home to the trust during life, keeping the right to live there. This can avoid probate, but it has income tax and property tax effects, and mortgage lenders have rules about transfers. A transfer to a trust for your child is also a gift, so talk with an attorney about gift tax reporting. Our [transfer on death deed article](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) covers a lighter option for naming a trust as the person who receives the house when you die.

**The trust buys one.** A trust funded with an inheritance, an insurance payout or a settlement can buy a house or condo for the beneficiary. A first-party trust (funded with the person's own money) can do this too, and buying a home is one of the few big purchases that still benefits the person directly. The payback rule applies at death, so anything left, including the house, may be claimed first for Medicaid repayment. Our article on the [payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) explains how that works.

## When the answer is no, or not yet

The trust cannot own a house if the document does not allow it. Older trusts sometimes list only cash, stocks and "personal property." If the trustee has no power over real estate, the trustee may need a court order, or the trust may need an amendment. If the trust is irrevocable, changing it is harder than it sounds.

It also may not be the right move if the trust is small. A house with no money behind it does not provide security. Say Luis, 62, leaves his daughter Camila a house in her trust but only a modest cash account. Within a few years the account is drained by property tax, insurance, a furnace replacement and a plumber. The trustee then has to sell the house in a rush. If there is not enough cash, the house may be better sold now, with the proceeds funding the trust and paying rent in a place chosen to fit her needs. Or consider a [pooled trust](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts) combined with a modest home arrangement.

## How does Social Security treat a house the trust owns?

The house itself is not the beneficiary's resource, because the beneficiary does not own it and cannot make the trust give it to them. The monthly bills are the more sensitive issue.

When the trust pays for shelter (mortgage, rent, property tax, heating and similar), Social Security can treat that as in-kind support and maintenance and reduce SSI by a portion of the federal benefit rate. The reduction is capped, so it is smaller than the cost the trust is covering. The details are in our article on [how a special needs trust affects SSI and Medicaid](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules). Because the amounts change, ask the Social Security Administration or an attorney for current figures.

The usual choices are:

| Setup | Who pays housing costs | Effect on SSI |
|---|---|---|
| Trust owns the house, beneficiary lives there | Trust pays taxes, insurance, repairs | Possible in-kind support reduction |
| Trust owns the house, beneficiary pays a fair share from their own income | Both | A smaller reduction or none, depending on what is paid |
| Trust rents a place for the beneficiary | Trust pays the landlord | Possible reduction |
| Beneficiary lives with a parent or sibling | Family pays | Depends on the arrangement and written agreements |

None of these is wrong. The trustee should pick one on purpose and write down why.

## What should the trust say about a house?

If you want the home in the trust, the document should say, in plain language:

1. The trustee may buy, hold, improve, rent and sell real estate.
2. The trustee may pay property tax, insurance, utilities and repairs from the trust.
3. The beneficiary may live in the home rent-free or pay a stated share, and the trustee decides.
4. The trustee may allow a caregiver or housemate to live there.
5. If the home no longer works for the beneficiary, the trustee may sell and buy another.
6. Who receives the house or its sale proceeds when the beneficiary dies.

That last point is where families skip ahead. If a sibling is supposed to take over the house after your child dies, say so, and say whether the sibling pays the other siblings anything.

## What if I just leave the house to my child?

Leaving the house outright to a child on SSI does not always end benefits right away, because a home the person lives in is generally excluded as a resource. The trouble starts afterward. The child owns the house and has to pay taxes and keep it insured, and they cannot rely on others to handle a sale. If it sells, the proceeds count as a resource. A child with a cognitive disability may also be pressured into a bad sale. And a home owned outright can be exposed to a state's Medicaid estate recovery at death. That is why most families prefer the trust. For the broader picture of unequal inheritances, see [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child or sibling](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling).

## A worked example

Hannah and Peter, both 66, have two children: Grace, 40, who is independent, and Owen, 36, who has autism and lives with them. They own their home outright and have a retirement account and some savings. They sign a revocable trust that gives Owen's share to a third-party special needs trust, including the house.

On the second death, the trustee, Grace, keeps the house in the trust. The trustee pays the taxes and insurance. Owen stays there, with a part-time housemate who helps him and pays a modest rent to the trust. Savings in the trust cover repairs and Owen's therapies. Later, when Owen needs more support, Grace can sell the house, buy a smaller one near services, and keep everything inside the trust. When Owen dies, the remainder goes to Grace's children, and no state payback applies because Owen's own money was never in the trust.

## How does state law change this?

Property tax rules, deed forms, rules for transfers to trusts and each state's version of Medicaid estate recovery all differ. Some states also have housing programs and tax exemptions for people with disabilities that a trust may be able to use. Our state guides, such as [California](/estate-planning/california), [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) and [New York](/estate-planning/new-york), are a good place to see how your state handles trusts and real estate.
