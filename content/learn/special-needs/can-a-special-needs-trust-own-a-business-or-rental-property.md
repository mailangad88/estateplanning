Raymond owns a landscaping company and four rental duplexes. His daughter Alicia, 31, has Down syndrome and receives SSI and Medicaid. He wants to leave the business to his son and a share of everything to Alicia, and he has heard that a special needs trust can "own" things. Can it own a landscaping company? A duplex?

The short answer is yes to both. The longer answer is that an asset a trust can legally hold is not always an asset it should hold. This article walks through what changes for benefits, who should run the business, and what usually works better than leaving a disabled child a piece of a company. For the basics of the trust itself, see our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## Can a special needs trust legally own a business interest?

A trust can own shares in a corporation, a membership interest in an LLC, a partnership interest or a sole proprietorship's assets, as long as the trust document gives the trustee that power and the business documents allow the transfer. Many operating agreements limit who can be an owner. Some require other members' consent or give them a right of first refusal when an interest changes hands.

So there are two documents to read before anything moves: the trust and the operating or shareholder agreement. If the trust says "the trustee shall invest only in publicly traded securities," it will not work. If the LLC agreement bars transfers to a trust, it needs an amendment.

## What does owning a business do to SSI and Medicaid?

If the trust is a third-party trust, the beneficiary does not own the business, so the business is not counted as a resource when SSI is calculated. That is the whole reason the trust exists. What the trust pays out to the beneficiary changes the picture:

| What the trust does | Effect on SSI |
|---|---|
| Keeps profits inside the trust | None |
| Pays a vendor for something the beneficiary needs (a therapist, a phone, a trip) | Generally none |
| Pays the beneficiary's rent or mortgage | Counts as in-kind support, which can reduce SSI by a capped amount |
| Hands cash to the beneficiary | Counts as unearned income, with a dollar-for-dollar reduction after a small exclusion |

Our article on [special needs trusts, SSI and Medicaid rules](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) covers the formulas. For this question, the point is that business profits are not a problem until they leave the trust as cash.

A first-party trust works differently in one respect. If the business belongs to the beneficiary, say a small shop they started, and it goes into a (d)(4)(A) trust, the trust must pay back Medicaid at death out of all remaining assets, including the value of the business. See [the payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision).

## Who should run the business, and who should be trustee?

The person who runs the business and the person who decides what the beneficiary receives from it should not be the same person unless the document provides safeguards. This is the part of these plans that goes wrong most often.

Take Raymond's son, Tomas, who runs the landscaping company. If Tomas is also the only trustee of the trust that owns 30 percent of the company, he decides how much profit is distributed to the trust, how much stays in the company for trucks and payroll, and what his own salary is. Each choice affects his sister, and each is a place for a disagreement.

Safeguards our attorneys use:

1. A co-trustee or independent trustee who handles decisions about the business interest, valuation and distributions.
2. A written distribution policy, for example a minimum percentage of net profit to be distributed each year to the trust.
3. An annual report from the company to the trustee, with the right to see the books.
4. A trust protector who can replace the trustee and who is not the manager.
5. A buyout option, discussed below.

Our guide to [choosing a trustee for a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/who-should-be-trustee-of-a-special-needs-trust) explains the role in general terms.

## Is a business a good thing to leave a disabled child?

Often it is not, and the reason is cash. A trust needs a steady supply of money to pay for a beneficiary's extras. A closely held business can be profitable on paper and still pay no distributions, because owners reinvest the profit. The beneficiary then holds a share worth something and receives nothing.

A business also demands things the trust does not want: a vote, a manager, a decision when a partner wants to sell. The trustee takes on all of this as a fiduciary and can be held to account for risk. A trust full of one company is concentrated, and a bad year hurts the beneficiary most.

Three alternatives we see more often:

- **A buyout at death.** A buy-sell agreement, funded by life insurance, lets the sibling who runs the company buy out the trust's interest for a price and terms set in advance. The trust receives cash it can invest. See [buy-sell agreements](/glossary/buy-sell-agreement) and our article on [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).
- **Equalize with other assets.** Leave the business to the child who works in it and leave the trust a larger share of the investments, the house or life insurance. Our article on [life insurance for a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/life-insurance-for-a-special-needs-trust) explains how that works.
- **A preferred or non-voting interest.** The trust can hold an interest that receives a fixed distribution but has no vote. This takes careful drafting and a business attorney.

## What about rental property in the trust?

A rental property is simpler than an operating business, because the income is rent and the work can be handed to a property manager. The rent goes to the trust. If the trust pays a portion of the beneficiary's bills, those payments are subject to the same SSI rules as any other distribution.

Four issues come up. Our article on a [landlord estate plan](/learn/business-owners/landlord-estate-plan-rental-properties-llc-or-trust) covers the LLC side:

- **Liability.** If a tenant is injured, the trust and its other assets could be sued. Many trustees hold the property in an LLC owned by the trust and carry a liability policy.
- **Management.** Someone has to find tenants, handle repairs and keep records. A family trustee can do it, or the trust can pay a manager.
- **Housing the beneficiary.** If Alicia lives in one of the duplexes, the rent she pays matters for SSI. Paying less than fair rent can reduce her benefit. Our article on [whether a special needs trust can own a house](/learn/special-needs/can-a-special-needs-trust-own-a-house) covers the structure.
- **Taxes.** Rental income is taxed to the trust or the beneficiary according to the trust's terms. See [how a special needs trust is taxed](/learn/special-needs/how-a-special-needs-trust-is-taxed).

## What if the beneficiary owns the business?

A person who receives SSI can run a small business, but the earnings and assets are measured. Net earnings count as income, and property used in the business may be treated differently from personal savings. The rules are technical, and a mistake leads to an overpayment the person has to repay. Talk to a benefits counselor, and if the beneficiary later receives a lump sum from selling the business, plan for where that money goes before it lands in a personal account.

## A worked example

Raymond decided against leaving Alicia a share of the landscaping company. His will leaves the company to Tomas and the four duplexes to a third-party special needs trust for Alicia, with a bank as co-trustee alongside Tomas and a property manager paid from rent. A $500,000 life insurance policy, owned by an irrevocable trust, adds cash for the special needs trust, and Tomas agrees in writing to cover a set amount of Alicia's care if rental income falls short. Alicia's trust pays her rent supplement and activity fees directly to the vendors. She keeps SSI and Medicaid.

For Illinois and New Jersey families the same structure applies, with a state-specific look at the LLC and trust documents. See [special needs trust rules in Illinois](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-rules-in-illinois) or [special needs planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/special-needs-planning-in-new-jersey).
