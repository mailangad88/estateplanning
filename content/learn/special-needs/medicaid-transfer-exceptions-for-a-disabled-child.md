If you may need nursing home care and you have a child with a disability, you have something most people do not: federal Medicaid law carves out special treatment for your child. The usual advice not to give your house away within five years of applying for Medicaid has exceptions, and your child is one of them.

The exceptions are real, but they are narrower than they look in a family conversation. This page explains which transfers are protected, what the agency will want to see, and where a protected transfer can still go wrong.

## The short version

- A home can be transferred to a child who is blind or disabled without a penalty period.
- Other assets can go to a trust solely for the benefit of that child, or to a trust for a disabled person under 65, without penalty.
- The caregiver child exception is separate and does not require disability.
- None of this helps your child's own eligibility if the asset lands in their name.
- Talk to an attorney before you sign a deed, because a deed cannot always be undone.

## Why does Medicaid penalize gifts at all?

Medicaid for long-term care is for people who have limited resources. To stop families from giving everything away and then applying, the agency reviews your transfers for the past 60 months in most states. Gifts made in that window create a penalty: a stretch of time when Medicaid will not pay for your nursing home care. Our page on the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains how the penalty is calculated.

Exceptions exist because Congress decided some transfers should not be punished. A few of them involve children with disabilities, and that is where families have more room than they usually realize.

## Which transfers to a disabled child are exempt?

| Transfer | Exempt from the penalty? | Main condition |
|---|---|---|
| Home to a child who is blind or disabled | Yes | Child meets the Social Security definition of disability |
| Other assets to that child outright | Yes under federal law, but a bad idea | Child's own SSI and Medicaid can end |
| Assets to a trust solely for the benefit of a disabled child | Yes | Trust must meet the agency's sole benefit rules |
| Assets to a trust for any disabled person under 65 | Yes | Sole benefit and payback or similar terms |
| Home to a caregiver child | Yes | Lived there two years and provided qualifying care |
| Home to a sibling with an equity interest | Yes | Sibling lived in the home at least a year |

The federal definition of disability here is the Social Security Act's. A child who already receives SSI or SSDI on the basis of disability almost always qualifies. A child with a diagnosis but no benefits may need a medical determination to prove it, and the agency, not you, decides.

## Should you deed the house to your disabled child?

Often not directly. Consider Rosa, 79, who lives in the house with her son Luis, 52. Luis has an intellectual disability and receives SSI and Medicaid waiver services. Rosa expects to need a nursing home in a few years.

If Rosa deeds the house to Luis, Medicaid will not penalize her. For SSI, a person's home is generally an excluded resource if he lives there. But this arrangement has weak spots:

- Luis now owns the house and is responsible for taxes, insurance and repairs. If he has to leave, for a group home or hospital stay, the exclusion can be lost.
- If Luis later needs Medicaid long-term care himself, his state may seek recovery from his estate.
- Luis cannot easily manage a sale or a decision to move, and may need a guardian or conservator.
- Because the house passed as a lifetime gift, Rosa's basis carries over to Luis. If he or the trust sells later, the gain is measured from what Rosa paid, not the value at her death. Inherited property would usually get a step-up. See our [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) entry.

The usual alternative is to deed the house to a trust solely for Luis's benefit. The trustee owns the title, pays the bills and can sell if Luis moves. Our page on [whether a special needs trust can own a house](/learn/special-needs/can-a-special-needs-trust-own-a-house) covers how the trust and SSI interact. The trade-off is more drafting, and the state agency will read the trust language closely.

## What counts as a trust for the sole benefit?

States read "sole benefit" strictly. In general the person must be the only one who benefits from the trust during their life. The agency may require that the trust spend down on an actuarially sound basis over the person's life expectancy, or that it pay back the state when the beneficiary dies. Some states accept a trust without payback if the payout schedule meets the rule. Others do not. A trust that gives your other children any current benefit, or lets the trustee spend freely for the whole family, can destroy the exception.

That is why this planning is separate from a normal [third-party special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust), which usually has no payback because the money came from you. A sole-benefit trust used to protect a Medicaid transfer is a different design, and the [payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) question changes when the trust is funded to protect your eligibility.

## What about the caregiver child exception?

If your adult child lived with you for at least two years before you moved to a facility, and provided care that kept you at home longer than you otherwise could have stayed, you may transfer the home to that child without penalty. The child need not be disabled.

Doctors' letters, records of appointments, and a log of daily help are how families prove this. Many agency denials turn on thin documentation. For related background, read our guide to [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).

## What are the risks even when the transfer is exempt?

- **Loss of control.** After a deed, the house is no longer yours. If your child's circumstances change, you cannot take it back.
- **Estate recovery.** Medicaid can seek repayment from certain assets after a recipient dies. Federal rules protect the home from recovery while a disabled child survives, but the protection ends. Read [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) before relying on any plan.
- **Tax.** Lifetime gifts can require a gift tax return, even if no tax is due, and the carryover basis described above can mean income tax on a sale.
- **Wrong disability proof.** Without a determination, the agency can treat the transfer as an ordinary gift.
- **Other children.** A deed to one child changes what the others will inherit. Talk before, not after.

## How should you do it?

1. Confirm your child's status. Gather the SSI or SSDI award letter, or medical records if there is none.
2. Decide the goal: keep the house in the family, protect your Medicaid eligibility, protect your child's benefits. They pull in different directions.
3. Have an attorney compare an outright deed, a deed to a trust, and keeping the house with a plan for later.
4. If a trust is used, confirm in writing how your state agency reads "sole benefit."
5. Sign, record and fund the trust, and keep copies of every document and the dates.
6. Report the transfer accurately on the Medicaid application, with the exception explained.

## How state law changes this

Medicaid is run by states within federal limits. States differ on how they define sole benefit, whether they require payback for a trust funded for a disabled child, how they handle estate recovery, and how they document the caregiver child exception. See your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois) for local points, and use our [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) to see your own five-year window.
