If you have been told your family needs a special needs trust, the next question is often which kind. The two main types look similar on paper and behave very differently when it matters: when money goes in, when the beneficiary gets help from Medicaid, and when the beneficiary dies.

This guide compares the two, explains the Medicaid payback rule and pooled trusts, and shows how to decide which one fits a given situation. As always, state rules differ, and an attorney who focuses on this area should confirm the details for your state.

## What is the basic difference?

The difference is the source of the money. The rules follow from that.

| | Third-party trust | First-party (self-settled) trust |
|---|---|---|
| Source of funds | Money from anyone other than the beneficiary | Money that belongs to the beneficiary |
| Typical examples | Parents' estate plan, life insurance, gifts from grandparents | Settlement or court award, back SSI or SSDI, inheritance received outright, child support arrears owed to the person |
| Who can create it | Parents, relatives, anyone | Beneficiary, parent, grandparent, guardian or court (federal law, (d)(4)(A) trusts) |
| Medicaid payback | None | Required at the beneficiary's death |
| Age limit | None | Generally the beneficiary must be under 65 when funds are put in |
| Can be revocable | By the creator, until death | No, irrevocable |
| Leftover goes to | Anyone the creator names | Medicaid first, then others |

The legal references are to a federal statute that authorizes certain trusts to be disregarded as resources. Families do not need to memorize the citations, but a good attorney will know which one applies. For the basics of how these trusts protect benefits, start with [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).

## How does a third-party special needs trust work?

A third-party trust is the tool of choice for family estate plans. Because the assets were never the beneficiary's, federal law does not require a Medicaid payback. The creator controls what happens to the leftover assets.

Common features:

- Parents fund it through their will or a living trust, and name it as beneficiary of life insurance and retirement accounts.
- Grandparents, aunts, uncles and friends can leave money to the trust in their own wills or make gifts.
- The trustee has full discretion and uses funds to supplement benefits.
- At the beneficiary's death, the remainder goes to siblings, other relatives, a charity, or whomever the creator chooses.

There is no limit on how much can be put in and no age limit. The trust can last the beneficiary's whole life.

Because there is no payback, this is the trust families should try to use whenever the money is theirs to give. A grandparent who plans a gift can send it to the third-party trust, and the person with a disability never owns it.

## How does a first-party special needs trust work?

A first-party trust, sometimes called a self-settled or (d)(4)(A) trust, holds money that belongs to the beneficiary. Without the trust, putting these funds in the person's name would end their SSI and could disrupt Medicaid.

Federal law lets such a trust be excluded from SSI and Medicaid resource counting if:

1. The beneficiary is under 65 when the trust is created and funded.
2. The beneficiary meets the Social Security definition of disability.
3. The trust is irrevocable and for the sole benefit of the beneficiary.
4. The trust says that when the beneficiary dies, the trust will repay states for Medicaid assistance provided during the person's life, before other people receive anything.

The payback is a bigger deal than it sounds. Medicaid can cover decades of services, and a settlement of $200,000 can be largely absorbed by a state claim. However, the trust can pay for the beneficiary's needs during life, and some expenses at death, such as taxes and funeral costs, may be paid before the state claim, depending on the trust's wording and state law. Because the beneficiary can use the funds throughout their lifetime, many families still find it the right tool.

### What happens when someone over 65 receives money?

A first-party trust of this type generally requires the beneficiary to be under 65. Over that age, funding it is generally not an option, and a transfer into a trust may be treated as a disqualifying transfer for Medicaid. Some pooled trusts accept funds from older beneficiaries, but whether a transfer triggers a Medicaid penalty depends on state law. This is one more reason to see an attorney before putting money anywhere.

## What is a pooled special needs trust?

A pooled trust is managed by a nonprofit organization. Each beneficiary has a separate account, but the nonprofit invests and administers the funds together. Federal law treats these trusts as a distinct category, and they can accept first-party money.

Benefits of pooled trusts:

- They cost less to set up than a custom trust, and professional management is built in.
- They may accept smaller amounts, which makes them a fit for a modest settlement.
- Some allow beneficiaries over 65 to contribute, under state-specific rules.
- A nonprofit trustee does not retire or die.

Differences to understand:

- At death, the nonprofit may keep some of the remaining funds for its other beneficiaries, with the rest repaid to Medicaid, or it may repay Medicaid. The rule depends on the trust's terms and state law.
- Fees and rules vary, so compare several, and ask how distributions are approved.
- Not every state treats pooled trusts the same way for Medicaid, especially for transfers by people over 65.
- Some pooled trusts accept third-party funds in a separate account, which can have no payback. Ask for it if that is your situation.

## Which one do you need?

Answer these questions in order.

1. **Whose money is it?** If the money is parents' or relatives', use a third-party trust. If it belongs to the person with a disability, use a first-party or pooled trust.
2. **How old is the beneficiary?** Under 65 gives more options for a first-party trust.
3. **How much money is involved?** For a small settlement, a pooled trust is often the practical choice. For larger amounts, a stand-alone first-party trust gives more control.
4. **Does the family want leftover funds to go to relatives?** Only a third-party trust makes that possible.

### Example: Marcus

Marcus, 31, receives SSI and Medicaid after a brain injury. He wins a $240,000 settlement. His attorney sets up a first-party trust, with his mother as trustee. The trust pays for therapy, an accessible van, and home modifications. When Marcus dies, the state submits a Medicaid claim against the trust. Meanwhile, Marcus's aunt plans to leave him $50,000. She is told to leave it to a third-party trust that Marcus's parents created, so that this money has no payback and the leftover goes to his sister's children.

That second step is important. Families often make the mistake of mixing the two kinds of money in one trust. Third-party money placed in a first-party trust may become subject to the payback. Keep them in separate trusts.

## What common mistakes should you avoid?

- Leaving money outright to a person who receives benefits
- Naming the person with a disability directly as beneficiary of life insurance or an IRA
- Combining first-party and third-party funds in one trust
- Missing the under-65 cutoff
- Failing to name a successor trustee
- Skipping the trust because the child "doesn't get benefits yet," when the child may qualify later
- Using a trust that is not drafted for the current rules; older documents may be out of date
