Grandparents are often the first people to ask what they can do. They have savings, a house or a life insurance policy, and a grandchild they love who has autism, epilepsy or a genetic condition. The difficulty is that the generous thing, putting the grandchild's name on a check or a will, is exactly what can cause harm.

This page walks through the safe ways to leave or give money to a grandchild who has special needs. It also covers the part many families skip: telling the rest of the relatives so nobody undoes the plan by accident.

## Why is leaving money directly to the grandchild risky?

Because needs-based benefits such as SSI and most Medicaid programs count what a person owns and receives. A gift in a will arrives as an asset in the grandchild's name. Once the grandchild's countable resources pass the program limit, benefits can stop until the money is spent down. Our page on [what happens when an SSI recipient is left money](/learn/special-needs/ssi-recipient-inheritance-gift-or-settlement-what-to-do) goes through the steps, and the what-if article on a [disabled heir who inherits directly](/learn/what-if/disabled-heir-inherits-directly) shows how it plays out.

A minor grandchild brings a second wrinkle. A gift to a child under 18 often goes into a court-supervised account or a custodial account, which the child gets at the age of majority. That could be an unwelcome surprise at 18 or 21. A third-party special needs trust avoids both issues because the trustee controls the money and the grandchild has no right to demand it.

## What are the options for grandparents?

| Option | How it works | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| Name the parents' existing special needs trust | Your will and forms leave the gift to the trustee of the trust by name | The parents already have a trust |
| Create a trust in your own will or living trust | The grandchild's share is held in a testamentary or sub-trust | Parents have not set one up |
| Fund a stand-alone trust now | You sign a trust during life and add money over time | You want to give while alive |
| Contribute to an ABLE account | Gifts go into a tax-advantaged account the grandchild owns, with yearly contribution caps | Smaller, regular gifts meant for spending |
| Pay bills directly | You pay a provider for therapy, a device or a trip | Occasional help, no account needed |

Most families land on the first one. It keeps all the money for the grandchild in a single trust and a single set of records, with one trustee who knows the situation.

### Ask the parents for the trust details

Before you change anything, ask the parents for the full legal name of the trust, its date, and the trustee's name. A will that says "to my grandson's special needs trust" with no specifics invites a fight. A line like "to the trustee of the Garcia Family Supplemental Needs Trust dated May 3, 2024" does not.

If the parents' trust is named in your documents, check that it is a third-party trust, not a trust for the grandchild's own money. A trust for the grandchild's own assets usually has a payback to the state when the grandchild dies. Money from you should go to the third-party kind, so any remainder passes to the people you and the parents choose. See [first-party vs. third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust).

### Build a backup into your own will

Even if the parents have a trust today, your will should include a fallback. Write that if a grandchild is receiving or may apply for means-tested benefits when you die, their share goes to a special needs trust with a named trustee, whom you choose. If the grandchild is not on benefits at the time, the share can pass outright, or in another trust, as you prefer.

Doris, 78, has four grandchildren. Her son Evan's daughter Ellie, 11, has a seizure disorder and may need benefits as an adult. Doris splits her estate in thirds among her children, with each child's share going to that child's own children. Her attorney adds a clause: Ellie's share is held by a trustee Doris names, in a discretionary trust, until Ellie is 30. At that point the trustee can distribute it if Ellie is not on needs-based benefits, or continue to hold it if she is. Doris's other grandchildren get their shares in a standard trust. Evan and his wife feel the family has treated Ellie fairly, with no one's share reduced to pay for her.

## What if the grandparents' money would pass through the parents first?

The usual problem is the order of death. Suppose your estate goes to your daughter, and she is expected to provide for her son. If she dies before you, or dies soon after you without changing her own plan, the money goes wherever her will says, which may be her spouse or other children. Naming the trust directly, as the beneficiary, removes that gap. Our article about [leaving an inheritance to grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/leaving-an-inheritance-to-grandchildren) covers the broader approach, including what happens if a parent dies first.

## How do you tell the other relatives?

This is the step that matters most and gets skipped. An aunt writes a will leaving "$20,000 to my niece's son." A neighbor names the child on a life insurance form. A cousin sets up a college account. None of them know about benefits, and the next thing the parents hear is a letter from a bank.

A simple one-page sheet helps:

1. State the grandchild's name and say that benefits matter.
2. Give the exact legal name of the trust and the trustee's contact details.
3. Explain, in a sentence, that gifts should go to the trust, not the child, and why.
4. List the types of gifts that work: gifts by will, by beneficiary form, or toward bills paid directly.
5. Offer to put relatives in touch with the family's attorney.

Send it at holidays, not at a funeral. Update it if the trustee changes. Keep the tone light. Most relatives are glad to be told what helps.

## Do grandparents face special tax questions?

A few. Gifts to a trust that gives the trustee discretion usually do not qualify for the annual gift tax exclusion, which is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, because the beneficiary has no immediate right to the money. A gift tax return may be needed, but with the federal lifetime exclusion at $15,000,000 per person in 2026, actual tax is rarely owed. Gifts that skip a generation can also raise the generation-skipping transfer tax, covered in [our GST tax article](/learn/estate-tax/generation-skipping-transfer-tax), though most families fall well below the thresholds. Tuition or medical bills paid directly to the provider may have separate gift tax exclusions. Ask your tax adviser.

## What should you do first?

1. Talk with your child, the grandchild's parent, about the trust and their plans.
2. Collect trust details or decide you will create your own.
3. Update your will, living trust and every beneficiary form: life insurance, IRAs and annuities. Use our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) to list them.
4. Add a fallback in case the plan changes.
5. Share the one-page sheet with relatives.
6. Review every few years, because a grandchild's needs, and benefits, change.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how testamentary trusts are set up and supervised, on whether a court has to oversee a trust for a minor, and on how the state Medicaid agency treats third-party trusts. Our [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas) shows what one state's rules look like, and a local attorney should confirm the language for yours.
