Carol, 67, has a daughter, Renee, 38, who has lived with bipolar disorder since her early twenties. Renee has been hospitalized three times, has an on-and-off history with alcohol and sometimes goes months without calling. Carol's estate is a house and some savings, and her current will splits everything equally between Renee and her two brothers.

Carol is afraid of what happens if Renee receives $200,000 in a lump sum. She is also afraid of leaving Renee out and being wrong. This page is for parents in that position. It explains which trust to use, who should manage it, and what language does the work. It is part of our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs), and it assumes the child is an adult.

## What kind of trust should you use for an adult child with mental illness or addiction?

The choice turns on one question: does your child receive, or will they probably need, a benefit with a financial limit, such as SSI or Medicaid? If yes, use a third-party [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust). If no, use a discretionary trust with a spendthrift clause. If you cannot tell, draft the second with a switch that makes it the first.

| Situation | Trust to use | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| Child receives SSI or Medicaid | Special needs trust | Trustee has sole discretion; child cannot demand money; payments avoid cash |
| Child receives SSDI only and works little | Discretionary spendthrift trust, with a special needs backup | Flexible, but ready to convert if Medicaid is needed |
| Child is not on benefits, history of addiction | Discretionary spendthrift trust | Trustee pays bills directly; can withhold cash |
| Child is stable but impulsive with money | Staged trust with a trustee to ask | Larger payments at later ages with trustee consent |

The word "discretionary" means the trustee, not the child, decides if and when money is paid. The word "spendthrift" means the child cannot sign over or borrow against the trust, and most creditors cannot reach it. Both Illinois and New Jersey have adopted trust codes that recognize spendthrift clauses, though each has exceptions for things like child support. Our page on [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) explains the limits.

## Does a mental illness diagnosis mean my child needs a special needs trust?

No. A diagnosis does not decide it, the benefits do. Someone with schizophrenia who works part-time and has no benefits might do better with a discretionary trust. Someone with the same diagnosis who relies on Medicaid for medication and housing needs a special needs trust, because a gift or inheritance could cut off coverage. Our article on [planning by diagnosis](/learn/special-needs/who-qualifies-as-disabled-and-planning-by-diagnosis) explains how the Social Security definition of disability works.

SSI looks at income and savings. SSDI looks at past work, not savings. Medicaid depends on the state and the program. A parent should check which benefits the child receives now, which they might need later and whether either is affected by an inheritance. The article on [SSI and Medicaid rules for a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) covers what a trust can pay without reducing benefits.

## How does a trust protect a child with addiction?

It stops the money from becoming cash in the child's hands. A well-written trust gives the trustee tools that an outright inheritance does not:

- **Pay vendors directly.** The trustee pays rent, utilities, insurance and treatment costs to the provider.
- **Use small, regular payments.** A modest monthly amount is less dangerous than a lump sum, and it can be reduced or paused.
- **Test before cash.** The trust can require a negative drug or alcohol screen or proof of treatment participation before any cash is paid.
- **Pay for treatment.** The trustee may be required to pay for rehab, therapy, sober living and medication, even when the child objects.
- **Reward recovery.** Some parents add an incentive: the trustee can add funds for a certain period of stability, such as one or two years of documented sobriety.
- **Share information.** The trust can allow the trustee to talk to doctors and treatment providers. A [HIPAA authorization](/glossary/hipaa-authorization) signed by the child makes that easier.

Most of these provisions would be no good if the child could force the trustee to pay. That is why the trust must give the trustee discretion and must not set payouts at fixed ages. Our article on [trust distribution standards](/learn/trusts/trust-distribution-standards-hems-and-discretion) explains the difference between a "health, education, maintenance and support" standard and full discretion.

## Who should be the trustee for a child with a mental illness or addiction?

An independent trustee, and not the child. Giving the child control of their own trust defeats the point. A brother or sister can be a good choice, but only if they can say no without fear of ruining the relationship, and only if they will not be stuck as a daily bank. A professional or corporate trustee costs money and has less personal knowledge, but also less emotional pressure.

Many families split the job. A sibling serves as the trustee who decides, and a bank or a trust company handles the investments and record keeping. Another approach is a trust protector who can replace the trustee if the first choice becomes unable to serve. The page on [who should be trustee of a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/who-should-be-trustee-of-a-special-needs-trust) walks through the choice, and the guide on [keeping an inheritance safe from a child's divorce, addiction or bad spouse](/learn/trusts/keeping-an-inheritance-safe-from-a-childs-divorce-addiction-or-bad-spouse) covers the broader spendthrift options.

## What would Carol's plan look like?

Carol leaves one-third of her estate to each of the three children, but Renee's share goes into a separate lifetime trust. Her son Marcus, the oldest, is the trustee, with a trust company as co-trustee for investments. The trust says the trustee may pay for housing, treatment, medical care, phone and transportation directly, may give Renee a monthly allowance of a size the trustee chooses, and may pause cash during a relapse. It adds a clause: if Renee begins receiving SSI or Medicaid, the trust converts to special needs trust terms.

When Renee dies, what remains passes to her children, if any, and otherwise to her brothers. Because the money is Carol's, no payback to Medicaid is required, which is a major difference from a trust funded with Renee's own money. See the [payback provision article](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) for the other case.

## What other documents should you sign alongside the trust?

A trust handles money. Your child's health and safety need paper too.

1. Ask your adult child to sign a [mental health advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/mental-health-advance-directive) while they are well, naming who can speak to doctors and what treatment they prefer.
2. Ask about a durable power of attorney and a HIPAA release, signed by the child, so a trusted person can step in during a crisis. The [guardianship alternatives article](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) lists less restrictive options.
3. Update every beneficiary form. A retirement account or a life insurance policy that names the child directly bypasses the trust. The [special needs trust as IRA beneficiary](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-as-ira-beneficiary) article explains the retirement account rules.
4. Write a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) for the trustee that describes what works, what has failed and who to call.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for a diagnosis or a crisis. A child with a long-standing problem needs a trust now, not after the next hospitalization.
- Naming the child as the trustee or co-trustee.
- Leaving the child the "same as the others" because it feels fair. Equal dollars are not equal protection.
- Cutting the child out without telling anyone why. A short, kind letter to the trustee prevents years of guessing.
- Setting a payout at age 40 because the child will be "mature by then."
