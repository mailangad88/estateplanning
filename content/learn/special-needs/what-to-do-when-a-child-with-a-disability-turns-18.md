The 18th birthday changes the paperwork more than it changes your child. The day before, you sign the school forms, talk to the doctor and manage the benefits. The day after, the law sees an adult, and the doctor's office, the school and Social Security may all stop talking to you.

This page is a timeline for the 6 to 12 months before the birthday, written for parents of a child with an intellectual, developmental or physical disability. It touches the main topics, then links to the pages that go deeper. If you have not read our overview of [special needs planning](/learn/special-needs), start there.

## What changes legally when my child turns 18?

Four things change at once.

- **Decision-making.** Your child makes their own medical, legal and money decisions unless a court says otherwise.
- **Privacy.** Under HIPAA, health information belongs to the adult, so a parent needs a signed release to talk to the doctor.
- **School rights.** Under the federal special education law, the rights you held as a parent transfer to the student at the age of majority, with notice to both of you. The student can still choose to have you participate.
- **Benefits.** Social Security evaluates the young adult under adult rules.

Notice what does not change. A parent has no legal authority just because the child is 18 and has a disability. The authority has to be created by a document or a court.

## Should I apply for SSI at 18?

Yes, in most cases, even if your child was denied as a minor. For a child under 18, Social Security counts part of the parents' income and assets (called deeming). At 18 that stops, and only the young adult's own income and resources count. A family earning too much to qualify at 17 may qualify at 18, as long as the young adult's own savings stay under the limit.

SSI also determines the medical test again under adult standards. A child on SSI before 18 is reviewed, and a child who was never on SSI applies for the first time. To prepare:

1. Gather medical records, school evaluations, the IEP and a list of every provider.
2. Collect identity documents, such as the birth certificate and Social Security card.
3. Open a bank account in the young adult's own name, or in a representative payee arrangement, and keep it below $2,000.
4. Apply by phone or at ssa.gov, ideally a few months before the birthday so the review can begin right away.
5. If you will manage the money, ask to be named representative payee.

Our guide to [what counts as income and resources for SSI](/learn/special-needs/what-counts-as-income-and-resources-for-ssi) shows how much a young adult can hold.

## What documents does my young adult need to sign?

Many young adults can sign the following themselves, with plain language and support, if they understand what each does:

| Document | What it does |
|---|---|
| HIPAA authorization | Lets named parents talk to doctors and see records |
| Healthcare power of attorney or advance directive | Names who makes medical choices if your child cannot |
| Durable power of attorney for property | Lets a parent manage bank, benefits and bills |
| Education release (FERPA) | Keeps the school talking to you |
| Supported decision-making agreement | Where a state recognizes it, names helpers without taking away rights |

The test is not intelligence. It is whether your child understands who they are trusting and can say so. An attorney can often meet with the young adult and confirm that in a short conversation. Read [guardianship alternatives for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) for how these documents hold up in practice, and our article on [types of power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) for the property side.

## When is guardianship the right choice?

Guardianship is the answer when your child cannot understand or sign documents, cannot make or communicate safe decisions, and no lighter option works. A court finds the person unable to make some or all decisions and appoints a guardian, who may be a parent. The cost depends on the county and the case, and the biggest cost drivers are whether a lawyer, a medical report and a court visitor are required and whether anyone contests it. In a straightforward uncontested case with parents as guardians, the process is shorter than most families fear. It also requires reports to the court each year, which is a duty many parents underestimate.

Courts increasingly ask whether a narrower order would do. A limited guardianship covers only the decisions your child cannot make, such as medical care, and leaves everything else with them. See [guardianship in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) and [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey) for local steps.

## What about school and transition planning?

Federal law requires an IEP to include transition goals by the time a student turns 16, and some states, including Illinois and New Jersey, ask districts to start earlier. Use those meetings to put adult services on the agenda, such as day programs, supported employment and the state's developmental disability agency. A student on a waiting list for adult services should be registered well before graduation, because school entitlements end and adult services are not entitlements.

## How do I protect money my child may receive?

Once benefits begin, a gift or inheritance can undo them. A grandparent's $10,000 check written to your child would put them over the $2,000 resource limit. The standard fix is a third-party special needs trust that grandparents and other relatives can use instead, plus an ABLE account for everyday savings. Our article on [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling) shows how, and a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) records daily routines for future caregivers.

## What mistakes do families make around 18?

The most common one is waiting until a crisis. Picture Dana, whose son's neurologist stops discussing his seizure medication three weeks after his birthday because no release is on file. She needs a signed HIPAA form that day. Her son could have signed it easily a month earlier, but by now he is in the hospital and less able to understand it.

Other mistakes come up again and again:

- Putting the young adult's name on a parent's bank account, which makes the balance countable to SSI in some situations.
- Letting a relative buy a car or hand over cash without checking how SSI will count it.
- Assuming the school will explain the rights transfer. It must give notice, but many families skim the form.
- Signing a guardianship petition without asking whether a narrower order is possible.
- Leaving the parents' own will untouched, so that a share passes outright to the child who can least safely hold it.

None of these is hard to fix a few months out. After the birthday, each one costs more.

## A year-before-18 timeline

- **12 months before:** Talk with the school about transition goals and ask the doctor about a capacity opinion.
- **6 months before:** Meet an attorney, decide between guardianship and alternatives, begin the SSI application.
- **3 months before:** Sign powers of attorney and releases, or file for guardianship if that is the plan.
- **At 18:** Confirm SSI, Medicaid and the representative payee setup.
- **Within 3 months after:** Update your own will so no asset passes straight to your child.
