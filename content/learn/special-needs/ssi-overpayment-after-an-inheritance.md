The letter says Social Security paid you too much, and the amount is in the thousands. It usually follows an inheritance, a lump-sum gift or a settlement that sat in a bank account while the SSI checks kept arriving. If that is where you are, the first thing to know is that the notice is not a final bill. It starts a clock, and there are three separate things you can do with it.

This page covers what to do after the overpayment letter has already come. If the money has not arrived yet, our article on [what a person on SSI should do about an inheritance or gift](/learn/special-needs/ssi-recipient-inheritance-gift-or-settlement-what-to-do) is the better place to start. The rest of the series sits under the [special needs planning pillar](/learn/special-needs).

## Why does an inheritance cause an SSI overpayment?

SSI is a needs-based benefit, so the agency checks your money on the first moment of every month. If you have more than $2,000 in countable resources ($3,000 for a couple) at that moment, you are not eligible for that month. An inheritance is counted twice in a sense: as income in the month you receive it, and as a resource starting the next month if you still have it.

Here is a simplified example. Dana, 34, gets $1,000 a month in SSI. In March her aunt's estate sends her $18,000, which she deposits into her checking account and does not report. The March check is wiped out by the income, and from April 1 she has far more than $2,000 in resources. Social Security does not learn this until a bank data match in September. It treats April through September as months she was not eligible, and the six checks she received become an overpayment of about $6,000. The example is rounded, and real figures depend on your benefit rate and other income.

Not every overpayment comes from a windfall. Unreported rent help from a relative, a change in who you live with, or a wage that was not passed along can cause one too. The steps below work for any of them.

## What does the overpayment notice tell you?

The notice states the amount, the months it covers, the reason and how Social Security plans to collect. It also tells you your rights, and it asks for the full amount within 30 days. Read the reason line first. If it says you were over the resource limit, check whether the figure Social Security used was your balance on the first of the month and whether any of the money was in an exempt form, such as a vehicle or a prepaid funeral plan.

The notice also states how much will be taken from your check. For someone who still receives SSI, Social Security generally proposes withholding the lesser of 10 percent of the monthly payment or the whole payment. That rate is a default, not a rule you must accept.

## What are your options when you get the notice?

You have three, and you can use more than one at once.

| Option | What it argues | Form or step | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Appeal (reconsideration) | The overpayment is wrong, or the amount is wrong | Written request to Social Security | 60 days from receiving the notice |
| Waiver | You were not at fault and repaying is not possible or not fair | Form SSA-632-BK, or a phone request if the amount is $2,000 or less | Any time |
| Smaller repayment | You owe it but cannot afford 10 percent | Form SSA-634 | Any time |

If you appeal within the 60 days, your current SSI payments continue while the decision is pending. That alone is a reason to file even if you are not sure you will win. Ask for the appeal in writing, keep a copy, and note the date you received the letter.

## When can you get an overpayment waived?

Social Security grants a waiver when you were not at fault in causing the overpayment, and when repaying would leave you unable to cover ordinary living costs or would be unfair for another reason. Both parts matter.

"Not at fault" looks at what you knew and what you did. Someone who reported the inheritance on time and in writing, and still got paid, has a strong case. Someone who got a letter asking about their accounts and ignored it has a weak one. The agency also considers age, education, and any intellectual or mental limitation, which is why families of people with a disability should say so in the request.

On the second part, you list your monthly income and bills. A person living on SSI alone, with rent and groceries taking nearly everything, usually shows that repayment is a hardship. If you were paid a $2,000 overpayment or less and were not at fault, you may be able to request the waiver by phone at 1-800-772-1213. Ask for a written confirmation either way.

## How do you stop it from happening again?

The overpayment fixes the past. The inheritance is still there, and the next check is at risk until the money is placed in a form that does not count. The options depend on where the money came from.

1. Report in writing now. Late reporting is the common cause of repeat overpayments. Reports are due by the 10th of the month after the change.
2. Move the money out of your name in a way the rules allow. Money that belongs to you can be spent on exempt items or placed in a payback-style trust, which is explained in our comparison of [first-party and third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust). Smaller amounts can go into an [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts), subject to the yearly contribution limit.
3. If the amount is small and you have no trust, spending down to under $2,000 on things you need, with receipts, is usually safer than leaving a large balance.
4. For future gifts from family, ask them to leave money to a trust instead of to you. Our guide to [pooled special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts) covers the option when no family member can serve as trustee.
5. Keep a folder with the notice, your appeal, your bank statements for the months at issue and every receipt.

Do not give the money to a relative to hold. Money moved out of your name for less than it is worth can create other problems with Social Security and with Medicaid. If you are considering refusing the inheritance, read why [disclaiming is usually a poor fix](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) before you do.

## What if the inheritance is already spent?

Spending is not the same as hiding. Money used for a home repair, a needed vehicle, a debt, medical equipment or a funeral plan is money turned into exempt things, which Social Security accepts. The overpayment for the months you were over the limit may still stand, but the resource problem ends once the balance is below $2,000.

The mistakes our attorneys see are a pile of small cash withdrawals with no record, and a large gift to a relative who "needed it more." Neither is easy to explain to a caseworker. A short written list of what was bought, with dates, is far more useful than a long explanation.

## What does this mean for Medicaid?

SSI and Medicaid are separate programs, but in many states they move together. If your SSI is cut off for the months you were over the resource limit, ask your state Medicaid office whether coverage was affected and whether you must file a new application. Our article on [how an inheritance affects Medicaid and SSI](/learn/after-a-death/does-an-inheritance-affect-medicaid-or-ssi) describes the Medicaid side.

## Who should handle the response?

You can file an appeal and a waiver yourself. The forms are short. If you have a guardian, an agent under a power of attorney, or a family member who is the representative payee, that person should sign, and Social Security must be told who is acting. Consider talking with an attorney when the amount is large, when the money is a settlement that should go into a trust, or when the notice says Social Security found fraud. A local legal aid office or a Social Security advocacy program can also help at no charge in many places.
