When Ana asked about a special needs trust for her son Mateo, 22, the first question was not about benefits. It was: "What is this going to cost, and is that a one-time thing?" It is a fair question, and the honest answer has two parts that people tend to merge.

One part is the legal work to create the trust. The other is the cost of keeping it alive for the rest of the beneficiary's life, which can be decades. This article explains both without quoting a single price, because prices vary by state and by firm and a number printed here would be wrong for someone. What it gives you instead is a way to compare quotes. For the basics of the trust itself, see our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## What do you pay for up front?

You pay for a lawyer's time to understand your family, choose the structure, draft the documents and help you fund them. The work differs a lot depending on which structure you pick.

| Structure | Relative up-front cost | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Trust clause in a parent's will (testamentary) | Usually the least, since it sits inside a will you may need anyway | Starts only at death, and goes through probate |
| Separate third-party trust signed now | More than a will clause; a stand-alone document | Relatives can leave money to it; works during your life |
| Special needs sub-trust inside a living trust | Part of the cost of a living trust | Avoids probate and pours the child's share into a separate trust |
| First-party (d)(4)(A) trust for the person's own money | Often the most drafting, because payback and state review matter | Needed after a settlement or inheritance in the beneficiary's name |
| Pooled trust enrollment | An enrollment fee instead of drafting | The nonprofit already has the trust; you give up some control |

If you are a parent, the common route is a plan that includes a will, powers of attorney, a health care directive and the trust together. That bundle usually costs less in total than building the pieces one at a time. Our article on [how to set up a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/how-to-set-up-a-special-needs-trust) walks through each step.

## What moves the price?

Five things move a quote more than anything else:

1. **Structure.** A will clause is the lightest, a stand-alone first-party trust the heaviest.
2. **Complexity of your assets.** A retirement account that names the trust as beneficiary needs extra language. A family business, rental property or a house the beneficiary will live in takes more work. See [can a special needs trust own a house](/learn/special-needs/can-a-special-needs-trust-own-a-house).
3. **Number of people.** Several children, a second marriage or a grandparent adding funds means more drafting and more conflict planning.
4. **State.** Some states require court approval or agency review for certain first-party trusts. Local rules and local rates for lawyers affect the fee.
5. **Funding help.** Retitling accounts, updating beneficiary forms and coordinating with insurance take time.

How the lawyer charges matters too. Many estate planning firms quote a flat fee for a planning package, which makes the cost predictable. Hourly billing is common where a court or state agency is involved. Ask which applies and what the quote includes.

## What does it cost to keep a trust running?

Once the trust exists, three kinds of cost recur. A family trustee who serves without pay keeps them modest. A professional trustee adds a fee.

**Trustee fees.** A family member may serve for free, which is common. A bank or trust company typically charges an annual percentage of the assets with a minimum, and an individual professional may charge by the hour. Our article on [who should be trustee of a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/who-should-be-trustee-of-a-special-needs-trust) compares the options. Fees must be reasonable, and the document should say how they are set.

**Tax and accounting.** A trust with income files its own income tax return, and trust income reaches the top federal rate at a low level, so a trustee often hires a CPA. A trust that qualifies as a qualified disability trust may claim a larger exemption. See [how a special needs trust is taxed](/learn/special-needs/how-a-special-needs-trust-is-taxed). Bank and investment fees apply as well.

**Recordkeeping and reporting.** The trustee keeps receipts for every distribution. For a first-party trust, many state Medicaid agencies ask for periodic accountings, and SSI may ask for a review. The cost is mostly time, but a trustee who prefers not to do it may hire a bookkeeper.

Mateo's trust might hold $180,000 in investments. Ana, as trustee, keeps the bills for his rent supplement, phone and dental work in a folder, pays a CPA once a year to prepare the return, and pays the custodian's investment fee. Her costs stay low because she does the bookkeeping herself. If Ana could no longer serve, her sister would step in, and the trust would pay the sister a stated hourly rate. Naming that rate ahead of time saves arguments later.

## What do pooled trusts charge?

A pooled trust charges in its own way, and fee schedules differ. Typical elements include a one-time enrollment fee, an annual administrative fee that may be a flat amount or a percentage of the account, and sometimes extra charges for processing requests or paying bills. Some programs keep part of the balance when the beneficiary dies, which is a real cost even if no check is written. Ask for the full schedule in writing. Our guide to [pooled special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts) lists the questions to ask.

Pooled trusts often cost less than a custom trust for a smaller sum, and more than a family trustee on a large one. The break-even point depends on the fee schedule you are quoted.

## What does a mistake cost?

The cheapest document is not always the cheapest outcome. Three expensive mistakes come up often:

- **A beneficiary named outright.** A will or beneficiary form that leaves money to a person on SSI, with no trust, can mean lost benefits and a spend-down. See [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child or sibling](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling).
- **A first-party trust without the payback clause.** The trust may not qualify, and the money counts as a resource.
- **A trust that pays cash.** Distributions of cash to the beneficiary can reduce SSI and may cause overpayments the person must repay. Our article on [SSI and Medicaid rules for special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) explains the reduction.

None of these is exotic. They happen when a form is used without reading it against the beneficiary's benefits. A fee that looks high at signing can be small next to even one year of lost Medicaid.

## What should you ask before you hire anyone?

Use this list at the first call:

1. Is the fee flat or hourly, and what exactly does it include?
2. Does the quote cover the will, powers of attorney and health care directive, or only the trust?
3. Is there an extra charge for helping fund the trust or update beneficiary forms?
4. Who will draft the document, and how often do you draft special needs trusts?
5. Based on what I told you, what will the trust cost to run each year?
6. If a court or state agency must approve the trust, who pays the filing costs?
7. Will you review the trust again when the law or the beneficiary's benefits change, and is there a charge?

## How can you keep costs sensible?

Plan the whole set of documents at once and choose the simplest structure that meets the need. Name a trustee who is willing and organized. Keep a folder from the first day. Review the document every few years. And do not use cost as the reason to put the money in the child's name. That choice is the one that tends to cost the most.
