Every parent of an adult with a disability eventually asks the same question: where will she live when I can no longer help? It rarely has one answer. Most adults with disabilities live with family for years, some move to a group home or an apartment with support, and a few need round-the-clock care. What decides the outcome is usually money, staffing and a place on the right waiting list.

This article explains how Medicaid waivers work, the main housing options, and where a special needs trust and an ABLE account fit. Specific programs, names and waiting times differ by state, so treat it as a map of the system and not a description of your state's rules. Our pillar on [special needs planning](/learn/special-needs) covers the surrounding legal documents.

## What is a Medicaid HCBS waiver?

An HCBS waiver (home and community-based services waiver) lets a state use Medicaid money for support at home or in the community, instead of in an institution. The word waiver refers to the federal rules the state is allowed to set aside, such as requiring every service to be offered statewide to everyone who is eligible.

Services vary by state and by waiver, and may include:

- Personal care and help with daily routines.
- Day programs and supported employment.
- Respite care, which gives family caregivers a break.
- Residential support in a group home or in the person's own apartment.
- Behavioral therapy, nursing visits and equipment.
- Transportation to services.

Two features catch families by surprise. First, waivers usually do not pay for rent, mortgage or food. They pay for the people who provide support. Second, enrollment is usually capped. The state decides how many slots it funds, and everyone else waits.

Some of the same programs serve older adults, and our article on the [Medicaid home care waiver](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver) covers that side. Adults with developmental disabilities generally use a separate waiver run by a state disability agency.

## Why are there waiting lists, and how should you handle them?

The waiting list exists because the money is limited. The wait can be short, long, or tied to a priority category in cases of emergency, such as when a parent dies or becomes unable to care.

What helps:

1. **Apply early.** Put the person on the list as soon as they are eligible, even if nothing is needed yet. In many states, the date of application counts.
2. **Keep the file current.** Respond to every mailing and update addresses. People lose places for failing to answer a letter.
3. **Ask how emergencies are handled.** Find out what counts as a crisis and what documentation is required.
4. **Learn the entry points.** Many states route services through a regional agency or a county board. A school transition coordinator can often explain it.
5. **Do not assume it will arrive.** Plan for the case where a slot comes in five years and the case where it never does.

Because the waiver generally depends on Medicaid eligibility, the person must also keep their assets under the state limit. That is one more reason to keep money in a trust, or in an ABLE account, instead of in their own name. See [what counts as income and resources for SSI](/learn/special-needs/what-counts-as-income-and-resources-for-ssi) for the basics of that test.

## What housing options exist?

The choices run from living with family to a supervised residence. The table gives the main ones, with who usually pays for what.

| Option | What it is | Who usually pays for support | Who usually pays for housing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family home | Adult lives with parents or a sibling | Family, plus waiver services if available | Family, with SSI effects if rent is charged |
| Supported living | Own apartment or shared home, with staff visiting | Waiver or state funds | The person, using SSI and any subsidy |
| Group home | Licensed home with several residents and staff | Waiver or state funds | The person's income plus any trust or family help |
| Host home or shared living | Lives with an unrelated family who is paid | Waiver or state funds | Often covered through the host arrangement |
| Independent living | Lives alone, may get few services | Self, plus waiver hours if any | The person, perhaps with a voucher |
| Nursing facility or ICF | Institutional care for high needs | Medicaid | Medicaid, with the person's income applied |

Group homes are often misunderstood. They are not a single kind of place. Some are run by nonprofits, some by for-profit companies, and some by the state. Quality varies. Visit more than once and at different times of day, talk to staff and families, and ask about turnover.

Supported living tends to appeal to families who want more independence, and it depends heavily on staffing. A person who cannot reliably get help at set times can end up in a bad situation.

## What about HUD and other housing assistance?

Federal and local housing programs are a separate system from Medicaid. Common ones include Section 8 housing choice vouchers, which pay part of the rent in a private apartment, public housing, and a HUD program for people with disabilities (Section 811) that funds supportive housing. Each has its own application and its own waiting list, usually run by a local housing agency or a nonprofit.

Some points matter for planning.

- **Waiting lists may be closed.** Local agencies open and close lists, so ask when they next open.
- **Rent is set by income.** A voucher recipient typically pays a share of income as rent, which is why SSI matters.
- **Assets can matter.** Housing programs have their own rules about assets, and a trust is generally reviewed.
- **Vouchers and Medicaid services work together.** An apartment from a voucher plus waiver support staff is a common arrangement.

## How does paying for housing affect SSI?

Rent and other shelter costs paid by someone else can reduce SSI, as explained in [what counts as income and resources for SSI](/learn/special-needs/what-counts-as-income-and-resources-for-ssi). A trustee who pays the landlord directly accepts that reduction. Many families do that on purpose, because the trust gives more value than the lost SSI.

An alternative is to let the person pay rent from their own funds, or to use an ABLE account. Our guide to [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) covers housing as a qualified expense. A trust that owns a home for the beneficiary is another option, with its own tax and maintenance questions. See [can a special needs trust own a house](/learn/special-needs/can-a-special-needs-trust-own-a-house). The article on [what a special needs trust can pay for](/learn/special-needs/what-can-special-needs-trust-funds-pay-for) covers the larger picture.

## Can a family member be paid as the caregiver?

In many states, yes. Some waivers offer a self-directed option, where the person or the family manages a budget and hires staff, which can include a relative. Rules differ on which relatives qualify, how many hours are allowed, and whether the caregiver needs training or an agency. It is worth asking. For adult children caring for aging parents the same idea appears in [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

## What happens to the money after the person dies?

Medicaid has the right to recover some of what it spent after the person dies. For people who received waiver services, states generally can pursue recovery from the estate once the person is 55 or older, among other categories, though states have discretion and some have narrowed it. Property held in a third-party trust is not part of the person's estate. Money in a first-party trust or an ABLE account may be subject to a payback claim. We explain the rule in [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) and the trust side in [the payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision).

## What should parents do now?

1. Confirm the person has applied for SSI, Medicaid and the state disability waiver or services list.
2. Ask the state agency for the current waiting list estimate in writing.
3. Visit residential options while you are well and can compare them.
4. Put housing preferences in a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) so a successor knows what you wanted.
5. Name a trustee who will work with service providers, and review the plan described in [who cares for a person with a disability after the parents die](/learn/special-needs/who-cares-for-a-person-with-a-disability-after-parents-die).
