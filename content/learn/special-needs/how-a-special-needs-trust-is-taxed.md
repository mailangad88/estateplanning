Families set up a special needs trust to protect benefits, and then a tax question appears that nobody mentioned. Does the trust need its own number? Who files? Does the money the trust pays out count as income to the person who gets it? And can you still claim your adult child as a dependent?

This page answers those questions at a level a family can follow, so you know what to ask your accountant. It is general information, not tax advice, and the rules for trust returns are technical enough that you should have a preparer who does them regularly.

## The short version

- A third-party trust is normally a separate taxpayer, with its own tax ID and a yearly return.
- A first-party trust is often set up so the beneficiary reports its income on their own return.
- Retained income is taxed at trust rates, which climb fast. Paying income out shifts it to the beneficiary, who is usually in a lower bracket.
- Income tax and SSI rules are different systems. Something can be taxable and still not count for SSI, and the reverse.
- Hire a preparer who knows trusts. A missed return is a cost with no benefit.

## Does a special needs trust need its own tax ID?

In most cases, yes. A third-party special needs trust is an irrevocable trust once the person who created it has died. At that point it becomes its own taxpayer and the trustee obtains an employer identification number (EIN) from the IRS. The EIN is used to open the trust's bank and brokerage accounts and to file its returns. Our page on the [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election) explains how an EIN is obtained for a trust after a death.

A trust that is revocable during the parents' lifetimes does not need a separate number until it becomes irrevocable. Many couples use the parents' Social Security numbers for the living trust, then obtain an EIN for the special needs sub-trust at the first death.

First-party trusts are different. A trust funded with the beneficiary's own money, like a settlement or inheritance, is commonly drafted as a grantor trust for tax purposes, so the beneficiary reports its income on their personal return. Sometimes the trust still has an EIN for banking. The trustee and preparer should confirm the reporting method before the first year ends.

## Who pays income tax on the trust's earnings?

Either the trust or the beneficiary, depending on whether the income is kept or paid out.

If the trustee keeps interest, dividends or gains in the trust, the trust pays tax on them. Trust tax brackets are compressed, which means a trust reaches the top federal rate at an income far lower than an individual does. A trust earning even a modest amount on a long-held portfolio can face a tax rate higher than the beneficiary would.

If the trustee pays out money, the trust takes a deduction and the beneficiary is taxed on the same amount, up to what the trust's taxable income was that year. The trust gives them a Schedule K-1 showing how much. This is why many trustees look at paying out income to cover real needs near year-end. The tax benefit is real but secondary, and a trustee should not make a payment that damages SSI or Medicaid just to save tax.

A related rule is the qualified disability trust. Some trusts for people with disabilities qualify for a larger exemption than ordinary trusts. Ask the preparer whether the trust qualifies and what the exemption is for the year. The amounts change and depend on current law.

| Money in the trust | Who is taxed | How it is reported |
|---|---|---|
| Interest and dividends the trust keeps | The trust | Form 1041, trust pays |
| Income paid out to or for the beneficiary | The beneficiary | Schedule K-1 to their return |
| Principal paid out (rent, equipment, travel) | Usually no one | Not income, but SSI rules still apply |
| Sale of a stock at a gain, kept in the trust | The trust | Capital gain on Form 1041 |
| First-party trust income | Often the beneficiary | Grantor trust reporting |

## Do distributions count as taxable income to the beneficiary?

Sometimes. A distribution is taxable to the beneficiary when it carries out the trust's income. A payment of principal generally is not. The tax rule is separate from SSI. Under SSI, cash given directly to the person counts as income; payments the trustee makes to a landlord or a store for the person are treated differently, and rent paid for the person can reduce the benefit as in-kind support. Our page on [how a special needs trust affects SSI and Medicaid](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) covers those rules. What counts as income for tax purposes does not decide what counts for benefits.

Most people on SSI have little other income and often owe no federal income tax. That does not eliminate the need for a return. A trust that has income must report it, even if the beneficiary's own tax is zero.

## Can I claim my adult disabled child as a dependent?

Perhaps. The test has nothing to do with the trust, and the trust does not decide it.

A child can be a qualifying child at any age if permanently and totally disabled, which waives the usual age limit. The child generally has to live with you for more than half the year and not provide more than half of their own support. A child who does not meet those tests may be a qualifying relative, which turns on how much the child earns and whether you provide more than half their support.

Support gets complicated when a trust pays for housing, food or care, and when Medicaid and SSI contribute. Whether those count as the child's own support or yours is something the IRS rules address in detail. The important point is that claiming a dependent can matter for filing status and credits, so ask a tax preparer, and keep a record of who paid for what.

Consider Alan and Maria, who care for their son Theo, 26, at home. Theo receives SSI and a modest SSI check goes toward his share of groceries. A third-party trust pays for his therapy and a day program. Alan and Maria provide the housing, most food, and daily care. They may be able to claim Theo; their preparer confirms it each year based on the actual facts.

## Are there gift or estate tax issues?

Leaving money to a third-party trust at death is not an income tax event, and the federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families owe no estate tax. Our article on the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) covers lifetime gifts. Gifts to a discretionary trust may not qualify for the annual exclusion unless the trust has special withdrawal rights, so a gift tax return may be required even where no tax is due.

Some states also have estate or inheritance taxes at lower thresholds. Check your state guide at [estate planning by state](/estate-planning).

## What the trustee should do each year

1. Confirm the trust's EIN and the accounts it is in.
2. Keep a ledger of income, distributions and expenses by date.
3. Give the preparer the brokerage and bank statements by the January deadline they set.
4. File Form 1041 and issue K-1s if income was paid out.
5. Check the payments against SSI and Medicaid rules, separate from the tax analysis.
6. Keep a copy of every return with the trust documents, since the successor trustee will need them.
