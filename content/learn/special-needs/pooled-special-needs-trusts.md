Suppose your brother Paul, who receives SSI and Medicaid, wins a modest injury settlement. Or your daughter, 34, gets an inheritance from a grandfather who never knew about trusts. The money is hers, it is not huge, and it will end her benefits if it sits in her bank account. A full custom trust with a lawyer and a separate trustee may cost more to run than the money can bear.

That is the situation a pooled special needs trust was built for. This article explains what it is, how it differs from the stand-alone first-party trust, what it costs in practice, and where families get surprised. For the wider picture, start with our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs).

## What is a pooled special needs trust?

A pooled special needs trust is one master trust run by a nonprofit organization, with a separate account for each beneficiary. The nonprofit is the trustee. It invests the accounts together to save on cost, but it tracks every beneficiary's balance separately and spends only on that person's behalf.

Federal Medicaid law recognizes this arrangement in its own subsection, which is where the label (d)(4)(C) comes from. The stand-alone first-party trust sits in the neighboring subsection, (d)(4)(A). Both let a person with a disability keep their own money without it counting toward the SSI and Medicaid resource limits. They are different tools with different rules. Our article on [first-party and third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) covers the source-of-funds question that decides which family you are in.

The beneficiary must meet the Social Security definition of disability. The trust is irrevocable. The beneficiary cannot take the money out on request, and that is what keeps it from being counted.

## How is a (d)(4)(C) trust different from a (d)(4)(A) trust?

The practical differences are about who runs it, who can join, and what happens at death.

| | Stand-alone (d)(4)(A) trust | Pooled (d)(4)(C) trust |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets it up | A parent, grandparent, guardian, court or the beneficiary | The beneficiary, a family member, guardian or court joins an existing program |
| Trustee | Usually a relative, a professional or a bank | The nonprofit |
| Drafting cost | A custom document, so legal fees up front | A joinder agreement; the master trust already exists |
| Typical minimum size | Makes sense for larger sums | Often workable for smaller sums |
| Age rules | Generally the beneficiary must be under 65 when funded | Federal law does not set the same limit, but state Medicaid rules on transfers after 65 differ |
| At death | State Medicaid claim comes first | Nonprofit may retain a share, and the rest may go to the state; terms vary |
| Control | More flexibility in the trust language | You accept the nonprofit's rules and approval process |

None of these differences makes one type better in every case. A person with a $400,000 settlement and a capable sibling willing to serve usually wants a custom trust. A person with $35,000 and no one to manage it often belongs in a pooled program.

## When does a pooled trust make sense?

A pooled trust fits best in four situations.

1. **The amount is modest.** A custom trust has fixed costs: drafting, a trust tax return each year, and a trustee who needs training. On a small balance those costs eat a visible slice of the principal.
2. **No one is available to be trustee.** Maybe the parents are gone, the siblings live across the country, or no one wants to track receipts for twenty years. A nonprofit does not retire.
3. **The beneficiary is over 65.** A stand-alone (d)(4)(A) trust is generally closed to people 65 and older. Pooled programs are often the only trust route left, though some states treat the deposit as a transfer for less than fair value and apply a Medicaid penalty. This varies sharply by state, so get local advice before you sign anything.
4. **You want a second safety net.** Some families use a pooled sub-account in addition to a family trust so there is a professional on the bench.

### What if the beneficiary is over 65?

Do not assume it will work, and do not assume it will fail. Some states say that a deposit into a pooled trust by someone over 65 is a transfer that triggers a penalty period for long-term care Medicaid. Other states allow it, provided fair value is received through spending for the person's benefit. The [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains how those penalties are counted. If you are close to this question, a short consultation before the deposit is worth the cost.

## What happens to the money at death?

This is the part families skim and later wish they had read. When the beneficiary dies, one of three things generally happens to what is left in a first-party pooled account:

- The nonprofit keeps it, to fund services for its other beneficiaries.
- The state is repaid for Medicaid it paid on the beneficiary's behalf, and the nonprofit or family receives whatever remains.
- A split of the two.

Federal rules allow the nonprofit to retain a portion, but the exact formula comes from the trust document and, in some places, state policy. Some programs are very open about this. Others bury it. Ask the question in plain words: "If my brother dies with $20,000 in his account, who gets it?" Then ask for the answer in writing. Our companion piece on the [payback provision](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) covers how Medicaid repayment is measured and what a trust can pay before the state is repaid.

Third-party money is different. If the sub-account is funded by a parent or grandparent, many programs treat it as a third-party account with no state claim, though the nonprofit may still keep some of it. If you want the remainder to go to a niece or a charity instead, a family-drafted third-party trust is usually the cleaner route, as described in [special needs trusts and how they protect benefits](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).

## What does it cost and what should you ask?

Fees vary widely and change. Nonprofits typically charge some combination of an enrollment fee, an annual administrative fee that may be a percentage of the account or a flat amount, and charges for special services such as handling a real estate purchase. Do not rely on a brochure from three years ago. Ask for the current schedule.

Before you enroll, use this checklist.

1. Is the trust operated by a nonprofit, and how long has it operated?
2. What are the enrollment, annual and per-transaction fees?
3. What is the minimum deposit?
4. What happens to the balance at the beneficiary's death, in writing?
5. Does the program accept both first-party and third-party money in separate accounts?
6. How quickly are requests for payment reviewed?
7. Can the trust pay for housing costs, a vehicle or a home purchase, and what does it do about the SSI effect?
8. Who reviews or audits the trust, and can you see recent financial statements?
9. What is the plan if the nonprofit closes?
10. Does your state's Medicaid agency accept this trust? Local practice matters.

### Common mistakes

The most frequent error is enrolling the wrong money. A grandmother's gift deposited into a first-party account can be treated as the beneficiary's own and subject to payback. The second is signing the joinder paperwork without reading the death provision. The third is assuming a pooled trust handles everything. It pays bills; it does not manage the beneficiary's care, choose a residence or serve as a guardian. A [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) still matters, because the nonprofit staff will know the beneficiary only through paperwork.

## Example: Dolores and her son Marcus

Dolores, 71, learns that her son Marcus, 38, who has an intellectual disability and receives SSI, has been named in his late uncle's will for about $48,000. Dolores is a widow, her daughter lives overseas, and Marcus is not able to manage investments. A stand-alone trust would cost thousands to draft, and Dolores worries about who would serve after her.

Their attorney explains that the uncle's estate cannot simply send the funds to a trust Dolores creates, because the money is Marcus's own once distributed to him. Instead the personal representative can distribute to a pooled trust sub-account for Marcus, if the will does not say otherwise, or a court can authorize it. Dolores picks a nonprofit that publishes its fee schedule, accepts Marcus's money in a first-party account, and states what happens at his death. Dolores also asks that the uncle's other relatives route future gifts to a separate third-party trust. She keeps the paperwork in a folder with Marcus's benefit letters, so her daughter can find it.

## How do you decide?

Start with whose money it is, then how much, then who is available to serve. If the money is a settlement, back benefits or an inheritance that already belongs to the beneficiary, you are choosing between a stand-alone first-party trust and a pooled one. If the money is the family's to give, a third-party trust usually wins. The [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) article shows how to route life insurance and retirement accounts so family money never lands in the first-party bucket by mistake.
