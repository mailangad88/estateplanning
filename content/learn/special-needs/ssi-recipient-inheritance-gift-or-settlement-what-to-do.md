Aunt Rosalind died in March. Her will leaves her niece, Dev, a share of her savings. Dev is 34, has an intellectual disability, and has received SSI for most of his adult life. His mother opens the lawyer's letter and her stomach drops. The money is meant to help, and it could cost him his monthly check and his health coverage.

This page is for the family that is in that spot right now, and for the relatives who want to prevent it. It covers what Social Security counts, when to report, which options keep the benefits, and which common moves cause trouble.

## The short version

- Money Dev receives in his own name is income in the month he gets it. Money he keeps is a resource afterward.
- SSI has a resource limit, and exceeding it for even a month can stop benefits. The limit is set by Social Security and changes, so check the current figure with the agency.
- Report quickly. An unreported windfall that is discovered later becomes an overpayment.
- Protect the money before it is kept in his name: a trust, an ABLE account, or spending on exempt needs.
- Do not refuse the inheritance without advice. It can look like giving away assets.

## Does an inheritance count against SSI?

Yes. SSI treats an inheritance as unearned income in the month it is received, and the benefit for that month can be reduced or eliminated. If any is left on the first day of the next month, it counts as a resource. A person whose countable resources are over the limit is not eligible for that month. Because most states tie Medicaid to SSI, the loss can spread to health coverage and to waiver services that a person may have waited years to get. Our page on [how a special needs trust affects SSI and Medicaid](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) explains the mechanics in more detail.

The same logic applies to cash gifts, life insurance proceeds, a share of a house sale, or a settlement. It is the receipt that matters, not the label.

An SSDI recipient who does not also receive SSI has a different position, as the first FAQ above explains, because SSDI is not means-tested.

## How fast must it be reported?

Quickly. Social Security expects changes in income and resources to be reported, generally by the tenth day of the month after the change. Reports can be made by phone, in person, online for some changes, or in writing. Whatever method you use, keep a copy and note the date and person you spoke with.

Reporting is not the same as losing the money. Social Security will tell you what they count. The goal is to be on record while you arrange a protected home for the funds. A late report, on the other hand, gives the agency a reason to ask why it was not reported, and the resulting overpayment can have to be repaid out of future checks.

## What are the ways to protect an inheritance?

In rough order of how often they are used:

1. **A first-party special needs trust.** A person under 65 with a disability can have money placed in a trust for their sole benefit without losing eligibility. The trade-off is a Medicaid payback at death. Our page on [first-party vs. third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) explains the difference.
2. **A pooled trust.** A nonprofit manages a master trust with a separate account for each person. It is often practical for a smaller amount, or when no family member can serve as trustee. See [pooled special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/pooled-special-needs-trusts).
3. **An ABLE account.** Funds can be deposited up to the annual limit and used for qualified disability expenses. It is a good tool for part of a windfall, not a place for a large inheritance. Read [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts).
4. **Paying for exempt or needed items.** Spending on things SSI does not count, such as a primary home, a vehicle used for transportation, medical equipment, or paying off debts, can be legitimate. Paying for a purchase that has no value to the person is not.
5. **Paying a legal fee or funeral cost** that the person is responsible for.

Dev's mother, working with an attorney, moves his inheritance into a first-party trust before the money is ever deposited into his account. His SSI continues. The trustee pays his rent, a phone plan, a gym membership, and an annual trip to see his brother. At Dev's death, the state may be repaid for his Medicaid costs from what is left, which is the cost of the arrangement.

## Why disclaiming is usually the wrong fix

Disclaimer is the legal word for refusing an inheritance. Families sometimes try it because it seems clean. For an SSI recipient, Social Security can treat a disclaimer as an improper transfer of a resource and apply a penalty period, during which the person is ineligible for some benefits. The penalty look-back for SSI is 36 months, and Medicaid has its own transfer rules. Our article on [disclaiming an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) explains the technical rules, and [whether an inheritance affects Medicaid or SSI](/learn/after-a-death/does-an-inheritance-affect-medicaid-or-ssi) has more on the interaction.

## What about gifts from relatives during life?

Smaller gifts cause small problems. Cash is income in the month it is given. A one-time birthday check in the wrong month can reduce a payment. Gifts of items, like a laptop or a bicycle, generally do not. Help with housing costs can reduce the monthly benefit as in-kind support. Food no longer counts, as of late 2024.

The safest habit is to give through the trust. A relative who wants to send a gift can pay a bill or give to the trustee directly, rather than to the beneficiary. Our article on [what a special needs trust can pay for](/learn/special-needs/what-can-special-needs-trust-funds-pay-for) has practical examples.

## How to prevent this problem before it happens

If you are the one leaving money, the cleanest fix is to leave nothing to the person directly. Write your will or trust so their share goes into a third-party special needs trust. A third-party trust, funded with your money, has no Medicaid payback, so what remains can pass to siblings or other family. Tell other relatives as well, because the usual cause of this problem is a well-meaning grandparent whose will leaves an outright share.

## Steps if the money is coming now

1. **Do not deposit it in the person's account.** If a check arrives, keep it undeposited and ask the executor or the court how it can be redirected.
2. **Tell Social Security,** and write down the date.
3. **Call an attorney who handles special needs planning.** Many first-party trusts need court approval or must meet strict wording rules.
4. **Gather the documents:** will or letter, the estate's notice, benefit letters, bank statements.
5. **Pick a trustee** and a plan for the money. Our page on [how to set up a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/how-to-set-up-a-special-needs-trust) walks through the process.
