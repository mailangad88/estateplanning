Maybe you have three children and the youngest, Ben, has an intellectual disability and lives with a support worker. Or you are single, your older brother Walter has relied on SSI his whole life, and you are the one person who looks after him. In both cases the question is the same: how do I leave something, and not hurt the benefits that keep the lights on?

This article covers outright gifts, the trust that replaces them, how to fund it, and the family questions that come up. It sits within our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs), which covers the other pieces.

## What happens if you leave money directly to a disabled person?

The money becomes theirs, and means-tested programs treat it as theirs. SSI and most Medicaid programs limit the assets a recipient can hold. An outright inheritance can push the person over the line, and benefits can stop until the money is spent down. In many states the person also loses the Medicaid attached to SSI, which pays for services that no family can easily replace.

Spending down is not simple either. Benefits agencies have rules on what counts as a legitimate purchase. Giving the money away, or paying a relative back an old favor, can bring a penalty. Families who see this happen after a parent's death often describe it as the second loss.

An outright gift has other problems that have nothing to do with benefits. The person may be vulnerable to financial exploitation. They may not be able to manage a lump sum. They may need a guardian to manage the money, and a guardianship case is expensive and public. A trust avoids all three.

## What is the better way to leave the money?

Leave it to a third-party special needs trust. This is a trust you or another relative fund with your own assets, for the person with a disability. A trustee has discretion over distributions, the beneficiary cannot demand money, and the assets are generally not counted for benefits. Because the money was never theirs, there is no Medicaid payback at death, and you decide who receives anything left. Our [special needs trust article](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) explains how the structure protects eligibility, and the [payback article](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-payback-provision) shows what you avoid by keeping it third-party.

You can fund the trust through several routes.

| Source | How to route it to the trust |
|---|---|
| House, savings, investments | A will, or a living trust that names the special needs trust as recipient |
| Life insurance | Name the trust as beneficiary on the policy form |
| Retirement accounts | Name the trust, with language that works for retirement rules; ask an attorney before filing the form |
| Bank and brokerage accounts | Payable-on-death designation to the trust |
| Gifts during life | Transfers to the trust, subject to the gift tax annual exclusion reporting rules |

The forms matter more than the will. A beneficiary designation usually overrides the will, so a will that names the trust is undone by a policy that names the person. Check every form, and see our guides on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

## Can you do this for an adult sibling?

Yes, and it is more common than people expect. If your brother or sister relies on SSI and you have no children, or your children are not close, you can create a third-party trust in your own estate plan. You are not legally responsible for a sibling, but many people choose to be. The trust lets you decide how much to leave, who will manage it, and what happens to the rest.

Three things deserve extra attention when the beneficiary is a sibling.

1. **Who is the trustee?** A sibling's other relatives, such as nieces and nephews, may not know him well. Consider a professional or nonprofit trustee, or a family member paired with one. Our guide to [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) lays out the criteria.
2. **Who gets what is left?** Without a plan, the remainder follows the beneficiary's own estate, which means state default rules. Name remainder beneficiaries in the trust.
3. **What if your sibling dies first?** Name a backup so the money does not default to a place you did not intend.

The same logic holds for a niece, a grandchild, a close friend. If you are single and want to leave something to someone who relies on benefits, the structure is identical. Our article on [estate planning for single people](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people) covers the surrounding decisions.

## Is an unequal split fair?

It can be, if it is explained. Parents of a child with a disability commonly leave that child a larger share because the other children have jobs and families, and this child will need support for decades. Others leave equal shares because an unequal split feels like favoritism. There is no rule. What matters is that the plan is written, and that the siblings hear the reasoning from you while you are alive.

Another common choice is to give the other children a role: successor trustee, an advisory seat, or a say in selecting a care manager. That keeps their support deliberate rather than assumed. If you are leaving unequal shares, read [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) for the drafting points that reduce the risk of a dispute.

Be honest with yourself about whether a sibling can serve. Asking a daughter who lives three hours away and has two toddlers to manage her brother's care for forty years is a big ask. A trust that lets a professional handle the money, while a sibling stays involved as an advocate, often works better.

## What should you do, step by step?

1. List the assets you expect to leave and what comes from each source.
2. Write down the benefits the person receives now and what they might need later.
3. Choose a trustee, a successor and a way for a family member to oversee them.
4. Decide the remainder beneficiaries and a backup.
5. Create the third-party trust, in your will or a living trust.
6. Change beneficiary forms on insurance, retirement accounts and bank accounts to name the trust.
7. Tell relatives the trust's exact name, so their wills route gifts to it.
8. Write a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent) describing the person's routines and preferences.
9. Review every few years, and after any change in benefits, health or family.

### Example: Marta and her brother Walter

Marta, 61, is single and has no children. Her brother Walter, 58, has lived on SSI and a Medicaid waiver since a childhood illness. Marta owns a condo and a $300,000 retirement account, and she wants Walter housed and cared for after she is gone. Her first draft is a will leaving everything to Walter. Her attorney explains that this could end his benefits, and that Walter might be unable to manage a condo sale alone.

They set up a third-party special needs trust in Marta's living trust. Marta's niece Kaya is successor trustee alongside a corporate trustee. The retirement account names the trust as beneficiary with language reviewed for that purpose. The trust says that on Walter's death, the remainder goes to Kaya and her brother. Marta also writes a four-page letter about Walter's favorite foods, his doctor, and the program staff who know him. She tells Kaya where it is.
