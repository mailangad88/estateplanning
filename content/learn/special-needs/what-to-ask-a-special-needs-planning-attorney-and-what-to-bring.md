Priya and Dev have a 19-year-old daughter, Anika, who has an intellectual disability and has just started receiving SSI. Priya has phone numbers for three law firms and a printed template she found online. She has been staring at a blank page labeled "questions for the lawyer" for a week.

This page is for the parent in that position. It explains what a special needs planning attorney does, how that work differs from an online form or a financial planner, what to gather, and what to ask. If you would like a map of the whole subject first, our [special needs planning pillar](/learn/special-needs) is the place to start.

## What does a special needs planning attorney do?

A special needs planning attorney is an estate planning attorney whose practice regularly covers trusts, guardianship alternatives and public benefits for people with disabilities. The phrase is not a title the state grants, so ask how much of the attorney's work is in this area rather than relying on the label.

In a first engagement the attorney usually does five things:

1. Reviews the person's benefits, such as SSI, Medicaid, a waiver, and anything from Social Security Disability Insurance.
2. Chooses the trust type, which might be a third-party trust funded by parents, a first-party trust for the person's own money or a pooled trust. The [first-party vs third-party article](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust) explains the split.
3. Drafts the trust, the parents' wills, powers of attorney and healthcare directives.
4. Works through who decides things for the adult child, from supported decision-making to guardianship. See [alternatives to guardianship for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children).
5. Fixes the pieces that undo the plan, such as a life insurance form that names the child directly.

## How is this different from an online trust or a financial planner?

An online trust is a document. A special needs plan is a document that fits a person's benefits, state and family. A financial planner, who may call themselves a special needs financial planner, builds a budget, a savings and investment strategy and projects how long money will last. The planner does not draft the legal papers.

| | Online form | Special needs attorney | Special needs financial planner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drafts the trust | Template | Custom to the person and the state | No |
| Checks SSI and Medicaid rules | No | Yes | Understands the effect on savings |
| Reviews beneficiary forms and wills | No | Yes | Sometimes flags them |
| Projects lifetime costs | No | General only | Yes |
| Usual cost | Low | Fee for the plan, often a flat amount | Hourly or a percentage of assets |

The problem with a template is not that the language is wrong. It is that the template cannot ask you the questions. Does Anika live at home or in a supported setting? Does she have a payee? Will grandparents leave her money? Will she ever marry or work? Each answer changes how the trust should be written. A trust that is too restrictive hurts her quality of life. One that is too loose can cost benefits.

## What should you bring to the first meeting?

Bring paper, because the attorney will otherwise have to guess. This is the checklist we give families.

- Benefit award letters for SSI, Medicaid, SSDI and any state waiver or services plan.
- A recent bank statement for every account in the person's name.
- A list of what you own, with the way each is titled: house, retirement accounts, life insurance, and who is named as beneficiary.
- Your current wills, trusts and powers of attorney, if any.
- Diagnosis reports, school or evaluation records and a list of providers.
- Names of people who could serve as trustee, guardian or agent.
- A rough description of the life you want for your child: where they will live, how they spend the day, who matters to them.
- Your own questions, written down.

Add a short note about anything that worries you. Parents who skip this step often forget the one question that mattered.

## What questions should you ask?

These questions get past the sales conversation to what you will actually receive. Use them in the interview, and write the answers down.

1. **How often do you draft special needs trusts?** Look for regular experience, not an occasional case.
2. **Which type of trust do you recommend for us, and why?** The reasoning matters more than the label.
3. **How will you protect SSI and Medicaid?** A good answer names the distribution rules and the trustee's limits.
4. **Who do you suggest as trustee, and what is the backup?** See [who should be trustee](/learn/special-needs/who-should-be-trustee-of-a-special-needs-trust).
5. **What happens to the money when my child dies?** The answer should mention a payback clause where it applies.
6. **What else will you review besides the trust?** Beneficiary forms, wills and guardianship planning should come up.
7. **What does it cost, what is included and what happens when the law changes?** Ask for the quote in writing. Our article on [special needs trust costs](/learn/special-needs/how-much-a-special-needs-trust-costs-to-set-up-and-keep-running) gives a range of what to expect.
8. **Will you help with the letter of intent and the funding steps?** A trust with no money in it is only paper.
9. **How do you suggest we review the plan?** A yearly check works for many families.

## How do you find the right attorney?

Search your state bar's lawyer-finder service, ask your child's case manager, call your state developmental disabilities agency, and ask other parents. In Illinois, families often register with the Illinois Department of Human Services for the state's waiver waitlist, and in New Jersey with the Division of Developmental Disabilities. Those offices do not recommend lawyers, but their staff and the parent groups around them know who is familiar with the local process. Meet at least two attorneys, notice whether they explain things in plain words, and choose the one you trust to still be there in 20 years.

If you want a picture of the next steps before you call, read [how to set up a special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/how-to-set-up-a-special-needs-trust). Parents of a teenager should also read what happens when a child with a disability [turns 18](/learn/special-needs/what-to-do-when-a-child-with-a-disability-turns-18).

## What should the attorney explain about your child's own decisions?

A good first meeting also covers who will make decisions for Anika. At 18, parents lose automatic authority in most states, even when the young adult has a significant disability. The attorney should lay out the choices: a healthcare proxy and financial power of attorney if she has capacity to sign them, supported decision-making, a representative payee for Social Security, and guardianship only if nothing lighter works. Guardianship is a court process and is public, so ask what it would cost and how long it would take. Families in Illinois and New Jersey can read the state pages on [guardianship alternatives for adult children](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) before they go, and ask the attorney which local forms the courts expect.

Ask also about where the money goes while you are alive. Some parents put small amounts into an [ABLE account](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) for everyday costs and reserve the trust for larger needs. The attorney should explain how those fit together.

## What should you do after the first meeting?

Write down what you were told, compare the proposals and ask for a draft. If you feel rushed, slow down. A good plan is rarely urgent in a way that requires signing on the first visit, apart from a situation such as a pending settlement or a parent in poor health.
