Families who plan for a loved one with a disability hear about ABLE accounts early, and often hear conflicting advice about them. Some people treat them as a miracle, and others dismiss them because of the Medicaid payback. Both reactions miss what the account is actually for.

This guide explains who is eligible, with an important change taking effect in 2026, how much can go in, what the money can be used for, how ABLE interacts with SSI and Medicaid, and when to pair an ABLE account with a special needs trust.

## What is an ABLE account?

An ABLE account (named for the Achieving a Better Life Experience Act of 2014) is a savings and spending account for a person with a disability, set up under Section 529A of the tax code. States offer the plans, often through a state treasurer's office or an outside provider, and you can usually open one in any state that accepts out-of-state residents.

The account works like a bank account with an investment option. Earnings grow tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified disability expenses are tax-free. Most plans offer a debit card, online bill pay, and several investment options. The key benefit is that the money, up to a limit, does not count against the SSI resource limit of $2,000 for an individual.

## Who can open an ABLE account?

You are eligible if your disability began before a specific age and you meet disability criteria.

**The age of onset.** Until the end of 2025, the disability had to begin before the person turned 26. Under the ABLE Age Adjustment Act, enacted in 2022, the cutoff rises to before the person turns 46, starting January 1, 2026. Millions of adults whose disabilities began between 26 and 45, including many veterans and people with conditions like multiple sclerosis or acquired brain injury, are newly eligible. The age limit applies to when the disability began, not to the person's age when opening the account.

**Disability criteria.** A person qualifies automatically if they receive SSI or SSDI based on blindness or disability that began before the age limit. If they do not receive those benefits, they can still open an account by certifying they have a physical or mental impairment that meets Social Security's definition of disability and that it began before the cutoff, with a physician's diagnosis available on request.

Each person may have only one ABLE account, with some narrow exceptions, such as rollovers. The account owner is the person with the disability. A parent, guardian or someone with power of attorney can act as authorized representative if the person cannot manage it themselves.

## How much can go into the account?

Annual contributions from all sources are generally capped at the federal gift tax annual exclusion, $19,000 in 2026. That includes contributions from the person, parents, grandparents, friends and a trust. If a grandfather gives $10,000, only $9,000 more can go in that year.

Other limits:

- **Account cap.** Each state plan sets a maximum total balance, often in the range of $300,000 to $500,000, tied to the state's 529 college savings limits.
- **ABLE to Work.** A beneficiary who earns wages and does not participate in a workplace retirement plan can contribute additional amounts above the annual limit, up to the lesser of their earnings or the federal poverty level for a one-person household in the prior year. The rules have conditions, so check with the plan.
- **529 rollovers.** A family can roll money from a 529 college savings plan into the beneficiary's ABLE account, within the annual limit, which is helpful when a child's education plans change.

For the tax side of gifts, see [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).

## How does an ABLE account affect SSI and Medicaid?

The first $100,000 is disregarded for SSI. Medicaid generally disregards the whole account.

| Balance | SSI | Medicaid |
|---|---|---|
| Up to $100,000 | Not counted as a resource | Generally not counted |
| Over $100,000 | SSI cash payments suspended while above the threshold, resumes once the balance falls below | Generally continues |
| Used for housing in the month | May reduce that month's SSI | Not affected |

Because ABLE accounts are exempt, withdrawals for qualified expenses do not count as income. If a withdrawal is not a qualified disability expense, it can be taxed and penalized, and if it is spent on housing outside the rules it may reduce SSI.

## What counts as a qualified disability expense?

The definition is broad. Qualified expenses relate to health, independence and quality of life:

- Housing, including rent, mortgage payments, utilities and modifications
- Education and training
- Transportation, including a vehicle or public transit
- Employment support
- Health, prevention and wellness
- Assistive technology and personal support services
- Financial management and legal fees
- Funeral and burial costs
- Basic living expenses, such as food

Keep records. A plan can ask for receipts, and the tax rules put the burden of proof on the account owner. Housing expenses are qualified for tax purposes but are special for SSI: they can reduce the SSI payment, so many beneficiaries use other income for rent when possible, and use the ABLE account for other costs.

## What happens to the account at death?

After the owner dies, the plan may pay outstanding qualified expenses, such as funeral costs. A state can then file a claim for Medicaid benefits paid after the account was opened, subject to rules, and the remainder goes to the owner's estate or named beneficiary. This payback applies to ABLE accounts but not to a third-party special needs trust.

This is one reason an ABLE account is usually a complement rather than a replacement. Large inheritances, settlements, and long-term support usually belong in a trust, while the ABLE account is for day-to-day use.

## How do ABLE accounts and special needs trusts work together?

They solve different problems.

| | ABLE account | Special needs trust |
|---|---|---|
| Who manages | The beneficiary, or an authorized representative | A trustee |
| Annual contribution limit | $19,000 (2026) | None |
| Total size | State cap | Unlimited |
| Payback at death | Possible Medicaid claim | None for third-party trust; required for first-party |
| Flexibility for the beneficiary | High, debit card and online access | Depends on the trustee |
| Cost | Low | Higher |

A practical arrangement is for the trustee to transfer money to the ABLE account each year so the beneficiary can pay for everyday items and gain independence, while the trust holds the bulk for larger needs. A trustee who pays for housing from the trust will cause an SSI reduction; paying through the ABLE account within allowed rules may offer more flexibility. For details on the trust, see [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) and the comparison in [first-party vs. third-party trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust).

A trust that is a first-party trust can contribute to an ABLE account, although payback issues need coordination, so ask your attorney.

### Example: the Reyes family

Carlos, 24, has an intellectual disability and receives SSI. His parents open an ABLE account in his name and his grandparents contribute $5,000 each birthday. Carlos uses the debit card for transit passes, a gym membership with an aide and groceries. His parents' third-party trust holds most of the family assets, and the trustee moves $12,000 a year into the ABLE account. Carlos's balance stays well below $100,000.

## What should you do first?

1. Check eligibility, especially if the disability began after 25, because many people became eligible this year.
2. Compare state plans for fees, investment choices and the cap. You can generally use any state's plan.
3. Open the account and add a checklist: owner, authorized representative, and contributors.
4. Tell relatives how to contribute, and agree on a yearly amount.
5. Keep receipts for large withdrawals and notify the Social Security Administration of changes as required.
6. Review how the account fits with the rest of your plan, including a [letter of intent](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent).
