Parents of a child with a disability tend to learn the SSI rules the hard way. A grandmother wants to give a birthday check. An uncle offers to cover a month of rent. A used car is left to a grown son. Each of these is a kind gesture, and each can interrupt benefits the family depends on.

This article explains how Social Security sorts money into income and resources, what is exempt, and where families most often go wrong. It does not cover every exception, and the dollar limits change, so we point you to the places to check them instead of quoting them here. The [special needs planning pillar page](/learn/special-needs) lists the other pieces of the picture.

## How do SSI, SSDI and Medicaid differ?

Three programs get mixed up constantly, and the rules in this article apply to only one of them.

| Program | Who pays | Based on | Resource limit? | Health coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SSI (Supplemental Security Income) | Federal, sometimes with a state supplement | Need, with a disability or age 65 or older | Yes, a low federal limit | Usually Medicaid |
| SSDI (Social Security Disability Insurance) | Federal | The person's own work record | No | Medicare after a waiting period |
| Medicaid | Federal and state | Need, with state rules | Yes, set by each state and program | Itself |

SSDI is earned through payroll taxes, so savings do not reduce it. A related benefit, sometimes called disabled adult child benefits, is paid on a parent's work record when a parent retires, becomes disabled or dies and the person's disability began before 22. It is not means tested, and it can change a family's whole picture. Anyone who loses SSI that way can often keep Medicaid under special protections, but the paperwork needs care.

The rest of this article is about SSI, since that is where the strict asset and income tests live.

## What counts as a resource for SSI?

A resource is cash or anything the person owns that could be sold or converted to cash. Social Security counts what the person owns as of the first moment of the month. If the total is over the limit on that date, there is no payment for that month.

Counted resources usually include:

- Checking and savings accounts, including joint accounts, unless the person can show the money belongs to someone else.
- Stocks, bonds, mutual funds and cryptocurrency.
- Retirement accounts the person can withdraw from.
- Cash value of life insurance above a small face-value threshold.
- A second vehicle, boat or recreational property.
- Land or a house that is not the person's home.
- Money owed to the person that can be collected.

## What is exempt?

Several things are not counted. The ones that matter most to families are these.

- **The home** the person lives in, and the land around it.
- **One vehicle** used for transportation of the person or someone in the household, whatever its value.
- **Household goods and personal effects,** like furniture, clothing and a wedding ring.
- **Burial spaces and a funeral fund,** including irrevocable burial contracts, within limits.
- **Property essential to self-support,** such as tools or a small business used in work, within limits.
- **Money in an ABLE account,** up to the cap, covered in our [guide to ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts).
- **Assets in a correctly written special needs trust,** because the person cannot demand them. See [special needs trusts and SSI rules](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules).

A rule of thumb: you cannot make a countable asset exempt just by moving it. A sibling who adds a young adult to a savings account has not made the money safe. It is a countable resource in the young adult's name and can end benefits.

## What counts as income for SSI?

Income is anything received in cash or in kind that can be used for food or shelter. Social Security divides it into earned and unearned.

**Earned income** is wages and net earnings from self-employment. Some of it is ignored, and the rules are designed so that working improves total income. A person can try a job without losing SSI immediately.

**Unearned income** is everything else: Social Security or pension payments, interest, dividends, rental income, an inheritance or gift received as cash, and workers' compensation. Most unearned income reduces the SSI check dollar for dollar, after a small general exclusion.

Not everything that looks like income is. Reimbursement for a bill you already paid, a tax refund, and the proceeds of selling a countable resource are generally treated differently. An inheritance is income in the month it arrives and then a resource afterward if it is kept, which is why it can cause trouble twice.

## What is in-kind support and maintenance?

In-kind support and maintenance (ISM) is food or shelter that someone else provides. The most common version is a parent paying the rent or the electric bill directly for an adult child who gets SSI.

For shelter, which is rent, mortgage, property tax and utilities, Social Security reduces the SSI payment when somebody else pays. The reduction is capped, so the SSI check does not disappear, but it falls. For food, Social Security removed it from this calculation in 2024, which was a real change for families who had avoided buying groceries for an adult child. Because the agency has changed this rule before, confirm the current treatment on ssa.gov before relying on any article, including this one.

There are two practical ways to handle housing. The person can pay a fair share of the household expenses from their own money, which can eliminate the reduction when the arrangement is properly set up. Or a trustee can pay housing costs, accepting the SSI reduction as a known cost, which is sometimes the better choice for a person who gets generous support. Our article on [what a special needs trust can pay for](/learn/special-needs/what-can-special-needs-trust-funds-pay-for) goes through both.

## What is the deeming rule?

Deeming means that someone else's income and resources are counted as the applicant's. The rule that matters most to parents is for children. While a child is under 18 and lives at home, part of the parents' income and resources is deemed to the child, after allowances for the parents and other children. A family with a middle-class income may find a young child ineligible for that reason alone.

Deeming stops at 18. A young adult who is applying for the first time is judged on their own money, even though they still live in the family home. That is why many families apply the month after the 18th birthday. Be aware that a spouse's income and resources are also deemed to an applicant, so marriage can change eligibility. See [capacity and marriage for adults with disabilities](/learn/special-needs/capacity-and-marriage-for-adults-with-disabilities).

## A worked example

Teresa has a 24-year-old son, Daniel, who receives SSI and lives with her. Her sister dies and leaves Daniel a savings account in her will. The money arrives in Daniel's name.

1. The inheritance is income in the month it arrives and reduces that month's check.
2. The balance counts as a resource the next month, which will probably end his SSI and his Medicaid.
3. Teresa contacts an attorney quickly. Depending on the state and the timing, the money may be able to go into a first-party trust, and it will need to be reported to Social Security either way.

A will drafted earlier, leaving the money to a third-party trust instead, would have avoided every step. That is the reason [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child or sibling](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling) belongs in every family member's plan, not just the parents'. If an unplanned inheritance has already happened, see [what to do with an SSI recipient's inheritance, gift or settlement](/learn/special-needs/ssi-recipient-inheritance-gift-or-settlement-what-to-do) and [how an inheritance affects Medicaid or SSI](/learn/after-a-death/does-an-inheritance-affect-medicaid-or-ssi).

## What mistakes should families avoid?

- Putting an SSI recipient's name on a bank account, deed or title that someone else meant to be a gift.
- Paying rent directly without understanding the effect, then getting an overpayment notice months later.
- Waiting to report a change. Report income, living arrangement and resource changes by the deadline Social Security gives, because late reports lead to overpayments that must be repaid.
- Assuming a family member's good intentions are enough. The agency looks at titles and bank records, not intentions.
