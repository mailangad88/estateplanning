If you are the parent of a person with a disability, you know things no document captures: which sock seams she cannot tolerate, what he does when the bus is late, who makes him laugh, and what a certain sound means. When someone else takes over, they will not have those details unless you write them down.

A letter of intent is how you do that. It is not a legal instrument. It is the human instruction manual that gives meaning to trusts, guardianships and care plans.

## What is a letter of intent, and why does it matter?

A letter of intent is a written description of your loved one's life, needs, preferences and your hopes for the future, addressed to the people who will care for them. Trustees, guardians, case managers and service providers use it to make decisions the way you would.

It matters because legal documents are written in general terms. A [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) says the trustee may pay for the beneficiary's "health, education and quality of life." The letter explains what that looks like for your son: swimming lessons, a weekly visit with his grandmother, a particular brand of headphones, a day program that has been a good fit.

Courts also pay attention. In a guardianship proceeding or a dispute about care, a thoughtful letter shows what a parent considered important. It is evidence of your intent, though not a binding command.

## What should the letter include?

Organize it in sections with headings, in order of how urgently someone might need the information. Start with a one-page summary.

### 1. Emergency summary

- Full name, birth date, and a recent photo
- Diagnoses, medications and allergies
- Emergency contacts and who has legal authority
- What to do first if a parent cannot be reached
- The one thing a caregiver must know immediately, such as seizure protocol or a choking risk

### 2. Personal profile

- What your loved one likes and dislikes
- How they communicate, including signs, devices and behavior cues
- What calms them and what upsets them
- Daily routines: waking, meals, bathing, bedtime
- Favorite foods, music, shows, clothes and places

### 3. Health care

- Providers' names, phone numbers and what each treats
- Medical history, hospitalizations and equipment
- Medications, doses and pharmacy
- Insurance and Medicaid information
- Dental, vision and therapy schedules
- Preferences about end-of-life care, if you have discussed them

Pair this with a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) so the people named can actually talk to providers.

### 4. Education, work and daily activities

- School history, IEP details and what helped
- Work or volunteer placements
- Day programs, clubs and recreation
- Transportation arrangements

### 5. Living arrangements

- Where your loved one lives now and why it works
- Your hopes for future housing, such as staying in the family home, a group home or supported living
- Which features matter, such as a quiet setting, a roommate or proximity to family
- Programs or agencies you have researched and ones you would avoid

### 6. Relationships and community

- Family, friends, neighbors and faith communities who matter
- Who should visit, call or take outings, and how often
- Social events your loved one enjoys
- Relatives who may not understand the disability and need guidance

### 7. Benefits and finances

- Types of benefits, including SSI, SSDI, Medicaid, waivers and housing assistance, and who handles each
- Names of the representative payee and benefits case managers
- Where the trust, ABLE account and other accounts are held
- How you hope funds are used, and which purchases you consider priorities
- Names of your attorney, accountant and financial advisor

Do not put account numbers or passwords in the letter. Instead, say where a secure record exists and who can access it. For guidance, see [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access).

### 8. Legal arrangements

- Who is the guardian, supporter, agent under a power of attorney, or healthcare agent
- Where legal documents are stored
- Your hopes about guardianship and alternatives, which are explained in [turning 18 with a disability](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children)

### 9. Your hopes and values

Close with a personal passage. Describe what you hope life looks like for your loved one, what matters about independence and dignity, how you would like them to be treated, and what you want caregivers to know about who they are.

## How do you write it without being overwhelmed?

You do not need to write it in one sitting. The following approach works for many parents.

1. Set a 30-minute timer and write the emergency summary first.
2. Add one section a week, starting with the one you think about most.
3. Ask siblings, teachers and aides to add what they know.
4. Record short videos of routines such as a medication, a bedtime ritual or a communication method. These can be stored with the letter.
5. Read the draft as if you were a stranger with no background. Fill in what is missing.
6. Share it with your trustee and guardian, and ask what they would still want to know.

Write in plain language and use the person's name and pronouns. Describe strengths as well as needs. Say "Maya uses a communication device and understands more than she can say," not "Maya is nonverbal and low-functioning."

## Who should receive the letter?

- The trustee and successor trustee
- The guardian, supporter or agent
- Close relatives who will be involved
- Your attorney, to keep with the estate planning file
- The service coordinator or case manager, if you wish

Keep an up-to-date copy in the place your family knows to look, and give digital copies to people you trust. Tell your loved one about the letter too, in a way that is right for them. Many people with disabilities have strong views and want to help write their own section, which is a good thing. A person who can express preferences should have a say in how the plan describes them.

## How often should you update it?

At least once a year, and whenever there is a significant change: a new medication, a move, a change in providers, a new job or day program, or a death in the family. Put a reminder on your calendar. Date each version and mark the old ones as superseded so no one follows outdated instructions.

## What should the letter not do?

- It should not contradict the legal documents. If the trust says the trustee has discretion, the letter should guide, not command.
- It should not hold secrets that caregivers need. Include safety information even if it is difficult to write.
- It should not become a place for family grievances.
- It should not include information that could be misused, such as account numbers, Social Security numbers or passwords.

### Example: Ruth's letter

Ruth, 67, writes a letter for her son Ben, 38, who has autism and lives in a supported apartment. The letter lists his morning routine, the weekly ride to his job at a garden center, the aide who knows how to read his signs of stress, and his love of train schedules. It says that Ben should keep going to Sunday dinner at his sister's house, that a particular rule of the trust about housing should be revisited with him present, and that loud buzzers upset him. When Ruth is hospitalized, her daughter hands the letter to a temporary aide, who knows how to help Ben within an hour.

## How does the letter fit with the rest of the plan?

A complete plan includes a trust, backup decision-makers, powers of attorney, a plan for benefits and a letter of intent. The letter ties them together.
