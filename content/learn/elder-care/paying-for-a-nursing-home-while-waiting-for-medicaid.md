A parent's fall turns into a hospital stay, and then a social worker says the hospital is ready to discharge to a nursing home. Within a day the family is asking about money. Medicaid will probably be needed, but nobody believes it will start tomorrow. The question that keeps people up is simple: who pays in the meantime?

This article walks through the bridge between admission and approval. It covers how the first bills get paid, how long the wait tends to be, what retroactive coverage can do for you, what to gather so the application does not stall, and what to do if the answer comes back no.

## The short version

- Until approval, the resident is private pay. The facility will bill monthly.
- Apply as soon as the resident is eligible, not when the bills get uncomfortable.
- Medicaid can often reach back a few months, but not in every state and only for months the resident actually qualified.
- Keep the receipts. Overpayments are refundable, and the paper trail is what gets them back.
- A missing document is the most common reason an application drags.

## How does the first bill get paid?

The first bill is paid from the resident's own resources. That usually means monthly income, savings, and cash from selling things that Medicaid would otherwise count. Families sometimes use a short-term source as well, such as a loan secured by the resident's home or a sale of a second car. Those need care, and we will come back to why.

Carol, 84, enters a nursing home after a stroke. Her Social Security and pension together come to less than the monthly rate. She has $62,000 in savings and a house. Her daughter Dana applies for Medicaid the first week, and Carol pays privately from income and savings. Four months later Medicaid approves the application and treats Carol as eligible from the month she applied. Dana submits receipts for payments made during those months, and the facility refunds the part Medicaid should have paid, minus the monthly contribution from Carol's income.

### What counts as a bridge source?

| Source | How it works | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| Resident's monthly income | Social Security, pension, annuity payments go to the bill | Medicaid will later take most of this anyway |
| Savings and checking | Paid down until the state's asset limit is reached | Keep records of every payment |
| Sale of a non-exempt asset | A second car, a rental, an extra property | The sale price should be fair, or it can look like a gift |
| Life insurance cash value | Surrendered or borrowed against | Check the effect on eligibility first |
| Long-term care insurance | Pays a daily benefit after an elimination period | Check the elimination period and what is covered, see [long-term care insurance](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance) |
| VA benefits | Possible for qualifying veterans or spouses | The process also takes months, see [VA Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance) |
| Family loan or help | A relative fronts money | Record it as a loan, not a gift to the resident |

## How long does the wait really take?

The state agency has a general federal deadline of 45 days to decide most applications and 90 days when it must decide whether the person is disabled. The clock runs from the date the application is filed, not from the date it is complete. States often stop the clock to ask for more documents, and a bank statement missing two pages can add a month.

So the wait is not a fixed number. A clean application from a well-prepared family may come back in six weeks. One with a missing deed, an unexplained transfer and a bank that takes ten days to send records may take four months. Treat the first 90 days as the planning period, and have a plan for what happens if it runs longer.

## What is retroactive Medicaid coverage?

Retroactive coverage lets Medicaid pay for care received in the months before the month of application, if the person met every requirement in each of those months. Federal law has long provided for up to three months. A number of states have asked for and received permission to shorten or drop it, so you cannot take it for granted.

Two practical points. First, retroactive coverage depends on eligibility on the first of each month in many states, so a person who was over the asset limit in the first month and under it in the second may be covered only from the second. Second, it is not a reason to wait. Filing late can mean a month of care that Medicaid never covers.

If you are curious about what happens when care started months ago and the family did nothing, our article on [Medicaid crisis planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-crisis-planning) covers the options.

## What do you need to apply?

The application is long, but the thing that slows it is paper. Gather these before you file:

1. **Identity and residency.** Photo ID, Social Security card, Medicare and insurance cards, proof of address.
2. **Income.** Award letters for Social Security and any pension, and annuity statements.
3. **Bank and investment statements.** The look-back window is 60 months in most states, so expect to be asked for a long history. Our article on the [five-year look-back](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains why.
4. **Real estate.** Deeds, tax bills, and any mortgage statements.
5. **Insurance.** Life insurance policies with cash value, long-term care policies and burial contracts.
6. **Legal documents.** A [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) or court appointment, so the person filing has authority.
7. **Medical forms.** The facility usually provides the level-of-care forms. Ask for them on day one.

### Who files for a parent who cannot sign?

Someone with legal authority has to. A named agent under a financial power of attorney can usually file. Without one, a spouse, the facility's business office, or a family member may be able to apply as an authorized representative, depending on the state. If none of those works, the family may need a court appointment, which takes longer. This is the single best reason to have a power of attorney in place before the crisis.

## What can go wrong with the bridge?

- **Paying the facility from the wrong place.** If the parent's money is in a joint account with a child, withdrawals can get tangled. Keep a clean record of what came from whom.
- **Gifts that appear during the wait.** A family under stress sometimes moves money to children to "protect" it. That can create a penalty period, which is a separate, longer wait. See [gifting to children before Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid).
- **Borrowing against the house.** A home equity loan can turn an exempt asset into cash, which Medicaid counts. Talk to an attorney first.
- **Bills that pile up.** If nobody pays, the facility may begin discharge proceedings, which are governed by notice rules. Keep talking to the business office.
- **Treating the pending application as a safety net.** Denial is possible, and a facility can ask the resident to pay for the months before.

## What if the application is denied?

A denial letter must state the reason and the deadline to appeal. The deadline is often short, in the range of weeks. Read the notice the day it comes, and file the appeal even if you are still gathering proof.

Most denials fall into three groups. The first is missing information, which is usually fixed by sending the document. The second is excess assets, which may mean spending down or reclassifying what counts, covered in [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down). The third is a transfer penalty from the look-back, which turns on whether the money can be returned or whether the transfer was for a purpose the rules allow.

Ask for a fair hearing in writing and keep a copy. Hearings are often informal, and an attorney who has seen how your state's agency reads its manual can make a real difference.

## How state law changes this

Three things differ most by state. The first is the retroactive window, which can run from none to three months. The second is the asset limit and how the agency counts a home, vehicle and life insurance, summarized in [what assets are exempt](/learn/elder-care/what-assets-are-exempt-from-medicaid). The third is how quickly the agency actually processes applications, which varies by county and by year. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) is a good starting point for local details.
