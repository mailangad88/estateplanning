A parent has $38,000 more than Medicaid allows, and a nursing home bill arriving every month. Someone says to "spend it down." What does that actually mean, and what can you safely buy? The honest answer is that the rule is simple and the application is not: you may use money for the applicant's benefit at fair value, but you may not give it away without consequences.

This article lists the common uses, what to avoid, how to document everything and how the rules change for married couples. Medicaid is run by each state within a federal framework, so check your state's manual before relying on any item below.

## What does "spend-down" mean?

The term is used in two senses. In this article it means reducing countable assets to your state's limit, commonly about $2,000 for a single person in many states, before Medicaid pays for long-term care, by paying for things you would otherwise buy. Some states have a "medically needy" program in which a person whose income is too high must also incur medical costs up to a set amount each month, sometimes also called a spend-down or share of cost. The second kind is about income and monthly bills. The first is about assets, and it is the subject here. For the framework, see [Medicaid planning for long-term care: the basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## What is the principle?

Pay fair market value, and make sure the applicant, or in many states the community spouse, receives the benefit. A purchase for a good price is an exchange. A gift is a transfer for less than fair value, and it is the thing the look-back searches for. Federal law, 42 U.S.C. 1396p(c), sets the penalty rule, and the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) article explains how penalty periods are calculated.

## What can you spend money on?

These uses are commonly accepted, subject to state rules and good documentation.

| Use of money | Usually allowed? | Why | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pay off debts, including credit cards and a mortgage | Often | Value received by reducing a legal debt | A mortgage on an exempt home turns cash into exempt equity; check the home equity cap |
| Home repairs, a new roof, accessibility changes | Often | Improves an exempt asset the applicant uses | Work for the benefit of a relative's own property is not the same; get invoices |
| Irrevocable prepaid funeral and burial | Often | Many states exempt it | Must be irrevocable and within state terms; refundable plans may count |
| One vehicle | Often | One vehicle is commonly exempt | Buying a car for a child is a gift |
| Medical, dental, hearing, vision, mobility equipment | Often | Direct benefit to the applicant | Reasonable prices; keep records |
| Paid care under a written agreement | Sometimes | Services at fair value | The agreement must come first; see [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) |
| Legal and planning fees | Usually | Fair-value services | Reasonable fees, itemized bills |
| Clothing, furniture, personal items | Often | Personal property is generally exempt | Large unusual purchases attract questions |
| Gifts, loans to family, paying a relative's bills | No | No fair value received | Likely to cause a penalty |

### Debts and the home

Paying down a mortgage or buying needed repairs often works because the applicant receives value. The home is usually exempt while the applicant lives there or intends to return, within a state home equity limit. Federal law sets the limit range at $752,000 to $1,130,000 for 2026 and states pick within that range. California has no cap in 2026, while a cap is scheduled to begin later. Because estate recovery can reach the home after death in many states, see [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) before treating the house as permanently safe.

### A prepaid funeral

An irrevocable funeral contract, usually bought from a funeral home or through an insurance-funded plan, is exempt in many states, with different caps and rules. Revocable arrangements and plain savings labelled "for burial" often count. Ask the funeral home whether the contract meets your state's definition.

## What triggers a penalty?

- **Gifts to children and relatives.** Cash, a car, an "advance" on inheritance and the cost of a grandchild's tuition are all transfers.
- **Paying someone else's bills.** If you cover a child's mortgage or credit cards, you received nothing.
- **Selling at a discount.** Selling a house or car to a relative for less than it is worth transfers the difference.
- **Undocumented loans.** A family "loan" without a written note, interest, a repayment schedule and actual repayment is usually treated as a gift.
- **Adding names to accounts or deeds.** It can be a transfer of part of the asset.
- **Paying family for care without a prior written agreement.**

### Example

Elaine, 82, is entering a nursing home in a state with a $2,000 asset limit. She has $52,000 in savings. Her daughter suggests giving $25,000 to each grandchild "so the state doesn't take it." The gifts could produce a penalty of several months, depending on the state's divisor, during which Medicaid will not pay while the nursing home bills continue. Instead, Elaine pays $4,500 to settle a credit card, $9,000 for a new roof on her house that she plans to return to, $7,200 for an irrevocable funeral, buys a hearing aid and a lift chair, and pays the nursing home privately until she reaches the limit. Each dollar bought something she needed, and every expense has a receipt.

## How is spend-down different from gifting or a trust?

Spending is an exchange, gifting is a transfer without value, and a trust is a legal structure that holds assets under rules. A trust set up to move assets out of the applicant's name is itself a transfer and starts the look-back. Our [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) article covers how they work and what they cost you in control, and the [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) article shows where they fit.

## What paper trail do you need?

The agency reviews the look-back window of statements, so documentation is everything.

1. Keep every receipt, invoice and contract for each spend-down purchase.
2. Pay by check or electronic transfer so the bank statement shows who was paid and when.
3. For large items such as a vehicle or equipment, keep a quote or valuation showing the price was fair.
4. Keep a short written explanation of why each large withdrawal was made.
5. Keep statements for all accounts for at least the full look-back period, and for transfers to a spouse, the proof of transfer.

An agent acting under a power of attorney should keep an accounting, because the agent is a fiduciary and the agency may ask for one.

## How is it different for a married couple?

When one spouse needs care and the other stays at home, federal law lets the community spouse keep a protected share of the couple's assets. For 2026, the federal range is $32,532 to $162,660, and states choose how to apply it. Transfers between spouses are exempt from the penalty, so assets can often be moved to the healthy spouse. The spouse at home must then plan for their own possible future need, and a will that leaves property outright to the community spouse can be a problem. Our pillar on [long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care) explains the other protections.

## What if you have already given money away?

Do not try to hide it, and do not make a second move without advice. Options sometimes include returning the gift, which can reduce or eliminate a penalty in some states, or combining a partial gift with other strategies. The right choice depends on the amount, the timing and your state's rules. This is the point at which to see an attorney rather than guess.

## Does long-term care insurance change this?

Yes. A policy that pays toward care means fewer assets need to be spent. In states with partnership programs, a qualifying policy may also let you keep extra assets. See [long-term care insurance and your estate plan](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance).
