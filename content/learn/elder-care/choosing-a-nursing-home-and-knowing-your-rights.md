Choosing a nursing home usually happens in a hurry. A hospital discharge is two days away, the social worker hands you a list, and the beds that are open are not necessarily the ones you would pick. Still, an hour of homework before signing is worth more than almost anything else you will do in this process.

This page covers how to compare facilities, what to look at on a visit, what to question in the admission agreement, and the rights a resident has once they are living there. It belongs to our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## How do you choose a nursing home?

Start with the facts you can look up, then check them against what you see. Neither is enough on its own. A facility with good ratings can have a bad unit on a bad night, and a plain-looking building can have staff who have worked there for fifteen years and know every resident.

Follow these steps in order.

1. **Narrow by location and payment.** Distance matters more than people expect. A facility twenty minutes from a daughter's home gets visited; one an hour away gets visited on holidays. Ask each place whether it accepts Medicaid and how many beds are certified for it.
2. **Check the official record.** Medicare's Care Compare site lists inspection findings, staffing numbers and ratings for certified facilities. Look for repeat findings on the same problem, not only the star count.
3. **Visit twice.** Once by appointment, and once without one, ideally around a meal or in the early evening when fewer managers are around.
4. **Talk to the people who see it daily.** Aides, nurses and visiting families will tell you more than the brochure.
5. **Meet the social worker and the director of nursing,** and ask the questions below.
6. **Read the admission agreement at home,** not at the front desk.

### What should you look for on a visit?

- Do call lights get answered quickly, and do residents look clean and dressed?
- Is there a smell of urine in the hallways or common rooms?
- Are residents out of their rooms, or lined up in front of a television?
- Does staff speak to residents by name and kneel to talk at eye level?
- Is the same nurse or aide usually on the same hall, or do they rotate through agencies?
- What does the meal look like, and does anyone help those who cannot feed themselves?

Ask about staff turnover and how many aides cover a unit at night. Ask how the facility handles residents with dementia and whether there is a secured unit. Ask how long the average resident has stayed. A good director of nursing answers without defensiveness.

## Should you sign the nursing home admission agreement?

Read it first, and do not sign it in any capacity you do not hold. The admission agreement is a contract between the facility and the resident. When a family member signs, the capacity should be written next to the signature, for example "as agent under power of attorney for Ruth Alvarez," not as a personal party.

Federal rules bar a nursing home that takes Medicare or Medicaid from requiring a third party to personally promise payment as a condition of admission. A child may still choose to pay, and a child who signs personally as the payer can be held to that promise. Our page on [whether a nursing home can make your children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) covers that in depth.

Questions to bring to the agreement:

| Term | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Daily rate | What is included, and what is billed extra, such as laundry, incontinence supplies or therapy? |
| Rate increases | How much notice is given, and how often can the rate change? |
| Medicaid conversion | If the resident runs out of money, can they stay in the same bed, or are only certain beds for Medicaid? |
| Arbitration | Does the contract require disputes to go to private arbitration? It cannot be a required condition of admission under federal rules, so you can usually decline that section. |
| Deposits and refunds | How much is due at signing, and how quickly is the balance returned after discharge or death? |
| Agent signature | Is the signature line for the resident or for the family member personally? |

## Can a nursing home discharge a resident?

Yes, but only for a short list of reasons, and with a process. Under federal rules a certified facility may transfer or discharge a resident in a few situations. It cannot meet the person's needs. Their health has improved so they no longer need the care. Their presence endangers others. The bill has gone unpaid after reasonable notice. Or the facility is closing.

It must generally give written notice thirty days ahead, with the reason, the effective date, the new location and information about how to appeal. Shorter notice is allowed in emergencies. A resident cannot be discharged simply because they became Medicaid eligible or have a difficult family.

If you receive a notice, take these steps.

1. Do not move your parent on the date in the notice without understanding the appeal right.
2. Request a hearing within the time shown, in writing, and keep a copy.
3. Call the long-term care ombudsman, whose services are free.
4. Ask the hospital or the facility's social worker to identify another placement as a backup.

## Who do you call about neglect or abuse?

Call the long-term care ombudsman. Every state has an ombudsman program that takes complaints from residents and families and works to resolve them, often confidentially. The state agency that licenses and inspects nursing homes also investigates complaints, and adult protective services handles abuse of vulnerable adults in some states.

Document what you see. Take dated photographs of injuries, write down names and times, and request the care plan and incident reports. Warning signs include unexplained bruises, repeated falls, bedsores, sudden weight loss, medication errors and a resident who becomes withdrawn or afraid of a particular staff member. If you believe there is immediate danger, call 911 first. Money problems are separate; see [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) for the signs of someone exploiting the resident's accounts.

## How do you raise the move with your parent?

Gently, early and more than once. People resist nursing homes because they hear "you are being put away." Lead with the specific problem, a fall at night or a medicine missed three times in a week, and then ask what would make them feel safe. If a parent has capacity, the decision is theirs, and your role is to make the choices real. Our page on [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) has more on starting these talks before a crisis.

If your parent can no longer decide, the person named in a power of attorney or healthcare proxy makes the call. Without those documents, a family may need court help, which is why signing them early matters. See [what a power of attorney agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do).

## How state law changes this

Federal law sets the core resident rights. States license facilities, run the ombudsman and survey programs, and add their own rules on notice, staffing and arbitration. See your [state guide](/estate-planning/california) for a starting point.
