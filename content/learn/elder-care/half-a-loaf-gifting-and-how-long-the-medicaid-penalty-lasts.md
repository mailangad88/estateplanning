Most people first hear about the Medicaid penalty period as a vague threat: if you give things away, Medicaid will punish you. The real rule is more mechanical than that, and the mechanics are what decide whether a family can plan around it. A penalty is a number of months, it comes from a division problem, and it begins on a date that surprises nearly everyone.

This page explains how long a penalty lasts, when it starts, how the strategy known as half-a-loaf works, and which transfers carry no penalty at all. It sits under our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care). The five-year window and how it is audited are covered in the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) article, so I will not repeat that here.

## How is the length of a Medicaid penalty calculated?

The penalty is the total value of everything you gave away or sold for less than it was worth, divided by a monthly figure your state sets, called the divisor. The answer is the number of months Medicaid will not pay for nursing home care. The divisor is meant to reflect the average monthly private-pay cost of a nursing home in the state, and states update it periodically.

There is no maximum. A large enough gift produces a penalty of several years, longer than most people would live in care. Partial months are handled differently from state to state. Some round down, some count the leftover as a fraction of a month, and a few add a day count.

Take an invented divisor of $10,000 a month. It is a round number for the example, not any state's figure. Dolores, 81, gave her son $60,000 and her daughter $40,000 two years ago. Those gifts total $100,000, so she faces a 10-month penalty. During those months Medicaid pays nothing toward the nursing home, even though she has no money left.

## When does the penalty start?

It starts on the date you are otherwise eligible for Medicaid long-term care, which means you are in a nursing home or approved for care, you have applied, and your remaining assets are at or below your state's limit. The gift date does not start it. The date you ran out of money does not start it either.

This was a change made by the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, and it explains most of the grief families feel. Before then, a gift made in January started its clock in January. Now the clock sits idle until you are poor enough to qualify and have asked for help. If Dolores gave away the money while healthy and kept $4,000 in the bank, she has to spend down to the limit, apply, and only then does the 10 months begin to run.

That has a practical consequence. Someone who gifts and then has no way to pay private rates during the penalty has created a gap that someone else, usually the child who received the money, has to fill. Anyone considering a gift should map this gap first.

## What is half-a-loaf gifting?

Half-a-loaf is the practice of giving away roughly half of your savings and keeping the other half to pay the nursing home during the penalty that the gift creates. The name comes from the old saying that half a loaf is better than none.

The arithmetic is simple when the monthly nursing home bill is close to the divisor. Raymond, 84, has $200,000 and needs a nursing home that charges about $10,000 a month. If the divisor is also $10,000, a gift of $100,000 creates a 10-month penalty, and the remaining $100,000 pays for those 10 months. His children keep $100,000, and Medicaid takes over when the penalty ends.

If the nursing home costs more than the divisor, the retained half runs out early. If it costs less, there is money left over. So the real calculation is a ratio of the actual bill to the divisor, and it changes with room type and any income the person has coming in each month.

### Why half-a-loaf is harder than it sounds

Because the penalty only begins when you are otherwise eligible, Raymond cannot sit on $100,000 and wait. With that much money he is not otherwise eligible, so the penalty never starts. The usual solutions are to convert the retained money into something that does not count as a resource, such as a Medicaid-compliant annuity that pays him income each month, or a promissory note that meets the state's rules. See [Medicaid-compliant annuities](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-compliant-annuity) for the requirements, including the state being named as a remainder beneficiary.

Some states also allow a gift to be partly returned to shorten a penalty. Others do not. A few treat an annuity purchase as a disqualifying transfer if it is not structured exactly. This is not a plan to improvise from a website, and a mistake costs months of unpaid nursing home bills.

## What is reverse half-a-loaf?

It is a variation for married couples. Instead of the applicant giving away half, the applicant first transfers nearly everything to the healthy spouse, which is an exempt transfer. The healthy spouse then gifts a portion to the children or to a trust and keeps the remainder in a form that produces income, usually an annuity. The healthy spouse's income is not counted against the applicant, and it can cover the cost of care during the penalty.

Its appeal is that the couple keeps control of the order of events. Its drawbacks are that not every state accepts the sequence, that some states look through the transfers and count the healthy spouse's gift against the applicant anyway, and that the plan depends on the healthy spouse staying well. The married-couple rules are in [how Medicaid works for a married couple](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples).

## Which transfers carry no penalty?

Federal law lists transfers that are exempt from the penalty entirely. A gift that fits one of these is not a penalty problem, although you will be asked to document it.

| Transfer | Exempt when |
|---|---|
| To your spouse, or to a trust solely for your spouse | Always, though the spouse's later gifts can count |
| Your home to a child under 21 | The child is a minor |
| Your home to a blind or disabled child | The child meets the Social Security definition of disability |
| Assets to a trust solely for a disabled child of any age | The trust meets the sole-benefit rules |
| Assets to a trust for any disabled person under 65 | The trust is a qualifying disability trust, with a payback to the state |
| Your home to a caregiver child | The child lived with you at least two years before you entered care and provided care that kept you at home |
| Your home to a sibling | The sibling already has an equity interest and lived there at least one year before you entered care |
| Sale for fair market value | You received something of equal value, and the money is counted |

The details of the disabled-child exceptions are in [transferring a house to a disabled child](/learn/special-needs/medicaid-transfer-exceptions-for-a-disabled-child). A transfer also does not carry a penalty if the property is returned in full, or if you can show convincingly that you made it for some purpose other than qualifying, such as paying a long-held obligation. Agencies read that last one narrowly. A gift made while you were healthy and doing a normal thing, like paying for a grandchild's tuition, is the kind of argument people try, and it is rarely accepted without a paper trail.

## What happens if the penalty is bigger than I can cover?

Families usually fall into this when a gift was made before anyone understood the start-date rule. The options in order of preference:

1. **Ask whether a return is allowed.** Calculate whether returning some or all of the money would end the penalty, and confirm your state agency accepts partial returns.
2. **Use income.** Pension, Social Security and annuity payments can pay part of the bill during the penalty, with the gap covered by savings.
3. **Check for an exception.** The gift may qualify as an exempt transfer for a disabled child or caregiver child.
4. **Request a hardship waiver.** Many states have a process for cases where the penalty would deprive the person of food, shelter or medical care. It is a narrow door and takes paperwork.
5. **Look at the facility.** Some nursing homes will work out a payment plan; others discharge for nonpayment, with notice rights described in [choosing a nursing home](/learn/elder-care/choosing-a-nursing-home-and-knowing-your-rights).

Discharge for unpaid bills is a real risk during a penalty. Children who signed the admission agreement as personally responsible may be pursued, as explained in [whether a nursing home can make your children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay).

## How do small gifts and the gift tax figure in?

They are two separate systems. The federal gift tax exclusion of $19,000 per recipient in 2026 is a tax rule, and Medicaid ignores it. A $19,000 gift to each of three grandchildren is fully tax-free and still counts as a $57,000 transfer for Medicaid. Some states ignore very small gifts, such as birthday money, but few put a dollar figure on what counts as small. A power of attorney who makes gifts for a parent should read [what an agent can and cannot gift](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts) first, since an agent may only gift if the document allows it.

## Is it ever too late to plan?

People ask this on the day they find out a parent needs a nursing home. The honest answer is that some options close and others stay open. Once you are in care and the savings are being drained, you can no longer rely on the five-year window, but you can still protect a spouse, use exempt transfers, set up an income-based plan or build a half-a-loaf strategy. What genuinely is too late is the point at which the money has already been spent on private care with no plan. The article on [Medicaid crisis planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-crisis-planning) walks through what is possible at that stage.
