Long-term care is expensive. A private room in a nursing home often costs well over $100,000 a year in many parts of the country, and care at home or in assisted living can add up to a similar amount. Most families do not have that much cash, and many do not have long-term care insurance. Medicaid is the program that ends up paying for a large share of nursing home care in the United States.

This guide explains how Medicaid works for long-term care, what you can keep, what a spouse is protected, and how planning fits in. **An important warning:** Medicaid is run by the states within a federal framework. Asset limits, home equity limits, the look-back details, income rules and estate recovery practices vary by state, and they change. Treat the numbers here as general ranges and confirm current figures with an attorney or your state Medicaid agency. This guide is part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## Does Medicare pay for nursing home care?

Not for long-term care. Medicare covers skilled nursing facility care for a limited time after a hospital stay of at least three days, and only if you need skilled care such as rehabilitation. It pays in full for the first 20 days, then you owe a daily copayment for days 21 through 100, and coverage ends after day 100. Custodial care, meaning help with bathing, dressing, eating and supervision, is not covered at all.

That gap is why families turn to Medicaid, long-term care insurance or their own savings. Medicaid is the payer for people who have used up their resources.

## Who qualifies for Medicaid long-term care?

Applicants must meet four kinds of tests. Rules differ by state, so this is a general outline.

1. **Medical need.** A state assessment finds that you need a nursing home level of care, or help with several daily activities if you apply for home and community-based services.
2. **Income.** Some states use an income cap, often tied to a multiple of the federal SSI benefit, and require a special trust for income above the cap. Other states use a "medically needy" approach that compares income to the cost of care.
3. **Countable assets.** For a single applicant the limit is typically very low, commonly around $2,000 in many states, though some states allow more.
4. **Look-back compliance.** Transfers for less than fair value in the prior 60 months, in most states, can cause a penalty period. See [the five-year look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

Applicants also need to be citizens or qualified noncitizens and to reside in the state.

## What assets count and what doesn't?

Medicaid sorts assets into countable and exempt. Exempt assets do not count toward the limit, though some may be reached by estate recovery later.

| Usually counted | Usually exempt |
|---|---|
| Bank and brokerage accounts | Primary home, within the home equity limit |
| CDs, stocks, bonds | One vehicle |
| Second homes and investment property | Household goods and personal items |
| Cash value of most life insurance above a small face amount | Prepaid irrevocable funeral contract |
| Retirement accounts in many cases, depending on payout status | Burial plots |
| Annuities that are not compliant | Certain small life insurance policies |

Retirement accounts are treated differently by state. In some, an IRA is counted at its value. In others, it is exempt once the owner is taking required distributions. Ask your state's rules.

### What about the home?

Federal law lets states exclude the primary home from countable assets if equity does not exceed a limit. As of 2026 that federal range runs from roughly $750,000 to about $1.1 million, and each state picks a figure in that range. Some states, including a number of large ones, use the higher number. The exclusion also usually requires that you intend to return home or that a spouse or dependent relative still lives there. Our article [can you protect your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) covers the options in detail.

## How is a spouse protected?

Federal law protects the spouse who is not in the nursing home, called the community spouse, from impoverishment. Two main protections apply.

- **Community spouse resource allowance.** The couple's countable assets are totaled at the start of the institutional stay, and the community spouse may keep half up to a federal maximum, with a federal minimum. Both figures are adjusted each year, and states choose whether to apply the half rule or a flat amount. Roughly, the minimum is in the low $30,000s and the maximum is in the $160,000s, but check current figures.
- **Minimum monthly maintenance needs allowance.** If the community spouse's income is low, some of the institutionalized spouse's income may be diverted to bring them up to a minimum amount that is set by federal rules and adjusted yearly.

The spouse can also keep the home and one car. Transfers between spouses are exempt from the look-back. Because the rules are complex, spouses can sometimes protect more through planning, such as a court order for a higher allowance or an annuity for the community spouse.

### Example

Walter, 82, enters a nursing home. He and his wife Joan, 79, have $240,000 in savings and a $300,000 paid-off home. In a state using the half rule, with a cap in the $160,000s, Joan may keep half, $120,000, plus the home and a car. Walter's remaining $120,000 must be spent down to the state's limit before he qualifies. Spending on a new roof, medical equipment, a prepaid funeral and elder-care costs is allowed, but gifts to the grandchildren within five years are not.

## What planning tools exist?

Planning is about using legal options that Medicaid allows, not hiding assets. Common ones are:

- **Spend-down on things you need.** Pay off debts, repair the home, buy a vehicle, prepay funeral costs, buy hearing aids and dental work.
- **Exempt transfers.** Transfers to a spouse, to a child who is blind or disabled, or to a trust solely for the benefit of a disabled person under 65 do not cause a penalty. A home can generally be transferred without penalty to a spouse, a minor or disabled child, a sibling with an equity interest who lived there for at least a year, or an adult child who lived there and provided care for at least two years.
- **Irrevocable trusts.** Assets given to an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) generally are not countable once the look-back period has passed. You give up ownership and control, so this tool must be used well in advance.
- **Medicaid-compliant annuities.** In certain circumstances a single-premium annuity converts countable assets into income, and is used mainly for a community spouse.
- **Half-a-loaf gifting.** Gifting part of the assets and using the rest to pay for care during the penalty period. This is highly technical and varies by state.
- **Special needs trusts.** A [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) can protect assets for a disabled child or spouse.

Which tool fits depends on timing, the state and the family. The wrong move, such as simply adding a child to a bank account, can lead to denied benefits or a loss of the asset.

## What is estate recovery?

Federal law requires states to try to recover the cost of Medicaid long-term care from the estates of recipients who were 55 or older, or who were permanently institutionalized. Recovery can reach the home and other assets in the probate estate, and some states define the estate more broadly to include assets that pass outside probate, such as a living trust or joint accounts. Recovery is delayed or waived when there is a surviving spouse, a minor child, or a blind or disabled child, and states must have hardship procedures. Practices differ widely.

## What documents should be in place first?

Before any application, make sure these exist. They matter more because Medicaid applications are paperwork-heavy and often handled by family members.

- A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) that permits Medicaid planning, including gifting and trust creation, because many forms limit gifting powers.
- A healthcare power of attorney and living will.
- A will or trust that matches the plan.
- A list of every account, policy, deed and loan, with five years of statements.

If the person no longer has capacity and the power of attorney does not allow planning moves, a court-supervised guardianship may be required.
