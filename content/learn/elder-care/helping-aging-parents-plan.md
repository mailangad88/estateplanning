When a parent starts to slow down, a friend's parent has a stroke, or a doctor mentions memory, adult children begin to worry about paperwork. Who can talk to the doctor? Who can pay the bills if Dad can't? What happens to the house? These questions are easier to answer before a crisis, but the conversation itself can be awkward.

This guide gives you a practical approach: how to open the discussion, what to look for, which documents matter, and where your role ends. It is one article in our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## Why does timing matter so much?

Because legal documents require capacity, and capacity can be lost. To sign a will, a person must understand what they own, who their family is and what the document does. To sign a power of attorney, they must understand that they are giving someone authority over their money. Early memory loss does not necessarily end capacity, but a decline can, and after that the only path may be court-ordered guardianship.

Guardianship, sometimes called conservatorship, is available when no documents exist and the person can no longer manage. It is public, usually takes weeks or months, requires a hearing and may impose ongoing court reporting. A few hours with an attorney can often prevent it. Our article on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains the difference.

## How do you start the conversation?

Start with their wishes, not your worries. People resist being managed and respond to being asked.

- **Pick the setting.** A quiet, unhurried time, not a holiday dinner or right after a medical scare.
- **Use a story.** "A coworker's family spent months in court because her mother had no power of attorney. I'd like to be sure we're ready."
- **Ask open questions.** "If you were in the hospital and couldn't speak, who would you want to decide?" "Is there anything you'd want us to know?"
- **Name your goal.** You are not asking for their money. You want to carry out their wishes.
- **Offer help.** Find attorneys, schedule the appointment, drive them, take notes.
- **Accept slow progress.** One conversation rarely finishes it.

If a parent refuses, ask whether another person might help, such as a sibling, their doctor, their clergy or an old friend. For more ideas, see [how to talk to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan), which also helps when siblings need to align.

## Which documents does your parent need?

Four core documents, plus a review of the forms that override them.

| Document | What it does | Why now |
|---|---|---|
| [Durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) | Names an agent to pay bills, manage accounts and deal with taxes if the parent can't | Avoids guardianship; banks and agencies need it |
| [Healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) | Names someone to make medical decisions when the parent can't | Doctors need a decision-maker in a crisis |
| Living will or advance directive | States preferences on life support, feeding tubes and comfort care | Guides the agent and spares family conflict |
| [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) | Lets named people receive medical information | Without it, providers may refuse to talk to family |
| Will or revocable trust | Directs who receives property and who handles the estate | Avoids intestacy; a trust can also avoid probate |

Add two reviews. First, **beneficiary designations** on retirement accounts, life insurance and annuities, which pass outside the will. Second, **account titles and deeds**, since jointly owned property passes to the co-owner regardless of what a will says. Many parents have out-of-date forms naming a deceased spouse or an ex.

If the parent has dementia or a progressive illness, read [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) for guidance on capacity and timing.

## What should you gather before meeting an attorney?

Having the facts in one place saves time and fees. Ask your parent to help assemble:

1. A list of assets, accounts, real estate, vehicles and approximate values
2. Debts, mortgages and monthly obligations
3. Existing wills, trusts, powers of attorney and directives
4. Insurance policies, including life and long-term care
5. Beneficiary forms for retirement accounts and insurance
6. Deeds, titles and business interests
7. Names and contact details for the attorney, accountant, financial advisor and doctors
8. A list of online accounts and where passwords are kept

Do not copy full account numbers or Social Security numbers into emails or shared documents. Keep the list in a secure place and tell the agent where it is.

## How do you choose who serves?

The parent chooses. You can help them think about it:

- **Financial agent.** Someone organized, honest and living close enough to act. It does not need to be the oldest child. Some parents name a trusted professional or two co-agents who must act together.
- **Healthcare agent.** Someone who can stay calm, follow the parent's wishes and speak to doctors, even if it differs from their own views.
- **Successors.** Backup agents in case the first one is unable to serve.

Our guide on [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) lists the questions to ask. If you are named, you take on a fiduciary role: you must act in your parent's interest, keep their money separate from yours, keep records, and avoid self-dealing even if siblings disagree.

## What if siblings disagree?

Plan for it. The most common conflicts are about who is named, whether one child who provides care should be paid or favored, and whether the parent is being influenced. Parents can reduce conflict by explaining their choices in a family meeting or a letter, by naming co-agents or an independent trustee, and by being clear about any unequal gifts and why. An attorney can also record the parent's capacity and independent decision at signing, which is helpful if a document is challenged later.

## What about long-term care costs?

Once the basics are signed, ask about how care would be paid. Options include savings, long-term care insurance, home care, assisted living and Medicaid. If a parent may need care within five years, speak with an attorney early about [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning), the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) and protecting the home. Acting early opens more options. Medicaid rules vary by state, so local advice matters.

## What can you do if your parent has already lost capacity?

If your parent has no documents and cannot understand them, you cannot sign for them. Options:

- **Existing documents.** Look for powers of attorney signed years ago. They may still be valid.
- **Joint accounts and beneficiary forms.** These may allow some access, but they are not a substitute for authority.
- **Guardianship or conservatorship.** A court appoints someone after a hearing. Costs and procedures vary by state.
- **Limited fixes.** Representative payee arrangements for Social Security or the VA may handle some benefit income.

Speak with an attorney quickly. Costs mount and bills continue during delay.

## How do you protect your parent from being taken advantage of?

Elderly people with cognitive decline are targets for scams and sometimes for relatives. Warning signs include sudden changes in documents, new "friends" who isolate the parent, unexplained withdrawals and unpaid bills. Our guide on [protecting older adults from financial exploitation](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) lists the red flags and reporting channels.
