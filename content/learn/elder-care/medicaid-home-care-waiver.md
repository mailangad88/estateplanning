Most people who need long-term care would rather stay in their own homes. Medicaid knows that, partly because home care costs the program less than a nursing home bed does. Over the past few decades the program has built a second path, the home and community-based services waiver, that pays for help at home. It works well for the families who find it. Plenty never do.

This page explains how the waivers work, who gets in, what they pay for and whether you can be paid to care for your own parent. It sits alongside our [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) in the [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What is a Medicaid home care waiver?

A waiver is a state program that Medicaid allows to depart from its usual rules. The common version is called a home and community-based services waiver, or HCBS waiver. Federal law lets a state offer services at home to people who would otherwise qualify for a nursing facility, and lets the state limit how many people it enrolls. Hence the term: the standard Medicaid requirements are waived.

States name them differently. You may hear "Community Choices," "Elderly Waiver," "Aged and Disabled Waiver," "Independence Care" or a managed long-term care plan. The labels do not matter much. What matters is that each state runs one or several, and each has its own rules for who is covered, how many slots exist and what is paid.

### What does a waiver typically pay for?

- Personal care aides who help with bathing, dressing, meals and moving around.
- Homemaker and chore services.
- Adult day programs.
- Respite care for a family caregiver.
- Home-delivered meals.
- Home modifications such as ramps and grab bars, and sometimes emergency alert devices.
- Care management that coordinates the rest.

Most waivers do not pay rent, a mortgage, groceries or utilities. For assisted living, the usual arrangement is that Medicaid pays for the services and the resident pays room and board from income. Not every state covers assisted living at all.

## Who qualifies?

Two tests apply, and a person must pass both.

1. **The care test.** The state assesses whether the person needs a nursing-home level of care. This looks at help needed with daily activities such as bathing, eating, toileting, transferring and medication, and at memory and safety needs. Needing some help is not enough in most states.
2. **The financial test.** The person must meet the Medicaid income and asset limits that apply in their state. These are generally the same ones that apply to nursing home Medicaid, with the home and one car usually exempt. Higher income can be handled by an [income cap trust](/learn/elder-care/income-cap-trust-miller-trust) in states that require one.

Married applicants get the spousal protections as well, though a few details differ for home care. See [how Medicaid works for a married couple](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples).

## Does the look-back apply to home care?

Often it does. Federal law applies the look-back to long-term care services, and most states count home and community-based waiver services in that group. A gift to a child can therefore delay not only a nursing home stay but waiver services too. A few states treat it differently, or apply the penalty only to institutional care, so check the rule for your state. The details of the penalty are in the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) guide.

This matters for people who think, "I only need a little help at home, so I do not have to worry." The review of past transfers does not care how much help you need.

## Why are there waiting lists?

Because the state can cap how many people a waiver enrolls. Nursing home Medicaid is an entitlement: if you qualify, you get it. A waiver is not. When the funded slots are full, eligible people wait.

Waits vary widely. Some states have no list for certain waivers and others have lists of many months or years. Some prioritize by urgency, such as someone leaving a hospital or at risk of nursing home placement. While waiting, a family can sometimes use a state-plan personal care benefit that has no cap, local aging services programs, adult day care paid privately, a [VA benefit for a veteran](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance), or paid family help under a written agreement.

Put the name down early. Many lists run by the date of first contact, not by when the family decides it is ready.

## Can a family member be paid as the caregiver?

In many states, yes, but through narrow doors.

Some states run self-directed or consumer-directed programs. In these, the participant, or an authorized representative, hires the worker, sets the schedule within the approved plan and the worker is paid through the program. Many of these programs allow adult children, grandchildren or friends to be hired. Many exclude a spouse, and some exclude anyone who is the participant's legal guardian or holds the power of attorney.

If you want to try this, the usual path looks like this.

1. Confirm your parent is enrolled or about to be and that the program offers self-direction.
2. Ask whether relatives can be hired and which are excluded.
3. Complete the worker enrollment: background check, training and timesheet process.
4. Keep the hours within the written plan. Hours beyond it are not paid.

Where no such program exists, a parent can still pay a child privately for care, but it must be done carefully so that Medicaid does not treat it as a gift. We cover that in [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver). The tax questions, including whether the child receives income, are covered there as well.

## Home care or nursing home: which fits?

| Question | Home care waiver | Nursing home Medicaid |
|---|---|---|
| Is a place assured once you qualify? | No. Slots can be capped. | Yes, if you meet the criteria. |
| Where do you live? | Your home or assisted living. | A facility. |
| What about the house? | Usually exempt. | Exempt only with intent to return or a spouse at home. |
| Level of care needed | Nursing-home level, but safe at home. | Nursing-home level. |
| Family role | Often large, sometimes paid. | Visits and advocacy. |

The honest answer is that home care works while the person is safe there, which often means a family member or paid aides are present for many hours. When nights become unsafe or two-person lifts are needed, a facility may be the more realistic choice. Our overview of [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) covers how to bring that up.

## What should you do first?

Call the state Medicaid agency or local Area Agency on Aging, ask for the home and community-based services screening and request a care assessment. Meanwhile, collect the same documents you would need for a nursing home application: five years of statements, the deed, insurance policies and the power of attorney. If your parent lacks a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), getting one signed while they still have capacity makes the whole process far smoother.

## How state law changes this

States decide whether to offer waivers at all, how many slots, which services, whether relatives may be paid and whether assisted living is included. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/new-york) is a starting place, but the current program rules come from the state agency.
