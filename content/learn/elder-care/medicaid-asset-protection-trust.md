Margaret, 71, owns her house, has $250,000 in savings and a daughter named Priya. Her mother spent four years in a nursing home, and Margaret has watched the money disappear. She asks her attorney whether a trust can protect what she has. The answer is that one type can, with conditions, and the usual trust she may have heard about cannot.

This article explains the Medicaid asset protection trust, shortened to MAPT, the features that make it work, and the common reasons it does not. It belongs to our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care). Medicaid is a state-run program, so the rules about trusts and look-back details differ by state.

## What is a Medicaid asset protection trust?

A MAPT is an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) written so that its assets do not count when you apply for Medicaid. You, as the grantor, transfer assets to the trust. A trustee other than you manages them for the beneficiaries, who are usually your children. Because you no longer own or control the property, Medicaid does not count it, once the look-back period has passed.

The federal rule behind this is in 42 U.S.C. 1396p(d). In plain words, if any payment from the trust could be made to you or for your benefit, the amount available is counted as your resource. If no payment can be made to you, the trust is not counted, but the transfer into it is a gift that can cause a penalty during the look-back.

## What makes an irrevocable trust safe or unsafe?

Safe depends on how it is written and how it is used. These are the points a careful drafter checks.

| Feature | Safe for Medicaid | Unsafe for Medicaid |
|---|---|---|
| Revocability | Irrevocable, no power to undo it | You can revoke or amend freely |
| Access to principal | None for you; principal goes to others | Trustee can pay principal to you |
| Income | You may receive the income | You may take it, but income paid to you counts as income |
| Trustee | Child or independent person | You as trustee with discretion over principal |
| Right to live in the house | Allowed, as a retained right | Power to sell the house and keep the proceeds |
| Timing | Funded more than 60 months before applying | Funded last month |
| Limited power of appointment | Allowed to change who gets the principal among family | A general power to appoint to yourself |

You can keep the right to live in the home and to get income from the trust. Some trusts also keep the power to change which children inherit. You cannot spend the principal or take the house back.

## What is the difference between a Medicaid trust and a revocable living trust?

They sound alike and do opposite things. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) lets you change your mind, so Medicaid treats everything in it as yours. It is excellent for avoiding probate and handling incapacity. It does nothing for Medicaid eligibility, and in states that define the estate broadly it does not stop recovery either.

| Question | Revocable living trust | Medicaid asset protection trust |
|---|---|---|
| Can you take assets back? | Yes | No |
| Counted by Medicaid? | Yes, as your asset | Not after the look-back, if drafted correctly |
| Avoids probate? | Yes | Yes |
| Handles incapacity? | Yes, through a successor trustee | Partly; you still need a power of attorney |
| Control | Full | Limited to what the document reserves |
| Typical user | Anyone wanting a flexible plan | Someone with a real risk of needing long-term care |

Many people end up with both. A revocable trust or a will for the assets they keep, and the MAPT for the home and an agreed amount of savings. Our guide on [whether you need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) helps with the first decision.

## What are the steps to set one up?

1. **Decide what to put in.** The usual candidates are the home and some savings or investments. Keep enough outside the trust to live on and cover emergencies.
2. **Choose a trustee and successor.** Someone you trust but who is not you. Discuss it with them first.
3. **Draft the trust.** It should say who gets the income, who gets the principal, what powers you keep, and how a sale or replacement home works. It should address taxes on purpose.
4. **Sign and fund it.** For a house, that means a new deed recorded in the county. For accounts, retitling. An unsigned or unfunded trust protects nothing. See [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) for the mechanics.
5. **Start the clock.** The look-back runs from the date of each transfer. Keep records of dates and values. The [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) can help you find a date to plan from.
6. **Update the rest of the plan.** Powers of attorney, health directives and the will should match.

## What are the tradeoffs?

Families sometimes sign a MAPT because a salesperson called it a shield. It is not that simple. Real costs include:

- **Loss of control.** A trustee holds the property. If you fall out with your child, you cannot undo it.
- **Exposure to the children's problems.** Property in a trust for children can be reached, in some cases, by a trustee's creditors or a divorce, depending on how it is written.
- **The five-year wait.** If care is needed in year three, the transfer is still penalized. Some couples combine a trust with a partial gift strategy so they have a fallback.
- **Income tax and basis.** A badly drafted trust can lose the home sale exclusion or the step-up in basis. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).
- **Cost.** Drafting is more work than a simple will. Funding adds recording fees and sometimes an appraisal.
- **State differences.** Some states treat trusts, retained income and the home differently, and the rules change.

### A short example

Margaret transfers her house, valued at $340,000, to a trust naming Priya as trustee. Margaret keeps the right to live there. She keeps $90,000 in savings to live on. Four years and nine months later she has a stroke and enters a nursing home. Her attorney waits three months for the look-back to end before applying, covering the nursing home from savings and Margaret's pension and Social Security. After that, the house is not counted. Without the trust, Margaret would have faced a spend-down or a state lien. With it, she keeps the house for Priya, and the house is generally outside her estate at death, though states differ on how far recovery can reach a trust.

Had she needed care in year two, the house transfer would have created a penalty that could not easily be reversed. That is the bet she was making.

## Who should consider a MAPT?

It tends to fit people who are in their sixties or seventies, healthy now, own a home and have a family history of needing care. It fits poorly for people with limited assets, those who might need the money, or anyone who cannot trust the trustee. It also needs a living arrangement: you must be comfortable owning a home that is technically owned by a trust. For a broader look at the choices, read [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).
