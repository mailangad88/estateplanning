When a parent or spouse moves into a nursing home, the first practical question is usually about money. Who pays the bill each month? Does the check from Social Security still come? What happens to the account that pays the electric bill at the house?

The short version is that your money remains your money, but how it gets spent changes with the way the stay is paid for. This page walks through income, bank accounts and retirement money in turn, and then the special rules for a spouse who is still at home. It is part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What happens to Social Security when you enter a nursing home?

Social Security continues to be paid in full. Entering a nursing home does not reduce a retirement benefit, and the Social Security Administration does not send it to the facility. The check, or direct deposit, goes where it has always gone. What changes is what the money is used for.

On private pay, the monthly benefit simply becomes the first source of money for the bill. Gerald, 84, gets a benefit that covers roughly a third of his monthly charge, and the family draws the rest from savings. On Medicaid, the rules are different, which we cover below.

One practical problem: if Gerald can no longer manage his money and has no one named to act for him, nobody can legally deposit checks or pay the facility. The Social Security Administration can appoint a representative payee for the benefit, but that person has no power over his other accounts. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) signed beforehand avoids both problems, and our page on a [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) explains what families do without one.

## How is income treated once Medicaid pays?

A Medicaid nursing home resident generally pays nearly all of their monthly income toward the care, and Medicaid covers the rest of the bill. This is sometimes called the patient liability or share of cost. The resident keeps:

1. A personal needs allowance, set by the state. It is small.
2. Money for health insurance premiums, such as a Medicare supplement or Part B.
3. In some cases, an amount set aside for a spouse or dependent family member at home.

All pension payments, Social Security, annuity income and required IRA payments count as income. If the person's monthly income is above a certain limit in some states, they need a [qualified income trust](/learn/elder-care/income-cap-trust-miller-trust) to be eligible at all.

Here is an example that shows how this works. Dolores receives Social Security and a small pension. Her nursing home bill is far higher than her income. After the personal needs allowance and her Medicare supplement premium come out, the rest of her income goes to the facility each month. Medicaid pays the difference between that and the Medicaid rate. Dolores does not see a bill for the balance, though she does need the facility to receive her income each month without gaps.

## What happens to your bank accounts?

A bank account stays in the name of whoever owns it, and the nursing home cannot touch it. The question is what the account is for. There are three situations.

| Situation | What happens to the account |
|---|---|
| Private pay, no Medicaid | Money is spent on care until it runs low. Nothing is frozen. |
| Applying for Medicaid | The balance on the application date counts toward the state's asset limit, and statements for the past five years in most states are reviewed. |
| Receiving Medicaid | The account has to stay under the limit every month, so a big deposit, such as an inheritance, can jeopardize coverage. |

Most states have a small account limit for a single applicant, and it is quite low. Our page on [what assets are exempt from Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/what-assets-are-exempt-from-medicaid) lists what does not count, such as the home in some circumstances, one car and a prepaid funeral. Money that is over the limit has to be spent on the person's own benefit, which is covered in [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down).

### What about a joint account with a child?

Medicaid usually presumes the whole balance belongs to the applicant. If a daughter's name is on the account only so she can pay her father's bills, the full amount is still counted for him. She can show that some of the money is hers, with deposit records, but the burden is on her. The reverse mistake is common too: families add a child to an account right before the move, hoping to protect it. That is a transfer that can lead to a penalty under the [look-back rules](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

## What about a pension, an IRA or a 401(k)?

A pension is income, so it is paid toward the care. Retirement accounts are less uniform. In some states, an IRA or 401(k) is a countable asset, so it must be spent down to the limit before Medicaid pays. In others, the account is exempt when the owner takes regular required payments, and then the payments count as income. Taking a large withdrawal also creates an income tax bill, which can be a good reason to pause and plan the order of spending.

Do not cash an IRA in a panic. A person who is 85 will usually be taking required minimum distributions anyway, and a trial of a few months of private pay before an application gives a clearer picture of what the real numbers are.

## What does the spouse at home get to keep?

A spouse who is not in the nursing home is called the community spouse, and federal law gives that spouse protections so they are not left with nothing. They keep their own income in full. If their income is below a minimum set by the state, they may be allowed to receive part of the resident's income, and in some states can keep a larger share of the couple's savings than a single person could. How this works is the subject of our page on [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples).

Do not assume that, because one spouse is going into care, both of you are now poor. The rules are built so the one at home can stay in the house and pay the ordinary bills.

## What should families do before the move?

- Make sure someone has authority over the accounts, ideally an agent under a signed durable power of attorney.
- Collect five years of statements for every account, both spouses' and any joint ones.
- Keep paying the facility from the correct account, and keep receipts. A bill paid from the wrong account can cause questions later.
- Do not move money to children without advice. It rarely helps and often creates a penalty.
- Ask the facility's business office for a written breakdown of charges and who is expected to pay.

## How state law changes this

States set the personal needs allowance, the asset limits, whether an IRA counts, and whether a qualified income trust is required. Whether you live in a community property state also changes how a couple's money is viewed. Our [state guides](/estate-planning/ohio) outline where to start; the Medicaid agency has the current numbers.
