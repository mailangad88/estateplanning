Vera bought a long-term care policy at 55 and paid for it faithfully for twenty years. At 76 she gets a letter from the insurer announcing a premium increase. She is on a fixed income. Her son asks whether she should drop it. Another family has the opposite problem: a father needs help bathing and dressing, they file a claim, and the insurer says no.

Both situations are common. This page, part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care), covers the three ways long-term care insurance goes wrong, with practical steps for each. It assumes you already have a policy; if you are still deciding whether to buy one, start with [long-term care insurance and your estate plan](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance).

## The short version

- Read the actual policy, not the marketing summary. The answers are in it.
- Put every request and every reply in writing and keep copies.
- Ask the insurer for your options before you stop paying. Many people drop coverage when a cheaper version was available.
- A decision about the policy is also a Medicaid and tax decision. See an attorney before you move money.

## Why do long-term care premiums go up?

Most traditional policies are sold with a level premium, but the insurer is allowed to raise rates for a whole class of policies if it can show state regulators that its claims experience is worse than it priced for. Many insurers underestimated how long people would live, how long they would claim, and how low interest rates would stay. Older policies in particular saw large increases. State insurance departments review the requests, and the approved amounts differ from state to state.

An increase is not a personal judgment about you. It applies to everyone in the group, and your health does not matter to it. That also means it is rarely worth arguing with the insurer about the amount. The better question is what choices the increase gives you.

## What are your options when the premium goes up?

Insurers are generally required to explain your options when they send a rate notice. The usual menu looks like this:

| Option | What changes | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Pay the new premium | Nothing | You can afford it and the benefit is valuable |
| Reduce the daily or monthly benefit | Smaller payouts, lower premium | You want to keep coverage but the budget is tight |
| Lengthen the elimination period | You pay longer before benefits start | You have savings to cover the first months |
| Drop or reduce inflation protection | Benefits stop growing | Often a late-life trade, since you are closer to needing them |
| Shorten the benefit period | Coverage ends after fewer years | Trade a long tail you may not use |
| Take a paid-up or reduced-benefit option | No more premiums, smaller total benefit | You can no longer pay at all |

Ask the insurer to quote two or three of these side by side, in writing. The numbers are often better than people expect. Vera, for example, can accept a lower daily benefit and keep the policy in force for another thirty years of potential care at a premium she can afford.

## What happens if you stop paying the premiums?

The policy lapses at the end of the grace period, and you generally lose coverage, though not always everything you paid for. Two protections matter:

- **Nonforfeiture benefits.** Some policies, particularly ones where the buyer paid for this feature, convert to a paid-up policy with a smaller maximum benefit if you stop paying. Many states also require a reduced-benefit option after a large cumulative rate increase.
- **Third-party notice.** Many states let you name someone to receive a lapse notice, so a missed payment from confusion or memory loss does not end coverage. If you are the child of a policyholder, check that you are listed, and ask the insurer about reinstatement if a parent's lapse was caused by cognitive decline. Insurers commonly allow reinstatement within a limited time with proof that the policyholder could not manage their affairs.

An agent under a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) can pay premiums and file claims, which is one more reason to have one in place. Without it, the premiums stop when your parent can no longer write checks.

## What if you cannot afford the policy?

First decide whether the policy is likely to be needed. A policy a person has paid into for twenty years is an asset. Giving it up the year before a diagnosis would be a costly mistake. If the premium is the problem, work down the table above before lapsing.

If you cannot keep it in any form, the next question is how you will pay for care. The usual routes are savings, family help and eventually Medicaid. Moving assets to qualify raises the look-back rule, covered in our page on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period). Ask an attorney how a lapsed policy fits with your plan. Some states also run partnership programs that allow a person with a qualifying policy to keep more assets when applying for Medicaid, which makes some policies worth keeping for that reason alone.

## Why do insurers deny long-term care claims?

Denials usually have one of six causes, and most can be fixed.

1. **The benefit trigger is not met.** Policies usually require that the insured cannot perform a set number of basic daily activities, commonly two of six, or has a cognitive impairment, as certified by a licensed health professional. A doctor's note that says "frail" is not enough. It needs to describe the activities.
2. **The elimination period is still running.** The claim is valid but not yet payable.
3. **The care is not covered.** The caregiver is a family member the policy excludes, or the facility does not meet the policy's definition.
4. **Missing paperwork.** No plan of care, no provider license, a late notice of claim.
5. **A misstatement on the application.** If the insurer says you left out a condition, this can be serious inside the contestability period, usually two years. After that, it generally has to prove fraud.
6. **A genuine dispute about the facts,** such as whether Dad can really dress himself.

## How do you appeal a denial?

Work through the steps in order.

1. Read the denial letter for the stated reason and the deadline to appeal. Calendar the deadline.
2. Ask for the complete claim file and the policy, including any riders.
3. Ask the doctor to write a letter that tracks the policy's wording, naming the specific activities the insured cannot do and the cognitive findings if relevant.
4. Gather care-provider records, therapy notes and a daily log kept by the family.
5. File the written internal appeal. State the policy number, the facts, and what you want paid.
6. If the insurer upholds the denial, ask whether an independent external review is available. Many states offer one.
7. File a complaint with your state insurance department. It costs nothing and insurers respond to it.
8. Talk to an attorney before the final deadline, particularly if a large claim is at stake.

An agent or family member needs the insured's signed authorization to speak with the insurer. A [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) and a power of attorney solve that problem before the claim exists.

## How does a denied or lapsed policy affect Medicaid planning?

If the policy fails, the family may need Medicaid sooner than planned, and the five-year look-back (60 months in most states) applies to transfers made in the meantime. A common error is to move money to a child while a claim is still being fought. Wait. A successful appeal may pay the bills outright, and the transfer will have created a penalty for nothing. When Medicaid is a real prospect, read [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and our look-back tool.
