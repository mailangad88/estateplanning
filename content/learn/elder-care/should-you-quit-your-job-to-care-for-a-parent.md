It rarely starts as a decision. A mother has a fall, you take a few days off, then a few weeks. Soon you are driving her to appointments in the afternoons and answering pharmacy calls at your desk. Eventually someone says, usually a sibling, that you might as well just stay home with her.

Before you give notice, it helps to take the question apart. This page covers the money side: what leaving work really costs you, what leave and pay options exist first, and how to handle the cash you spend on a parent. It sits within our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care). It does not cover how to draft a caregiver agreement; our page on [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) covers that in depth.

## The short version

- Leaving a job is hard to reverse. Use leave, flexibility or part-time work first if you can.
- If you do stop working, ask your parent to pay you under a written agreement, and expect scrutiny.
- Never become a bank account co-owner just because it is convenient.
- Everything you spend should be recorded, whether or not you expect repayment.

## What does it really cost you to quit?

The salary is the obvious loss. The less obvious ones tend to be larger over the years.

| Cost | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Lost income | Often the biggest item; add up months or years, not weeks |
| Retirement contributions | No employer match and no contributions of your own while you are out |
| Social Security record | Fewer years of earnings can lower your future benefit |
| Employer health insurance | You may need an individual plan or a spouse's coverage |
| Career re-entry | Gaps are hard to explain; some fields move fast |
| Savings drawn down | You may spend your own retirement money to cover your own bills |

Caregiving also tends to last longer than the first estimate. A short rehab can turn into years of dementia care. Ask yourself what you will do if it runs three years, and whether you would still have the means to return to work afterward.

Think about the question this way: a caregiver who leaves at 52 and returns at 57 has lost five years of peak earnings plus the growth on what they would have saved. That number belongs in the family conversation, next to the cost of paid help.

## What should you try before resigning?

Several options let you keep a foot in the workforce.

1. **Federal family leave.** Under the Family and Medical Leave Act, eligible employees at covered employers can take up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a year to care for a parent with a serious health condition. Coverage depends on employer size and your tenure, so ask human resources whether you qualify.
2. **State paid family leave.** Some states provide partial wage replacement for caregiving leave. Check your state labor department.
3. **Flexible schedule or remote work.** Many employers will agree to a trial period.
4. **Respite and adult day programs.** The area agency on aging can point to local programs that give you working hours back.
5. **Paid help for part of the week.** A home health aide for a few shifts costs less than a salary lost.
6. **Share the load.** Splitting tasks among siblings turns one person's crisis into several people's contribution.

Our broader page on [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) covers the family conversation, including the money.

## Can your parent pay you?

Yes, if it is structured properly. A parent can pay a child for care under a written personal care agreement, signed before the work begins, at a rate that matches what an agency or aide would charge, with a log of services. That protects you in two ways. It shows your siblings that the money was payment for work. And it protects your parent's eligibility if Medicaid is needed later, because Medicaid reviews transfers for 60 months in most states and may treat payments with no agreement as gifts.

Three cautions.

- Payments are income to you, and your parent may owe household employer taxes. See [tax breaks when you pay for a parent's care](/learn/elder-care/tax-breaks-when-you-pay-for-a-parents-care).
- If you are also your parent's agent, paying yourself needs express authority in the power of attorney. Our page on [power of attorney gifts and self-dealing](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts) explains why.
- Pay set by the parent for loving care but far above market looks like a gift to a regulator. Do not let affection set the rate.

A parent who qualifies for a state Medicaid self-directed program, or a veteran with Aid and Attendance, may have a better source of pay than their own savings.

## Should you pay a parent's bills and be reimbursed later?

It is very common for a child to cover a pharmacy bill, a home repair or a month of aide fees from their own account. It can work well with a little discipline.

1. **Keep every receipt,** and note what the money bought and for whom.
2. **Agree on repayment in writing,** even by email, before you spend large sums.
3. **Reimburse yourself by check or transfer** from your parent's account, with a note referencing the receipts.
4. **Tell your siblings.** Quiet reimbursements breed suspicion.
5. **Do not take repayment in cash.** A cash trail looks like a gift in one direction or the other.

If you hold a power of attorney, reimbursing yourself for real expenses is generally allowed, but the agent should still document it and pay in the open. If your payments were instead a gift from you to your parent, you might owe nothing back, and that is a fine choice as long as everyone understands it. Muddy intentions are the problem, not generosity.

## Should you be added to a parent's bank account?

Often it feels simplest, and often it is a mistake. A joint account means:

- The money is exposed to your creditors, a lawsuit against you, or your divorce.
- At your parent's death the funds may pass to you outright, which can override what the will says and leave siblings out.
- Adding you may be treated as a gift to you, which matters for Medicaid.
- If you are not careful, the bank may freeze the account on one owner's incapacity or death.

An agent under a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) can pay bills with none of those side effects. Some banks also have a limited access arrangement or "convenience" account, though the paperwork should say clearly that the helper does not own the money. The reasoning behind that advice is in [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

## How do you avoid a fight with your siblings?

Caregiving without a written understanding is one of the most reliable sources of sibling conflict. Typical stories: one sibling feels they gave up a career and everyone else gave nothing, or the others suspect the caregiver of taking money. Few of these fights are about the amount.

Habits that help:

- Hold a short family meeting before you quit, and write down who agrees to what.
- Agree on how much the caregiver is paid, by whom, and for how long.
- Share a monthly summary of the parent's spending.
- Set a review date, such as six months out, to ask whether the arrangement still works.
- If the parent wants to reward the caregiver more at death, put it in the will or trust, not in a handshake.

The aftermath of a bad arrangement is covered in [siblings fighting over a parent's estate](/learn/what-if/siblings-fight-over-a-parents-estate).

## A short worksheet

Before deciding, put numbers on these questions.

- What is my take-home pay, and what happens to health coverage?
- How many hours of care does my parent need each week, and what would a paid aide cost for those hours?
- What can my parent pay from income and savings, and for how long?
- Would a Medicaid or veterans benefit help, and how soon?
- Who else in the family can share the work or the cost?
- What is my plan to return to work, and by when?

If the numbers show that paid help is cheaper than your lost pay, the family may decide that hiring help and staying employed is kinder to everyone, including your parent.
