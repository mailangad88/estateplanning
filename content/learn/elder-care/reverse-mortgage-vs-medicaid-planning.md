Delia, 76, owns a house worth far more than the rest of her savings put together. A neighbor told her to take out a reverse mortgage and use it to pay for an aide, and someone else said it would ruin any chance at Medicaid. Both people were partly right. A reverse mortgage can fund care at home, and it can interfere with the very plan that would pay for a nursing home later, depending on what happens next.

This page explains how a reverse mortgage works, how Medicaid sees the money, what a move into a nursing home does to the loan, and how it compares with other ways to turn a house into care. It sits within our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What is a reverse mortgage?

It is a loan against the equity in a home you live in, available to owners 62 or older, where you make no required monthly payments. The balance grows as interest and fees are added, and the loan is repaid when the last borrower dies, sells, or moves out permanently. The most common type, a Home Equity Conversion Mortgage, is insured by the Federal Housing Administration and requires counseling with an approved counselor before you can sign.

You can take the money in several ways: a lump sum, a monthly payment, a line of credit that grows if unused, or a mix. You stay on the deed and you still owe property taxes, homeowner's insurance and upkeep. Missing those can put the loan in default, which is how many older borrowers lose the home.

The loan is non-recourse for the common federally insured type. If the balance ends up larger than the house is worth, the lender takes the house and cannot pursue the heirs or the rest of the estate for the difference.

## How does Medicaid treat reverse mortgage money?

Medicaid sees the advances as loan proceeds. A loan is not income, so a lump sum or monthly draw does not count against the income cap, and it does not make you "too rich" for long-term care coverage in the month you receive it.

The trouble comes one month later. Cash left in a checking account on the first day of the month is a countable asset, whatever its source. A $40,000 lump sum sitting in the bank can disqualify Delia for any program with an asset limit. Monthly advances she does not spend pile up the same way.

Three habits keep the cash from causing trouble:

- **Draw only what you will spend.** A line of credit has the advantage that money stays in the house until you need it, and it is not a bank balance.
- **Spend it on real needs.** Paying an aide, home repairs and medical bills are fair-value purchases. Handing proceeds to a child is a gift subject to the [look-back](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).
- **Keep receipts.** The caseworker will ask where the money went, and the loan statements show the draw dates.

If Delia also receives Supplemental Security Income, that program has its own rules for loan proceeds and cash on hand, so she should check them before she draws.

## What happens to the loan if you move into a nursing home?

The loan is generally due once you no longer live in the home as your principal residence. For the federally insured loan, a borrower who is absent for more than 12 consecutive months for a medical reason, such as a nursing home stay, triggers repayment. The lender will send notices, and there is a short window to respond.

This is the central conflict. Medicaid planning often keeps the home exempt on the theory that you may return, or that your spouse lives there. A reverse mortgage works only while you live in the house. If Delia breaks her hip and spends 14 months in a facility, the lender can call the loan, the family must sell or refinance, and the sale proceeds become countable money for Medicaid.

If a spouse is still living in the house, the situation is better. An eligible spouse who was not a borrower can generally stay in the home after the borrower enters care or dies, provided the loan paperwork named that spouse correctly and the taxes and insurance are paid. Check this when the loan is opened, not after.

## How does a reverse mortgage compare with the other options?

The right tool depends on whether you expect to stay home or to need a facility.

| Approach | Best for | Main cost or limit |
|---|---|---|
| Reverse mortgage | Paying for care at home while you intend to stay | Upfront fees, growing balance, loan due when you leave |
| Selling the house | Someone moving to a facility anyway | Gives up the home; proceeds count for Medicaid |
| Spending down on fair-value needs | Reaching Medicaid eligibility | Needs savings, not just a house |
| Home-care waiver | Someone who needs care at home but meets Medicaid income and asset rules | Waiting lists in many states; see the [home care waiver](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver) |
| Irrevocable trust or caregiver-child transfer | Protecting the house for heirs | Five-year look-back; loss of control |

A reverse mortgage and a Medicaid asset protection plan generally pull in opposite directions. The first uses up the equity so you can live on it. The second preserves the house for family. A family that has not yet decided which goal comes first will find the choice made for them by the loan balance.

## What does a reverse mortgage do to the estate?

It shrinks it. Every month of interest and the mortgage insurance premium is added to the balance, so a house with $400,000 of equity can be mostly spoken for after a decade or more of borrowing. What remains after the lender is paid goes to heirs under the will, trust or [state default rules](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

The practical points for heirs:

1. **Notify the lender.** When the borrower dies, the loan becomes due, and heirs usually have a short period to respond and several months to sell or refinance, with extensions available in some cases.
2. **Ask for a payoff figure and an appraisal.** The balance is not always what the heirs expect.
3. **Decide whether to keep the house.** Heirs can keep it by paying the balance, or a lesser percentage of the appraised value on federally insured loans. See [mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).
4. **Remember estate recovery.** If the borrower was also on Medicaid, the state's claim ranks after the lender in the sale proceeds. See [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery).

A reverse mortgage also complicates plans to leave the house to a child. The child may need to pay off the loan or sell. If one child wants the house and another wants the money, the debt leaves less room to equalize, a theme in our article on [leaving your home to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children).

## When is a reverse mortgage a reasonable choice?

It is worth considering when four things are true: you own the home outright or nearly so, you want to stay there, your health makes a nursing home move unlikely in the next few years, and you have no practical alternative for paying for help. A widow in good health who needs a few hours of help a day, or a couple who want to pay for repairs that make the house safe, fits that picture.

It is a poor fit when a facility move is likely, when you expect to need Medicaid soon, when family members want to inherit the house, or when the borrower cannot keep up with taxes and insurance. In those cases the cost and the call on the loan outweigh the benefit.

Also be careful about who sells it. A reverse mortgage is a financial product with commissions, and families report pressure to take larger amounts than they need. Counseling is required for a reason; use it, and bring a trusted adult child or an attorney. A reverse mortgage taken by an agent for a parent also needs authority in the power of attorney, and many documents do not give it.
