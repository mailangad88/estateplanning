The planning conversation for a couple usually centers on what happens when the spouse at home runs out of money. The reverse event is less discussed and, in my experience, causes more damage: the healthy spouse dies first. Suddenly the person in the nursing home is a widow or widower, a new heir and, in the eyes of Medicaid, someone who just received money.

This page walks through that sequence. It builds on [how Medicaid works for a married couple](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples), which explains the protections that apply while both spouses are alive. Here the question is what changes when one of them dies, and which documents decide the answer. It belongs to our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What does Medicaid do when the healthy spouse dies?

Medicaid treats the death as an event that needs a fresh look at the surviving spouse's eligibility. The community spouse resource allowance, which let the healthy spouse hold a protected share of the savings, falls away. From then on, the person in care is a single applicant with a single applicant's asset limit.

Three things then matter: what the surviving spouse inherits, how income changes, and whether the home stays protected. Each is shaped by paperwork the healthy spouse signed years earlier, often without thinking about it.

Here is a typical case. Walt, 82, is in a nursing home on Medicaid. His wife Janet, 80, lived at home and held about $140,000 in savings, a 401(k) and the house. Janet's old will, written 20 years ago, leaves everything to Walt. She dies in March. If nothing is done, Walt inherits the savings and the 401(k) outright, and a month later the caseworker sees that his countable assets are far over the limit. His Medicaid is closed. Now he pays the nursing home privately until the money is nearly gone, and the children cannot reverse it.

## How does an inheritance affect the spouse in care?

An inheritance is treated as an asset the day the surviving spouse has a right to it, not the day it arrives. Money Walt is entitled to counts even if he never touches it. Passing the assets through a will, a beneficiary form, a joint account or a state's default rules all lead to the same place: they become his.

| How the money would pass | Result for the spouse on Medicaid |
|---|---|
| Will leaves it outright to the spouse | Counts as an asset; coverage can end |
| No will; state law gives the spouse a share | Same result, plus delays while the estate is opened |
| IRA or 401(k) names the spouse | Counts as the spouse's retirement account |
| Joint account with right of survivorship | Passes to the survivor and counts |
| Will leaves it to a trust for the spouse's sole benefit | Not counted if the trust is drafted correctly |
| Will leaves it to children, spouse does not claim a share | Possible transfer penalty if a claim was available |

The question of what a nursing home resident can inherit without losing benefits is a wider one, covered in [whether an inheritance affects Medicaid or SSI](/learn/after-a-death/does-an-inheritance-affect-medicaid-or-ssi).

## Can the healthy spouse leave the money to a trust instead?

Yes, and that is the standard fix. Federal law exempts a transfer to a trust held solely for the benefit of the spouse. Janet's will can name a [testamentary trust](/learn/trusts/testamentary-trusts), created at her death, with a trustee such as a daughter who spends on Walt's extras: a better room, clothing, dental work, a wheelchair, visits and a funeral plan. Medicaid still pays the nursing home, and Walt keeps his coverage.

A few cautions apply:

- **The wording matters.** The trust has to be solely for the spouse's benefit, with distributions at the trustee's discretion and no right for Walt to demand money. Boilerplate "supplemental" language from an online form may not meet your state's standard.
- **Payback may be required.** Depending on how the trust is built and the state, the remainder at Walt's death might have to repay the state for his care. Ask what happens to what is left and who gets it.
- **The same applies to other designations.** The IRA, the life insurance and any payable-on-death account should name the trust, not Walt. See [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) for the tax rules on naming a trust.

The reverse is just as true. If Walt is the one holding the money and Janet is the one in care, the same planning belongs in his documents.

## What about the elective share?

In most states a surviving spouse has a right to claim a minimum portion of the deceased spouse's estate even when the will says otherwise. That right is the elective share, or a similar spousal allowance, and a few states have a different name for it. It usually has to be claimed within a short time after death.

This matters for Medicaid in two ways. First, a spouse in a nursing home who is left out of a will, or left a very small share, may be expected by the agency to claim the share. If nobody does, the agency can treat the failure as a transfer of an asset for less than fair value and impose a penalty. Second, a guardian or agent acting for Walt has to consider it, because the right is his and it expires.

Families who want the children to inherit rather than the surviving spouse should not simply write the spouse out. The elective share and the Medicaid penalty can undo that. A trust for the spouse is usually the cleaner way to keep the money in the family while keeping benefits. The question of leaving a spouse out of an estate generally is covered in [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).

## What happens to income when a spouse dies?

Income shifts in a few ways, and each is worth reporting.

1. **The pension may change or stop.** Many pensions pay a reduced survivor amount. If Walt was entitled to a joint-and-survivor benefit, it is reported as his income.
2. **Social Security may go up.** A widow or widower can take the larger of their own benefit or the deceased spouse's. Walt's higher benefit goes toward his cost of care, with his small personal needs allowance kept.
3. **The community spouse's income allowance ends.** There is no one at home to support, so the share of Walt's income that used to go to Janet stays in his care payment.
4. **The state asks for a report.** Medicaid usually requires a change in circumstances to be reported within a set number of days, with penalties for silence.

The [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) article explains survivor benefits and the one-time payment.

## What happens to the house?

While a spouse lives in it, the home is exempt, so it stays protected as long as Janet is there. If Janet dies first and Walt is the surviving spouse in the nursing home, the home is no longer a spouse's residence. Walt's own home exemption depends on whether he intends to return and on the equity limit. Often the house must be sold or transferred to a person who qualifies for an exempt transfer, such as a caregiver child. See [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).

If Walt dies first and Janet stays in the house, the state's claim is postponed until she dies. After that the state can reach what Walt owned at death, under your state's definition of the estate. Some states reach only probate assets, while expanded-estate states can reach assets that avoid probate, including a share of a joint home. Our [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) page sets out what each state can claim.

## What should a couple do now?

Most of this is avoidable if the healthy spouse's documents are updated while both people are alive. An hour of review can save a Medicaid case.

1. **Read the will.** Does it leave everything to the spouse? Replace it with a will that routes money to a sole-benefit trust.
2. **Check every beneficiary form.** Retirement plans, life insurance, annuities and payable-on-death accounts.
3. **Look at how the accounts are titled.** Joint accounts pass to the survivor automatically. Move them if the survivor will be the one on Medicaid.
4. **Prepare the same documents for both.** Each spouse should have a durable power of attorney and a healthcare directive. The guidance in [estate planning after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies) covers the survivor's side.
5. **Keep a note of the state's reporting rule.** Know who to call and how soon after a death.
