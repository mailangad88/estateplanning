Walter, 84, moved into a nursing home after a fall. The first month's bill came to the home's private rate, which his daughter Dina paid from his savings. Walter's account will not last two years at that rate. The business office has mentioned "converting to Medicaid" and Dina has no idea what that means for the bill, the room or her father's income.

This page lays out private pay vs Medicaid in a nursing home in plain terms, and it belongs to our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care): what each costs, what changes the day Medicaid begins, and what to ask before a parent signs an admission agreement. If you are still working out how to get through the gap before approval, read [paying for a nursing home while waiting for Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/paying-for-a-nursing-home-while-waiting-for-medicaid) alongside this one.

## What does private pay mean in a nursing home?

Private pay means the resident, or someone using the resident's money, pays the facility's own daily rate. Savings, Social Security, a pension, long-term care insurance benefits and sale proceeds all fall in this category. Medicare is a different thing. It covers a limited stay in a skilled nursing facility after a qualifying hospital stay, and it does not cover long-term custodial care. The [Medicare long-term care article](/learn/elder-care/what-medicare-covers-for-long-term-care) explains where that coverage ends.

The private rate is the highest the home charges for the room type. It often comes with extras billed separately, such as incontinence supplies, salon visits or a phone line. Ask for the rate sheet and a list of what is and is not included before moving in, not after the first invoice.

## What changes when Medicaid starts paying?

Three things change: who pays the home, how much comes from the resident's income, and how the resident's money is treated.

| | Private pay | Medicaid |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets the daily rate | The nursing home | The state Medicaid program |
| Who pays | Resident or family from savings and income | Resident's income plus Medicaid for the balance |
| Resident's spending money | Whatever the resident has | A personal needs allowance, set by the state |
| Savings limit | None | Strict asset limit, usually a few thousand dollars for a single person |
| Extras (salon, phone, upgraded room) | Billed to the resident | Resident pays from the allowance |
| Bed | Any available bed | A Medicaid-certified bed |

Walter gets $2,400 a month from Social Security and a small pension. Suppose, for illustration only, his state allows him to keep $60 a month and deduct a $175 health insurance premium. He would hand the home about $2,165 each month, and Medicaid would pay the home the difference between that and the state rate. His savings no longer pay the bill, and he cannot hold more than the asset limit. The [income and bank accounts article](/learn/elder-care/income-and-bank-accounts-in-a-nursing-home) shows where each source of income goes.

If Walter were married and his wife Joan still lived at home, she could keep a share of the couple's income and savings under the spousal impoverishment rules. That changes the arithmetic a great deal, and [how Medicaid works for a married couple](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples) explains it.

## Why does a Medicaid-certified bed matter?

A bed can only be paid for by Medicaid if the facility and that bed are certified. Some homes certify every bed. Others certify only some, or take Medicaid only for people who were already living there when their money ran out.

That policy shapes a family's choice more than the star rating does. A home that accepts only private-pay admissions and has no certified beds can force a move at exactly the moment a resident is least able to handle one. A move at 87 with dementia can do real harm.

At admission, ask these questions and write down the answers:

1. Is the bed I would be in Medicaid-certified?
2. How many residents here are on Medicaid now?
3. If my parent's savings run out, will the home keep my parent in the same room if Medicaid begins to pay?
4. Is there a minimum private-pay period before the home converts someone?
5. What is the notice period if the home wants to move a resident?

If you cannot get a clear answer, that is useful information about the home.

## Can the nursing home ask me to sign for my parent?

The home can ask, and many do, but the federal nursing home rules do not let a facility make a personal promise to pay by a third party a condition of admission. A child can sign as the parent's agent under a power of attorney, which is a limited signature that makes the parent responsible. A child who signs as a "responsible party" in the personal sense might be taking on the bill. Our article on [whether a nursing home can make your children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) explains the difference and the common wording traps.

Read the signature line before you sign. If it says the signer will pay from personal funds, cross it out or have an attorney review it first.

## What do families get wrong about the switch?

Four mistakes come up again and again in our work with families.

- **Waiting too long to apply.** Medicaid can reach back for some bills, but not forever. Apply when the money is clearly heading toward the limit, not after it is gone. The [application walkthrough](/learn/elder-care/how-to-apply-for-medicaid-for-a-parent) lists what a caseworker will ask for.
- **Spending on gifts to "help" the process.** A gift in the last five years can trigger a penalty. See [the look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) before anyone moves money.
- **Leaving the power of attorney too narrow.** An agent who cannot deal with Medicaid, change accounts or create a qualified income trust cannot finish the job.
- **Assuming the home will be taken away at once.** Estate recovery usually comes after death, not on admission. It varies by state and is covered in [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery).

## What rights does a resident keep either way?

A nursing home that takes Medicare or Medicaid must follow federal resident rights, and those rights do not depend on who pays. A resident can see their own records, keep personal belongings, be free from restraints used for convenience, receive visitors and complain without retaliation. The home must give written notice and a reason before it discharges or transfers a resident, and the resident can appeal.

Nonpayment is one of the allowed reasons for discharge, which is why the order of events matters. A family that files a complete Medicaid application on time and keeps the home informed is in a far stronger position than one that stops paying and hopes. If the home sends a discharge notice, do not ignore it. The appeal deadline is short, and the long-term care ombudsman can help you write one. Our article on [choosing a nursing home and knowing your rights](/learn/elder-care/choosing-a-nursing-home-and-knowing-your-rights) covers the rights in more detail.

## How does this work in Illinois and New Jersey?

Both states run Medicaid long-term care programs with their own application, income rules and facility lists. In Illinois, our guide to [nursing home eligibility and planning in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) gives the local details. In New Jersey, read [Medicaid and nursing home planning in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning). Each state also has a long-term care ombudsman, a free advocate for residents, whose office can tell you about a home's complaint history before you commit.
