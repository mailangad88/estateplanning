Most families believe Medicare will pay for a nursing home. Many find out otherwise on the day a hospital social worker says, "Medicare will cover about three weeks, and then we need to talk about what's next." That is a hard conversation to have in a hallway, with a parent who cannot go home.

This page explains what Medicare really pays for in a nursing facility, in assisted living and at home, where the coverage stops, and what families usually do when it does. It is one part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## Does Medicare pay for a nursing home?

Medicare pays for a stay in a skilled nursing facility only when the person needs skilled care after a hospital stay. It does not pay for a nursing home where someone simply lives because they cannot manage alone. The difference between skilled and custodial care drives almost every answer on this page.

Skilled care means services that need a nurse or therapist: wound care, IV medicine, physical therapy after a hip replacement, speech therapy after a stroke. Custodial care means help with the daily basics: bathing, dressing, eating, getting to the toilet, being supervised because of memory loss. Custodial care is what most elderly people need over the long run, and Medicare does not cover it anywhere.

### What are the rules for the skilled nursing benefit?

A person has to meet each of these conditions:

1. Be enrolled in Medicare Part A.
2. Have been admitted as an inpatient to a hospital for at least three days in a row, not counting the day of discharge.
3. Enter a Medicare-certified facility, generally within 30 days of leaving the hospital.
4. Need daily skilled care that a doctor has ordered, for a condition connected to the hospital stay.

If all four are met, original Medicare pays in full for the first 20 days. From day 21 through day 100, the person pays a daily coinsurance set by Medicare each year (you can find the current figure on medicare.gov). After day 100, Medicare pays nothing for that benefit period.

In practice, many people leave before day 100 because the skilled need ends. A woman who has a stroke and gets therapy for six weeks might use 42 days. Few reach 100 days.

### The three-day rule and observation status

Here is where families get caught. A hospital can keep a person for several nights as an outpatient under "observation." Those nights do not count toward the three days. Ask early, and keep asking: "Is Dad admitted as an inpatient, or under observation?" If he is under observation, you can ask the doctor to review the status, and you can appeal later, but the better time to raise it is during the stay.

Some Medicare Advantage plans waive the three-day rule. Original Medicare does not.

## Does Medicare pay for assisted living?

No. Medicare does not pay for room and board in assisted living, and it does not pay for the personal care staff there. Assisted living is mostly paid privately, through long-term care insurance, or in some states through a Medicaid waiver that covers services but not rent. Our page on [Medicaid home care waivers](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver) explains how that works.

Medicare will still pay for medical care a resident receives there, the same way it does at home: doctor visits, therapy and hospital care. A visiting nurse who comes to the facility under a home health plan can be covered. The apartment, meals and help getting dressed are not.

## Does Medicare pay for home health aides?

Medicare pays for part-time home health when a doctor certifies that the person is homebound and needs skilled care. Homebound does not mean bedridden. It means leaving the house takes a major effort and happens only for things like medical appointments, church or an adult day program.

When those conditions are met, Medicare can pay for these services.

- Skilled nursing visits, usually a few times a week.
- Physical, occupational or speech therapy.
- A home health aide for personal care, but only alongside skilled services.
- Some medical supplies and equipment, with a coinsurance.

It does not pay for 24-hour care, full-time aides, homemaker help, meals delivered to the home or a companion. Lorraine, 81, has had two falls and lives alone. Her doctor orders a nurse and a physical therapist twice a week for a month. Medicare pays. When the month ends and she still needs someone to help her shower and cook, Medicare stops, and the family has to find another way.

## What does Medigap cover?

Medigap is supplemental insurance sold by private companies to people on original Medicare. Most Medigap plans cover the daily coinsurance for days 21 through 100 in a skilled nursing facility. They do not extend the benefit beyond 100 days, and they do not cover custodial care. If your parent has a Medigap policy, it can soften the cost of a rehab stay. It will not pay for a long-term nursing home stay.

| Type of care | Original Medicare | Medigap | Medicaid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rehab in a nursing facility, up to 100 days | Yes, with conditions and copays after day 20 | Usually covers the copays | Yes, if eligible |
| Long-term custodial care in a nursing home | No | No | Yes, if eligible |
| Assisted living rent and board | No | No | Usually no |
| Part-time skilled home health | Yes, if homebound | Covers some coinsurance | Yes, if eligible |
| Full-time personal care aide at home | No | No | Sometimes, through a waiver |
| Hospice | Yes | Not needed | Yes, if eligible |

## What happens when Medicare stops paying?

The family has four realistic routes, and which one fits depends on money and health.

1. **Pay privately.** Savings, retirement accounts and a home sale cover the bills for a time. This is how most people begin, and the [Medicaid spend-down rules](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down) describe what a person can keep.
2. **Use long-term care insurance,** if there is a policy. Read the benefit triggers and the elimination period, which we cover in [long-term care insurance](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance).
3. **Apply for Medicaid.** Medicaid does pay for custodial nursing home care for people who meet its financial and medical rules. See [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).
4. **Go home with services,** whether paid by a waiver, by the family or by a private caregiver, as described in [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

A gap often opens between the last Medicare day and the first Medicaid day. We address that in [paying for a nursing home while waiting for Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/paying-for-a-nursing-home-while-waiting-for-medicaid). If your parent is in the hospital now, start the planning conversation this week, not when the notice arrives.

## How do you appeal when Medicare ends early?

Every skilled nursing facility must give written notice before ending Medicare-covered services. That notice explains how to ask for a fast review from the Medicare quality improvement organization, and the request usually must be made by noon of the day before coverage ends. While the review is pending, the person generally cannot be billed for the disputed days. Families win some of these appeals, especially when the therapy notes show continuing need.

Keep a folder with the notice, the therapy notes you can obtain and the names of everyone you spoke to. The person's health care agent can file the appeal, which is one more reason to have a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) signed beforehand.

## How state law changes this

Medicare is federal, so the core rules are the same everywhere. The state matters for what comes next: whether Medicaid has a waiver for assisted living, how it treats the home, and how long the application takes. Look at your [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) for a starting point, then confirm current rules with the state Medicaid agency.
