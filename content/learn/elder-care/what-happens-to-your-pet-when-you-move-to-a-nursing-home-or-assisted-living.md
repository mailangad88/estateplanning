Your mother, Joan, is 84 and about to move into assisted living. She has Biscuit, a twelve-year-old beagle mix who sleeps on her bed. The family has spent weeks on the move, the finances and the doctors. Nobody has asked the one question Joan keeps asking: "Who is going to take Biscuit?"

That question deserves a real answer, and it has a legal side that families rarely think about. This article covers what facilities allow, how to pick and prepare a caregiver for the animal, when a pet trust makes sense, and how the money for the pet interacts with Medicaid. For the wider picture of paying for care, see our [elder care and Medicaid planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## Do assisted living and nursing homes allow pets?

Assisted living communities often allow pets, and nursing homes sometimes do, but every facility writes its own rules. There is no general right to bring an animal into a long-term care facility, and no general ban either.

What you will usually find:

| Setting | How pets are usually handled | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Independent or senior apartment | Often allowed, with a deposit or monthly fee and size limits | Weight limit, number of pets, breed rules |
| Assisted living | Frequently allowed for small dogs and cats if the resident can care for them or has help | Who walks the dog if the resident cannot, and what happens after a hospital stay |
| Memory care | Rare for a resident's own pet; therapy animals are more common | Whether visits are allowed |
| Nursing home | Resident pets are uncommon; many have visiting-animal programs and a house cat or bird | Whether family can bring the pet for scheduled visits |

Ask for the policy in writing and ask the second question too: what happens when you can no longer look after the animal yourself? Some communities require the resident to name a person who will remove the pet if care becomes too much. A pet that arrives without that plan is a problem the staff will raise within weeks.

Assisted living often costs extra. A monthly pet fee and a deposit are common, and these are private expenses even if some of your care is paid by a waiver program. Our guide to [who pays for assisted living, memory care and hospice](/learn/elder-care/assisted-living-memory-care-and-hospice-who-pays) explains why the housing portion is usually private pay.

## Who should take the pet?

Pick a person, not "the family." Ask them directly, ask a second person as a backup, and write both names down. This is the same advice we give about pets in an estate plan, and our article on [choosing a pet caretaker and a backup](/learn/property-and-assets/choosing-a-pet-caretaker-and-a-backup-in-your-estate-plan) goes through it in detail. The care-facility version has three extra points.

**Timing.** The decision is urgent before the move, not after death. Joan's daughter, Renee, may be able to take Biscuit for a trial week while Joan settles in. If Joan's new community allows a small dog, the family can decide later whether the move is permanent.

**Visits.** A pet that goes to a relative's home does not have to vanish from the owner's life. Many residents do better when the dog or cat comes on a regular visit, and many facilities welcome that. Ask whether visits need a vaccination record and a leash or carrier.

**Honesty about age and health.** A twelve-year-old dog may live two more years, a parrot may live thirty. A cat with kidney disease needs daily medication. Tell the caregiver what they are agreeing to. People say yes out of love and then struggle quietly.

## What should be in writing?

A one-page letter does more than most families expect. It does not need a lawyer, though an attorney can fold it into your plan. Include:

1. The pet's name, age, microchip number and the vet's name and phone number.
2. Feeding schedule, medications, allergies and quirks (Biscuit hates the car and will not eat from a metal bowl).
3. The name of the primary caregiver and the backup, with phone numbers.
4. What you want if the pet outlives the caregiver's ability or willingness, such as a named rescue.
5. A small budget and who holds it, with a statement that the caregiver may spend it on food, vet care and boarding.
6. Permission for the caregiver to authorize emergency vet treatment.

Give a copy to the vet clinic, the caregiver, your power of attorney agent and the facility's social worker. The letter is not a contract, but it shows intent, and it takes the guesswork out of a stressful day. If you want it enforced, move to a pet trust, covered below.

## Should you use a pet trust?

A pet trust is a legal arrangement in which a trustee holds money and a caregiver looks after the animal, with instructions the trustee must follow. Both [Illinois](/learn/illinois/pet-trusts-in-illinois) and [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/pet-trusts-in-new-jersey) have statutes that allow them, and our general article on [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) explains how they work.

A pet trust is not always necessary. A pet trust usually earns its cost in these cases:

- the animal is young or long-lived, and years of care are expected.
- care will cost real money, such as a horse, or a dog with a chronic condition.
- you do not trust the family to agree, or the caregiver is not a close relative.
- you want someone other than the caregiver to hold the money and check on the animal.

For a ten-year-old cat with a friendly niece waiting, a letter and a few thousand dollars set aside may be plenty. For a young dog who may live fourteen more years, the trust is usually better.

There are two ways to create one. A trust that begins at death is built into your will and costs less to set up, but does nothing while you are alive. A trust that starts now, funded with a modest amount, protects the animal from the day you move. The second choice is the one that interacts with Medicaid.

## How does Medicaid treat money set aside for a pet?

Pet-related spending can be a transfer for less than fair value, and in most states Medicaid reviews five years of bank records when you apply for long-term care coverage. Our guide to the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains the rule and how penalties are calculated.

What this means in practice:

- **Paying a vet or buying food yourself is normal spending.** Receipts for veterinary care, food, grooming and boarding for your own pet are ordinary expenses, not gifts.
- **Handing a lump sum to a friend "for the dog" can be a gift.** A caseworker may see a $6,000 transfer to a person with no record of what it was for and treat it as a penalty-causing transfer.
- **Funding a pet trust during life can be treated as a transfer** unless the trust is drafted so the money is spent on the animal and reasonably sized. Reasonableness matters: a trust of $5,000 for a cat looks very different from $200,000.
- **A trust created in your will starts after you die.** It does not affect your eligibility, but the state may be able to file an estate recovery claim first, depending on your state, as described in [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery).

If you expect to apply for Medicaid within a few years, talk to an attorney before you move money. The goal is not to avoid paying for the pet. It is to pay for the pet in a way that leaves an honest paper trail.

## What about your agent and your power of attorney?

Your agent under a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) will often be the person who pays the vet and the boarding bill. Many forms do not mention pets at all, and a cautious agent may hesitate, particularly if siblings are watching the account. A sentence authorizing "reasonable expenses for the care of my pets" removes the doubt. Our article on [limiting an agent's powers and adding safeguards](/learn/power-of-attorney/limiting-an-agents-powers-and-adding-safeguards-in-a-power-of-attorney) shows how to add this kind of language without opening the door to abuse.

## A step-by-step checklist before the move

1. Ask each community you are considering for its pet policy, in writing, before you tour a second time.
2. Decide whether the pet comes, goes to a relative, or goes temporarily.
3. Ask a primary caregiver and a backup. Tell them honestly about age, health and cost.
4. Write the one-page care letter and give copies to the vet, caregiver and agent.
5. Update the vet record, microchip registration and license with the caregiver's name as an emergency contact.
6. Add pet-expense language to your power of attorney if it is silent.
7. If you want a trust, or Medicaid is likely within five years, schedule a meeting before moving any money.
8. Revisit the plan each year. Pets age, and so do caregivers.

## What about the move itself?

Moving an older pet out of the home it knows is hard on the animal and on the owner. Keep a worn blanket or toy with the pet. Plan the first visit within the first week. If the pet is going to a relative, ask for a photo or short video each week, which many residents find a real comfort. A resident who is told the animal is well and loved usually settles faster than one who is left wondering.

If the pet dies while you are in care, the grief is real and often under-recognized. Tell the staff, who can usually arrange a visit with a volunteer or a therapy animal.
