Families often start by asking what mom's care will cost. A better first question is what kind of care she needs, because the answer decides almost everything else: the monthly bill, who pays, and what documents have to be ready.

The vocabulary is slippery. Brochures use "senior living," "residential care," "personal care home" and "memory neighborhood" for overlapping things. This article defines each setting, shows where Medicare and Medicaid come in and where they do not, and flags the contracts worth reading twice. For the whole picture of paying for care, begin with our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What is assisted living?

Assisted living is housing with a layer of personal help. Residents typically have their own room or apartment, eat in a common dining room, and get help with some activities of daily living: bathing, dressing, toileting, moving around, or taking medication on schedule. Nurses are usually on call or part-time rather than at the bedside around the clock.

It fits a person who can no longer live safely alone but does not need constant medical care. It is regulated by states, not the federal government, so what a community may offer, how it is licensed and who can live there differ widely. Ask to see the state inspection reports and the written list of services.

### How is it different from a nursing home?

A nursing home provides round-the-clock nursing and medical supervision for people with significant health needs. Some residents are recovering from a hospital stay; others live there long term. Assisted living is more residential and less medical. Our guide to [choosing a nursing home and knowing your rights](/learn/elder-care/choosing-a-nursing-home-and-knowing-your-rights) covers the other end of the range.

## What is memory care?

Memory care is care for people with Alzheimer's disease or another dementia. Units are typically secured to prevent wandering, staff have dementia-specific training, and daily routines are structured to lower stress. It is often a wing inside an assisted living community and sometimes a stand-alone building.

It costs more than standard assisted living because staffing is higher. Pay attention to the fine print on "levels of care." Some communities charge extra as a resident needs more help, and a bill can climb well past the advertised starting price.

A resident with dementia who has no [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) in place creates a second problem: who can sign the admission agreement? Sort out authority before a move, not after.

## What is a continuing care retirement community?

A continuing care retirement community, or CCRC, puts independent living, assisted living and skilled nursing on a single campus. A resident enters while healthy and is promised access to higher levels of care later.

The money works differently from other settings. Most CCRCs charge an entrance fee, sometimes large, plus monthly fees. Contracts range from "life care," where monthly fees stay roughly the same as care needs rise, to fee-for-service plans that charge more at higher levels. Refund terms differ: some return a portion of the entrance fee, others do not. Read the contract with an attorney and look hard at the financial health of the operator, because you are paying up front for a promise. Also ask whether the community accepts Medicaid when a resident's money runs low, and under what conditions.

## What is hospice?

Hospice is comfort-focused care for someone with a terminal illness. The goal changes from curing the illness to easing pain and supporting the patient and family. A doctor must certify that the person likely has six months or less to live if the illness follows its usual course, and patients can be re-certified if they live longer.

Hospice can be provided at home, in a nursing home, in an assisted living community or in a dedicated hospice facility. A care team usually includes a nurse, an aide, a social worker, a chaplain and a doctor. Hospice often helps families more than they expect, because a nurse is reachable at 2 a.m.

This is also the moment documents matter. A [POLST](/glossary/polst), a [DNR](/glossary/dnr) and a clear [healthcare proxy](/glossary/healthcare-proxy) keep the care team and the family on the same page. Our comparison of [advance directives, living wills and healthcare proxies](/learn/healthcare-directives/advance-directive-vs-living-will-vs-healthcare-proxy) explains how they fit.

## What is respite care?

Respite care is temporary relief for a family caregiver. It can be a few hours of in-home help, an adult day program, or a stay of days or weeks in an assisted living or nursing facility. A daughter who has cared for her father alone for two years may need a week to rest, to travel, or to have surgery herself. Respite is not a luxury; caregivers who never rest tend to break first.

## Who pays for each type of care?

This is the table families wish they had on day one.

| Setting | Medicare | Medicaid | Other sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assisted living | No | Some states, through a waiver, for care services only | Private pay, long-term care insurance, VA benefits |
| Memory care | No | Some states, limited availability | Private pay, long-term care insurance |
| Nursing home, long term | No for custodial care; limited days after a qualifying hospital stay | Yes, after financial eligibility | Private pay, insurance, VA |
| CCRC | No for housing; may cover medical services | Depends on contract and state | Entrance fee, monthly fees, insurance |
| Hospice | Yes, care for the terminal illness | Yes | Most private insurance |
| Respite | Limited, for hospice patients | Some waivers | Private pay, insurance, state caregiver programs |
| In-home help | Limited | Some waiver programs | Private pay, insurance, VA |

Three points are worth highlighting. First, Medicare does not pay for custodial care, which means help with bathing, dressing and supervision. That is most of what assisted living and memory care deliver. Our article on [what Medicare covers for long-term care](/learn/elder-care/what-medicare-covers-for-long-term-care) goes through the limited nursing home days it does allow.

Second, hospice covers the care, not the rent. If your father has hospice in an assisted living apartment, hospice pays for the hospice team and related items, but the monthly bill for the apartment remains his.

Third, Medicaid is the safety net for long stays, and it has strict financial rules. Many states cover some assisted living or memory care through [a home and community-based waiver](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver), usually for the care services but not for room and board, and often with a waiting list. Where Medicaid pays, the usual look-back and asset tests apply, as covered in our article on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

## How do families fill the gaps?

Most people draw on several sources over time.

1. **Savings and the home.** Many families start by paying privately and selling or renting a house.
2. **Long-term care insurance.** Policies often cover assisted living and memory care if the insured meets the benefit triggers. See [long-term care insurance](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance).
3. **Veterans benefits.** A wartime veteran or surviving spouse may qualify for [Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance), which can help pay for care in assisted living.
4. **Medicaid.** When money runs low, Medicaid may take over, though in assisted living the choice of communities that accept it may be narrow.

A common mistake is to move a parent to an assisted living community that does not take Medicaid, spend down to nothing, and then face a forced move. Ask any community upfront about its Medicaid policy and put the answer in writing.
