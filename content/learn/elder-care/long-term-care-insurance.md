Most people over 50 have thought about long-term care insurance at least once and then put it off. The premiums are real, the benefits are far away, and the paperwork is not appealing. Meanwhile the costs of care continue to rise, and Medicare does not cover most of them. The question for many families is whether insurance belongs in their plan, and how it should work alongside their will, trust and powers of attorney.

This article is neutral education. We are a law firm, we do not sell insurance, and we receive nothing from insurers. It explains what the main options are, how partnership policies work, who tends to benefit, and what to ask before buying.

## What does long-term care cost, and who pays?

Costs vary widely by state and by setting. Recent industry cost surveys show private nursing home rooms with medians from about $90,000 a year in lower-cost states to $200,000 or more in the highest-cost states, assisted living commonly in the $60,000 to $85,000 range, and a full-time home health aide often $70,000 or more a year. Your local figures are the ones that matter, and a local agency can quote them.

Four sources pay.

1. **Medicare.** Not for long-term custodial care. It covers a limited stay in a skilled nursing facility after a qualifying hospital stay, with copayments after day 20 and a cap at day 100.
2. **Your own money.** Savings, income, and sometimes home equity.
3. **Insurance.** Traditional or hybrid policies.
4. **Medicaid.** It pays after you have spent down to your state's limit, as covered in [Medicaid planning for long-term care](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

Veterans may add VA benefits, discussed in [VA Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance), and family members may provide care, sometimes paid under a [caregiver agreement](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

## How likely are you to need care?

Federal data from the government's long-term care site, last updated in 2020, estimates that about 70 percent of people turning 65 will need some long-term care in their lives, with an average need of around three years. Averages hide a wide spread: many people need little or none, and some need care for many years, particularly with dementia. That uneven risk is the reason insurance exists.

## What types of coverage exist?

| Approach | How it pays | Strengths | Weaknesses | Typical reader |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional long-term care insurance | Reimburses care costs up to a daily or monthly limit after a trigger | Highest benefit per premium dollar; partnership option | Premiums can rise; "use it or lose it" if never needed | 50s to early 60s, moderate assets, healthy |
| Hybrid life or annuity with care benefit | Death benefit and a pool of care benefits | Fixed premiums in many designs; heirs get something if care is not needed | Costs more per dollar of care; often a large upfront payment | Those with assets to reposition who want certainty |
| Short-term care policy | Limited benefits for a year or less | Easier underwriting, lower cost | Ends quickly | Older buyers who cannot qualify for full coverage |
| Self-funding | Savings, income, home equity | Flexibility; no premium risk | One long illness can drain a plan | People with substantial assets |
| Medicaid | State pays after spend-down | No premium | Limits on choice of facility, asset limits, estate recovery | Those with limited assets or after savings are used |

Benefits and prices change, and products differ by company, so use this as a map to the questions.

## What is a partnership policy?

A state partnership policy, available where your state participates, is a qualified policy that earns a special Medicaid asset protection. For each dollar the policy pays in benefits, you may keep one dollar of assets above the normal Medicaid limit and still qualify. A policy that pays $250,000 in benefits could let you keep $250,000 more than the standard limit, subject to your state's rules. Under federal law, 42 U.S.C. 1396p(b)(1)(C), these policies are also treated specially for estate recovery in many states.

Points to check:

- **Qualification.** The policy must meet state and federal standards when issued, including inflation protection for younger buyers.
- **Reciprocity.** Some states honor partnership policies bought in other states, and some do not.
- **Your state.** Participation and details differ, so confirm with your state insurance department or an attorney.

## How does coverage fit an estate plan?

Insurance pays bills. An estate plan decides who is in charge. The two answer different questions, and each leaves gaps the other fills.

- **Who decides?** A [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets an agent file claims, pay premiums, and manage money. Without one, a court process may be needed.
- **Who speaks to doctors?** A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) let your chosen person confirm that you meet the policy's trigger.
- **Who inherits what is left?** A policy with a death benefit or a partnership asset allowance still needs a will or trust and correct beneficiary designations.
- **Is a spouse protected?** Insurance reduces the pressure on a healthy spouse's share of the assets, which the Medicaid rules for married couples do not fully remove.
- **Does the plan still make sense after a policy lapses?** Keep records of the policy number, the insurer's contact information and who pays premiums, so a lapse does not happen because no one knows.

Keep the documents in the same place as your plan and tell your agent where they are.

## Who might benefit, and who might not?

Insurance often suits someone with moderate assets who wants to avoid exhausting savings and avoid depending on Medicaid, and who is healthy enough to qualify. It may be less suited to people with very few assets, since Medicaid is available, and to people with very large assets who can pay for care themselves. Those in between often have the hardest choice.

Risks to weigh:

- **Premium increases.** Insurers have raised rates on older policies, sometimes sharply. Ask about the company's history.
- **Underwriting.** A health condition may make you uninsurable, and buying earlier is cheaper.
- **Lapse.** If you stop paying, you may lose everything you paid.
- **Complexity.** Policies are long and the triggers matter.

### Example

Carol and Ben, both 58, have $900,000 in savings and a paid-off house. A neighbor's long stay in memory care alarmed them. They compare a traditional policy for each of them with a hybrid policy and self-funding. They decide to buy partnership policies, update their powers of attorney to authorize their agents to deal with the insurer, and save a portion of what they would have paid into a dedicated account. Another couple with $120,000 might instead focus on Medicaid planning and a caregiver agreement.

## What should you ask before buying?

1. **What triggers benefits?** Usually inability to do two of six daily activities or cognitive impairment, certified by a professional.
2. **What is the elimination period?** The waiting time before benefits start, often 30 to 90 days.
3. **What are the benefit limits?** Daily or monthly maximum, and the total pool.
4. **What inflation protection is included?** Costs rise, so a flat benefit loses value.
5. **What care is covered?** Home care, assisted living, nursing home, family caregivers, respite.
6. **What is the insurer's financial rating and rate history?**
7. **Is it a partnership policy in my state?**
8. **What happens if I stop paying?** Ask about nonforfeiture options.

An independent insurance professional or fee-only adviser can compare policies. An attorney can review how a policy fits the rest of your plan.

## Does insurance affect Medicaid planning?

It can. A policy may mean you never need Medicaid, or that you need it later with more assets protected. Premium payments are not gifts, but moving money around to buy a hybrid policy can raise look-back questions, so read [the look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) first and consider [spend-down rules](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down). Estate recovery is covered in the [pillar on long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care).
