A parent's health turns, and the family reorganizes. One daughter suggests Dad move into the spare room. A son in another state wants Mom closer to him. Everyone is thinking about caregiving and distance, and nobody has asked what the move does to Medicaid.

It does quite a lot, and some of it is easy to get wrong. This article covers the two moves families make most: across state lines, and into a child's home. It also answers the questions that tag along, such as whether Medicaid looks at your own finances and how to be repaid for the help you give. The wider framework is in our guide to [long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care).

## Can a parent move to another state and keep Medicaid?

No. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program, but each state runs its own, and eligibility is tied to where a person lives. A parent who moves must apply in the new state once they are a resident there. Old coverage ends.

The new state then applies its own rules, which can be very different. Income and asset limits, the way the home is treated, the availability of home and community waivers, and the length of the waiting list all vary. A parent who qualifies easily in one state can face a long wait in another.

Moving still leaves a paper trail. The new state reviews the same look-back history, usually 60 months, so a gift made before the move will count after it. Our guide to the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains how penalties are calculated.

### How to move without a gap in coverage

1. **Ask first, move second.** Contact the new state's Medicaid office, or an attorney licensed there, and ask about long-term care eligibility, waiting lists and what counts as residency.
2. **Keep care in place until the new coverage starts.** Do not close the old arrangement until the new application is approved, if you can help it.
3. **Gather the same records twice.** Bank statements and proof of income will be asked for again; our walk-through on [how to apply for Medicaid for a parent](/learn/elder-care/how-to-apply-for-medicaid-for-a-parent) lists them.
4. **Update the legal papers.** A power of attorney and health care documents written in one state usually work in another, but banks and hospitals sometimes balk. A fresh set signed in the new state avoids arguments.
5. **Check the home.** If the parent keeps a house in the old state, its treatment depends on the old state's rules while the application sits in the new one.

If your parent's priority is the care itself, ask the new community directly whether it accepts Medicaid and whether beds are reserved for Medicaid residents. Our state guides, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), are a starting point for state-level questions.

## Does Medicaid look at my children's finances?

No. When your parent applies, the agency reviews your parent's income and assets and, if your parent is married, the spouse's. An adult child's salary, savings and home are not part of the test.

Two cautions. First, a few states have filial responsibility laws that let a care provider try to collect from adult children in limited cases. Our article on [whether a nursing home can make your children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) separates the myth from the real risk, which usually comes from what a child signs on the admission papers. Second, money your parent gave to you is examined, so gifts to a child create a paper trail that must be explained.

## What happens when a parent moves in with a child?

Moving in does not, by itself, make a parent ineligible for Medicaid. The parent's resources are what count, and they remain the parent's. The problems come from money that flows between the households.

| Situation | How Medicaid usually treats it |
|---|---|
| Parent pays the child a fair monthly rent or board under a written agreement | Generally fine; it is payment for a service |
| Parent gives the child cash "to help with expenses" | A gift; may create a penalty period |
| Parent uses sale proceeds to remodel the child's house | Often a transfer; can be penalized |
| Child provides care for free | No problem; unpaid help is not a transfer |
| Child is paid under a caregiver agreement for future care at a fair rate | Generally accepted if documented and reasonable |
| Parent repays the child for past care | Usually treated as a gift |

Take Janet, 80, who sells her condo for $310,000 and moves into her son Paul's home. Paul builds a bedroom and bath onto his house with $90,000 of her money, "so she'll always have a place." If Janet needs a nursing home two years later, that $90,000 can be viewed as a gift to Paul within the look-back period. Medicaid will not pay for care for a number of months. Had the family used a written agreement or a properly structured arrangement approved by an attorney, the outcome might differ. This is not a place for improvisation.

## Can I pay my children back for helping me?

The answer depends on timing. Medicaid treats payment for past help as a transfer without fair value, because the services were already given and nothing was owed at the time. A child who drove Mom to appointments for three years and now wants to be reimbursed is, to the agency, receiving a gift.

Paying for future care is different. A written [caregiver agreement](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) that sets an hourly rate near what an agency charges locally, describes the tasks, and calls for payment as the work is done holds up much better. Records matter: a log of hours, payments from the parent's account, and a plain statement of the arrangement. The child owes income tax on what is paid, and the parent may have employer tax obligations in some cases.

Reimbursing a child who paid your bills out of pocket is a little easier. If a child paid for a roof repair on the parent's home and has receipts, repayment can often be supported. Keep the paper.

## What is the caregiver child exemption?

Federal law lets a parent transfer the home to a child without a penalty if the child lived in the parent's home for at least two years immediately before the parent entered a care facility and provided care that allowed the parent to stay home that whole time. The care has to be real and meaningful. A child who lived in the house but did little would not qualify.

States ask for proof. A doctor's note describing what the parent needed during those two years, a record of the child's role, and evidence of where the child lived are all typical. It is a narrow exemption but a useful one. Read our guide to [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) for how it compares with other home transfers, and [gifting to children before Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid) for the risks of ordinary gifts.

Transfers to a spouse, or to a child who is blind or permanently disabled, are also generally allowed without a penalty. The federal rules include those exceptions, and many states follow them. Confirm with your state agency, and see our article on [giving your house to your child](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs) for the tax side, including the loss of the step-up in basis.

## A few decisions to make before anyone packs a box

- Who will be the parent's agent under a power of attorney, and is the document current?
- Has anyone checked the new state's Medicaid rules, or the rules for a parent living in your home?
- Is there a written agreement for care or rent, and does it match what is actually done?
- Are the siblings informed? Silence now becomes a dispute later.
