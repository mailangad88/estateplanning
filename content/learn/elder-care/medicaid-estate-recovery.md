Your mother spent her last three years in a nursing home, and Medicaid paid the bills. She has died, the house is empty, and now a letter arrives from the state's Medicaid agency. It says the state intends to recover what it paid. If you are holding that letter, the first thing to know is that the state does not take everything, and you usually have more room to respond than the tone of the letter suggests.

This article explains how estate recovery works, whom it protects, what it can reach and what a family can do about it. It is part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care). Medicaid is run by each state inside a federal framework, so definitions and procedures differ. Use this as a map, then confirm local rules with an attorney or the state agency.

## What is Medicaid estate recovery?

Estate recovery is a federal requirement that each state seek repayment from the estates of certain Medicaid recipients after they die. The rule comes from 42 U.S.C. 1396p(b), and every state has a program.

At a minimum, states must pursue recovery for people who were 55 or older when they received nursing facility care, home and community-based services, and the hospital and prescription costs tied to those services. They must also pursue it for anyone of any age who was permanently institutionalized. States may go further and recover for other Medicaid services received after 55.

Recovery is limited to the amount Medicaid actually paid. If the state spent $140,000 on your mother and her house sold for $300,000, the claim is $140,000, not the house. The rest belongs to her heirs.

## Who is protected from recovery?

Federal law blocks or delays recovery in several situations.

- **A surviving spouse.** The state cannot recover while the spouse is alive.
- **A child under 21.**
- **A blind or disabled child of any age.**

The state may still come back later. When the spouse dies, the claim can be pursued against the remaining property, subject to the same protections. Some assets are also off limits by federal rule, including certain property and payments of American Indian and Alaska Native recipients.

States must also have an undue hardship process. The usual grounds are that the property is the family's main income source, such as a working farm or small business, or that selling the home would leave an heir without shelter. The standards are narrow and the application is usually made in writing within a short window, so do not wait.

## What can the state reach?

This is where states differ most, and it is the question that decides whether a living trust or joint account protects anything.

| Type of asset | Reached in every state? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Assets that pass through probate | Yes | The traditional "estate" |
| Home titled solely in the recipient's name | Yes | Often the largest asset |
| Joint accounts and joint tenancy property | Depends on the state | Expanded-estate states may claim the recipient's share |
| Revocable living trust assets | Depends on the state | Expanded-estate states reach them; revocable means the person kept control |
| Life estate interest | Depends on the state | Some states can claim the value of the life estate |
| Beneficiary-designated accounts (IRA, life insurance, POD) | Depends on the state | Some expanded-estate states can claim these as well |

Federal law lets states define "estate" to include anything the person had a legal interest in at death, which is how some states reach assets that avoid probate. Other states stay with the probate estate only. If your parent used a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) hoping to shield the house, the answer in an expanded-estate state is usually no. A [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) has the same issue in those states.

### What about liens during life?

Some states record a lien on the home of a person who is permanently in a nursing home and not expected to return. The lien does not force a sale while a spouse, a dependent child or a sibling with an equity interest lives there. It simply puts the state first in line when the house is sold or the owner dies. If your parent has a recorded lien, the payoff figure is likely already on file.

## How does the process work after a death?

Here is the usual sequence. Timing and forms differ by state.

1. **The state learns of the death.** Often from the nursing home, the probate court filing, or the family's own notice.
2. **The estate is opened.** The executor or administrator is appointed through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate). In many states Medicaid is entitled to notice of the filing.
3. **The state files a claim.** It lists payments made and the amount it seeks. Recovery claims usually sit behind some costs of administration and funeral expenses, though the ranking is set by state law.
4. **The executor reviews it.** Ask for an itemized statement. Check dates of service and verify the age of 55 was met for each charge.
5. **The family responds.** Pay it, negotiate, apply for a hardship waiver, or contest errors.
6. **The estate closes.** After the claim is settled, the house can be sold or transferred to heirs.

The state is a creditor like any other, and the executor has a duty to pay valid claims before distributing to heirs. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) for how claim priority works generally. An executor who distributes the house to the children and ignores the claim can become personally responsible for it.

## An example

Raymond, 81, entered a nursing home after a fall. He lived alone, owned his home outright, valued at $290,000, and had about $3,000 in savings. Medicaid paid for his care for 26 months, a total of roughly $190,000 in state spending. Raymond's daughter Alicia was named executor.

After he died, the state filed a claim for $190,000. Alicia had options. She could sell the house and pay the claim, leaving about $100,000 less costs for the heirs. Or she could keep the house and pay the claim from a loan. She also checked whether any exception applied. Raymond's son Dale is disabled and had lived in the home, so recovery against the house could be delayed while Dale lived there. That one fact changed the plan.

If Raymond had deeded the house years earlier to Alicia after she provided care, the result might have been different. The [caregiver child exemption](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) allows a no-penalty transfer in that situation, and a home no longer in his name is not in his estate at death. That is the sort of detail that a family can only use if somebody looks at it before the crisis, not after.

## Can estate recovery be reduced or avoided?

Some of it can be avoided, but only with planning, and not for every family.

- **Exempt transfers.** A transfer of the home to a spouse, a disabled child, or a caregiver child who qualifies is allowed without a penalty.
- **An irrevocable trust set up early.** An [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) built for Medicaid purposes and funded outside the 60-month look-back generally keeps the home out of the recipient's estate. It has costs, covered in that article.
- **A partnership long-term care policy.** In states that participate, a qualifying policy can protect assets from recovery to the extent of benefits paid. See [long-term care insurance and your estate plan](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance).
- **Spending down on things you need.** Repairs and mortgage payoff reduce countable assets, and the equity you keep is still your home. See [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down).

Gifts made without a plan are the usual trap. A transfer in the [look-back window](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) can cause a penalty period, and it may not even keep the house out of recovery.

## What should you do if a claim arrives?

1. Do not distribute the house or accounts yet.
2. Ask the agency for an itemized statement of payments and the legal basis for the claim.
3. Look for protected family members: a surviving spouse, a child under 21, a disabled child.
4. Gather evidence for a hardship waiver if the home is a family business or a disabled relative's residence.
5. Compare the claim with the value of the estate. If the house is worth less than the claim, ask about a reduced settlement.
6. Talk to an attorney before the response deadline.
