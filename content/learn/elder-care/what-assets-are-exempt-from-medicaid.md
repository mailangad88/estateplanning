Ask a nursing home business office how Medicaid works and you will hear that you must "spend down to two thousand dollars," or whatever the number is in your state. It sounds like losing everything. In practice, a good share of what a person owns never counts. The house, a car, a funeral and personal belongings are usually set aside, and a couple has more room still.

This guide sorts what counts from what does not, asset by asset. It is the detail behind the short list in our [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care). **The caution:** the categories are federal, but the dollar caps and some of the treatments belong to each state, so check current figures with your state agency.

## How does Medicaid decide what counts?

Medicaid divides what you own into countable resources and exempt resources. Countable resources are added up and compared with a single limit. If you are over it, you are not eligible until you reduce what you hold, by spending on things you need, by using an exempt transfer, or by paying for care yourself. Exempt resources are left out of that total.

Two ideas help. First, ownership and access matter more than what the account is called. If you can withdraw it, it generally counts. Second, a resource is measured at its current value, usually what you could sell it for, less a debt secured by it.

## Which assets are usually exempt?

| Asset | Usual treatment |
|---|---|
| Primary home | Exempt up to a state equity cap, while you or a spouse live there or you intend to return. |
| One vehicle | Exempt, one per household in most states. |
| Household goods, clothing, jewelry for personal use | Exempt. |
| Burial spaces and plots | Exempt for you and immediate family. |
| Prepaid irrevocable funeral contract | Exempt, sometimes with a cap. |
| Life insurance, small face value | Often exempt up to a limit set by state. |
| Term life insurance | No cash value, so it generally does not count. |
| Assets in a special needs trust or ABLE account | Treated under separate rules. |
| Income-producing property or business assets | Exempt in some states and circumstances. |

The cap on home equity is set by each state within a federal range. As of 2026 that federal range runs from roughly $750,000 to a little over $1 million. The exclusion generally stops applying if no one will return to live there and no spouse or dependent relative does. For ways to keep a house in the family, see [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).

## Which assets usually count?

Cash and anything easily turned to cash count: checking and savings accounts, CDs, money market funds, stocks, bonds and mutual funds, and cash value of life insurance above the exempt limit. Real estate other than the home counts, including a vacation house or land. A second car, a boat or an RV usually counts unless the state allows more than one vehicle.

Money in a joint account generally counts in full for the applicant unless the other owner can prove they contributed it. A child who is a co-owner on a parent's account is a common reason applications stall. Our [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) article shows how that problem starts.

## What about retirement accounts?

This is the area where states disagree most. Some count the whole balance of an IRA or 401(k) as a countable resource, since the owner can withdraw it. Others exempt the account while the owner is taking required minimum distributions or periodic payments and treat the payments as income. A spouse's account is handled differently again, and in some states a community spouse's retirement plan is protected in part.

Practical points for families:

- Do not cash out a retirement account to pay a nursing home without advice. The withdrawal is taxable income and may push income above the cap in an income-cap state.
- Converting a countable account into a payout stream can change its treatment. In a few states that is a recognized planning step.
- If a child will inherit the account, naming matters too; see [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) for how the 10-year rule works for heirs.

## How do life insurance and burial planning work?

Whole life and universal life policies have a cash value. If the combined face value is under the state's limit, the policy is typically exempt. If it is over, the cash value counts. Term policies have none, so they usually do not.

Many families deal with an over-limit policy by cashing it in and spending on exempt items, or by assigning it to a funeral home to fund an irrevocable contract. An irrevocable funeral contract is exempt in nearly every state because it cannot be turned back into cash. Burial plots, caskets and markers are exempt too. The overall details of how to use this are in [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down).

## What can you turn into an exempt asset?

Spending is not gifting. Swapping countable cash for something exempt is generally allowed, as long as you pay fair value.

1. **Repair or improve the home.** A new roof, a ramp, a walk-in shower.
2. **Replace the car.** A reliable vehicle for the household.
3. **Pay off debts.** A mortgage, a car loan, a credit card.
4. **Buy a funeral contract.** Irrevocable, and in the state's allowed amount.
5. **Buy personal items.** Hearing aids, dental work, a hospital bed, clothing.

What does not work is paying a child for a "loan" never made, or buying something for someone else. Those are transfers and fall under the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

## Does exempt mean Medicaid can never touch it?

No. Exempt during life is not the same as protected after death. States must seek to recover the cost of long-term care from the estate of a recipient, and the home is often the main asset. Recovery is delayed or waived for a surviving spouse and certain children. Read [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) before assuming an exempt house is safe for your heirs.

Also, exemptions change with the facts. A house that is exempt today can stop being exempt if the owner declares no intent to return and no spouse lives in it.

## How state law changes the list

States pick their own home equity cap, vehicle rules, life insurance limits, funeral contract caps and retirement account treatment. Some also have different rules for a community spouse. Treat this page as the map and your own state's current figures as the legend. Our [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) are a starting point for state-specific context.
