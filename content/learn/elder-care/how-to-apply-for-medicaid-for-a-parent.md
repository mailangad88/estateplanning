Usually the call comes from a hospital social worker or a nursing home business office. Your mother has been admitted, the private-pay rate is more than her income, and someone says the word "Medicaid." You are handed a stack of forms and a date.

The application itself is not mysterious. What wears families down is the paperwork and the question of who is allowed to sign. This article takes you through the process in order: who can file, what to gather, how to submit, how long it takes, and what to do if it goes wrong. For the planning side, start with our overview of [long-term care and Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care).

## Who can apply for Medicaid for a parent?

The person applying must have legal authority, or the parent's written permission, to deal with the agency. Common routes look like this.

| If your parent... | Then the applicant is usually... |
|---|---|
| Can understand and sign | The parent, or you as a named authorized representative |
| Has a durable power of attorney | The agent named in the document, if it covers benefits and government programs |
| Has a spouse at home | Either spouse, since both spouses' finances are reviewed |
| Cannot sign and has no document | A court-appointed guardian, or in some states a family member or facility under state rules |

If your mother has dementia and never signed a power of attorney, you may be stuck until a court names someone. Our article on a [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) explains that path and its cost in time. Do not assume the nursing home can fill the gap. Business office staff can help with forms but generally cannot act as your parent's legal decision maker.

## What do you need to gather first?

Collect these before you file, because the clock matters and caseworkers rarely call to chase a missing page.

1. **Identity and status.** Photo ID, Social Security card, Medicare and any insurance cards, and proof of citizenship or immigration status.
2. **Income.** Social Security award letter, pension statements, annuity payments, and any rental income.
3. **Bank and investment statements.** Every account, joint accounts included, for the full look-back period.
4. **Property records.** Deed to the home, vehicle titles, and any other real estate.
5. **Insurance and funeral contracts.** Life insurance policies with cash value, long-term care policies, and any prepaid funeral contract.
6. **Legal documents.** Power of attorney, trust documents, any recent will, and a marriage certificate if there is a spouse.
7. **Medical documentation.** The doctor's statement of need or the state's functional assessment, which shows your parent needs nursing home level of care.

The look-back review is the part that takes time. Most states review five years, or 60 months, of statements, and a few handle it differently. You can read how this works in our guide to the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period), and our [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) will count back from the date you plan to apply.

## How do you actually submit the application?

Most states let you apply online, by mail, by phone or in person at a county or state human services office. Some use a separate long-term care unit, and the nursing home may point you to it.

A workable sequence is below.

1. Confirm who is signing and whether that person holds a valid document.
2. Ask the facility for its admission date and its monthly private-pay statement, which becomes part of the file.
3. Complete the state's long-term care Medicaid application. Answer every question; leave nothing blank.
4. Attach copies, never originals, of the records above.
5. Keep a copy of the whole package and note the date and method of submission.
6. Calendar a follow-up for one week later to confirm the file is complete.

If a caseworker asks for more information, there is usually a short deadline stated in the letter. Answer it quickly and in writing, and keep proof you did.

## How long does it take?

Federal rules generally require a decision within 45 days of a complete application, or longer where a disability determination is needed. In practice it can run longer when documents are missing. In many states, an approved application can cover some earlier months if the parent was eligible then, so keep every nursing home bill from the period.

During that wait, someone has to pay. The facility may bill privately or accept a pending Medicaid status; see [paying for a nursing home while waiting for Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/paying-for-a-nursing-home-while-waiting-for-medicaid) for how families handle that stretch.

## What if your parent is married?

Married applicants add a layer. The agency looks at both spouses' finances, and the healthy spouse who stays home keeps a protected share of assets and income. The rules are covered in [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples).

For the application, it means the community spouse has to provide statements and sign certain forms. A spouse who refuses, or who is estranged, can stall everything. Some states let the applicant proceed and then assign the state a right to seek support from the spouse, but this is not the same everywhere and is not a casual shortcut. Bring in an attorney early if a spouse is unwilling or cannot be found.

## Will the agency see everything?

Yes, or nearly everything. Caseworkers verify with the bank, the Social Security Administration and sometimes the county records office. Concealed accounts and undisclosed gifts almost always surface, and a false statement can lead to a longer penalty or worse. If you find something you forgot, disclose it now.

Gifts are the usual problem. Suppose Linda's father gave $18,000 to his grandson three years ago for a car. That transfer falls inside the look-back and may create a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay. The penalty is generally calculated by dividing the amount given by a state figure for the average monthly cost of care, so disclosure and a lawyer's review before filing beat a surprise afterward. Our overview of [what assets are exempt from Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/what-assets-are-exempt-from-medicaid) shows what the agency does not count at all.

## What if the application is denied?

Read the notice first. It lists the reason and the deadline for requesting a fair hearing, and the deadline is short. Common reasons include:

- Missing or incomplete bank statements.
- Countable assets over the state's limit.
- An unexplained transfer during the look-back.
- Failure to respond to a caseworker's request in time.
- A level of care finding that the parent does not yet need a nursing home
.
Some of those are fixed with paperwork. Others call for a spend-down or planning step, and a few turn on a real disagreement that a hearing can resolve. Ask for the hearing if you disagree, even while you gather what is missing.

## A few habits that help

Keep one folder, paper or digital, for the whole application. Write down the name and number of every caseworker you speak to. Never hand over the only copy of a document. If the agency's letter confuses you, call before the deadline rather than after.

Planning in advance changes this process dramatically. Families who learn the rules before a crisis have options that a family applying from a hospital bed does not. Our article on [Medicaid crisis planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-crisis-planning) covers what still helps once care has already begun.
