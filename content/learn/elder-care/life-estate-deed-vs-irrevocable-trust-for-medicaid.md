You own your house. You want to be sure that if you need a nursing home, Medicaid does not take it. Two names come up again and again: the life estate deed and the irrevocable trust. They look alike on a brochure, since both move the house out of your name while you keep living in it. They behave very differently when something goes wrong.

This page compares them side by side, so you can see what each costs, what each protects and where each fails. It belongs to our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care), and it assumes you are planning ahead rather than in a crisis.

## What is the difference between a life estate and an irrevocable trust?

With a life estate deed, you sign a new deed that splits ownership in two. You keep a "life estate," meaning the right to live in or rent the house for the rest of your life. Your children get the "remainder," which becomes theirs automatically when you die. The deed is recorded with the county, and no probate is needed for the house at your death.

With an irrevocable trust, you sign a trust agreement and a deed moving the house to the trustee. The trust says you may live there, who the trustee is and who inherits. You cannot revoke the trust or take the house back. The trustee, who is usually a child or a professional, holds title instead of the children owning outright.

In both cases you have given up ownership of something, which is why Medicaid treats the transfer as a gift.

## How do they compare?

| Feature | Life estate deed | Irrevocable (Medicaid) trust |
|---|---|---|
| Set-up cost | Lower; one deed | Higher; trust document plus deed |
| Starts 60-month look-back | Yes | Yes |
| You can live in the house | Yes, for life | Yes, if the trust says so |
| Sale during your life | Needs you and every child to sign | Trustee can sell; proceeds stay in trust |
| Children's creditors and divorce | The child's share is exposed | Held by trustee; usually shielded |
| If a child dies first | Share goes to that child's heirs unless the deed says otherwise | Trust terms decide |
| Step-up in basis at your death | Generally yes | Generally yes if drafted to be included in your estate |
| Flexibility to change heirs | Very little | Limited, depends on the drafting |
| Estate recovery risk | Depends on state | Depends on state and on trust type |

## Does a life estate protect the house from Medicaid?

It can, if you complete it more than 60 months before you apply for long-term care Medicaid and the deed names the remainder owners correctly. The value of the remainder you gave away is the gift, calculated from your age and the home's value. If you live five years past the deed, the transfer is outside the look-back and the house is not a countable asset.

The weak spot is the children. They own a real share of the house today, so their problems become the house's problems. A lawsuit judgment against a son, a divorce, a bankruptcy or even a tax lien can attach to his remainder interest. Illinois and New Jersey both let creditors reach what a child owns, and a house with a life estate can be dragged into a dispute you had nothing to do with.

### What about selling the house later?

Say you move into assisted living and want to sell. You and every child must sign. If one lives abroad or refuses, the sale stalls. Your share of the proceeds is yours, which means it could be counted as your resource, while the children's share is theirs. That split is awkward and often yields a much smaller benefit than the plan promised.

## Does an irrevocable trust do better?

On creditors, control and sale, yes. Because the trustee owns the house, the children have no present ownership for a creditor to attach. A properly written trust lets the trustee sell, buy a replacement home or hold the proceeds, and it keeps the proceeds protected from the look-back as long as the five-year period has passed. It can name contingent beneficiaries, so a child's early death does not send a share to an in-law.

The trade-offs are cost, rigidity and drafting risk. You cannot take the house back. You cannot be the trustee in most Medicaid plans. A trust that gives you too much control, such as a right to the principal, can make the house countable again. Our page on the [Medicaid asset protection trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) explains those drafting limits, and the general [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) article covers the trust form itself.

## What happens in estate recovery?

Estate recovery is the state's claim against a Medicaid recipient's estate after death, and it is the reason many families look at these tools. The answer depends on how your state defines the estate.

New Jersey defines it broadly. The state agency's rules reach the person's interest in property passing by survivorship, a life estate or a living trust, so a life estate may not keep the house out of reach. Read our [New Jersey estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery) article before you rely on one.

Illinois' Department of Healthcare and Family Services says that assets passing directly to a beneficiary outside probate are generally not part of the estate. That is helpful, but it does not settle the narrower question of life estates and trusts, so use the [Illinois estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) page and ask an attorney about your specific deed or trust.

## A worked example

This is an invented example. Lena, 74, owns a house in Edison, New Jersey worth $420,000. She has two children, Marcus and Dana. Marcus is going through a divorce.

If Lena signs a life estate deed today, half of the remainder belongs to Marcus the moment the deed is recorded. His wife's lawyer can list that interest in the divorce. If Lena needs to sell in four years, she has to find Marcus and get his signature.

If Lena signs an irrevocable trust instead, the trustee, Dana, holds title, and Marcus owns nothing yet. The divorce does not reach the house. The trust costs more to set up, and Lena must accept that she cannot change her mind. In both cases, a Medicaid application in the first five years would face a penalty, so Lena has to be able to pay for care privately until the clock runs out.

## What do the taxes look like?

Two tax points decide more families than the Medicaid rules do. First, a gift of a remainder interest or of a house to a trust is a taxable gift on paper, even though almost nobody owes gift tax. With the federal basic exclusion at $15,000,000 per person in 2026, the usual result is a gift tax return and no tax due, but ask your preparer to file it.

Second, the step-up in basis. Property you still own, or in which you keep a life estate or the right to live and benefit, is generally included in your estate at death, so your heirs receive a basis equal to the date-of-death value. A house bought for $90,000 and worth $420,000 at death can then be sold with little gain. A gift that gives up every interest loses that step-up, which is why a plain deed to a child is usually the worst tax choice. A trust written so the house is still included in your estate keeps the step-up and still counts as a completed gift for Medicaid. That balance is exactly what an experienced drafter manages, and a trust form from the internet seldom does it.

## Which should you choose?

Use this list to narrow it down.

1. **Are you more than five years from needing care?** If not, neither tool helps in time and another strategy may fit better, such as the exemptions covered in [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).
2. **Is a child in a risky position?** Debt, divorce, a business or a lawsuit points to a trust.
3. **Is the house likely to be sold?** If you may downsize, a trust is more practical.
4. **Is cost the main limit?** A life estate costs less, and it can be reasonable when the children are stable and the house will stay in the family.
5. **Do you want to keep control?** Neither gives you control. If that matters, look at an exempt transfer, a caregiver arrangement or [long-term care insurance](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance).
