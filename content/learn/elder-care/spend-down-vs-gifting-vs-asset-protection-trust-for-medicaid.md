Margaret, 76, lives alone in a house in Joliet worth about $240,000. She has $60,000 in savings and a small pension. Her doctor has told her family she will probably need a nursing home within a few years, and her son asks the question every family eventually asks: should she spend the money, give it away or put it in a trust?

There is no single answer, and anyone who offers one without asking about your health, age and family has not done the math. This page sets the three common routes next to each other, with Margaret's numbers, so you can see where each one helps and where it breaks. It belongs to our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What is the difference between spend down, gifting and an asset protection trust?

Each one reduces the countable assets Medicaid looks at. They differ in how fast they work, what you give up and what can go wrong.

| | Spend down | Gifting to children | Asset protection trust |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it works | Pay for things you need | Give money or property away | Transfer assets to an irrevocable trust |
| Penalty period | None | Yes, if inside 60 months | Yes, if inside 60 months |
| Works right now | Yes | Only with a penalty plan | No, needs lead time |
| Who owns it afterward | You, then what you bought | Your child | The trust |
| Main risk | Money is gone | Child's divorce, debt, death | Loss of control, funding mistakes |
| Cost to set up | Low | Low to moderate | Highest |

Medicaid is a means-tested program, and in most states a single applicant can keep only a few thousand dollars in countable assets. The details for your state are in our guide to [what counts as a countable asset](/learn/elder-care/what-counts-as-a-countable-asset-for-medicaid).

## How does spend down work, and when is it the right choice?

Spend down means using your own money for your own benefit until you reach the asset limit. It is the only route with no penalty period, so it is the right one when care is needed now. The key rule is that you must receive fair value. Medicaid does not penalize you for buying a roof, but it will penalize you for giving the roof money to your nephew.

Legitimate uses include repairing or modifying the home, paying off a mortgage or other debts, replacing a worn-out car, hearing aids and dental work, a prepaid funeral contract, and paying the nursing home privately for the months before the application is approved. The full list and the traps are in our [Medicaid spend-down article](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down).

For Margaret, spend down would work like this. She keeps the house, which is exempt while she lives there or intends to return. She uses $20,000 for a new roof and a walk-in shower, buys a $9,000 funeral contract, and pays for care at home out of the rest. By the time she moves to a nursing home her savings are near the limit and the application is simple.

What spend down does not do is protect anything that is left. The house is still hers, so after she dies the state can seek repayment from it. The article on [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) explains how that works.

## When does gifting to your children make sense?

Gifting makes sense in two cases: when the amount is small, and when you can run the math on a partial gift while still paying privately for the gap. A gift made inside the 60-month look-back creates a penalty period. The length is the amount given divided by your state's average monthly nursing home cost, a number set by each state.

Example. Margaret gives her son $40,000. If her state divisor is about $10,000 a month, the penalty is four months. Medicaid will not pay for four months after she applies and is otherwise eligible. She must find another way to pay for those months, and if she cannot, the gift was a mistake. That is the reasoning behind [half-a-loaf gifting](/learn/elder-care/half-a-loaf-gifting-and-how-long-the-medicaid-penalty-lasts), which pairs a gift with an income stream to cover the gap.

The bigger problem with outright gifts is who owns the money afterward. Margaret's son owns it. If he divorces, owes a creditor or dies, it can leave the family. Our article on [gifting to children before Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid) walks through those risks, including the exempt transfers, such as a house to a disabled child or to a caregiver child who lived there for two years.

A power of attorney agent cannot make these gifts unless the document says so. See [whether an agent can make gifts](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts).

## How does an asset protection trust compare?

A Medicaid asset protection trust is an irrevocable trust. You transfer the house or savings to it, name a trustee, and give up the right to take the principal back. After 60 months, the transfer is outside the look-back, and the assets are not counted for Medicaid. Our page on the [Medicaid asset protection trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) covers drafting details.

Compared with an outright gift, the trust gives you control over what the children receive and when. You can also keep the right to live in the house and, in many designs, to receive the income. A trust helps with the tax side too, because a well-drafted one can keep the step-up in basis at death, as described in our [step-up in basis article](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

The cost is that you cannot change your mind. The money is no longer yours. A trust is also useless if you need care in two years, because the transfer is still inside the look-back. For that reason, most trust clients are people in their 60s and early 70s with good health, a house and savings that they want to leave to their children.

## Which one should Margaret choose?

Probably a mix, and the mix depends on one question: how soon does she need care. Here is how the same $300,000 of house and savings plays out under three timelines.

1. **Care needed within a year.** Spend down. Put the cash into the roof, the shower, the funeral and private pay. The house remains exempt. An attorney can check whether half-a-loaf gifting or an annuity adds anything, but a trust is too late.
2. **Care likely in two to four years.** A hybrid. Move part of the savings to a trust now, accept that part of it will be inside the look-back if care starts early, and keep enough outside the trust to pay privately through any penalty.
3. **Healthy, no care expected for five years or more.** Put the house in an irrevocable trust, keep the savings, and review the plan every year. This is where the trust earns its cost.

Notice that in each timeline the question comes before the tool. The article on [crisis planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-crisis-planning) covers the first scenario in more depth, and [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) covers the rest.

## What mistakes do families make with these strategies?

- Gifting first and asking later. Once a deed is recorded or money is moved, the penalty clock has started and the transfer cannot be unwound without cost.
- Moving money into a child's account. A child's name on your account is a gift when the child withdraws, and it exposes your money to the child's creditors.
- Forgetting the surviving spouse. If a spouse lives at home, special rules let the couple keep more, and a transfer to the spouse is usually free of penalty. Skipping that step wastes the best protection available.
- Treating the trust as a tax shelter. It is a Medicaid and probate tool. A trust that gives you the power to take the assets back is counted by Medicaid.
- Ignoring state rules. The look-back is 60 months in most states, but trust treatment, penalty divisors and rules on the home differ. For Illinois and New Jersey, see our guides to [Illinois Medicaid planning](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) and [New Jersey Medicaid planning](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning).
