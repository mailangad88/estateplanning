Two worries tend to arrive together. One is that care will cost more than you have. The other is that when the time comes, there will be nobody to speak for you. Plenty of people carry both: the widow with a small pension and no children, the lifelong bachelor whose siblings have died, the parent who is estranged from his kids.

The reassuring part is that the system does not leave people on the sidewalk. The less reassuring part is that you will get what the system provides, rather than what you would have chosen. This page explains how that works and what you can arrange in advance.

## The short version

- For nursing home care, Medicaid is the safety net, and having little money usually helps you qualify.
- Help exists, but you have to ask: social workers, the area agency on aging and the long-term care ombudsman are free.
- The sharpest gaps are assisted living, and decision-making when nobody is named to act.
- Setting up documents costs far less than a court case does.

## What if I have no money and need nursing home care?

Medicaid covers long-term nursing home care for people whose income and countable assets fall under their state's limits. Every state sets its own numbers, and they change, so check your state's Medicaid agency or an attorney for the current figures. In general, someone with little savings is likely to meet the asset test. Income is the other test, and there are special rules in some states for people whose income is above the limit; see our page on the [Miller trust](/learn/elder-care/income-cap-trust-miller-trust).

Once you are in a Medicaid-certified bed, the program pays the facility. You pay most of your monthly income, such as Social Security or a pension, toward the bill and keep a small personal needs allowance. The exact allowance varies by state. For how it works day to day, see [income and bank accounts in a nursing home](/learn/elder-care/income-and-bank-accounts-in-a-nursing-home).

The practical steps are these.

1. **Ask the hospital or facility social worker for help** with the Medicaid application. This is part of their job.
2. **Gather proof** of income, bank statements for the lookback period, ID and insurance cards. Our guide to [applying for Medicaid for a parent](/learn/elder-care/how-to-apply-for-medicaid-for-a-parent) lists what caseworkers ask for. The look-back review is 60 months in most states.
3. **Choose a Medicaid-certified facility.** Not every nursing home accepts Medicaid for every bed.
4. **Ask about payment while the application is pending.** Approval can take weeks, and the facility may need a plan for those weeks. See [paying for a nursing home while waiting for Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/paying-for-a-nursing-home-while-waiting-for-medicaid).
5. **Keep notices and deadlines.** A missed letter from the agency can cost you the coverage.

## What if my money runs out after I move in?

This happens often. A person pays privately for a year or two, savings fall, and a Medicaid application follows. A nursing home that participates in Medicaid generally lets a resident stay when private funds end, although some keep a limited number of Medicaid beds, and a few facilities have rules that favor private-pay residents. Ask these questions before admission, not after.

The facility cannot simply put you out for nonpayment. Federal rules require written notice, a reason and appeal rights before a certified nursing home discharges a resident. We cover those in [choosing a nursing home and knowing your rights](/learn/elder-care/choosing-a-nursing-home-and-knowing-your-rights). Even so, do not wait. Apply for Medicaid while there is still some money left, because a gap between private funds and approval is where bills pile up.

Are children on the hook? Generally not. Unless a child signed as a personal guarantor, the debt belongs to the resident. If you are a child being pressed to sign, read [can a nursing home make my children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay) first.

## What about assisted living and home care?

This is the weaker part of the safety net. Medicaid is built around nursing homes. For assisted living and home care, many states offer waiver programs, but they can have waiting lists, caps on enrollment and rules that cover services and not rent. Many assisted living communities do not accept Medicaid at all, or accept it only after you have lived there privately for a stated period.

| Setting | Does Medicaid usually help? | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Nursing home | Yes, if the bed is Medicaid certified | How many Medicaid beds, and can I stay when my money ends? |
| Assisted living | Sometimes, through a state waiver; rent often not covered | Do you accept Medicaid? After how long? |
| Home care | Some state programs and waivers | Is there a waiting list? Are family caregivers allowed? |
| Memory care | Rarely; policies vary widely | Will I have to move if I run out of money? |

Read [assisted living, memory care and hospice: who pays](/learn/elder-care/assisted-living-memory-care-and-hospice-who-pays) and the page on the [home care waiver](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver) before signing any lease. Hospice, if it is the right stage, is generally covered by Medicare.

## Who helps if I have no family?

Several places will, if you call.

- **The hospital discharge planner or nursing home social worker.** They coordinate care, applications and transfers every day.
- **The Area Agency on Aging.** It maps local services, meals, in-home help and benefits counseling. You can find yours through the national Eldercare Locator.
- **The long-term care ombudsman.** Free and independent, it helps residents with complaints about care, billing and discharge.
- **A state health insurance assistance program (SHIP).** Volunteers help with Medicare and Medicaid questions.
- **Legal aid for seniors.** Many areas have free legal help for people over 60.
- **Adult Protective Services.** If a person living alone is neglected or exploited.

None of them can make personal decisions for you, which leads to the second worry.

## What happens if I become incapacitated and nobody is named?

If you cannot make decisions and have named no one, a hospital will look for a next of kin. Many states have a priority list of family members or close friends who can consent to treatment. If there is no one on it, a court may need to appoint a guardian, sometimes a professional or a public guardian. That process takes weeks or months, is public, and the cost comes from your assets. Our articles on [adult guardianship](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-of-an-adult-parent-who-can-petition-and-what-the-court-needs) and [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) walk through the differences.

Dolores, 81, has no children and her brother died last year. A neighbor, Mei, checks in on her. If Dolores signs a durable financial power of attorney and a healthcare power of attorney naming Mei, with a professional fiduciary as backup, the hospital and the bank will know whom to call. If she signs nothing, Mei has no authority at all, however long they have been friends.

## What can I arrange now?

A few documents and decisions take a single afternoon and replace a court case.

1. Sign a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) naming a person you trust, plus a backup.
2. Sign a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and living will so your wishes are on paper.
3. Consider a professional fiduciary or a bank as agent or trustee if no friend fits. See [geriatric care managers, money managers and fiduciaries](/learn/elder-care/geriatric-care-managers-money-managers-and-fiduciaries).
4. Write down where your accounts and documents are.
5. Talk to an attorney about Medicaid timing if you have a home or savings.
6. Read [estate planning with no heirs or family nearby](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-with-no-heirs-or-family-nearby) for who should inherit and who should serve.

Our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care) gathers the rest.
