George, 84, is in a nursing home. His pension is $1,900 a month and his Social Security is $1,700. His assets are low, well under the limit. He should qualify for Medicaid, and the home bill is far more than his income. But the state says his income of $3,600 is over its monthly limit, and the application is denied. The family is confused: how can someone be too rich to get help with a bill that is three times his income?

The answer is a rule in a number of states, and the fix is a trust named after a court case. This article explains what the income cap trust does, how to run one, and what can go wrong. It is part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What is a Miller trust?

A Miller trust is a trust that lets a person with income over the Medicaid limit still qualify for long-term care. The name comes from a federal court case from the early 1990s. Congress later wrote the structure into federal law, in 42 U.S.C. 1396p(d)(4)(B), under the name "qualified income trust."

It matters only in states that use an income cap. Those states set a monthly ceiling, usually tied to 300 percent of the federal SSI benefit, which is currently roughly $3,000 a month in 2026. Income above the cap disqualifies the applicant, regardless of care costs. If the applicant puts the excess income through a qualified trust, the state does not count that income when applying the cap.

States that use other methods, often called medically needy states, compare income with the cost of care. They do not need the trust.

## How does a Miller trust work?

The trust is a simple, narrow account. These are the steps.

1. **Confirm that the state is an income cap state.** The Medicaid agency or an attorney can tell you. Many states have instructions and sample forms.
2. **Set up the trust.** It must be irrevocable and in writing, with the state named as a remainder beneficiary. A trustee, other than the person, is chosen. A family member is common.
3. **Open a bank account.** It must be in the trust's name and used for this purpose only.
4. **Fund it monthly.** Each month, income is deposited into the account. At least the amount above the cap must go in. The deposit must come from the person's income, not from savings.
5. **Pay from it, in order.** The trustee pays a personal needs allowance to the patient, a health insurance premium if there is one, an amount for a spouse at home if the state allows, and the share of cost to the nursing home.
6. **Keep records.** The state may ask for bank statements and receipts every year, when the case is reviewed.

The result is that George's pension is deposited in the trust and used to pay the nursing home. Medicaid pays the balance. His Social Security stays in his own account. George's personal money pays his small expenses.

## What can the trust pay for?

The list is narrow, and the state's instructions control. Typical allowed uses are:

| Allowed use | Notes |
|---|---|
| Personal needs allowance to the patient | A small monthly amount set by the state |
| Share of cost to the nursing home | The patient's required contribution |
| Health insurance premiums | Medicare supplement or similar |
| Allowance for a community spouse | Under the spousal rules in many states |
| Trustee fees, taxes on trust income | Only where the state permits |

A Miller trust is not a place to build a pot of money for the family. Using it as a spending account for other things, like paying a grandchild's tuition, can cause the state to treat it as noncompliant and deny coverage.

## What happens at death?

When the person dies, any money left in the trust goes first to the state, up to the total Medicaid paid on their behalf. Only after that does anything go to other beneficiaries, and often there is little left because the trust is meant to be paid out monthly. This feature is required by federal law, and trusts without it do not qualify.

If the person leaves other assets, those are separate. The state's claim against them is handled through [estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery), and the trust remainder is a different pot of money.

## Who should be the trustee?

The trustee has duties to follow the terms of the document and the state's rules. It is a monthly job, not a one-time signing. Good choices are an adult child who lives nearby, a sibling, or a professional if no family member can do it. The trustee should be organized, comfortable with the bank, and willing to keep clean records. Our article on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) covers what to look for.

A person who still has capacity can sign the trust. If they no longer do, the agent under a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) may be able to sign it, but only if the document allows the agent to create trusts. Many older forms do not, and a court may need to approve the step. That delay is one reason to review powers of attorney before a crisis.

## What are common mistakes?

- **Using the trust in a state that does not need one.** It adds cost and monthly paperwork for nothing.
- **Missing a deposit.** The state counts the month's income, and the person may lose coverage for it.
- **Depositing too little.** The excess above the cap must go in, and some states want a particular formula.
- **Mixing funds.** Paying personal bills from the trust account, or putting savings in.
- **Choosing a trustee who moves away or stops paying attention.** The monthly routine must continue as long as the person is on Medicaid.
- **Forgetting taxes.** The trust may need to file an income tax return, depending on how it is written and set up.

## How long does it take, and can it be done late?

A Miller trust is one of the few Medicaid tools that works at the last minute, because it does not depend on the look-back. It moves no assets and gives nothing away. Most trusts can be drafted and signed in a week or two, then the bank account opened. The practical delay is usually the bank, which may want the trust document, a tax identification number and the trustee's identification before it opens the account.

In many states, coverage cannot begin for a month until the trust exists and has been funded for that month. So the cost of waiting is real. If George's family sets the trust up in March, but the first deposit is not made until April, the March bill may stay unpaid. Ask the caseworker which month the state will use as the first month of coverage, then set the first deposit to meet it.

## How does it interact with a spouse?

If the patient is married, the Miller trust and the spousal rules work together. The healthy spouse's income is counted as theirs under the name-on-the-check rule, and a portion of the patient's income can be set aside for the spouse's maintenance needs. Read [how Medicaid works for a married couple](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples) for the allowance, and ask whether your state lets the trust pay it directly.
