Most people will need some help with daily life as they age, and a significant number will need it for an extended time. The questions come quickly when it happens: who pays, where will care happen, what happens to the house, and what about a spouse who is still healthy? The families who handle these questions best usually started thinking about them years earlier.

This guide covers the main pieces of long-term care planning: what care costs, who pays, how Medicaid works, the look-back period, protecting a home, helping a parent plan and preventing exploitation. Each section links to a deeper article. **A caution on Medicaid:** federal law sets a framework, but asset limits, home equity limits, look-back details, income rules and estate recovery vary by state and change over time. Confirm current rules locally before acting.

## What is long-term care?

Long-term care is help with the activities of daily living, such as bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, transferring and walking, and with managing a household. It is not mainly medical treatment. It can take place at home, in assisted living, in a memory care unit or in a nursing home.

The need often arises from dementia, stroke, Parkinson's disease, a fall or a progressive illness. Needs usually grow gradually, which makes planning possible, but a single event can change everything in a day.

| Setting | What it provides | Typical cost pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Home care | Aides for a few hours a day to around the clock | Hourly; costs rise quickly with hours |
| Assisted living | Housing, meals and help with daily tasks | Monthly fee plus add-ons for higher care |
| Memory care | Secured setting for dementia | Higher monthly fee |
| Nursing home | 24-hour nursing and personal care | Highest daily rate; often over $100,000 a year for a private room |

Actual prices depend on your region, and local surveys and facility quotes give better numbers than national averages.

## Who pays for long-term care?

Four sources pay for most care.

1. **Your own resources.** Savings, retirement income, pensions and sometimes home equity.
2. **Long-term care insurance.** Traditional policies and hybrid life or annuity policies with care benefits. Premiums rise with age, and health conditions can prevent purchase, which is why many buyers act in their 50s or early 60s.
3. **Medicare, for a short time only.** It covers limited skilled nursing after a hospital stay, with copayments after day 20 and a cap at day 100. It does not pay for ongoing custodial care.
4. **Medicaid.** The joint federal and state program that pays for long-term care for people who meet income and asset tests.

Veterans and surviving spouses may also qualify for VA benefits, such as Aid and Attendance, with their own rules. Check with an accredited representative.

## How does Medicaid pay for long-term care?

Medicaid pays after a person has used their countable resources down to a limit. For a single person the limit is low, commonly around $2,000 in many states. Exempt assets usually include a primary home within an equity limit, one vehicle, personal items and certain funeral arrangements. Income rules differ, and some states require income above a cap to pass through a special trust.

Federal law also protects a spouse who stays at home. The couple's assets are totaled when care begins, and the healthy spouse may keep a protected share, with federal minimums and maximums that are adjusted each year. The spouse may also keep the home and a car, and may be allowed some income from the institutionalized spouse.

The full overview is in [Medicaid planning for long-term care: the basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning), including what counts, how spouses are protected, planning tools and estate recovery.

## What is the five-year look-back?

When you apply for Medicaid long-term care, the state reviews transfers made in the previous 60 months, in most states. Gifts and sales for less than fair value in that window cause a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay for care. The length is the total transferred divided by a state-set monthly figure, and the penalty begins only when you are in care, have applied and are otherwise eligible.

Some transfers are exempt, including those to a spouse, to a disabled child or into certain trusts for a disabled person, and the home can pass to a qualifying caregiver child. The details are explained in [the Medicaid five-year look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

### Example

Samir, 77, gives his daughter $90,000 and soon after moves to assisted living. Two years later he needs a nursing home and applies for Medicaid. The gift falls inside the 60-month window, so a penalty applies. If instead Samir had set up a trust with the help of an attorney more than five years before he needed care, that portion would have been outside the look-back. The difference was timing and structure.

## Can you protect your home?

Often partly. During your life, the home is usually exempt if equity is under your state's limit and you or a spouse live there or you intend to return. After death, though, states must try to recover Medicaid costs from the estate, and some states reach assets that pass outside probate.

Common tools include exempt transfers to a spouse, a disabled child or a caregiver child, irrevocable trusts set up well in advance, and life estate deeds. Casual solutions such as signing the house over to a child can create a penalty, lose the tax basis step-up and expose the house to the child's creditors. See [can you protect your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).

## What documents should be in place before care is needed?

These should be signed while the person still has capacity, because after that the only route may be court guardianship.

- **Durable financial power of attorney.** Lets an agent manage money, pay bills and apply for benefits. Make sure it covers Medicaid planning, gifting and trust funding if desired. See [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney).
- **Healthcare power of attorney and living will.** Name a decision-maker and state preferences. See [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).
- **HIPAA authorization.** Allows family to talk to doctors.
- **Will or revocable trust.** Directs property and names a trustee or executor.
- **Beneficiary designations.** Reviewed for retirement accounts, annuities and life insurance.
- **Trusts for dependents.** If a family member has a disability, a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs) can protect their benefits while a parent uses Medicaid or leaves an inheritance.

Our article on [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) covers how to start the conversation, assemble records and avoid conflicts among siblings.

## What if the person is already losing capacity?

Capacity is judged at the moment of signing. People in the early stages of dementia can often still sign if they understand the document, and an attorney will assess this. Waiting risks losing the ability to sign altogether, after which a family may need a conservatorship or guardianship, which is public, slow and costly.

A cluster of tools helps when time is short: signing a power of attorney and healthcare documents immediately, collecting records for a Medicaid application, using exempt transfers, and focusing on spouse protections.

## How do you protect older adults from exploitation?

As finances and judgment change, risk rises. Most large losses are caused by someone known to the victim, such as a relative, caregiver or advisor, and scams add to the exposure. Warning signs include unexplained withdrawals, sudden document changes, isolation and unpaid bills.

Safeguards include naming a trusted contact at financial institutions, setting alerts, freezing credit, limiting powers in a power of attorney and requiring accountings. Reports go to adult protective services, the police and the financial institution. The details are in [protecting older adults from financial exploitation](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse).

## How does long-term care planning fit with the rest of an estate plan?

Care costs change what a family has left to pass on, so the rest of the plan should reflect them. Three connections stand out.

- **Trusts.** A revocable trust helps avoid probate and manage incapacity but does not protect against Medicaid. An irrevocable trust can, with tradeoffs. Our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) compares the types.
- **Special needs.** Parents who plan for a child with a disability need to coordinate their own care costs with the child's benefits. See [special needs planning](/learn/special-needs).
- **Power of attorney and healthcare directives.** The documents above are the foundation for any care plan.

## What are the common mistakes?

- **Assuming Medicare will pay.** Families often learn after a hospital stay that custodial care is not covered.
- **Giving assets away informally.** Gifts to children can start a penalty period, lose the tax basis step-up and expose the assets to the child's creditors or divorce.
- **Adding children to accounts or the deed.** It feels simple, but it can be a transfer, can cause conflicts and may not protect anything.
- **Waiting for a crisis.** After a fall or diagnosis, there is less time and fewer choices, and a person may no longer be able to sign.
- **Using a generic power of attorney.** Many forms do not authorize gifting, trust funding or Medicaid planning, which leaves the agent unable to act.
- **Treating the healthy spouse as an afterthought.** The spouse at home needs income and housing for the rest of their life, and federal protections help, but only if the paperwork is done correctly.
- **Ignoring the state.** A rule from a neighbor in another state, or an article that speaks nationally, may not match yours.

## How do you choose a care setting and keep the plan flexible?

Planning for money and planning for care go together. Visit places before you need them, ask how a facility handles residents whose private funds run out, and ask whether it accepts Medicaid, since some facilities limit the number of Medicaid beds. Keep the plan flexible, because needs change: a parent who starts with a few hours of help at home may later need assisted living or memory care. Revisit the plan after any hospital stay, change in diagnosis, death of a spouse or major change in finances.

## What about caregivers in the family?

Many families provide care themselves, and the time and money involved are real. A written personal care agreement, signed before care begins and paying a fair hourly rate, can compensate a family caregiver without creating a gift that Medicaid would penalize. Without one, payments to a child may be treated as transfers for less than fair value. Keep a log of the care provided as well, since it also supports the caregiver child exemption for the home.

## What is a sensible order of operations?

1. **Now.** Sign or update a durable power of attorney, healthcare power of attorney, living will and HIPAA authorization.
2. **Within the year.** Gather accounts, deeds, insurance and beneficiary forms. Review how care would be paid for and how long your resources would last.
3. **In your 50s and early 60s.** Compare long-term care insurance and hybrid policies while they are available and affordable.
4. **At least five years before likely care.** If Medicaid may be needed, ask an attorney about planning that works inside the look-back window.
5. **When care begins.** Apply with complete records, use spouse protections and exempt transfers, and keep a log of care and expenses.
