The call usually comes on a Tuesday. A fall, a stroke, a hospital discharge planner who says Dad cannot go home, and you have until Friday to choose a facility. Nobody has applied for Medicaid, nobody has read the five years of bank statements, and the family wants to know whether anything can still be protected.

Often something can. Crisis planning is the name for planning that begins after the need arrives. It has fewer tools than planning done years earlier, but it is far from hopeless, and the first few weeks matter more than any other stretch. This page covers what is usually possible and the order to do it in. The background rules are in our [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## What is Medicaid crisis planning?

Crisis planning is lawful rearranging of a person's finances after long-term care has become likely or has begun, so that the person can qualify for Medicaid sooner and keep more than they would by doing nothing. It is not hiding money. Every step is disclosed on the application, and every transfer is documented.

The difference from early planning is the clock. With five years' notice, a family can place assets in an [irrevocable trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) and let the look-back run out. In a crisis, that window is mostly gone. The tools left are the ones that work immediately.

## What tools still work after the need is here?

Most plans use some mix of the following.

- **Spend-down on things the parent needs.** Paying off a mortgage, repairing a roof, replacing an unsafe car, buying a hearing aid or dental work, prepaying a funeral. These swap cash for value and carry no penalty. See [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down).
- **Spousal transfers.** A transfer to a spouse is exempt from penalty. For a married couple this is the strongest crisis tool, because the well spouse can keep far more than the person in care. [How Medicaid treats married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples) explains the protected amounts.
- **Exempt transfers of the home.** The house can go without penalty to a spouse, a minor or disabled child, or a child who lived there and provided care for at least two years.
- **A trust for a disabled child.** A transfer to a trust solely for a disabled child is exempt; see [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).
- **An income trust or annuity.** Where income is above the state cap, a [Miller trust](/learn/elder-care/income-cap-trust-miller-trust) can fix eligibility. A compliant [annuity](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-compliant-annuity) can turn a spouse's countable savings into income.
- **Partial gift plus a private-pay plan.** In some states a family gifts a portion, applies, and uses the retained money to pay during the penalty. It is delicate and depends entirely on how the state starts the penalty clock, so run the numbers before anything moves.

## What should happen in the first two weeks?

Order matters. A workable sequence looks like this.

1. **Secure authority.** Check whether the parent signed a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney). Without one, and with the parent unable to sign, a guardianship may be the only route to manage money, and that takes longer.
2. **Freeze new gifts.** No more transfers to anyone until a plan is made, including loans that will not be repaid.
3. **Gather records.** Five years of statements for every account, deeds, insurance policies, tax returns, trust documents and a list of large withdrawals with explanations. Closed accounts count.
4. **Find the numbers.** The state's asset limit for a single applicant, its divisor, its home equity cap and the community spouse allowances. These change every year, so use current figures from the agency.
5. **Choose the facility with Medicaid in mind.** Ask whether it is certified for Medicaid, how many beds it keeps for Medicaid residents and how long a resident can stay if private funds run out.
6. **Build the plan, then apply.** Put the plan in writing, make the permitted moves, and file with a complete package.

## Does the application date matter?

Yes, a great deal. Many states can pay for care retroactively for a short window, often up to three months, before the month of application, if the person was eligible then. That means delay is not always fatal, but it is not something to count on, and the countable-asset test usually applies on a fixed date each month.

Federal rules give a state up to 45 days to decide most applications. Missing records, a disability determination or a question about a transfer stretches it. Keep the nursing home's business office informed, because many will wait on a pending application but expect payment if it is denied.

## What does a crisis plan look like for a married couple?

Take Dolores and Frank. Frank, 84, has a stroke and moves to a nursing home. They own a house worth $310,000, a car, and $180,000 in savings. Dolores is 80 and healthy.

The house and car are exempt while Dolores lives there. The savings are totaled at the start of the stay. Depending on the state's rule, Dolores can keep half, up to a federal cap, or a fixed amount. The rest above the limit for Frank has to be spent on allowed items, or turned into income by a compliant annuity for Dolores. She can also have Frank's retirement funds retitled to her, which is an exempt transfer. Gifts to the children are the one thing that would hurt.

Compare Frank alone and widowed. Same house, same $180,000, but no spouse to transfer to. The options shrink to spend-down, an exempt child transfer if one fits, and a gift-and-pay plan. That gap is the reason marital status shapes the whole conversation.

## What are the common crisis mistakes?

- **Co-signing the admission contract.** A nursing home cannot condition admission on a relative's personal promise to pay. Sign only in the parent's name, as agent.
- **Selling assets at a discount.** A house sold to a nephew for much less than it is worth is a gift.
- **Moving money to a child's account "to be safe."** It can be a transfer, and it exposes the money to the child's creditors.
- **Paying family back in a lump sum.** Reimbursement without a written agreement made earlier looks like a gift. Our piece on [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) covers what holds up.
- **Waiting to ask.** A single consultation in week one can prevent a penalty that costs months of care.

## How does state law change crisis planning?

Almost every number is set by the state: the asset limit, the divisor, the home equity cap, whether retroactive coverage exists, how it treats annuities and promissory notes, and whether it will cure a penalty by returning gifts. The framework is federal, but two families with the same facts can get different answers in neighboring states. Start with your [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) or the one for the parent's state, and then the agency's current figures.

Before you spend a dollar, read our piece on [Medicaid and gifting to children](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid) for what a gift does in the middle of a crisis.
