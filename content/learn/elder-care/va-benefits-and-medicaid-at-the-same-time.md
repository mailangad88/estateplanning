A veteran's family often learns about VA money and Medicaid at different times, from different people, and assumes one cancels the other. It usually does not. They can work together. They can also trip over each other in a few specific places: income counting, the pension cut for nursing home residents, and look-back periods that do not match.

This article covers those places. It assumes you already know the basics of the VA benefit, which are in our article on [VA Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance), and it follows the money from the VA to the nursing home. It belongs to our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## The short version

- You can hold VA benefits and Medicaid at once. Neither program excludes the other.
- VA money is tax-free at the federal level. Military retired pay is not.
- Medicaid counts VA payments as income, and the nursing home gets most of it.
- The VA pension drops sharply when Medicaid pays for a nursing home. It does not drop for assisted living or home care.
- A transfer that is safe for the VA after 36 months may still be a penalty for Medicaid at 40.

## Are VA benefits taxable?

No, not for federal income tax. VA disability compensation, VA pension, Aid and Attendance, and survivor payments such as Dependency and Indemnity Compensation are not included in gross income and are not reported on a federal return. Most states follow suit, but check yours if you have a state tax return.

Two things get confused with VA benefits and are taxable. Military retired pay, earned through years of service, is taxed like a pension. And Social Security, which many veterans also receive, may be partly taxable depending on total income. A veteran who waives some retired pay to receive VA disability compensation exchanges taxable money for tax-free money, which is a reason the arithmetic is worth doing once.

For the wider tax picture of a parent's care, see [tax breaks when you pay for a parent's care](/learn/elder-care/tax-breaks-when-you-pay-for-a-parents-care).

## How does Medicaid count VA income?

It counts it. Medicaid long-term care takes the person's gross monthly income, subtracts a small personal needs allowance and some other deductions, and directs the rest to the nursing home as the patient's share of cost. Disability compensation and pension both go into that total.

Take Arlen, 88, a veteran with Social Security, a small employer pension and a VA pension with Aid and Attendance. Medicaid adds all three. If the combined figure goes over the state's income cap for nursing home Medicaid, which applies in some states, he may need a [Miller or qualified income trust](/learn/elder-care/income-cap-trust-miller-trust) to qualify. In states without that cap, the extra income simply increases his share of cost.

The Aid and Attendance part of a pension is paid because of unreimbursed care costs. Some states count it as income anyway and some give it special treatment, so this is a question to ask the state or an attorney, not to assume.

## What happens to the VA pension when Medicaid pays for a nursing home?

It is reduced to a small monthly amount. Under federal law, when a veteran with no spouse or dependent child is in a Medicaid-covered nursing home, the VA cuts the pension to a small fixed figure, which the VA publishes and adjusts. The reduction takes effect after a short grace period following the start of Medicaid-paid care. Surviving spouse pensions are reduced as well. VA disability compensation for service-connected conditions is not reduced.

The point of the rule is that Medicaid is paying for everything, so the VA does not want to pay twice. The result is that Medicaid pays the bulk of the bill, the patient keeps the small VA amount, and the personal needs allowance is protected.

This matters for planning. A family that counts on a VA pension to supplement a nursing home on Medicaid will find it gone. A family deciding between assisted living and a nursing home should know that the reduction does not apply to assisted living, where Medicaid usually does not pay the room and board and the VA benefit continues in full. See [assisted living, memory care and hospice: who pays](/learn/elder-care/assisted-living-memory-care-and-hospice-who-pays).

| Setting | Who pays | What happens to the VA pension |
|---|---|---|
| At home with an aide | Private pay, VA, possibly a Medicaid waiver | Continues; Aid and Attendance helps cover the aide |
| Assisted living | Private pay, VA benefit, sometimes a Medicaid waiver | Continues in full |
| Nursing home, private pay | The family | Continues |
| Nursing home, Medicaid pays | Medicaid, plus patient income | Pension reduced to a small amount; compensation unchanged |
| VA or state veterans home | VA per diem and the resident | Depends on the home's rules; ask the admissions office |

## Do the VA and Medicaid count assets the same way?

No, and that is where families get hurt. The two programs have their own financial tests.

1. **The VA looks at net worth.** Assets and annual income combine into one number, with deductions for medical costs. The primary home is excluded, up to a lot-size limit.
2. **Medicaid looks at countable assets.** It tests the total of both spouses' resources against a low limit, with the home exempt up to an equity cap. See [what counts as a countable asset](/learn/elder-care/what-counts-as-a-countable-asset-for-medicaid).
3. **Look-back periods differ.** The VA looks back 36 months for pension claims. Medicaid looks back 60 months in most states.
4. **Penalties work differently.** The VA penalty is based on the amount transferred beyond what would have kept you under the limit, up to five years. The Medicaid penalty is the transfer divided by a state divisor, as shown in [how long the penalty lasts](/learn/elder-care/half-a-loaf-gifting-and-how-long-the-medicaid-penalty-lasts).

A gift made three and a half years ago is outside the VA's window. It may sit inside Medicaid's. If you are building a plan for both, use the longer clock.

## Should you apply for the VA benefit or Medicaid first?

It depends on where the person lives and what they have. A veteran in assisted living with savings above the Medicaid limit usually has no Medicaid option yet, and the VA benefit can start without spending the savings to the bone. A veteran already in a nursing home with few assets will probably qualify for Medicaid sooner, and the VA pension adds little once Medicaid starts paying.

A middle path is common: apply for the VA benefit while the person is in assisted living, then plan for Medicaid when a nursing home becomes necessary, knowing the pension will drop then. The VA claim can take months, so file before the money gets tight. Only VA-accredited representatives may help prepare the claim, as noted in the [VA article](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance). Families sometimes run into unaccredited businesses that charge for "benefits planning" and also suggest moving assets. Pause before moving anything on that advice, since it can create a Medicaid penalty.

A power of attorney should authorize the agent to deal with both agencies. Without it, a son or daughter may be refused. See [power of attorney and Social Security, Medicare and the IRS](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-a-power-of-attorney-work-with-social-security-medicare-and-the-irs) for how agencies treat the form. For a surviving spouse, the separate survivor benefits are covered in [claiming life insurance, pension and VA survivor benefits](/learn/after-a-death/claiming-life-insurance-pension-and-va-survivor-benefits).

## What about estate recovery?

The VA pension and compensation are not subject to a VA estate recovery program the way Medicaid is. Medicaid estate recovery is a separate matter and applies to the Medicaid benefits paid, not to the VA money. The rules and the assets a state can reach are in [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery).
