For many families the house is the largest asset and the one that matters most emotionally. When a parent needs nursing home care, the first fear is often that the state will take the home. The truth is more nuanced: in most cases the state cannot take it during life, but it may try to recover costs later, and the way a family responds in a panic can create new problems.

This guide separates the two risks, explains the common tools and their tradeoffs, and flags where state rules differ. **Asset limits, home equity limits, look-back details and estate recovery rules vary by state.** It is part of our [long-term care guide](/learn/elder-care).

## Does Medicaid count your home?

During your life, a primary home is usually exempt, within a limit. Federal law excludes the home from countable assets if equity does not exceed a minimum amount that, as of 2026, is roughly $750,000, and states may set the limit up to about $1.1 million. Many states use the lower figure and some use the higher. Equity above the limit disqualifies the applicant until it is reduced, for example by a reverse mortgage or a home equity loan, depending on state law.

The exclusion usually applies when at least one of the following is true:

- You say you intend to return home, even if doctors think you won't
- Your spouse lives there
- A dependent relative, such as a minor or disabled child, lives there (details vary by state)

Rules differ on what happens when a single applicant has no realistic chance of returning. Some states require a showing of subjective intent only; others look at medical evidence and may treat the home as countable. Check your state's wording.

## What is estate recovery, and how does it reach the house?

Estate recovery is the larger risk. States must try to recover the cost of Medicaid nursing home care, and related services, from the estates of recipients who were 55 or older, or permanently institutionalized at any age. The home is often the main asset.

Key points:

- **Probate estate.** Every state can reach assets that pass through probate. A house owned in your name alone is part of that.
- **Expanded estate.** Many states also reach assets that pass outside probate, such as a house with a transfer-on-death deed, a life estate interest, joint property or a living trust. Others do not. This variation is the single most important thing to check.
- **Deferrals.** Recovery is not allowed while a surviving spouse lives, or while a minor, blind or disabled child lives. States must also have hardship waivers, though they are narrow.
- **Liens.** Some states may place a lien on the home during life under limited circumstances, which can be enforced at sale or death.

A family that sells the house before applying may lose the exempt status and trigger a spend-down. A family that does nothing may find the state claims much of the house after death. Planning aims to reach the middle.

## What happens if you give the house to your children?

Giving a house to children is the most common and riskiest move. It is a transfer for less than fair value and starts a penalty period under the [look-back rule](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) unless an exemption applies. It has other costs:

- **Loss of the step-up in basis.** A child who inherits receives the home at its date-of-death value for tax purposes, which can erase gain. A child who receives it as a gift takes the parent's original basis and may owe capital gains tax on a later sale. See [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis).
- **Exposure to the child's problems.** The house can be reached by the child's creditors, lawsuits, bankruptcy or divorce, and if the child dies first it can pass to the child's spouse.
- **No right to stay.** Once you no longer own it, you have no legal right to live there unless you reserve one.
- **Property tax effects.** Some states reassess property on transfer, with exceptions for certain family transfers.

Our article on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) explains these problems in detail.

## Which transfers of the home are exempt?

Federal law permits transfers of a home without penalty to:

| Recipient | Condition |
|---|---|
| Spouse | None |
| Child who is under 21, or blind or disabled | None beyond disability proof |
| Adult child who lived in the home for at least two years before the parent entered care, and provided care that delayed institutionalization | The caregiver child exemption, documented |
| Sibling who has an equity interest in the home and lived there for at least one year before | A narrow exemption |
| A trust solely for a disabled person under 65 | Usually a payback trust |

The caregiver child exemption is the one most families can use. Evidence generally includes medical statements that the parent needed care, proof the child lived there, and records of care provided. A child who moved in only to help in the last few months generally does not qualify.

## What tools can protect the home?

| Tool | How it works | Strengths | Tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Irrevocable trust | Parent transfers the house to a trust, often keeping the right to live there | Protected after the look-back period; can avoid probate and estate recovery in many states | Loss of control; must be done years ahead; income tax and basis rules need review |
| Life estate deed | Parent keeps the right to live there for life; children own the remainder | Simple; passes outside probate | Starts look-back; children must join in a sale; some states still recover |
| Transfer-on-death deed | Parent keeps ownership, named person receives at death | Flexible; no look-back transfer during life | Does not protect from recovery in states that reach non-probate assets; see [TOD deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) |
| Sale to a child at fair value | Child pays market price, often with a note | Fair-value sale is not a gift | Needs appraisal and records; the note counts as an asset |
| Caregiver child transfer | Home deeded to the qualifying child | No penalty | Child must qualify; basis and creditor issues remain |
| Spend-down with improvements | Use cash for repairs and accessibility on the home | Raises the home's value but not countable | Not recoverable if you sell |

There is no tool that is best in every state. A trust may be excellent where recovery reaches only probate assets, and less useful where the state's recovery definition is broad.

### Example

Evelyn, 74, owns a $320,000 home in a state with a home equity limit of about $750,000 and an expanded estate recovery definition. Her son Jamal lived with her for six years and managed her medications and appointments after her stroke. When she moves to a nursing home, her attorney documents the care and deeds the house to Jamal under the caregiver child exemption, with no penalty. If instead Evelyn had deeded the house to both of her children, the transfer would have triggered a penalty, and her other child's divorce would later have put the house at risk.

## What if you have a spouse at home?

The home stays exempt while a spouse lives there, and a transfer to the spouse is not penalized. Some couples retitle the house solely in the community spouse's name so it is not held jointly with the institutionalized spouse, which can simplify later steps. After the community spouse dies, however, the home goes into their estate and the plan needs to address what happens next. Wills and trusts for the surviving spouse often are structured with Medicaid in mind.

## When should you plan?

The most flexible planning happens at least five years before you need care, when an irrevocable trust or deed does not trigger a penalty. If you are already in or near care, exempt transfers, a spouse's protections and a fair-value sale may still be available.

Do these first, in order:

1. Learn your state's home equity limit and estate recovery definition.
2. Get a current appraisal and a list of any mortgage or liens.
3. Confirm who lives in the home and for how long.
4. Review your [will or trust options for the home](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children).
5. Talk with an attorney before signing any deed.
