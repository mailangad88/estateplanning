When a parent's care is about to cost more than the family can pay, one of the first questions is whether to hire a lawyer, and what that will cost. The honest answer is a range, because Medicaid planning is not one product. A one-hour conversation, a trust and a full crisis application are different jobs with different prices.

This page explains what moves the fee, the usual pricing structures, what you get for the money and when a family can reasonably go it alone. It is part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care). We have left out dollar figures on purpose: they vary too much by region and by firm to be useful, and any number printed here would be out of date or wrong for you.

## What decides the price?

Five things do most of the work.

1. **Timing.** Planning five years ahead is slower and calmer than a crisis plan done in two weeks, and the fee reflects the hours.
2. **Complexity of the assets.** A parent with a bank account and a house is a different file from one with rental property, an annuity, an IRA, a business interest and a second state.
3. **Marital status.** A married couple involves the community spouse's allowances, an income calculation and often an annuity or retitling.
4. **Documents needed.** A trust, a deed, a power of attorney, a health care directive and a will each take drafting time.
5. **Whether the lawyer files the application.** Preparing the Medicaid application, gathering records and answering the agency's requests is a large job on its own.

## How do attorneys price Medicaid planning?

| Structure | How it works | Fits best |
|---|---|---|
| Consultation fee | A set fee for a meeting and a plan outline. | Deciding whether and how to proceed. |
| Flat fee for a package | One price for a defined list of documents or services. | Advance planning, trust and related papers. |
| Hourly | Billed for time at a stated rate, often against a deposit. | Open-ended work, appeals, negotiation. |
| Hybrid | A flat fee to plan, hourly after. | Crisis cases with an uncertain finish. |

Ask for the structure in writing. A good engagement letter says what the fee covers, what it does not, who does the work and what happens if the case takes longer than expected.

### What is usually billed on top?

Third-party costs are normally separate from the attorney's fee. Examples include recording fees for a new deed, an appraisal of real estate, certified copies of records, a notary, and any state filing charge. A trust-based plan may also carry a trustee or accounting cost later. Ask which of these apply so the total is not a surprise.

## What does the work include?

A fuller engagement usually covers a review of the last five years of finances, a written strategy, drafting and signing of the needed documents, help moving assets correctly, assembly of the application and follow-up with the agency. A narrower one might be just the review and a recommendation. Neither is wrong. They suit different families.

Here is a sample comparison for two parents. Marguerite, 78, is single, has a house and $90,000, and has made no gifts. Her son wants advice and a power of attorney signed. That is a small engagement. Walter and June have a house, a rental, an IRA, a trust created years ago and a gift to a grandson two years back, and Walter is entering care next month. That is a large one, because every piece needs analysis before the application is filed.

## When can you do it yourself?

Sometimes. A do-it-yourself approach has the best chance when all of these are true:

- One applicant, no spouse to protect.
- Assets are already below the state's limit, or only need ordinary spending on allowed items.
- No gifts or transfers in five years.
- No trust, annuity or real estate beyond an exempt home.
- Records are complete and easy to get.

Even then, the nursing home's business office or a county benefits worker can help with forms. They are not advising on strategy, though. If something looks wrong, a one-time legal review is cheaper than a denial.

## When is a lawyer clearly worth it?

The pattern is consistent: the more money, the more moving parts and the shorter the time, the more a lawyer saves. Gifts inside the look-back, a home that might be reached by estate recovery, a spouse at home, a business or a disabled child as beneficiary all raise the stakes. One avoidable month of penalty at a nursing home can exceed the cost of the planning engagement, which is why the fee tends to look small in hindsight.

A fair question to ask a lawyer is what they expect to save you, and on what assumptions. A careful answer names the exemptions, the state rule and the uncertainty. Be wary of a firm that promises a result or describes a trick.

## What does the fee not cover?

Planning fees do not include the cost of care itself, and they do not pay a penalty period if one is unavoidable. Families sometimes expect the attorney's work to make the nursing home bills disappear. It will not. What good planning does is shorten the time you pay privately, protect the amount a spouse or the family can keep, and avoid mistakes that add months. It also lines up the paperwork, so a denial for a missing statement does not cost you a month of coverage.

It helps to separate three kinds of cost. The first is the legal fee. The second is the cost of carrying out the plan, such as a deed recording or a trust account. The third is the cost of care during any gap before approval. Ask the attorney to estimate all three, even roughly, so you can see the whole picture before you commit.

## How do you compare quotes?

1. Ask what is included, by task, and what is not.
2. Ask who will do the work and who will answer your calls.
3. Ask how the fee changes if there is a denial or an appeal.
4. Ask whether the firm prepares the application or only advises.
5. Ask how they handle your state's specific rules, such as its divisor and treatment of annuities.
6. Check that the person has Medicaid experience in your state. Our guide to [choosing an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) lists questions that apply here too.

## Are there ways to pay less?

Yes. Gather the five years of statements before the first meeting so billable time goes to advice, not collecting. Bring the existing documents: deed, trust, will, power of attorney and any insurance policy. Ask for a phased scope, starting with a review. Our checklist of [what to bring to a consultation](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) works here too. And check your local legal aid, Area Agency on Aging and senior legal services program for no-cost help if your income is limited.
