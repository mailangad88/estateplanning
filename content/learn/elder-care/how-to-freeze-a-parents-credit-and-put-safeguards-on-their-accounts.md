Your father, Walter, is 81 and sharp about the Cubs and not so sharp about the phone. Last month he mailed a gift card number to a "grandson in trouble." The grandson was fine. Walter was embarrassed, and you found out only because the bank called you. Now you want to lock things down, and he does not want to feel like a child.

This article is a practical sequence for a parent who is still making their own decisions. It covers credit freezes, bank and brokerage settings, mail and phone, and what to do when generosity turns into a pattern. For a wider look at warning signs and who preys on older adults, see our guide to [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse). The planning side lives in our [elder care guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What can you do without any legal authority?

Quite a lot, as long as your parent agrees and sits beside you. Authority matters only when your parent cannot act. Until then, a parent can do each of these, with your help:

| Safeguard | What it blocks | Who must act |
|---|---|---|
| Credit freeze at all three bureaus | New credit accounts opened with their identity | Your parent, or an agent with documents |
| Fraud alerts on bank and card accounts | Unusual spending and large transfers go unnoticed | Your parent authorizes, you can receive copies |
| Trusted contact at bank and brokerage | Staff have someone to call about suspected exploitation | Your parent names you |
| IRS Identity Protection PIN | False tax returns | Your parent requests it |
| Opt out of prescreened offers and calls | Pre-approved credit mail and cold calls | Your parent |
| Separate low-balance spending account | Large losses from one account | Your parent opens it |
| Mail forwarding or a locked mailbox | Stolen statements and checks | Your parent |

Start with the first one, because it is the simplest and the most protective.

## How do you freeze someone's credit?

Each of the three nationwide bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) places a freeze for free, and a freeze at one does not cover the other two. Follow these steps:

1. **Gather identifying details.** You need your parent's full name, address, date of birth and Social Security number, plus a recent mailing address if they moved.
2. **Create an online account or call each bureau.** The online route usually takes a few minutes. Each bureau issues a PIN or a login. Write it down and store it with the household's important papers.
3. **Request the freeze at all three.** A freeze placed online or by phone is generally in effect within one business day. A freeze by mail takes longer.
4. **Record the PINs and dates.** If your parent forgets a PIN, recovery requires proof of identity, which is a nuisance.
5. **Plan for the thaw.** When your parent applies for a car loan, a new credit card or even some rental housing, they can lift the freeze for a set period and re-freeze it afterward.

A freeze does not affect a credit score, existing accounts or the parent's ability to use a card. It does not stop a thief who takes over an existing account, which is why the next steps matter. It also does not stop a fraudulent tax return or a Medicare scam.

If a parent can no longer act for themselves, the bureaus accept a freeze from someone with a valid power of attorney or a court appointment, though the paperwork differs from one bureau to the next. Call each bureau's customer line and ask what documents they want before sending anything.

## What should you ask the bank and the brokerage to do?

Ask the bank to set text and email alerts for large withdrawals, new payees and wire transfers. Ask the branch manager to note the file with a trusted contact. Financial firms handle this differently, but most now allow a customer to name a person the firm may call if it believes the customer is being exploited. A trusted contact is not an agent. That person cannot see the balance, move money or change anything, and does not have to be a relative.

Other settings to ask about:

- **Transaction limits** on the debit card and on online transfers.
- **A second signer or "view only" access** for you, with your parent's written permission.
- **Paper statements sent to two addresses**, or an online login that lets you view without moving money.
- **Account titling.** Adding a child as a joint owner for convenience puts that child's creditors, divorce and lawsuits in the picture. A power of attorney or "view only" access is usually safer. Our guide to [joint property risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) covers the same trap for real estate.

Walter might keep a checking account with a few thousand dollars for daily life and a savings account the scammers never see. If he gets fooled again, the loss is capped.

## Which protections are about the mail and the phone?

Stolen mail is still a major route for identity theft. A locked mailbox, a post office box or paying bills online removes the easy theft. A parent can opt out of prescreened credit offers through the credit bureaus' joint opt-out service and register the home and cell numbers with the National Do Not Call Registry. Registration does not stop criminals, who ignore it, but it cuts the legitimate calls and makes the odd ones stand out. Many carriers also offer free spam-call filtering.

A short script helps. Tell your parent: "If anyone calls about money, a prize, the IRS, a grandchild or your computer, hang up and call me. I will not be upset." Say it more than once. The common thread in these scams is urgency and secrecy, and a script that flips both is the strongest protection.

## What if your parent keeps giving money away?

A mentally capable adult has the right to give money to anyone, including people you distrust, and you cannot override that. The distinction is capacity and pressure.

| Situation | What is going on | Reasonable response |
|---|---|---|
| Large gifts to a church, charity or grandchild | A choice that reflects values | Make sure the larger plan can absorb it; talk about budget |
| Regular transfers to a relative who always needs more | A relationship that may be unbalanced | Honest conversation, an agreed limit, an independent bank account for gifts |
| Money to an online contact or "investment" | Likely fraud | Call the bank, report to the authorities, discuss scam patterns |
| New confusion, forgotten bills, odd purchases | Possible cognitive decline | Medical evaluation, power of attorney now, review of accounts |
| A new "friend" or caregiver who controls access | Possible undue influence | Contact adult protective services and an attorney |

If the pattern points to decline, the most useful step is a medical evaluation and a durable power of attorney while your parent can still sign it. Without one, the options narrow to a court process described in our article on [parents with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney). If a parent says no to every suggestion, the guidance in [when a parent refuses help or is not safe alone](/learn/elder-care/when-a-parent-refuses-help-or-is-not-safe-alone) applies.

Gifts also have a Medicaid dimension. Money given away in the last five years can cause a penalty period if your parent later needs nursing home coverage, so a pattern of "helping" a relative can have a cost that appears years later. The [Medicaid look-back guide](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains how.

## How do you see their accounts if they cannot or will not share?

You cannot demand account information from a bank because you are the child. A bank speaks to the account holder, a joint owner or an agent with a valid document. If you need visibility, ask your parent to sign a power of attorney, add you as a view-only user, or share online statements. For medical information, a signed [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) lets providers speak with you, and our what-if on a [caregiver who cannot get medical or bank records](/learn/what-if/caregiver-cannot-get-medical-or-bank-records) shows how families get stuck when none of this was done.

## When is it abuse, and who do you call?

If you suspect a specific person is taking advantage of your parent through pressure, deception, forged checks or controlling access, treat it as exploitation. Call the bank's fraud line first, then adult protective services in your parent's county. Contact local police if money has been stolen. If the person doing it is the agent, see our guide to [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse). An attorney can then look at whether to revoke the document, replace the agent or ask a court for help.

## A one-weekend checklist

- Sit down with your parent and explain what you are doing and why.
- Freeze credit at all three bureaus and record the PINs.
- Set bank alerts and name a trusted contact at each firm.
- Open a small spending account and cap the debit card.
- Opt out of prescreened offers and register the phone numbers.
- Request an IRS Identity Protection PIN if your parent is willing.
- Gather the power of attorney, advance directive and HIPAA form, or schedule an appointment to sign them.
- Write down who the contact is at each bank, and where the PINs are stored.
