Harold, 79, has Alzheimer's and has been living in a memory care unit for two months. His wife Ruth, 77, is at home and still drives. Their savings total $220,000, and Ruth is worried that the nursing home bill will leave her with nothing. She has heard that Medicaid will "take everything" until a couple is broke. That is not how the rules work for married couples, and the reason is a federal law passed in 1988.

This article explains how Medicaid treats the two spouses as a unit, what the healthy spouse is allowed to keep, and where couples go wrong. It sits under our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care). Federal law sets the structure. Each state chooses certain options, and the dollar amounts change every year, so verify current figures with your state agency or an attorney.

## What are the spousal impoverishment rules?

Spousal impoverishment rules are federal protections, found in 42 U.S.C. 1396r-5, for the spouse of a person who needs Medicaid long-term care. They exist because, before 1988, a husband or wife at home often had to spend nearly everything before the other spouse could qualify. Congress decided that the person who stayed home should not be left poor.

The rules use two labels. The **institutionalized spouse** is the one who needs a nursing home level of care. The **community spouse** is the one who lives at home. In most states the same protections extend to a spouse whose partner receives care through a home and community-based waiver, although waiver rules vary.

There are three protections: a share of the couple's assets, a minimum level of income, and the home.

## How much of the savings can the healthy spouse keep?

The healthy spouse can keep up to half of the couple's countable assets, within a federal floor and ceiling. This is the community spouse resource allowance.

The process works in a fixed order:

1. **Snapshot date.** Medicaid looks at what the couple owned on the first day of a continuous stay of at least 30 days in a nursing home or similar care.
2. **Total the countable assets.** Everything both spouses own is added together, regardless of whose name is on the account. Exempt items such as the home and one car are left out.
3. **Divide.** The community spouse's protected amount is half the total, but not less than the federal minimum and not more than the federal maximum. Both numbers are adjusted for inflation each January. As of 2026 the minimum is in the low $30,000s and the maximum is in the $160,000s. Some states use the maximum as a flat amount rather than the half rule.
4. **Spend down the rest.** The institutionalized spouse's half, above the state's asset limit for a single person, must be used on care or on allowed purchases before Medicaid starts paying.
5. **Retitle.** After approval, the community spouse's assets are moved into that spouse's name alone, within a time the state sets.

Back to Harold and Ruth. With $220,000 in countable savings, half is $110,000. If the state uses the half rule and the federal minimum is lower than $110,000, Ruth can keep $110,000. Harold's half, minus the small single-person limit, is spent on care, or on permitted purchases, until he qualifies. Ruth also keeps the house and the car.

Couples with modest savings sometimes find that the federal minimum protects nearly all of it. Couples with larger savings find the maximum caps the protection, and that is where planning matters. See [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down) for the purchases that reduce countable assets without a penalty.

## How does income work for a married couple?

A spouse's income is not used to pay for the other spouse's care. Medicaid generally follows the "name on the check" rule, which means income is attributed to the spouse whose name it is paid in. Harold's pension and Social Security go toward his care. Ruth's own Social Security and pension stay hers.

When the community spouse has low income, the rules allow a transfer from the institutionalized spouse. This is the minimum monthly maintenance needs allowance, or MMMNA. It is a floor that the state raises to, using a formula:

- A federal minimum figure, adjusted every year.
- Plus an extra amount for housing costs that are above a set share of that minimum, including utilities.
- Capped at a federal maximum, unless a hearing officer or court orders more.

If Ruth's income falls below her floor, the state diverts a portion of Harold's income to her before computing what he owes the nursing home. The institutionalized spouse keeps a small personal needs allowance, which is set by the state and is often well under $100 a month in some states and higher in others.

A family allowance can also be given for dependent family members living with the community spouse.

## What happens to the house?

The home is exempt if the community spouse lives in it. The home equity cap that applies to a single applicant does not apply when a spouse or a dependent child is living there. Ruth keeps the house regardless of its value, and can stay as long as she wants.

The home becomes an issue later. If Harold dies first, the state's estate recovery claim for his care is postponed while Ruth is alive. After Ruth dies, the state may look at the house and anything else Harold owned that passed to her. For how that works, read [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery). A spouse who plans to leave the house to the children should talk about the timing and the title.

## Are transfers between spouses safe?

Transfers between spouses are exempt from the look-back penalty. That is why a couple can legally move assets into the healthy spouse's name. But the rule has an edge: the healthy spouse's own later gifts to children or others count as transfers. If Ruth gives $50,000 to a grandchild while Harold is in care, the state can apply a penalty to Harold, because the transfer came from assets that were counted for him. The [look-back rules](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) cover how penalty periods are measured.

## Can a couple protect more than the allowance?

Couples who have more than the maximum protected amount have a few options, which depend on the state.

| Strategy | What it does | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Higher allowance by hearing | Shows that the standard amount can't produce enough income | Needs documentation of income and expenses |
| Medicaid-compliant annuity | Converts the spend-down amount into income for the community spouse | Strict rules; the state is named as a remainder beneficiary |
| Spend-down on needs | Repairs, a new car, prepaid funeral, home improvements | Must be fair value |
| Half-a-loaf gifting | Gift part, use the rest to cover the penalty | Complex, depends on the state's penalty divisor |
| Trusts set up early | Remove assets from the count after five years | Loses control; must be done while healthy |

Some of these lean on the fact that spouses are allowed to buy and sell among themselves. They require an attorney who knows the state's current rules. Nothing here works well if it is improvised at the nursing home front desk.

## What documents should a couple have in place?

A married couple often assumes the spouse can automatically handle everything. In practice, a bank or Medicaid caseworker may require authority. Each spouse needs a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) naming the other and a successor, and the form should allow the agent to do Medicaid planning, make gifts and create trusts. A healthcare proxy and living will are equally important. Without them, a spouse who has lost capacity may need guardianship, which is slower and public.

Couples in their early seventies who read [estate planning for retirees](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-retirees) will see the same advice from a different angle: sort out authority while both spouses can sign.

## Common mistakes

- **Moving everything into the healthy spouse's name early.** It can look sensible, but the snapshot rule counts both spouses' assets anyway, and you lose flexibility.
- **Gifting to children while the spouse is in care.** The healthy spouse's gifts can create a penalty for the patient.
- **Waiting to apply.** Coverage generally starts when the application is complete and the applicant is eligible, so delays cost months.
- **Dropping life insurance or paying it off without checking.** A policy's cash value can count, depending on the face amount.
- **Forgetting the second event.** Plan for the community spouse's own future care and for what happens after the second death.
