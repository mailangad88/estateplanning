Medicaid eligibility for nursing home care comes down to one test: after the exempt items are set aside, how much do you own? People get this wrong in both directions. Some believe everything is taken, which is false. Others assume that putting a child on the account or moving things into a trust makes the money invisible, which is also false and sometimes costly.

The companion article on [which assets are exempt from Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/what-assets-are-exempt-from-medicaid) lists what is set aside. This page covers the other half: what counts, and especially the gray areas where the answer turns on how something is titled or drafted. It sits under our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What is a countable asset?

A countable asset, also called a resource, is property you own and could convert to cash to pay for your own care. The key question is access. If you hold the legal right to the money, or could get it by asking, Medicaid counts it. If you cannot reach it, it may not be counted.

The usual countable items are checking and savings accounts, certificates of deposit, brokerage accounts, stocks and bonds, retirement accounts in most states while they are not in payout status, cash value of life insurance above the exempt threshold, a second vehicle, land, a second home and rental property. Income is a separate subject. A pension check is income in the month it arrives, as explained in [income and bank accounts in a nursing home](/learn/elder-care/income-and-bank-accounts-in-a-nursing-home).

Value is generally fair market value less secured debt. The date matters too: many states use the first moment of the month for bank balances, so a payment that clears on the second day can fall on either side of the line.

## How does Medicaid treat a trust?

It looks at who created it, who can get money out of it and whether it can be changed. Those three answers sort nearly every trust.

| Type of trust | Counted? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Revocable living trust you created | Yes, in full | You can take the money back at any time |
| Irrevocable trust you created, trustee may pay principal to you | Yes, the amount that could be paid | The money is available to you |
| Irrevocable trust you created, income only to you | Income counts as income; principal generally does not | Principal is out of reach, but funding it is a transfer subject to the look-back |
| Trust created by someone else, trustee has full discretion | Usually not counted | You have no right to demand distributions |
| Trust for a disabled person under 65 with a state payback | Not counted as an asset | A special federal rule, discussed in the special needs cluster |
| Testamentary trust for a spouse or child | Depends on who is the beneficiary and who set it up | A surviving spouse's trust is usually safe if drafted for sole benefit |

A "Medicaid trust" bought off a website is only as good as its wording. The irrevocable plan described in [Medicaid asset protection trusts](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) works because you give up the right to the principal and wait out the five-year window. A trust that lets you change your mind, or take money back, protects nothing. Our [guide to irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) explains the control you give up.

## What about a joint account with a child?

A joint account is counted in full as the applicant's unless the other owner can prove they put the money in. This is the presumption in most states, and it catches families who added a daughter to the account "just in case." Medicaid does not care that she was added for convenience. It cares who deposited the funds.

Rosa, 83, added her son Miguel to her checking account to help pay bills. The balance is $62,000, nearly all from Rosa's Social Security and savings. When she applies, the agency counts all $62,000. Miguel can try to show that part came from his own paychecks, but if he cannot document it, the account is hers.

The trap on the other side is withdrawing the money. If Miguel moves $30,000 to his own account to remove it from the count, the agency treats that as a transfer by Rosa, and a penalty can follow. The steps that work are more modest:

1. **Gather statements** for the joint account from at least the last five years.
2. **Identify deposits** that came from the child, with matching pay stubs or bank records.
3. **Write down the account's purpose.** A convenience account is one the child uses to pay the parent's bills.
4. **Let the caseworker see it** instead of waiting to be asked. Hidden accounts are what cause denials.

See [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) for the legal exposure that comes with joint ownership even outside Medicaid.

## Does a second home or other real estate count?

Yes, unless an exception applies. The exemption for a primary residence is for the home you live in or intend to return to. A lake cabin, a rental house, a condo in another state and an inherited property are all countable at their equity.

Some states give a limited exclusion for property that produces income and is necessary for self-support, such as a working farm or a small rental business, and some count the rental income only. Others expect a plan to sell. If the property is held with siblings after a parent's death and cannot be sold without their consent, you may be able to argue that the interest is unavailable; see [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) for how those titles arise.

Selling at a fair price is not a penalty event, but the cash from the sale counts the moment it lands. Selling to a relative below market value is a transfer. If the house is going to be sold anyway, it is worth discussing with a lawyer whether a payment plan, a care contract or an exempt transfer would fit before the listing goes up.

## Does Medicaid count my spouse's assets?

Yes. For a married applicant, the state adds up both spouses' countable assets, whoever's name is on them, as of the start of the continuous stay. The healthy spouse then keeps a protected portion. If your spouse owns a second home or an IRA, it is part of the pool. The calculation is covered in [how Medicaid works for a married couple](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples).

An unmarried partner's assets do not count, and neither do your adult children's. Medicaid does not look at the children's finances to decide your eligibility, though they may be asked for records that show where your money went.

## What about assets you cannot get to?

An asset that is not actually available to you is often excluded. Examples include a share of property held by several heirs where the others will not sell, funds frozen by a court, or an interest that cannot be sold because of its terms. Two limits apply. You must show you tried to make the asset available or that you cannot, and the exclusion ends the day the barrier does.

A pending inheritance or lawsuit is a similar issue. You generally do not own the money until you have a right to receive it. After that it counts, as explained in [whether an inheritance affects Medicaid or SSI](/learn/after-a-death/does-an-inheritance-affect-medicaid-or-ssi). Refusing an inheritance after you apply is usually treated as giving it away, though a formal disclaimer made before you need Medicaid is a different matter, and our glossary entry on [the look-back period](/glossary/look-back-period) shows why the dates matter.

## What should you do before you apply?

Find out what is counted before you decide what to do about it. A short sequence works well.

1. **List every account and asset** for both spouses, including anything held jointly or in trust.
2. **Mark each as exempt, countable or unclear.** The unclear list is the one to bring to an attorney.
3. **Pull five years of statements.** The application will ask for them anyway; see [applying for Medicaid for a parent](/learn/elder-care/how-to-apply-for-medicaid-for-a-parent).
4. **Do not move money yet.** Moving it can be a transfer, and moving it back can be an undo that works only in some states.
5. **Decide whether to spend, plan or pay.** Spending on fair-value needs is explained in [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down).
