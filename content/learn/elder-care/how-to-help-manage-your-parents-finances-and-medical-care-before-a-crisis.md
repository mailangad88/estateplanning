Marcus noticed it at Thanksgiving. His father, Leon, 79, had always balanced the checkbook to the penny, and now there was a pile of unopened envelopes on the counter, two shutoff notices among them. Leon said he was fine. Marcus wasn't sure whether to push or wait.

Most adult children are in that spot at some point. You are not their parent and they are not your child, and there is a real line between helping and taking over. This article covers when to step in, what you can do with no legal authority at all, which documents change the picture, and what to do if it is already too late for paperwork. It is general education, not legal advice, and the details differ in Illinois and New Jersey and by situation.

## When should an adult child get involved in a parent's finances?

Get involved when the signs are concrete, not when a birthday makes you nervous. A parent who is getting older is not a reason to take over. A pattern of problems is.

Common signs that it is time to talk:

- Unopened mail, late notices, or utilities shut off for nonpayment.
- Checks written twice, or the same bill paid three times.
- New "friends," a caregiver, or a relative who suddenly handles everything and discourages visits.
- Large gifts, wire transfers or gift-card purchases the parent cannot explain.
- Missed medications, repeated falls, or confusion at doctor visits.
- A parent who used to talk about money and now changes the subject.

If you see two or three, have the conversation now. If you see none, you can still have it as a planning talk, which is easier than a rescue talk. Our article on [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) has suggestions for how to open it.

## What can I do without legal authority?

Without paperwork you can help, but you cannot act for your parent. You work beside them, not in their place. Many families get through a year or two this way, and it is a normal part of [long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care).

| Step | What it gets you | What it does not give you |
|---|---|---|
| Sit with your parent for a monthly bill session | Seeing what is due and what is overdue | Authority to sign or pay on your own |
| Set up autopay and paperless statements with your parent's consent | Fewer missed bills | Access if the parent stops cooperating |
| Be added as a contact or "view-only" user on an account | Alerts and statements | The right to move money |
| A joint account (the parent adds you) | You can pay bills from it | Safety: the account is exposed to your creditors and divorce, and it can pass to you outside the will |
| Ask the bank about its own "agent" or "convenience" form | A narrow authority at that bank | Authority at other institutions |

A joint account is the shortcut people reach for first. It works, and it also carries side effects: your creditors can reach it, the money goes to you at death regardless of what the will says, and it can cause trouble if there are other children. For that reason, our attorneys generally prefer a power of attorney. See [the risks of joint ownership](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) for the full set of risks.

Do not log in as your parent or use their card without permission. Banks treat that as unauthorized access, even when your reasons are kind.

## How do I become my parent's power of attorney?

You do not become the agent by asking. Your parent names you in a signed document, and they can only do that while they understand what the document does. That is the whole reason to move early. The usual set is a durable financial power of attorney, a health care power of attorney (or health care proxy) and a HIPAA release.

The steps look like this:

1. Raise the idea as protection for your parent, not as control for you. "If you were in the hospital, who would pay the mortgage?" lands better than "I want access."
2. Ask who they would trust as agent and as backup. It may not be you, and that is fine. Our guide to [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) can help them decide.
3. Meet with an estate planning attorney together. The attorney talks to your parent, confirms they understand, and prepares documents that meet state rules.
4. Have the parent sign with the witness and notary your state requires. The article on [whether a power of attorney needs to be notarized](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-a-power-of-attorney-need-to-be-notarized) covers this.
5. Give copies to the banks and brokers now. Some institutions take a week or more to review a new document, and some refuse a form they have not seen. Our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) explains why preparing ahead helps.

If your parent is in the early stages of dementia, capacity is not all or nothing. A person may still be able to sign a power of attorney on a good day. Do not wait for a perfect day. An attorney can document capacity, and a doctor's note the same week helps.

## How do I get access to my parent's medical records?

A HIPAA authorization signed by your parent is the cleanest way. It names you, says what can be shared and for how long, and lets providers talk to you. A health care power of attorney does something different: it lets you make decisions when your parent cannot, and it normally includes a right to the records you need to decide.

Without either, privacy law lets a doctor listen to you but restricts what the doctor can say back. That is why families describe the experience of being on the phone with a hospital and getting nowhere. Our article on [what happens when a hospital won't talk to an adult child](/learn/what-if/adult-child-medical-emergency-no-hipaa) shows how that plays out, and [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) explains the form.

## What if my parent already cannot sign anything?

If your parent no longer understands what a power of attorney does, they cannot sign one, and nobody else can sign it for them. The court is the only route. In Illinois, a guardianship of the estate or person is a petition in the circuit court. In New Jersey, a guardian is appointed through the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part. Read [guardianship of an adult parent](/learn/guardianship/guardianship-of-an-adult-parent-who-can-petition-and-what-the-court-needs) and the New Jersey explanation in [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey).

A guardian is a heavier tool than a power of attorney. The court appoints, the parent may lose rights, and the guardian must file reports. It is also slower and costs more. Our attorneys point families to it when no document exists and money is at risk, and when a parent needs a decision-maker for placement. Look first at the other options in [guardianship vs power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## How do I stop a relative from taking advantage of my parent?

Act on the records first, and call the authorities second. Ask your parent for permission to view statements. Look for checks to an unfamiliar name, ATM withdrawals at odd hours, new joint owners and new loans. If your parent has dementia, check the title on the house as well.

Then, depending on what you find:

- Call the bank's fraud department and ask for a flag on unusual activity.
- Report suspected exploitation to your state's adult protective services agency. Both Illinois and New Jersey have one.
- Freeze your parent's credit with the three bureaus, as shown in [how to freeze a parent's credit and put safeguards on accounts](/learn/elder-care/how-to-freeze-a-parents-credit-and-put-safeguards-on-their-accounts).
- If the relative is the named agent, talk to an attorney about removal. See [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

Do not confront the relative before you have copies of the records. People who are caught tend to move money or isolate the parent further.

## A worked example

Marcus did three things. In December he asked his father to go over the mail with him on Sundays, which turned out to be a relief for Leon. In January they met an attorney, who talked to Leon alone for twenty minutes before drafting a durable financial power of attorney naming Marcus, with Marcus's sister as backup, plus a health care power of attorney and a HIPAA release. In February Marcus took copies to Leon's bank, his brokerage and his doctor. Four months later Leon had a stroke. The bills kept getting paid, and the hospital team talked to Marcus on day one.
