Financial exploitation of older adults costs victims billions of dollars a year, and it is widely under-reported. Victims are often ashamed, dependent on the person taking advantage of them, or unaware. The loss can be more than money: a home, a lifetime of savings and the ability to pay for care can disappear, along with trust in family.

This guide explains who commits exploitation, what the warning signs look like, how legal documents can protect or be misused, and what to do if you suspect abuse. It is one of the articles in our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## Who exploits older adults?

Both strangers and trusted people do. Researchers and protective agencies generally find that the most serious losses come from someone the victim knows.

- **Family members.** Adult children, grandchildren and other relatives who borrow money, pressure an older person to change a will or deed, or use a power of attorney for themselves.
- **Caregivers.** Paid or informal helpers who gain access to cards, passwords and homes.
- **Advisors and professionals.** Salespeople who push unsuitable annuities or products, and in rare cases lawyers or financial advisors who abuse a position of trust.
- **Strangers and scammers.** Callers, emails and online contacts running fraud schemes.
- **Romance and friendship schemes.** A new companion who isolates the person and asks for money.

## What are the warning signs?

No single sign proves abuse, but a pattern should prompt a closer look.

| Area | Warning signs |
|---|---|
| Accounts | Unusual withdrawals or transfers, new joint owners, ATM use when the person can't get out, checks that look different from their handwriting |
| Bills and property | Unpaid rent or utilities, missing valuables, a house suddenly listed or deeded |
| Documents | New powers of attorney, wills or beneficiary changes made quickly, especially with a new lawyer chosen by someone else |
| Behavior | Fear or anxiety about money, secrecy, a new "best friend," isolation from family |
| Caregiver | Refuses to leave the person alone with visitors, speaks for them, asks questions about the estate |
| Scams | Gift cards, wire transfers, prizes and urgent requests to keep a secret |

Some signs reflect dementia rather than abuse, for example forgotten bills. Both need attention.

## What are the common scams and schemes?

Scams change constantly, but the structure is steady: create urgency, secrecy and fear.

- **Grandparent scams.** A caller claims a grandchild is in jail or the hospital and needs money immediately.
- **Government and utility imposters.** A caller says there is a warrant, a lapsed benefit or a shutoff.
- **Tech support and refund scams.** A pop-up or call says a computer is infected, then asks for remote access or payment.
- **Romance scams.** An online partner needs money for an emergency.
- **Investment and annuity pitches.** Pressure to move money into products with surrender charges or high commissions.
- **Home repair and sweepstakes fraud.** Upfront fees for work or winnings that never come.
- **Cryptocurrency schemes.** Instructions to deposit cash in a crypto machine or move funds to an "investigator's" wallet.

Real agencies do not demand payment in gift cards or cryptocurrency, and do not require secrecy.

## How can a power of attorney be misused?

A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) gives an agent broad authority, often with no oversight. Misuse includes paying the agent's own bills, making gifts to the agent, changing beneficiaries, selling property at a low price, or commingling funds. An agent is a fiduciary and must act in the principal's interest, keep records and separate accounts, but nobody checks unless someone asks.

Ways to build in protection when signing:

1. **Choose carefully.** See [how to choose your agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent).
2. **Limit gifting and self-dealing powers.** Many states require explicit language for gifts, and you can cap them or forbid them.
3. **Require accountings.** Name a second person, such as a sibling or CPA, to receive annual accountings.
4. **Use co-agents or a monitor.** Two signatures for large transactions, or a trusted person with a right to see statements.
5. **Plan for a backup.** Name successor agents so no one can claim necessity.

## How do undue influence and will changes fit in?

[Undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) happens when someone takes advantage of an older person's dependence or frailty to change their documents in the influencer's favor. It is a common ground for challenging a will, as discussed in [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will). Red flags are a new beneficiary who is a caregiver or recent friend, a new lawyer chosen by the beneficiary, a sudden change from a long-standing plan and a signing without family knowledge. Attorneys often meet the client alone, ask open questions and document capacity to reduce the risk.

## What can families and institutions do to prevent exploitation?

Practical safeguards work better when set up before a crisis.

- **Trusted contact.** Ask banks and brokerages to record a trusted contact the firm can call about suspected exploitation.
- **Alerts.** Turn on transaction alerts and send duplicates to a family member.
- **Credit freeze.** Freeze credit with the three major bureaus to block new accounts.
- **Fewer ways in.** Opt out of unsolicited mail and calls, use a spending account with a limited balance, and use direct deposit for benefits.
- **Regular visits and statements.** A relative who opens mail or reviews statements, with the person's consent, can spot trouble early.
- **Passwords and access.** Manage logins safely. See [passwords and access in an estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access).
- **Professional help.** A geriatric care manager, an accountant or a professional fiduciary can provide oversight.
- **Talk about scams.** Explain the common ones without blame, so a loved one calls you before paying.

## What should you do if you suspect abuse?

Move in steps and document what you see.

1. **If someone is in immediate danger or a crime is happening, call 911.**
2. **Report to adult protective services** in the person's county or state. Reports can often be made anonymously, and reporters acting in good faith are generally protected in most states.
3. **Contact the financial institution.** Ask for the fraud department, report suspected exploitation and request holds or monitoring.
4. **File police and agency reports** for fraud, theft or identity theft. For scams, you can also report to the Federal Trade Commission.
5. **Freeze credit and change passwords.**
6. **Save evidence.** Keep statements, texts, emails and notes of what you observed.
7. **See an attorney.** Options may include revoking a power of attorney, restoring a prior document, seeking an accounting, recovering assets or petitioning for guardianship or a protective order.

If the person has capacity, they may refuse help. You can still report concerns, keep contact and revisit the issue later. If they lack capacity and no documents exist, a court-appointed guardian or conservator may be necessary.

### Example

Lena, 84, widowed, starts getting help from a neighbor, Carl, who is "just being kind." Over six months, her checks to him grow, her daughter Ines is told not to visit because Lena "is resting," and Lena signs a new power of attorney at an office Carl picked. Ines notices unopened bills and a withdrawal for $28,000. She calls adult protective services, asks the bank for an account review and consults an attorney, who helps revoke the new power of attorney while Lena can still understand what she is revoking, and restores the earlier one naming Ines.

## How do the laws protect older adults?

Every state has elder abuse laws, and most have adult protective services programs that investigate. Many states have laws making exploitation of an elder a specific crime, allowing enhanced penalties or civil recovery, and some let financial firms hold suspicious transactions. Specific protections, reporting duties and who must report differ by state.
