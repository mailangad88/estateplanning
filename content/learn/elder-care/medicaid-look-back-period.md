You may have heard that giving money to your children before a nursing home stay can cost you Medicaid coverage. That is true, but the details matter: what counts as a gift, how long the clock runs, and how the penalty is calculated. Families often learn about the look-back when an application is already pending, which is the hardest time to fix a mistake.

This guide explains the rule step by step. **State variation matters here:** the 60-month period is nearly universal, but the penalty divisors, the treatment of certain transfers and the procedures for cures and hardship waivers differ by state. Use this article as a map and confirm local rules. It is part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What is the Medicaid look-back period?

The look-back period is the window of time Medicaid reviews for transfers made for less than fair value. Federal law sets it at 60 months before the date you are institutionalized and apply for Medicaid, and most states use that length. Earlier federal law used 36 months for most transfers, and 60 for transfers to certain trusts; the 2005 Deficit Reduction Act extended the period to 60 months across the board.

The purpose is to stop people from giving away assets to qualify for a program meant for people with limited resources. The rule does not stop you from giving. It makes you wait, or pay for care yourself, long enough to offset the gift.

## What counts as a transfer?

Anything you give away or sell for less than it is worth can be a penalized transfer. Examples:

- Cash gifts to children, grandchildren or friends
- Adding a child as an owner on a bank account or deed, which gives away part ownership (see [adding a child to your deed or account](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks))
- Selling a house or car to a relative for much less than market value
- Forgiving a loan someone owes you
- Putting assets in an irrevocable trust you cannot get back
- Giving up an inheritance or choosing not to claim a spouse's elective share, in some states
- Large transfers to charity

Several things are not transfers. Paying your own bills, buying items you need, paying for home repairs, buying a car, prepaying a funeral and repaying a legitimate debt all exchange assets for fair value. Medicaid is concerned with gifts, not spending.

The federal gift tax rules are separate. The [annual gift tax exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) of $19,000 per recipient in 2026 means no gift tax return is needed for gifts at or below that amount. It does not protect a gift from the Medicaid look-back, so a $15,000 gift can still produce a penalty.

## How does the penalty period work?

When Medicaid finds uncompensated transfers, it calculates months of ineligibility. The steps are:

1. Add up the uncompensated value of all transfers made in the look-back window.
2. Divide the total by the state's divisor, an average monthly cost of nursing home care in that state.
3. The result is the number of months, and often partial months, of ineligibility.
4. The penalty starts on the later of the date of the transfer or the date you are in a facility, have applied and would otherwise be eligible.

The last point is the one people miss. Since 2006 the penalty clock does not start when you make the gift. It starts only when you are already in care, have spent down to the asset limit, and have applied. This is why gifting in advance, with no plan, can be risky: you may be without both the money and the coverage.

### Example

Gloria, 80, gives $60,000 to her son in March. In October she breaks her hip and moves to a nursing home, using the remaining savings until she reaches her state's asset limit in January. If her state's divisor is $10,000 a month, the penalty is six months. It begins in January, when she applies and is otherwise eligible. For those six months, she or her family must pay the home about $10,000 a month with no Medicaid help. If her son returns the full $60,000, the penalty may be cured, but the returned money counts toward her assets again and she would have to spend it on care.

Divisors differ a great deal by state, from roughly $7,000 to over $14,000 a month. Check yours.

## Which transfers are exempt?

Federal law exempts several transfers from penalty. Exact conditions vary, and some states add their own rules.

| Transfer | Notes |
|---|---|
| To a spouse, or to a trust solely for the spouse | No penalty. Used to rebalance assets between spouses. |
| To a child who is blind or disabled, or to a trust solely for that child | Disabled as defined by Social Security. |
| To a trust solely for a disabled person under 65 | Usually a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust); may have payback requirements. |
| The home, to a child who lived there for at least two years before institutionalization and provided care that delayed the move | The caregiver child exemption; requires documentation. |
| The home, to a sibling with an equity interest who lived there for at least a year before | A narrow exemption. |
| The home, to a minor child or a disabled child | Generally exempt. |
| Transfers made exclusively for a purpose other than qualifying for Medicaid | Requires convincing evidence. |

The caregiver child exemption is often misunderstood. Proof usually includes medical records showing the parent needed supervision, a statement from a doctor, and evidence of the child's presence and care.

## Can planning work with the look-back?

Yes, in several ways, and all depend on timing and state law.

- **Early planning.** Assets placed in an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) more than 60 months before an application are outside the look-back. The grantor gives up control, but can often keep the right to income and to live in the home.
- **Spending down.** Spending on needed items rather than gifts creates no penalty.
- **Partial gifting.** Some families gift a portion and use the rest to pay for care during the penalty period. Whether this works depends on state rules and the penalty start date.
- **Exempt transfers.** Moving assets to a spouse or to a trust for a disabled child.
- **Crisis planning.** Even after a nursing home admission, some strategies, including annuities, promissory notes and the exempt transfers above, may help. They are state-sensitive and often scrutinized.

Medicaid looks at the pattern, too. Reviewers examine bank statements for unexplained withdrawals, round-number checks and transfers to relatives. Be ready to document each one.

## How should you prepare the records?

Most states require 60 months of statements for every account, and sometimes for accounts that closed during the period. Start collecting now.

- Bank statements, every page, including savings and closed accounts
- Brokerage, IRA and annuity statements
- Deeds, titles and records of any sale or transfer
- Life insurance policies with cash value
- Trust documents and tax returns
- Receipts for large purchases and explanations for large withdrawals

A [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets a trusted agent gather the records and file the application. Without one, family members may be unable to access the accounts.

## What should you avoid?

- **Informal gifts.** Cash handed to children can trigger a penalty and be gone if a child has a divorce, a lawsuit or a death.
- **Adding children to accounts or deeds without advice.** It can be a transfer and also expose the asset to the child's creditors.
- **Waiting for a crisis.** Early planning has the most options.
- **Assuming a rule from one state applies in yours.**
- **Moving without documentation.** Unexplained transfers cause delay and denial.
