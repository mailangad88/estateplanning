The signs usually come one at a time. A burned pot. A stack of unopened mail. A bruise that nobody can explain. You mention it to your mother on the phone and she says she is fine and does not want to talk about it. Then you hang up and wonder whether you are overreacting or about to miss something serious.

This page explains how to read the signs, what capacity means in law, how an evaluation works and what you can and cannot do when a parent refuses help. It supplements our broader guide to [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) and sits within the [long-term care planning section](/learn/elder-care).

## What are the signs a parent is not safe living alone?

Watch for patterns across several areas of daily life. One missed appointment is ordinary. Several problems at once are not.

- **Health:** unexplained weight loss, missed medicine or double doses, untreated wounds, repeated falls.
- **Home:** spoiled food, a dirty kitchen, stoves left on, unpaid utilities, clutter that blocks walkways.
- **Money:** late or unopened bills, odd checks, new "friends" asking for help, large gifts to strangers.
- **Judgment:** wandering, getting lost, leaving the door open at night, answering every scam call.
- **Appearance:** same clothes for days, poor hygiene, a change in personality.

Spend a few days with your parent if you can, rather than an afternoon. Open the refrigerator. Look at the pill organizer. Check how the car looks. Carlos, 49, flew in to see his father and found three months of unpaid property tax notices in a drawer, along with a perfectly organized stack of birthday cards. His father was not careless. Something had changed.

## What does legal capacity mean?

Capacity is a person's ability to understand a particular decision, appreciate what it means for them and communicate a choice. It is not a yes-or-no label for the whole person. Someone with early memory loss may be unable to manage an investment account but fully able to decide who should care for them. A person can have a clear morning and a confused evening.

The legal tests differ by decision. Signing a will requires a modest level of understanding of one's property and family. A contract or a power of attorney requires understanding the transaction. Consenting to medical treatment requires understanding the risks and alternatives. Our page on [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) explains how documents work when memory is failing, and the glossary entry for [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) covers the will standard.

Two points matter for families. First, a diagnosis of dementia does not by itself remove capacity. Second, a parent who makes choices you dislike has not lost capacity for that reason. Refusing a walker, staying in the family house and spending money on a hobby are all choices an adult is allowed to make.

## How do you get a parent evaluated?

Begin with the primary care doctor and be specific about what you have seen. Send the doctor a letter or a list before the visit, because many older adults minimize problems in the exam room. A doctor can perform a brief cognitive screen and, if warranted, refer your parent to a neurologist, geriatric psychiatrist or neuropsychologist. Some memory problems are treatable, including medication side effects, thyroid problems and urinary tract infections, so an exam is worth doing even if you are sure you know the cause.

1. Call the doctor's office and ask how to share concerns in advance. Your parent may have to sign a [HIPAA authorization](/glossary/hipaa-authorization) for the doctor to speak with you.
2. Offer to drive and attend. Present it as a regular checkup.
3. Ask for a cognitive screening and a medication review.
4. If the question is whether your parent can sign legal documents, ask for a note from the doctor close to the signing date and tell the attorney about the findings.
5. Keep a record of the results, because a court or agency may later ask for them.

If your parent refuses any evaluation, you cannot generally force one without a court order. A geriatric care manager or a trusted friend sometimes has better luck than a child.

## What can you do when a parent refuses help?

Start with what the law will not allow: you cannot move a capable adult, take their keys by force, or pay their bills without authority. What you can do is more than people think.

### Try the least restrictive options first

- Offer small, specific help rather than a big plan: a cleaner twice a month, meals delivered, a pill dispenser with an alarm.
- Let someone other than you introduce the idea. Doctors, clergy and old friends are often listened to.
- Frame help as a way to stay at home longer.
- Ask what worries them. Fear of losing independence is the usual reason for refusal.
- Consider an adult day program or a part-time aide before a bigger step.

### Put the paperwork in place while your parent can still sign

This is the most useful thing you can do if your parent has any capacity left. A [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets a trusted person handle money, and a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) covers medical choices. Without them, the family may have no authority at the moment it is needed most, a problem we describe in [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

### Call adult protective services if the risk is serious

Adult protective services, or a similar state agency, investigates self-neglect and abuse. A caseworker will visit, speak with your parent privately and decide whether services or protective steps are needed. If your parent has capacity and says no, the agency generally stops there. If not, it can arrange services or refer the matter to a court.

## When is guardianship the answer?

Guardianship is the answer only when a person truly cannot make safe decisions and nothing less restrictive works. A court holds a hearing, may appoint a lawyer for your parent and, if it finds incapacity, gives someone authority over personal or financial decisions. It takes time and money, and the person loses legal rights. Compare it with a power of attorney in our page on [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship), and read about the process in [how to make guardianship legal](/learn/guardianship/how-to-make-guardianship-legal).

| Situation | Usual first step |
|---|---|
| Parent has capacity, refuses help | Respect the choice, offer small supports, sign documents now |
| Parent has some capacity, mild memory loss | Power of attorney and health care directive, with a doctor's note |
| Parent lacks capacity, no documents | Guardianship or conservatorship, or a state surrogate process for medical decisions |
| Parent is being exploited | Adult protective services, bank notification and an attorney |
| Immediate danger | 911 |

## What if someone is taking advantage of your parent?

Isolation, sudden changes to documents and unusual money movement are warning signs of exploitation and of undue influence. Our guides on [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) and the [glossary entry on undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) explain what the law looks for. Keep a log of dates, names and amounts, and bring it with you to an attorney.

## How state law changes this

States differ on who may report to adult protective services, whether a family member can consent to medical care without a guardian, and what a guardianship hearing requires. Look at your [state guide](/estate-planning/texas) and ask the local agency what it can do.
