Stanley and Grace, both 80, have $310,000 in savings. Stanley has moved into a nursing home. The state allows Grace to keep about half, and the rest must be spent before Medicaid starts paying. Their savings are too large to qualify and too small to feel comfortable. A financial salesperson tells them about a "Medicaid annuity" that could fix it. Is it a good idea?

It can be, for the right couple, if the contract is built to the rules. For other families it is a poor fit. This article describes what the product is, the conditions it must satisfy, and the situations where it helps. It is part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care), and the rules shown here are federal, with state choices on top.

## What is a Medicaid-compliant annuity?

An annuity is a contract in which you pay an insurance company a sum and it pays you back through regular installments. A Medicaid-compliant annuity is a special version that Medicaid agencies will not treat as a gift or as a countable asset. Congress set the conditions in the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, which tightened rules after abuses of the earlier annuity strategy.

The idea is simple. Medicaid counts the money in your savings account. It does not count a stream of monthly income in the same way. By converting a lump sum into a short, level income stream paid to the healthy spouse, a couple moves cash from "countable asset" to "income of the community spouse." Because the income belongs to the healthy spouse, it generally is not used to pay for the other spouse's care.

## What rules must the annuity meet?

The annuity has to satisfy each of these conditions. Missing one turns the purchase into a transfer for less than fair value.

1. **Single premium.** One payment up front, not a series of deposits.
2. **Irrevocable.** You cannot cash it out or change the terms.
3. **Non-assignable.** It cannot be sold or transferred to someone else.
4. **Actuarially sound.** The payout period cannot be longer than the payee's life expectancy under the tables the state uses.
5. **Equal payments.** The installments are equal or fairly level, with no deferral and no balloon payment at the end.
6. **State named as beneficiary.** The state must be the first remainder beneficiary, up to the total Medicaid paid for the applicant. If there is a community spouse or a minor or disabled child, the state may be allowed to stand behind that person instead. The details depend on the state.
7. **Disclosure.** The application must disclose the annuity, and the state's own forms may ask for the contract.

States can add conditions. Some insist on specific language in the contract. Others review the product for compliance before they approve the application.

## Who is it for?

The annuity is mainly a tool for married couples in which one spouse needs care and the other has excess savings. Three facts make it work:

- The excess assets above the community spouse resource allowance would otherwise have to be spent on care.
- The healthy spouse can buy the annuity and receive the income.
- The income is not counted for the nursing home spouse, in most states, because of the name-on-the-check rule. See [how Medicaid works for a married couple](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples).

For a single applicant, the same annuity buys a fair-value income stream, which the person would then have to pay to the nursing home. It converts assets into income but the income is simply spent on care, and the state is repaid at death. Most single applicants are better served by [spend-down on things they need](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down) or an exempt transfer.

## A worked example

Take Stanley and Grace again. Their $310,000 is countable. Suppose their state follows the half rule and the community spouse allowance is $155,000. Stanley's half must come down to the state's small single-person limit, which is roughly $2,000 in many states.

That leaves about $153,000 in "excess" to deal with. They could spend part on a roof, hearing aids, a prepaid funeral and a used car. Suppose they use $20,000 that way. They have around $133,000 left over.

Grace is 78, and the state's table gives her a life expectancy of about 11 years. A compliant annuity could pay out over, say, 36 months, with level monthly payments of a bit more than $3,600 before tax. Grace then has the annuity income on top of her own, which has a few side effects:

- If her income now clears the minimum monthly allowance, no income needs to be shifted from Stanley.
- Only the earnings portion of each payment is taxable, but she should expect a higher bracket for those three years.
- Stanley's resources are now low enough for Medicaid to start.

After three years the annuity ends. If Grace dies sooner, the state is paid back first, up to what Medicaid spent on Stanley.

This is an illustration with invented numbers. Real life depends on the state's resource limit, the divisor, the annuity pricing and tax law, so work through the math with an attorney.

## What are the risks?

| Risk | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Noncompliant contract | Treated as a gift; penalty period may follow |
| Insurer failure or fees | Money sits with one company; commissions can be high |
| Income tax | Payments raise the healthy spouse's taxable income |
| Early death of the payee | State is repaid first; other heirs may get little |
| Changes in state rules | Some states limit or scrutinize annuities heavily |
| Sold by someone with a commission | A salesperson may not know your state's Medicaid rules |

An annuity also uses up cash that the healthy spouse could have kept. If Grace had the legal right to a larger allowance by hearing, that route may be better. And it only helps once a spouse has actually entered care or is about to; buying early for an unclear future is a different matter and may do harm.

## What about retirement accounts?

People ask whether an IRA can be moved into the annuity. In some states, a retirement account that is already in payout status is treated as income, not as a countable asset. Moving it into a compliant annuity inside the IRA can sometimes work, but it can trigger tax consequences and, in some states, is treated differently. This is one place where the rules vary the most by state. Do not move an IRA until the state's treatment is confirmed.

## What should you check before you buy?

1. Is the community spouse the owner and payee?
2. Has the term been tested against the life expectancy table the state uses?
3. Is the state named as beneficiary as required, with the right order?
4. Has the contract been compared with other options, including a higher allowance by hearing?
5. Do you understand the tax effect on the healthy spouse for the term?
6. Is the application drafted to disclose the annuity correctly?

If you are still within five years of a prior gift, bring that to your attorney. The annuity and the [look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) interact, because other transfers may already carry a penalty. You may also want to read about [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies), which explains the tax side of lifetime gifts and why Medicaid treats them differently.
