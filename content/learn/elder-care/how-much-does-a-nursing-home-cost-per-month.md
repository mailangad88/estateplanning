Denise is 61. Her mother, Joan, fell in March and went from the hospital to a rehab wing, and now the social worker wants a plan for what comes next. The first thing Denise asks is the one everyone asks: "What is this going to cost every month?"

Nobody can give you the real number without knowing the county, the room and the level of care. What you can do is learn how each type of care is billed, turn each quote into a monthly figure, and build a budget that does not collapse when needs go up. That is what this article walks through, with an invented example you can copy for your own family. For the bigger picture, start with our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## How do nursing homes, assisted living and home care bill?

Each type of care uses a different unit, which is why quotes are hard to compare. Convert everything to one month before you decide anything.

| Type of care | How it is usually billed | How to get to a monthly number |
|---|---|---|
| Nursing home | A daily rate for the room, with some services extra | Daily rate x 30 (or x 30.4 for an average month) |
| Assisted living | A monthly base rent plus levels of care | Base rent + the care level add-on + extras |
| Memory care | Higher monthly rent, often all-inclusive | The monthly quote, then ask what is not included |
| Home care agency | An hourly rate, often with a minimum visit | Hourly rate x hours per week x 4.3 |
| Independent caregiver hired directly | An hourly wage, plus payroll taxes if you are the employer | See [payroll and taxes for a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/payroll-and-taxes-when-a-family-member-is-your-paid-caregiver) |

A fair warning about numbers you find online. National averages are built from surveys of facilities and say little about a particular town. A county in the Chicago suburbs and a county in southern Illinois will not look alike, and neither will northern New Jersey and the shore. Use averages to see the shape of the problem and use the facility's written rate sheet for the real figure.

## What does a nursing home cost per month?

A nursing home costs the daily rate multiplied by the number of days, and the total for a private room commonly lands above $100,000 a year. The room rate usually covers the bed, meals, nursing supervision and help with daily activities. Therapy, certain medications, incontinence supplies, barber visits and a private phone line can show up as separate lines.

Here is a hypothetical budget. The numbers are invented to show the arithmetic and are not a price quote.

| Item | Per day | Per month (30 days) |
|---|---|---|
| Semi-private room, base rate | $330 | $9,900 |
| Medication and supply charges | n/a | $350 |
| Hair care, phone, personal laundry | n/a | $120 |
| Total | | $10,370 |

Over two years that is about $249,000, before any rate increase. Most facilities raise rates once a year, so ask when the last increase was and how much notice residents get.

Joan's income is a pension and Social Security of about $3,100 a month. If the bill is $10,370, the gap is $7,270 a month, or about $87,000 a year, which has to come from savings, insurance or a program. That gap number is the one to bring to a planning meeting. You can run your own version in our [Medicaid savings runway tool](/tools/medicaid-savings-runway).

## What does assisted living cost per month?

Assisted living is priced as rent plus care, so the monthly bill depends on how much help the resident needs. A common structure is a base rent for the apartment, meals and housekeeping, and then a tiered charge for help with bathing, dressing, medication reminders and transfers.

The trap is the starting price. A building may advertise a low base rent, and then assess a resident at move-in and add a care level on top. A resident whose needs rise later gets moved up a tier and the bill grows. Ask these before you tour a second building:

1. What is the base rent, and what is the community fee at move-in?
2. How many care levels are there, and what does each one cost per month?
3. Who does the assessment, and how often is it repeated?
4. At what point does the building say it can no longer meet the resident's needs?
5. Does the contract allow the resident to stay if private funds run out and Medicaid is needed?

That last question matters more than people expect. Some buildings accept Medicaid only after a resident has paid privately for a set number of months, and some never do. Our article on [who pays for assisted living, memory care and hospice](/learn/elder-care/assisted-living-memory-care-and-hospice-who-pays) explains how Medicaid waivers fit in.

## What does home care cost per hour, and when is it cheaper?

Home care costs the hourly rate multiplied by the hours you buy, and it is cheapest when the need is limited. An aide who comes four hours a day on weekdays might be the right answer for a person who is safe alone overnight. Add overnight help and weekends and the bill climbs quickly.

Suppose an agency charges $34 an hour (an invented rate). Eight hours a day, five days a week is 40 hours, or $1,360 a week, about $5,900 a month. Round-the-clock care is 168 hours a week, or $5,712 a week, about $24,700 a month. At that point a facility is almost always the cheaper way to get 24-hour supervision.

Two practical points. Agencies usually carry the payroll tax, insurance and backup staff, which is why they cost more than hiring someone directly. And some states pay for part of home care through a Medicaid program, which we cover in [the Medicaid home care waiver article](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver). If a family member will be the paid caregiver, put it in writing first, as explained in [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

## What do custodial and skilled care mean for the bill?

The label on the care decides who pays. Skilled care needs a licensed nurse or therapist. Custodial care is help with the activities of daily living. Medicare covers a limited stay in a skilled nursing facility after a qualifying hospital stay, up to 100 days, with a daily copayment starting on day 21. It does not pay for custodial care, which is what most long stays are made of. See [what Medicare covers for long-term care](/learn/elder-care/what-medicare-covers-for-long-term-care) for the rules in detail.

This catches families off guard. Joan's rehab stay is probably billed to Medicare at first. When therapy ends and she still cannot be alone safely, the billing changes to private pay the same week, with the same bed and the same staff. Ask the facility for the date it expects the Medicare days to end, so you can plan the switch.

## How long should you budget for?

Budget for at least two years, then test what happens at five. Many stays are short, and some are very long. The people who run out of money are usually the ones whose stay was longer than the family guessed. A budget that works for 24 months and fails at 48 tells you that you need an insurance or Medicaid plan before the savings are gone.

Also count your cash flow, not just the savings. The resident's Social Security and pension go first toward the bill. If a spouse is still living at home, they are entitled to keep a share of income and assets, a point covered in [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples).

## Illinois and New Jersey: what is local about the price?

Both states set the Medicaid daily rate that a participating nursing home receives, and the resident's own income goes toward it first. The private-pay rate is whatever the facility charges, and in both states it is usually higher than the Medicaid rate. That gap is why some homes limit the number of Medicaid beds. Before admission, ask whether the bed will stay available if the resident converts to Medicaid.

Our state pages explain the local eligibility rules: [Illinois Medicaid nursing home eligibility](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-nursing-home-eligibility-and-planning) and [New Jersey Medicaid and nursing home planning](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-medicaid-and-nursing-home-planning). If you are choosing a facility, read [choosing a nursing home and knowing your rights](/learn/elder-care/choosing-a-nursing-home-and-knowing-your-rights) alongside the price sheet.

## A checklist before you sign an admission agreement

- Get the rate sheet in writing, including every charge outside the daily or monthly rate.
- Ask for the date and the amount of the last two rate increases.
- Read the discharge section. Under what conditions can the facility require the resident to leave?
- Do not sign as a "responsible party" who promises to pay from your own pocket. Federal law bars a nursing home from requiring a third party to pledge personal payment as a condition of admission. See [can a nursing home make my children pay](/learn/elder-care/can-a-nursing-home-make-my-children-pay).
- Find out whether the bed is certified for Medicaid and Medicare.
- Keep the agreement, rate sheet and every statement in one folder.
