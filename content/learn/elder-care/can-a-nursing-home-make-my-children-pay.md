You are standing at the front desk of a nursing home with a stack of admission forms and a pen. Your mother is being moved from the hospital this afternoon. The business office points to a line near the end and says it is just a formality. Many adult children sign it without reading, and a smaller number find out a year later that they agreed to pay thousands of dollars a month out of their own pockets.

This article explains what a nursing home may and may not ask of you, the difference between signing as an agent and signing as a guarantor, what filial responsibility laws are, and what to do if a bill is already in your name.

## The short version

- A nursing home that accepts Medicare or Medicaid cannot require you to promise payment from your own funds to admit or keep your parent.
- You can still choose to sign as a personal co-signer. Do not, unless an attorney has told you why.
- Your parent's bills belong to your parent and, after death, to the estate. They do not pass to you automatically.
- Money you handle for your parent as agent must go to the bill, and you must be able to show where it went.
- A few states allow certain relatives to be sued for a parent's unpaid care. It is rare, and it is real.

## What does federal law say about guarantors?

Federal rules for nursing homes that take Medicare or Medicaid forbid the facility from requiring a third party to promise payment as a condition of admission or of staying. That rule dates back to the nursing home reform law of 1987, and it exists because families were routinely pressured at the worst possible moment.

There is a catch. The facility may ask you to sign as the resident's representative, and it may ask you to promise to use the resident's income and resources to pay. That is lawful. A form can also blur the line, folding a personal promise into paragraph eleven under a heading that sounds harmless. The law bars the requirement, not the signature. A relative who signs voluntarily may be held to it.

| What you sign | What it means | Your personal money at risk? |
|---|---|---|
| Resident's representative or agent | You will handle paperwork and pay from the resident's funds | No, if you use the resident's money properly |
| Responsible party (wording varies) | Often the same as above, but read it | Depends on the exact words |
| Guarantor, co-signer, surety | You promise to pay if the resident does not | Yes |
| "Financially responsible" with no limit stated | Ambiguous, and courts have split | Possibly |

### Example

Gerald, 83, is admitted after a hip fracture. His daughter Patrice signs a stack of forms and one says she is "jointly and severally liable" for charges. Gerald's Medicaid application takes five months, and the facility bills for the gap. Because Patrice signed that clause, the facility may turn to her for what Medicaid does not later cover. If she had crossed out the clause and initialed the change, a facility that accepts Medicare or Medicaid could not have refused admission over it.

## What should you do before you sign?

Ask for the admission agreement ahead of time, even if you are in a rush. A facility will usually send it to you or let you take a photo of it. Then:

1. **Find the signature blocks.** Most agreements have one for the resident and a second for a representative. Sign only in the role you actually hold.
2. **Search for personal-liability language.** Look for "guarantor," "jointly liable," "responsible for payment," "in your individual capacity" and "surety."
3. **Strike it and initial.** Write "signing as agent only, not individually" next to your name, and have the staff member initial too.
4. **Check the arbitration clause.** Some agreements ask you to give up the right to go to court. A facility that takes Medicare or Medicaid cannot make that a condition of admission, either. If you are unsure, ask for a copy and a day to read it.
5. **Keep your own copy.** A photo on your phone is enough.

If your parent is still able to sign, the better course is to have your parent sign the contract, with you present. Capacity matters here. If your parent has dementia and has no agent in place, read [what to do when a parent has dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) before you sign anything.

## What are filial responsibility laws?

Filial responsibility laws are state statutes that make certain adult children responsible for the basic support of a parent who cannot support themselves. Many states still have them in some form. Most are old, vaguely drafted and almost never used.

Almost never is not never. A Pennsylvania appeals court in 2012 held an adult son liable for his mother's unpaid nursing home bill under that state's statute, even though he had not signed anything personally. The case surprised a lot of families because the son had not hidden or taken anything. The facility argued that she had resources she could have used, and that he had the means to support her.

Three things limit the risk. These statutes usually turn on the child's ability to pay, and they often excuse a child who was abandoned or abused as a minor. Some require that the parent be in need, which a pending Medicaid application can undercut. And the practical reality is that most facilities would rather get approved Medicaid payments than file suit against a family.

Because state law varies, look at the [guide for your state](/estate-planning/pennsylvania) or ask an attorney in the parent's state, not yours. The relevant law is generally the state where the parent lives or lived.

## Where do children actually get into trouble?

Almost always, the trouble comes from the way the money is handled, not from a statute.

- **Paying yourself first.** A child who is the agent under a power of attorney writes a check to themselves "for caregiving" with no agreement in place. The facility, or Medicaid, treats that as a transfer or even a conversion. See [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) for how to do it properly.
- **Letting the bill pile up.** An agent holds the checkbook, the parent's account has the money, and the nursing home bill goes unpaid. The facility can reasonably say the agent had control of the funds and did not use them as promised.
- **Gifting before applying.** Moving money to children can create a [Medicaid penalty period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) during which no one pays. The facility is then looking for someone to pay, and the children hold the money.
- **Joint accounts.** Money in a joint account with a child may be treated as the parent's for Medicaid. See [what assets are exempt from Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/what-assets-are-exempt-from-medicaid) for how accounts are counted.

## What if you are being asked to pay right now?

Do not ignore the letter and do not pay it blindly. A calm sequence works better than either.

1. **Ask for an itemized statement** showing dates, charges and payments received, including any from Medicare or a supplemental insurer.
2. **Pull out the agreement you signed** and find the clause the facility is relying on.
3. **Write to the billing office** and state that you signed in a representative capacity only, if that is true, and that you are using the resident's funds. Keep it short and send it in a way you can prove.
4. **Check Medicaid status.** If an application is pending, the facility may be able to wait. Our article on [Medicaid crisis planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-crisis-planning) covers the gap period.
5. **Talk to an attorney before you pay from your own funds.** A payment can be read as an admission that you were responsible.

## What happens to the bills after your parent dies?

Unpaid nursing home charges become a claim against the estate. The executor pays valid debts before heirs receive their shares, within the limits of state law. If the estate cannot cover everything, the unpaid balance is generally written off, not passed to the children. The order of payment and the notice rules are in [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

Two things are separate from that. If your parent received Medicaid, the state may try to recover from the estate. That is [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery), and it is a claim against the estate, not against you. And if you signed as a guarantor, the facility's claim against you is a separate right that does not depend on the probate estate at all.

## How state law changes this

States differ in whether they have a filial responsibility statute, who it reaches, and how often it is used. They also differ in how long Medicaid takes, whether it pays for months before the application date, and how nursing homes handle the gap. The federal rule against requiring a personal promise to pay applies nationwide to facilities that take Medicare or Medicaid. A small private home that takes neither may be outside it, so ask whether the facility is certified before assuming.
