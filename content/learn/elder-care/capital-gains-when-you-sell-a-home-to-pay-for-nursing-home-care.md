Arthur, 84, bought his Naperville ranch house in 1989 for $110,000. After a stroke he moved to a nursing home in March, and his daughter Joan wants to list the house in the spring. Her first question is the one this page answers: will the sale trigger a big capital gains bill on top of everything else?

For most families in Arthur's position the answer is that the tax is smaller than they fear, and the more serious problem is what the sale does to Medicaid eligibility. This page covers both. It sits in our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care), and the numbers below are examples, not predictions.

## Do you owe capital gains tax if you sell your home to pay for a nursing home?

Possibly, but usually only on the part of the gain that the main-home exclusion does not cover. The tax is figured on gain, not on the sale price. Gain is what you receive minus your basis, which is what you paid plus the cost of permanent improvements such as a new roof or a finished basement, and minus selling costs like the agent's commission.

Here is Arthur's house as an example. It sells for $420,000. He paid $110,000, spent about $30,000 on a kitchen and roof over the years, and the sale costs run $25,000. His taxable gain before any exclusion is $255,000.

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sale price | $420,000 |
| Minus original purchase price | $110,000 |
| Minus improvements | $30,000 |
| Minus selling costs | $25,000 |
| Gain before the exclusion | $255,000 |

Federal law lets a homeowner exclude a large part of that gain when the house was the main home. For a single owner the limit is a six-figure amount and for a married couple filing together it is double. The IRS publishes the current figures in Publication 523, and a tax preparer will have them. A gain of $255,000 may be wholly or mostly excluded.

## What is the two-out-of-five-year rule, and does a nursing home stay count?

To use the exclusion you must have owned the home and used it as your main home for at least two of the five years before the sale. The two years do not have to be in a row.

Moving to a nursing home does not end this. If you become physically or mentally unable to care for yourself and move into a licensed care facility, time in the facility counts as time living in the house, as long as you owned and lived in the house for at least one of the five years before you moved. For Arthur, who lived in the house for decades, the test is easy.

The rule is for licensed facilities, which covers nursing homes and most licensed assisted living. A stay with a relative does not count. If your parent moved in with you and the house sat empty, the clock runs against the house.

## What if the house has been empty for years or was never your main home?

Then the exclusion may be reduced or lost, and this is where families get surprised. A rental house, a second home or a house that has been vacant for more than three of the last five years fails the use test.

If your parent moved out of the house long before entering care, count backward from the closing date and ask whether two of the last five years remain as a main home. If they do not, the gain is taxed as long-term capital gain at federal rates. In Illinois and New Jersey the gain is also income for state income tax purposes. Neither state has a separate break for a nursing home sale.

## How does selling the house affect Medicaid?

Selling converts an exempt asset into cash, and cash counts. While Arthur lives in a nursing home and intends to return, or while a spouse lives in the house, Medicaid may treat the home as exempt. Once it sells, the $395,000 he has after costs sits in his bank account and he is far over the asset limit, which is only a few thousand dollars for a single applicant in most states.

That does not mean the sale is a mistake. Sometimes it is the only way to pay a monthly bill that can exceed what a pension and Social Security cover. It means you decide what to do with the proceeds before the closing, not after. The usual choices are:

1. Pay the nursing home privately until the money reaches the Medicaid limit. This is plain [spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down).
2. Spend on things that help the person: a prepaid funeral, a better wheelchair, hearing aids, a vehicle for a spouse.
3. Use a plan, such as a [Medicaid-compliant annuity](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-compliant-annuity) or a half-a-loaf gift, when the numbers support it. The article on [gifting and the penalty period](/learn/elder-care/half-a-loaf-gifting-and-how-long-the-medicaid-penalty-lasts) shows how the math works.

A spouse who still lives in the house changes everything. A home occupied by a spouse stays exempt, and selling it is rarely the right first move. The article on [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) goes through that situation.

## Should you give the house to your child before it is sold?

Usually no, for two separate reasons. The first is Medicaid. A gift of the house inside the 60-month look-back period creates a penalty, which is a stretch of time Medicaid will not pay. The exceptions, such as a caregiver child who lived in the house for two years, are narrow.

The second reason is tax, and it is the one people miss. A gift keeps your basis. If Arthur deeds the house to Joan and she sells for $420,000, her basis is his $110,000 plus improvements, and she loses the benefit of his main-home exclusion because the house was never her home. A house left to Joan at his death is different, because [the step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) resets her basis to its value on the date of death, which could be $420,000. The gain disappears.

| Path | Who sells | Basis used | Likely tax result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arthur sells now | Arthur | $110,000 plus improvements | Mostly or fully excluded if he qualifies |
| Arthur gifts to Joan, she sells | Joan | Arthur's old basis | Joan taxed on the full gain, plus a Medicaid penalty risk |
| Arthur dies owning the house, Joan sells | Joan | Value at death | Little or no gain |

A planned sale during life is often cleaner than a gift. Waiting for a death is not a tax strategy to rely on, because the nursing home bill keeps running and Medicaid may later claim against the house, as the article on [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) explains.

## Can nursing home costs reduce the tax on the gain?

They can, in the right year. Unreimbursed medical expenses are an itemized deduction above a percentage of adjusted gross income. If the stay is for a person who is chronically ill, the whole cost of the nursing home, not just the medical portion, may qualify. In a year when a large sale pushes income up, a six-figure care bill can wipe out much of the taxable amount.

The bigger point is timing. If the house sale and the large care payments fall in the same tax year, the deduction has income to offset. A year when the house sells in December and the bills come in January loses the match. Our article on [tax breaks when you pay for a parent's care](/learn/elder-care/tax-breaks-when-you-pay-for-a-parents-care) covers the dependent test and the documentation a preparer will ask for.

## Who can sign the sale if the owner cannot?

Whoever holds authority. If Arthur signed a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) that covers real estate, Joan can sign the deed as his agent, and title companies in Illinois and New Jersey will usually want to see the document and a lawyer's review of it. If he has no power of attorney and lacks capacity, the only route may be a court guardianship, which adds months. This is the quiet reason to sign the document early. Our page on a [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) explains what families do when the paper is missing.

## What to gather before you list the house

- The closing statement from the purchase, or any record of the price paid.
- Receipts or contractor invoices for improvements, with dates.
- A letter from the doctor or facility showing when care began and that it is a licensed facility.
- The current medical and care bills, so a preparer can estimate the deduction.
- The power of attorney or guardianship order, if someone else is signing.
- A written plan for the proceeds, reviewed before the closing date.
