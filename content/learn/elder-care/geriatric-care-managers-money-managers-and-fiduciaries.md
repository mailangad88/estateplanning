Most adult children who help a parent start by doing small things. They drive to appointments, sort the mail, call the pharmacy. Over a year or two the small things become a second job, and the family realizes that no one is quite sure who is allowed to do what.

At that point it helps to know the types of help that exist. This page compares the five most common, explains which need legal paperwork, and suggests how to decide. It is part of our [long-term care planning guide](/learn/elder-care).

## What is the difference between these roles?

The names sound similar, but the jobs and the legal authority are quite different. Start with the table.

| Role | What they do | Legal authority needed? | Usually paid by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geriatric care manager | Assesses needs, builds a care plan, arranges services, oversees care | No, but needs the person's consent and often a HIPAA release | Hourly fee, private pay |
| Daily money manager | Pays bills, sorts mail, balances accounts, organizes tax papers | Bank authorization or a power of attorney, depending on the task | Hourly fee |
| Representative payee | Receives and spends a Social Security or SSI benefit for the person | Appointed by Social Security | Usually unpaid; some organizations may charge a limited fee |
| Agent under a power of attorney | Handles finances or health decisions named in the document | Yes, a signed document | Often unpaid; compensation can be set in the document |
| Guardian or conservator | Makes personal or financial decisions after a court finds incapacity | Yes, a court order | Court-approved fees |

## What does a geriatric care manager do?

A care manager is the person who knows what your parent needs, who is providing it and what to do when something changes. Most are nurses or social workers. After an assessment, they may arrange home care, recommend a facility, attend doctor visits, talk to the hospital before a discharge and report back to the family.

Tamara lives four states from her mother, who has early dementia and a bad hip. She hired a care manager who visits every two weeks, reviews the medicines, notices when the home aide is cutting corners and sends Tamara a short note each time. Tamara still makes the decisions. She just no longer relies on phone calls with a mother who says everything is fine.

Rates are hourly and usually not covered by insurance or Medicare. Some long-term care insurance policies pay for care coordination, so check the policy. Ask about credentials, how quickly they respond in an emergency and whether they receive any payment from the agencies they recommend. Anyone who does should tell you.

## What does a daily money manager do?

A daily money manager takes over the routine paperwork of money. That includes sorting mail, paying bills, tracking deposits, reconciling statements, filing insurance claims and putting together records for a tax preparer. The service sits between "the family helps" and "someone has full control."

It suits a parent who can still make decisions but cannot keep up with the paper. For anything beyond preparing and organizing, such as signing checks, the manager needs permission from the bank or an agent's authority. Many families keep the manager's access limited: the manager prepares payments, and either the parent or an agent approves them. A second person receiving copies of the statements is a good safeguard.

## What does a representative payee do?

A representative payee manages a Social Security or SSI payment for someone who cannot handle it. The Social Security Administration decides who is appointed, often a family member, and the payee must use the money for the person's current needs, such as housing and food, and set aside any remainder in an account in the beneficiary's interest. The payee reports to Social Security each year.

The role is narrow. A payee has no power over a pension, a bank account or a house, so many families that appoint one also need a power of attorney. Our page on [caregiver access to records](/learn/what-if/caregiver-cannot-get-medical-or-bank-records) describes what a family can and cannot get without paperwork.

## What does it mean to be a fiduciary?

A fiduciary has a legal duty to put another person's interests ahead of their own. The glossary defines [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary) and [fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty) in brief. In practice, it means:

1. Use the person's money only for their benefit.
2. Keep their funds apart from your own.
3. Keep records and be ready to show them.
4. Avoid deals where you profit at their expense.

An agent under a power of attorney, a trustee, a guardian and a conservator are all fiduciaries. A care manager or money manager is not automatically one, though some professionals voluntarily accept the duty in writing. If you want an outsider managing assets with full authority, hire a professional fiduciary or a bank trust department, and ask for a written fee schedule. Our page on [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers the family version of the same decision.

## How do you decide what help to hire?

Match the help to the problem and add legal authority only as needed.

1. **Name the problem.** Is it care coordination, bills, benefits, or decisions your parent can no longer make?
2. **Check what your parent can still sign.** If they have capacity, a power of attorney and health care directive come first.
3. **Start with the smallest role that works.** A care manager and a money manager with no legal authority leave your parent in charge.
4. **Add a safeguard.** A second person should see statements, and large payments should need two approvals.
5. **Put the terms in writing.** Fees, duties, hours and how either side ends the arrangement.

Hiring help can feel like admitting defeat. Often it is the opposite. Paid help takes friction out of the family, and a sibling who lives nearby stops doing everything while a sibling far away stops feeling guilty. If a family member does the work, a written arrangement matters; see [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

## What should you check before someone handles money?

Money is where trouble starts. Ask for references and call them. Confirm any license or bond the state requires. Do a background check. Get a written agreement and a list of every account the person will touch. Never let a helper become the sole contact on the account, and watch for gifts, loans or changes to documents. If something looks wrong, our guide to [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) and the page on [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) describe what to do.

## What about an elder law attorney?

An attorney is the one professional who can create the documents the others rely on. A lawyer who focuses on elder law can prepare powers of attorney and health care directives, plan for Medicaid, and advise on guardianship when that is necessary. Fees vary; our page on [what a Medicaid planning attorney costs](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning-attorney-cost) explains the usual structures.

## How state law changes this

States regulate professional fiduciaries differently, and some license them while others do not. Daily money managers and care managers are generally unregulated as such, though nurses and social workers hold their own licenses. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois) is the place to begin.
