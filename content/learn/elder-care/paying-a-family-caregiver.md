Many families quietly solve the care problem themselves. A daughter cuts back her hours, a son moves into his mother's spare room, a niece drives over every morning. At some point someone asks whether the parent should pay them, and the next question is whether that money will cause trouble if the parent later needs Medicaid for a nursing home.

It can, but it does not have to. This article explains why Medicaid scrutinizes family payments, what a caregiver agreement is, how agencies judge one, what the tax side looks like, and where an adult child acting under a power of attorney can go wrong. Medicaid rules differ by state, so treat the details as a map to the questions to ask, not as your state's final answer.

## Why does Medicaid care about payments to family?

Medicaid reviews money that left the applicant in the look-back period, which is 60 months in most states, and treats transfers for less than fair market value as gifts. A gift causes a penalty period: a stretch of months in which Medicaid will not pay for long-term care even though the person is otherwise eligible. The full mechanics are in [the Medicaid five-year look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

Paying a child is exactly the kind of transfer an eligibility worker notices. Medicaid does not ask whether the child deserved the money. It asks whether the parent received something of equal value in return. Care is something of value, so a payment for care at a fair rate is an exchange, not a gift. The paperwork is how you prove it.

### Example

Denise, 79, has early dementia and $140,000 in savings. Her son Marcus moves in and provides about 25 hours a week of help: meals, medication reminders, rides, bathing assistance. Denise hands him $2,000 a month in cash. Three years later she needs a nursing home and applies for Medicaid. The worker sees 36 monthly withdrawals of $2,000 and no contract, no log and no tax records. Much of that $72,000 may be treated as a gift. If Denise and Marcus had signed an agreement at the start, set the rate near local aide pricing and kept a simple log, the same payments would be easy to defend.

## What is a caregiver agreement?

A caregiver agreement, often called a personal care agreement or personal services contract, is a written contract in which a parent agrees to pay a family member for specific care at a specific rate. It is ordinary contract law applied to a family setting. A good one covers:

1. **Who the parties are.** The person receiving care and the caregiver, with the agent's role noted if someone signs for the parent.
2. **The services.** A real list: personal care, meal preparation, medication reminders, transportation, shopping, light housekeeping, appointment management, supervision.
3. **Hours and schedule.** Expected hours per week and how changes are handled.
4. **The rate.** An hourly or weekly amount tied to local market pricing, with the basis noted.
5. **Payment timing.** Regular, often weekly or biweekly, paid by check or bank transfer from the parent's account.
6. **Term and ending.** When it starts, how it ends, and what happens if the parent moves to a facility or dies. Many agreements end automatically on a move to a nursing home, because paid care at home is no longer being delivered.
7. **Records.** The caregiver agrees to keep a log and the parent or agent agrees to review it.
8. **Taxes.** Who handles withholding and reporting.

Sign it before the care is paid for, date it, and keep it with the parent's financial records. An attorney can draft it so that it fits your state's Medicaid manual and so that the parent's power of attorney and other documents are consistent with it.

## How do agencies evaluate caregiver agreements?

Agencies generally look for four things: a written agreement, a payment rate equal to fair market value, credible documentation that the work was done, and payment for future services rather than past ones. New York's published Medicaid guidance, for instance, describes a personal service contract as a formal written agreement requiring fair market value and credible documentation, and credits services only at a reasonable wage for the work performed. Other states vary, and a few are stricter than that, sometimes treating any payment to a relative as presumed to be a gift unless the agreement is in place beforehand.

| Item | Good practice | What agencies look for |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement | Written, dated and signed before paid care begins | A contract that predates the payments |
| Rate | Matches local agency or aide pricing; keep quotes | A wage that is reasonable for the tasks |
| Services | Specific tasks the parent actually needs | Care that is genuine, not companionship that was already free |
| Log | Dates, hours, tasks, initials, kept as you go | Contemporaneous records, not reconstructed ones |
| Payment | Regular check or transfer from the parent | A clear trail from the parent's account to the caregiver |
| Timing | Pay for care going forward, in step with the work | No large lump sums, no payment for past help |
| Taxes | Wages reported and taxes handled | Evidence the arrangement is a real job |

A common limit is that agencies are skeptical of paying for what a family member would have done anyway, and some will not credit payment for care that is not medically or functionally needed. A doctor's note describing the parent's needs strengthens the file.

## Can you pay in advance for years of care?

Usually this is risky. Some families want to prepay a lump sum, hoping to move money out before it counts. Agencies often treat large prepayments as gifts, or credit only the portion of services actually delivered. Where a state allows any prepayment structure, it generally involves a carefully drafted contract that refunds unused amounts. This is a topic to take to an attorney, not to attempt from a template.

## What are the tax rules?

Caregiver pay is generally taxable income to the caregiver, and the person paying may be a household employer. For 2026 the IRS household employer rules (see [IRS Publication 926](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p926)) require Social Security and Medicare taxes once cash wages to a household employee reach $3,000 for the year. There are exceptions, including for certain payments to a parent by a child, and for some payments to a spouse or to a person under 18, which is why a professional should look at your facts.

Other tax points to raise with a CPA:

- Whether the parent qualifies for deductions for medical expenses that include paid care.
- Whether the caregiver needs to adjust withholding or estimated payments.
- Whether the caregiver's Social Security record and unemployment coverage matter to your family.

The tax records help in two ways: they make the arrangement look like the real job it is, and they keep the caregiver from an unpleasant surprise.

## What if you are the parent's agent under a power of attorney?

An agent who pays themselves from the parent's money has a conflict of interest, and many powers of attorney do not allow it. Under a durable power of attorney the agent is a fiduciary who must act for the principal's benefit. Paying yourself, even at a fair rate, should have clear written authority.

Practical steps:

1. Read the power of attorney. Look for language that permits compensation for services and self-dealing, or gifts to the agent. Many generic forms are silent or prohibitive.
2. If it is silent, do not assume it is permitted. Ask an attorney whether your state's law and the document allow it.
3. Ideally, have the caregiver agreement signed by the parent while the parent still has capacity, not by the agent on both sides of the table.
4. If the agent is also the caregiver, have another family member, or a professional, review the log and payments, and tell your siblings.

[Choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) discusses what to put in the document and why compensation language matters.

## What are the alternatives to paying out of pocket?

Before you build an agreement, look at who else might pay.

- **State Medicaid programs.** Some states have consumer-directed or self-directed care programs that let a Medicaid recipient hire certain relatives, sometimes including adult children, as paid caregivers. Rules about which relatives qualify, and whether spouses can be paid, differ widely.
- **Veterans benefits.** A veteran or surviving spouse who qualifies for VA pension with Aid and Attendance may receive an added monthly amount, which can fund family care. See [VA Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance).
- **Long-term care insurance.** Some policies pay for home care, and some pay a family caregiver, depending on the contract. See [long-term care insurance and your estate plan](/learn/elder-care/long-term-care-insurance).
- **Local programs.** Area agencies on aging may offer respite, caregiver support or small stipends.

## What mistakes do families make?

- **Informal cash.** No record means no proof, and cash withdrawals look like gifts.
- **Back-dating or reconstructing.** An agreement signed after the care began, or a log written from memory the day before the application, loses credibility.
- **A lump sum.** One large payment to the caregiver looks like a transfer.
- **An inflated rate.** Pay far above market is partly a gift, and it can also fuel sibling resentment.
- **No tax compliance.** An unreported wage can create a problem with the IRS and weaken the story that this is a legitimate job.
- **Hiding it from siblings.** Secrecy is the quickest way to turn a caregiving arrangement into a conflict later. Consider telling the family in writing.

## How do you keep it fair among siblings?

Money and care are tangled in most families, and a caregiver agreement can make the arrangement clearer. A written agreement shows that the caregiving child was paid for work, which helps avoid the later argument that they took advantage, and it helps the siblings who are not paid understand why the estate does not need to "make up" for the unpaid care. Where a parent wants to compensate a caregiving child beyond wages, the will or trust is the right place, with the effect on Medicaid considered.
