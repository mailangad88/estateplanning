Every year around tax season, an adult child shows up with a folder of receipts for a parent. Some are from the memory care unit and some from the pharmacy. A few come from a home health agency. One is a check to a brother "for the roof." The child wants to know what is deductible, whether the parent still counts as a dependent, and whether anything done for the parent will come back as a tax bill later.

The rules are narrower than most people hope and kinder than some people fear. This article covers deductions, dependents, gift tax, the taxes on benefits and caregiver pay, and the tax side of selling or transferring a parent's home. Where Medicaid planning pulls in a different direction, we say so.

## The short version

- Itemize medical costs and you may deduct the part above 7.5% of adjusted gross income.
- A parent can count as your dependent for medical expense purposes even if their own income is too high to claim them for other things.
- Pay care providers directly. Direct payment of medical bills is outside gift tax.
- Medicaid benefits are not income. Paid caregiver wages are.
- Moving a house to a child to save on tax or Medicaid can backfire on both.

## Can you deduct a parent's medical and nursing home costs?

Yes, if you itemize deductions and the total of unreimbursed medical costs passes 7.5% of your adjusted gross income. Only the amount over that line is deductible. A person with a modest income and a large nursing home bill is more likely to benefit than a person with a high income and a few prescriptions.

There are two separate questions. Whose expenses are they, and are they medical?

### Whose expenses?

You can deduct a parent's medical expenses that you pay if the parent qualifies as your dependent under a special test that ignores the parent's gross income. In practice, you must provide over half of the parent's total support for the year, and the parent must be a citizen or resident. Several siblings who together provide more than half can sign a multiple support declaration to let one of them claim the deduction. Keep the paperwork.

If your parent pays their own bills from their own money, the deduction is theirs, on their return. A parent whose income is mostly Social Security often does not owe enough tax for the deduction to matter.

### Are they medical?

Doctors, prescriptions, hearing aids, dental work, insurance premiums (including Medicare Part B and D premiums), and many home modifications for a medical purpose generally count. For nursing homes, the IRS looks at why the person is there.

| Situation | Deductible? |
|---|---|
| Nursing home, main reason is medical care | The full bill, including meals and lodging |
| Nursing home, main reason is custodial or personal | Only the part that is for medical care |
| Assisted living with a plan of care from a licensed health professional | The care portion, which the facility can often break out |
| Chronically ill person receiving qualified long-term care services | Qualified services under a plan of care, with limits |
| Hired companion only to keep a parent company | Generally not |

Ask the facility for an annual statement that splits medical care from room and board. Without it, you are guessing at the portion.

## Can you claim your parent as a dependent?

You can if the parent passes four tests: relationship, citizenship, an income ceiling, and support. The one that trips people up is support. You must provide over half of the parent's total support, and "support" includes housing, food, medical care and everything else that adds up to their cost of living, whoever pays it.

Take Wendell, 61. His mother Ruth lives in assisted living at a cost of roughly $6,000 a month. Her Social Security and small pension cover about half. Wendell covers the rest. To decide whether he provides more than half of her support, he adds up everything spent on Ruth that year, including her own money, and compares his share. If she also has a savings account she spends down, that counts in her column.

Claiming a dependent parent can also open the door to the credit for care expenses that let you work, if the parent cannot care for themselves and lives with you for more than half of the year. It does not apply to a parent in a facility full time, so check the facts before filing.

## Is paying for a parent's care a taxable gift?

Usually not. Medical payments made directly to the provider are excluded from gift tax, no matter how large. That is a separate exclusion from the annual one, which is $19,000 per recipient in 2026. A child who writes a check to the nursing home is not making a gift to the parent for tax purposes.

Handing the parent cash is different. It is a gift to the parent, small enough in most families to fall under the annual exclusion, and it should be reported only if it goes over. Our article on the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) explains the reporting.

Gifts in the other direction, from the parent to the child, are where Medicaid takes over. The IRS may not care that a parent gave $80,000 to a daughter, but Medicaid will, because it reviews transfers in the look-back period, which is 60 months in most states. See [the five-year look-back](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

## Are benefits and caregiver pay taxable?

| Money | Taxable? |
|---|---|
| Medicaid payments to the facility | No |
| VA pension, disability compensation and Aid and Attendance | Generally no, see [VA Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance) |
| Long-term care insurance benefits | Generally not, up to limits for certain policies |
| Social Security | Partly, depending on total income |
| IRA or 401(k) withdrawals used for care | Yes, as ordinary income |
| Wages paid to a family caregiver | Yes, to the caregiver |

A parent who pays a child for care can become a household employer with withholding duties. In some state Medicaid waiver programs, pay a caregiver receives for caring for a person in the same home can be partly excluded from income. That is a narrow rule, so check it with a preparer. For the Medicaid angle and the agreement itself, see [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

## What are the taxes on selling or giving away the house?

The house is where tax and Medicaid planning most often collide.

**Selling it.** A parent who owned and lived in the home for two of the last five years can exclude a large part of the gain on a sale. The amount is $250,000 for a single seller and $500,000 for a couple selling together. A parent who moved to a care facility late in that period may still qualify. If the sale proceeds sit in the parent's account, Medicaid counts them. There is more in [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).

**Giving it to a child.** A gift during life carries over the parent's original purchase price to the child. If the parent bought for $90,000 and the house is worth $400,000 when the child sells, the child's taxable gain starts from $90,000. If the child inherits the house instead, the basis resets to the value at death, which often wipes out most of the gain. That is the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). A gift to avoid Medicaid can cost the child real money, and it starts the look-back clock.

**Inheriting it.** A child who inherits and sells soon after generally owes little tax. See [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property).

Some transfers are allowed without a Medicaid penalty, such as to a spouse or a disabled child, and some structures handle both tax and Medicaid better than an outright gift. That is a conversation for an attorney.

## What records should you keep?

1. A yearly statement from the facility that separates medical care from room and board.
2. Proof of who paid each bill, by check or bank transfer.
3. A signed physician's statement that care was medically required, if the stay is borderline.
4. A multiple support declaration if siblings share the cost.
5. The parent's income for the year, so you can run the support test.

## How state law changes this

Federal rules govern most of this, but state income tax rules differ. A few states offer their own credits or deductions for caregivers, and some do not follow the federal medical deduction in the same way. State estate recovery and Medicaid rules also determine how a house transfer plays out, and the [state guide](/estate-planning/ohio) for your parent's home state is a useful place to look.
