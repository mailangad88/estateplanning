Three years ago Dennis, 79, started paying his daughter Carla $600 a week to help him at home. She quit part-time work, drives him to dialysis and manages his pills. He writes her a check every Friday. Nobody has filed a payroll form, and Carla did not report the money last April because it "felt like family."

That is the usual story, and it is fixable. This article is about the mechanics: who is the employer, which forms are required, when withholding applies, and what changes if Medicaid is in the picture. The bigger question of whether and how to pay a relative is covered in [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver). Deductions for the person paying are in [tax breaks when you pay for a parent's care](/learn/elder-care/tax-breaks-when-you-pay-for-a-parents-care). Both sit under our [elder care guide](/learn/elder-care).

## Is a family caregiver an employee or a contractor?

For most home caregivers the answer is employee. The IRS looks at who controls the work. If you decide when the caregiver arrives, what tasks come first and what supplies are used, and the caregiver works in your home, the relationship looks like employment. The IRS household employer guide, [Publication 926](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p926), describes the test and gives examples of workers it treats as household employees, including people who care for someone in the home.

A contractor is in business for themselves. They advertise, serve several clients, bring their own tools and decide their own methods. A home care agency fits that description. A daughter who works only for her father does not, even if she calls herself "self-employed."

This matters because the labels carry different taxes. An employer withholds and pays part of the Social Security and Medicare tax. A contractor pays the whole self-employment tax. Calling an employee a contractor does not change the law, and it shifts the burden onto the person who can least afford it.

## When do household employer taxes start?

Social Security and Medicare taxes apply once you pay a household employee $3,000 or more in cash wages during 2026. The threshold is set by the IRS each year, so confirm the figure when you file. The combined rate is shared between employer and employee, with each side owing half, and the employer may choose to pay the employee's half as well.

Federal unemployment tax is a separate test. It can apply to the employer when cash wages reach $1,000 in any calendar quarter. Some states also have their own unemployment and disability programs with separate rules.

Some relatives are exempt, which is where families get confused:

| Who is paid | Is Social Security/Medicare tax generally due? |
|---|---|
| Your adult child (age 21 or older) | Yes, once the threshold is reached |
| Your child under age 21 | Usually no, for work done for a parent |
| Your spouse | Usually no |
| Your parent, when you are the employer | Special conditions apply; ask a preparer |
| An unrelated friend or neighbor | Yes, once the threshold is reached |

Read the table as a starting point. Dennis pays Carla, who is 52, so her wages count. A 19-year-old grandchild paid by a parent may fall under the exception for children under 21 in that parent's household, but a grandchild paid by a grandparent generally does not.

## What does the employer have to do?

The employer, here the parent, takes five steps. If a spouse or an agent runs the household finances, that person can do the work for the parent.

1. **Get an employer identification number.** The parent applies for an EIN with the IRS.
2. **Verify the worker's work eligibility.** Complete Form I-9 and keep it. Have the caregiver complete Form W-4 so you know whether any income tax will be withheld.
3. **Pay wages and keep a ledger.** Record the date, the hours, the rate and the amount paid. Pay by check or transfer so the money leaves a trail.
4. **Withhold and deposit as needed.** Withhold the employee's share of Social Security and Medicare tax, add the employer's share, and settle it with the IRS through the parent's own income tax return. Most household employers do not deposit quarterly; they pay on Schedule H, which is attached to Form 1040.
5. **Issue a W-2 after year end.** The employer gives the caregiver a W-2 and sends copies to the Social Security Administration with a W-3, normally by the end of January.

A parent who does not file an income tax return, because income is low, can still have to file for household employment tax. This surprises people. Ask a preparer if you are in that position.

## Do you have to withhold income tax?

You do not have to withhold federal income tax from a household employee unless the worker asks and you agree. If the caregiver wants withholding, the worker submits a Form W-4, and you take it out of each paycheck and report it. If not, Carla can make quarterly estimated payments, or she can raise her withholding at her own job if she has one.

Many families choose withholding because it avoids a lump of tax at filing time for a caregiver with no other income. It is a choice, not a requirement, and it should be written into the caregiver agreement.

## What does workers' compensation have to do with it?

A paid caregiver can be injured lifting a parent, and a household employee's injury can become the employer's cost. Some states require workers' compensation coverage for household employees, some exempt small households, and many leave it as an option. Homeowner's insurance often has an endorsement for domestic workers. Ask your insurance agent. It is a modest premium compared with a lifted-patient back injury.

## How do payroll records help with Medicaid?

Payroll records turn a family payment into a documented employment expense. When a Medicaid caseworker reviews five years of statements and finds weekly payments to a child, the question is whether the payments were a gift, which can cause a penalty, or fair pay for services. Our article on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains why the agency asks.

The strongest file contains:

- A written caregiver agreement, dated before the work began, that lists the tasks and hourly rate.
- A time log or a note on the days worked, signed by both people.
- Payment by check or electronic transfer, matching the agreement.
- A Form W-2 and Schedule H filing for each year.
- A rate consistent with local agency pricing for similar care.

Payments for care already given in the past, often called back pay or a lump sum "in advance," draw hard scrutiny. Do not use payroll to disguise a gift. If the plan really is a gift, say so and look at [gifting to children before Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid).

## Is there a way to avoid running payroll yourself?

Sometimes. Many state Medicaid home and community-based programs offer self-directed care, in which the participant or family chooses the caregiver, and a state-contracted fiscal agent handles the paperwork. The agent withholds the taxes, files the forms and pays the worker. Some programs allow a relative to be hired, with limits that vary by state. Our guide on the [Medicaid home care waiver](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-home-care-waiver) explains how these programs work and how waiting lists affect access.

Private payroll services also exist for household employers and handle filings for a monthly or yearly fee. For one caregiver, the cost can be well worth the time saved.

## What if the paperwork was never done?

Fix it from here, quietly and in order. A preparer or attorney can help you decide whether to file back forms, amend returns or begin with the current year. The IRS has procedures for households that missed filings, and voluntary corrections are generally treated far better than discoveries.

Before you do, collect bank statements, a list of what was paid, and any texts that show the arrangement. If your parent is applying for Medicaid soon, tell the attorney so the corrected records and the application tell the same story. If an agent under a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) is paying the caregiver, confirm the document permits it, and add safeguards if it does not.

## Questions to ask a tax professional

- Is my caregiver an employee for my facts, or is there an exception?
- Do I need an EIN, and who files Schedule H if my parent does not file a regular return?
- Does my state require unemployment insurance or workers' compensation coverage?
- If the caregiver is in the same home as a Medicaid waiver participant, does the income exclusion for certain waiver payments apply?
- What documents should I hold for a Medicaid review?
