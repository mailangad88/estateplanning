Veterans and their surviving spouses often hear about Aid and Attendance late, usually when a family is already looking at assisted living bills. It can be a real help. It can also become a source of expensive mistakes, because the benefit comes with financial limits and a look-back rule, and some of what people try in order to qualify is neither permitted nor wise.

This article explains what the benefit is, who may qualify, how the financial test works, how the 36-month look-back differs from Medicaid's, and how the benefit fits with the rest of an estate plan. Figures are as of 2026 and change each December, so confirm them on VA.gov before you rely on them.

## What is VA Aid and Attendance?

Aid and Attendance is an increased monthly payment added to the VA pension for people who need regular help with daily life. The pension itself is a needs-based benefit for wartime veterans and surviving spouses with limited income. It is different from VA disability compensation, which is paid for service-connected conditions regardless of financial need.

Aid and Attendance money is paid directly to the beneficiary and can be spent on care: an aide at home, assisted living, memory care or a nursing home. A related higher rate, Housebound, applies to those who are largely confined to the home. A person cannot receive both at once.

## Who may qualify?

A person generally needs to meet service, care-need and financial tests.

- **Service.** A veteran must have served at least 90 days of active duty, with at least one day during a wartime period, and have a discharge other than dishonorable. Rules for service length have exceptions and details for those who served after 1980.
- **Surviving spouses.** A surviving spouse of a qualifying veteran may apply, subject to the marriage rules and the same financial test.
- **Care need.** According to the VA, the person must need another person's help with daily activities such as bathing, dressing or eating, or be bedridden, or live in a nursing home because of a physical or mental disability, or have very limited eyesight.
- **Financial test.** Income and net worth must fall within the limits below.

## How does the financial test work?

The VA looks at net worth, which is the total of countable assets and annual income, with deductions. The limit currently stands at $163,699 for the period December 1, 2025 to November 30, 2026, and is adjusted each December.

- **Income.** Annual income is counted but reduced by unreimbursed medical expenses, including the cost of assisted living or in-home care when needed. This is why Aid and Attendance can work for someone whose income looks too high on paper.
- **Assets.** Savings, investments, and other property count. Retirement accounts generally count at their value.
- **Home.** The primary residence is generally excluded, along with a limited lot size. A second home counts.
- **Vehicles and belongings.** An ordinary vehicle and personal property are generally excluded.

The final benefit amount depends on income after deductions, the number of dependents and the type of benefit, and it is calculated by the VA. Check the VA's pension rate tables for current amounts.

### Example

Walter, 84, a Vietnam-era veteran, lives in assisted living at $5,800 a month. He has $60,000 in savings, Social Security and a small pension totaling $2,600 a month, and a home he no longer lives in that is on the market. The sale proceeds will count. His medical-expense deductions for assisted living could offset much of his income, but the house sale could push him over the net worth limit. Whether and when to file depends on exact figures, which is where an accredited representative earns their keep.

## What is the 36-month look-back?

If a person transfers assets for less than fair value during the 36 months before filing a pension claim, the VA can impose a penalty period, up to five years, during which pension benefits are not paid. The rule applies to transfers made on or after October 18, 2018. The penalty length depends on the amount transferred above what would have kept the person under the net worth limit.

Points to keep in mind:

- **It is shorter than Medicaid's look-back.** Medicaid looks back 60 months in most states. The VA's 36 months does not mean a transfer that is safe for the VA is safe for Medicaid.
- **Returning assets can help.** The rules allow a penalty to be avoided in some cases if assets are returned within a set time. This depends on the timing and the type of transfer, so ask before acting.
- **Some transfers are treated differently.** Transfers into certain trusts for a child who cannot support themselves are an example. Details are technical.
- **A penalty is not a denial for all purposes.** It affects the pension, not other benefits, but it can still leave a family without help when care bills are running.

## How do VA benefits and Medicaid fit together?

They follow different rules, so planning for one can harm the other. A transfer that the VA ignores after 36 months may still cause a Medicaid penalty at month 40. Medicaid also counts a veteran's VA benefits as income, and the pension amount can change the Medicaid share of cost.

| Feature | VA pension with Aid and Attendance | Medicaid long-term care | VA disability compensation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Based on need | Yes, income and net worth | Yes, income and assets | No |
| Look-back | 36 months | 60 months in most states | None |
| Countable assets | Net worth limit combines assets and income | Low asset limit, varies by state | Not tested |
| Home | Excluded up to a lot-size limit | Exempt within an equity limit | Not tested |
| Estate recovery | No | Yes, rules vary by state | No |

Many families use the VA benefit first, because it can start while assets remain, and Medicaid later. A coordinated plan looks at both sets of rules. Our [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) article explains the other side, and [spend-down for Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down) covers what fair-value spending looks like.

## How do estate planning tools interact with the VA rules?

A revocable trust does not shelter assets from the VA, since the owner still controls them. An irrevocable trust can remove assets, but a transfer into one counts as a transfer for less than fair value inside the look-back, and the owner gives up control. Annuities, gifts to children and "income-only" trusts are sold by some businesses as solutions, and each has costs and risks, including loss of access to your own money and the tax cost of losing a basis step-up on gifted property.

Other documents matter as well:

- A durable power of attorney that lets an agent deal with the VA and the bank, and that is clear about gifting powers. See [powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney).
- A healthcare directive so a chosen person can speak with doctors who complete VA medical forms. See [healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).
- A [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs) if a child has a disability.

## Who is allowed to help with a VA claim?

Federal law limits who may prepare, present and prosecute VA claims. Only attorneys, claims agents and Veterans Service Organization representatives accredited by the VA Office of General Counsel may do this, and under VA regulations (38 CFR 14.636) they may not charge a fee for help with an initial application. Fees may be charged only after the VA issues an initial decision, and only within the regulation's limits. An unaccredited business that charges for "benefits planning" or application help is a warning sign.

Free help is available through VA-accredited Veterans Service Organizations and county veterans service officers, and the VA publishes an accreditation search tool. Our attorneys can help with the estate planning side, such as how a gift, trust or sale affects your overall plan, but you should use an accredited representative to prepare and file the VA claim itself.

## How do you apply, and what do you gather?

Applications are made through VA.gov, by mail or with an accredited representative. Gather these before you start:

1. DD-214 or other discharge papers.
2. Marriage and death certificates for surviving spouse claims.
3. A doctor's statement describing the need for help with daily activities, and any facility care records.
4. Bank, brokerage and retirement statements, and records of any transfers in the last three years.
5. Records of unreimbursed medical and care costs, including assisted living bills.
6. Social Security, pension and other income letters.
7. Information about the home, any other real estate and any trusts.
