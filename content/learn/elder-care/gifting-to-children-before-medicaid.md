Ruth is 81 and healthy, but her doctor has started using the word "eventually." She has $200,000 in savings and a paid-off house, and she wants to hand a chunk to her two daughters now so the nursing home cannot take it. It is one of the most common thoughts an older parent has, and one of the easiest to get wrong.

The short version is that Medicaid does allow gifts. It just does not let the gift and the benefit overlap. This page covers what a gift costs you, which gifts are free of penalty, and what families do instead. The rule itself is laid out in our [look-back period guide](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period); here we focus on the decision to give.

## How does Medicaid treat a gift to your children?

Medicaid treats any transfer for less than fair value within the look-back window as a reason to delay coverage. In nearly every state that window is 60 months before the date you apply and need care. A gift does not disqualify you forever. It buys your children a head start and costs you a waiting period.

The waiting period works like this. The state totals what you gave away, divides it by a monthly figure it sets for the average private-pay cost of a nursing home, and the answer is the number of months you are ineligible. Fractions of a month count.

Here is the part that catches families. Since 2006 the clock does not start on the day of the gift. It starts when three things are true at once: you are in a facility or receiving covered care, you have applied, and you have already spent down to the asset limit. Until then, no months are being "served."

### What does that look like with real numbers?

Say Ruth gives each daughter $50,000, a total of $100,000. Three years later she needs a nursing home and spends her remaining savings down to her state's limit. If her state's divisor were $10,000 a month (an illustration, not any state's figure), she would face ten months of ineligibility starting from that point. Someone has to pay roughly ten months of nursing home bills with no Medicaid and no savings. That someone is usually the daughters, from the money Ruth gave them.

## Is there a yearly amount that is safe to give?

No. This is the most repeated misunderstanding. The IRS lets you give $19,000 per person in 2026 without filing a gift tax return, and families assume that figure is also a Medicaid safe harbor. It is not. The [annual gift tax exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) belongs to the tax code. Medicaid is a separate program with a separate definition, and it counts a $5,000 gift the same way it counts a $5,000 gift above the IRS line.

In practice, caseworkers do not chase every holiday check. A pattern of modest gifts consistent with years of statements usually draws little attention. But "usually" is not a rule you can plan around, and a string of round-number withdrawals in the last year before an application reads very differently.

## Which gifts do not trigger a penalty?

Federal law lists transfers that are exempt. They are narrower than most people hope.

| Gift or transfer | Penalty? | What has to be true |
|---|---|---|
| To your spouse | None | Unlimited, though it may shift the burden to the spouse's later needs |
| To a trust only for a blind or disabled child | None | Disability as Social Security defines it |
| The home to a minor, blind or disabled child | None | The child must meet the definition |
| The home to a child who gave you care | None | The child lived there at least two years and the care kept you out of a facility |
| The home to a sibling with an equity stake | None | Sibling lived there at least a year before your admission |
| To a trust for a disabled person under 65 | None | Usually a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) with payback terms |
| Anything else given away | Yes | Even small, even to family |

The caregiver-child exemption deserves a warning. It is real, but it takes paper: a physician's statement that you would have needed a facility without the help, plus proof the child lived in the home. A child who "helped out a lot" while living elsewhere does not qualify.

## What if the gift was already made?

You have options, and they depend on timing.

1. **Count the months.** Find the date of each gift. If it is more than 60 months before you would apply, it falls outside the window and carries no penalty.
2. **Gather proof of purpose.** If a gift was made for a reason other than qualifying for Medicaid, such as paying a grandchild's tuition years ago, documents can sometimes persuade the agency.
3. **Consider returning it.** Many states reduce or erase the penalty if the gift comes back in full. A partial return may reduce it proportionally. The returned money is yours again, so it goes to care.
4. **Run the arithmetic on a partial approach.** Some families keep enough to pay privately through the penalty while the rest stays with the children. This is often called "half a loaf" planning and it is state-sensitive.
5. **Ask whether a hardship waiver fits.** If the penalty would leave you without food, shelter or medical care, most states have a process, though it is hard to win.

Do not return money to a child's account casually. If the child is going through a divorce, has a lien or has already spent it, the options narrow quickly.

## Why not just add a child to the account or the deed?

Because it is a gift, and a worse one. Adding a daughter to a bank account or a deed hands her a legal interest. Her creditors can reach it. If she dies first, part of it passes to her heirs. It also ties the gift's value to the date of the change, which sets a Medicaid clock, and for a house it can cost her the step-up in basis that she would otherwise get at your death. We explain the tax side in [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) and the ownership risks in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

A daughter who is a convenience signer on the account, with no ownership right, does not receive a gift. The paperwork has to say that clearly.

## What do families do instead of gifting?

Three routes come up most.

- **Pay for what you need.** Spending on a needed roof, a hearing aid or a funeral contract is not a gift. See [Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down) for what qualifies.
- **Use an irrevocable trust, early.** Assets placed in a [Medicaid asset protection trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) more than 60 months before an application are outside the window. You give up the principal; you can keep income and the right to live in the home.
- **Pay a caregiver child fairly, under a written agreement.** Compensation for services actually provided is not a gift if it is reasonable and in writing. Vague, back-dated payments are. See [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

If care is already needed, the conversation shifts to what can still be done. Our [home protection guide](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) walks through the house specifically.

## How does state law change this?

States set their own divisors, their own approach to partial months, how they treat returned gifts and how closely they audit small transfers. California's look-back has been handled differently from the rest of the country in recent years, so confirm the current rule. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/california) and the one for your own state are a starting point, but the Medicaid agency's current rules control.
