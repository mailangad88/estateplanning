A parent's accounts are in your name as agent, and a sibling asks you to send each grandchild a check. Or you are the parent, and you wonder whether the person you name should be allowed to give money away if you are ever unable to. Either way the question is the same: does a power of attorney let an agent make gifts?

The short answer is that it depends on the words in the document. This page covers when gifts are allowed, why gifts to the agent are the dangerous kind, and how Medicaid and the gift tax treat gifts made on someone else's behalf.

## The short version

- A broad grant of financial power does not, by itself, authorize gifts in most states. Gifting is a "hot power" that has to be written out.
- Gifts to the agent are the ones courts look at hardest. Allow them only deliberately, with a cap.
- The tax number and the Medicaid number are different. A $19,000 gift can be tax free and still cause a Medicaid penalty.
- An agent should never improvise a gift. Read the document, write down the reason, and keep the records.

## Can an agent give away the principal's money?

Only if the document says so, and the statute in your state agrees. An agent's job is to manage the principal's property for the principal's benefit. Giving property away does the opposite, so the law treats it as an extra power rather than part of the ordinary package.

Many states have adopted some version of the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which lists gifts, trust changes, beneficiary changes and survivorship changes as powers that must be expressly granted. Some states, including [New York](/estate-planning/new-york) and [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois), have statutory forms with their own gifting rules. Where the statute sets a default, such as a small annual amount or gifts only in line with the principal's past pattern, the document can raise or lower it.

Our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney) covers the whole subject, and the page on [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) lists the other hot powers. Gifting is the one that comes up most.

## What should a gifting clause say?

A useful gifting clause answers six questions. If yours leaves any of them open, an agent will have to guess, and guessing with someone else's money is how families end up in court.

1. **Who may receive gifts?** Name the class: the principal's children and grandchildren, a charity the principal has supported, or a list by name.
2. **How much per recipient per year?** Many documents tie the cap to the federal annual exclusion, currently $19,000 per recipient in 2026, so no gift tax return is needed.
3. **Is there an overall ceiling?** A cap on total gifts per year stops a large estate from being drained by many small ones.
4. **Does the principal's care come first?** Say that gifts may be made only when the principal's income and savings cover their own living and care costs.
5. **Can the agent receive gifts?** If yes, say how much. If no, say so.
6. **Does it match the estate plan?** A gift that favors one child over another can undo a will that split things equally.

The last point matters more than people expect. Take Hank, whose will leaves everything equally to his three children. His daughter Dee is his agent and, over four years, gives her own family $15,000 a year "to use up the exclusion." Hank's estate now has $60,000 less, all of it passed to one child. The siblings find out at the funeral. A clause that said gifts must be equal among the children, or that required the agent to follow the will, would have prevented the argument.

## Can the agent give gifts to themselves?

Rarely, and never casually. An agent who pays themselves is on both sides of the transaction, which is a textbook conflict of interest. Courts in many states presume such a transfer is suspect and put the burden on the agent to show it was authorized and fair.

There are three situations where self-gifts are reasonable:

- The agent is also the principal's child and the principal has a long habit of giving each child the same yearly amount.
- The document names the agent as an allowed recipient and sets a cap.
- The principal, while still able, approved the specific gift in writing.

Outside those cases, assume the answer is no. Paying the agent for real work is a different subject, handled through a written caregiver agreement; see [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) for how to do it so that it is not treated as a gift. Paying the agent's own bills out of the principal's account is not a gift and not a salary. It is the kind of conduct covered in [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

## How does the gift tax treat gifts made by an agent?

The tax rules look at the principal as the giver, not the agent. In 2026 a person can give $19,000 to each recipient without any reporting. A gift over that amount requires the agent to file a federal gift tax return, Form 709, for the principal. No tax is usually due, because the excess just reduces the principal's lifetime exemption, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026 and indexed for inflation after that.

So for most families the tax angle is paperwork, not money. The real issues are fairness and Medicaid. For the tax side in more depth, see [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).

One more point: gifts made during life do not get the step-up in basis that inherited assets receive. A child who is given appreciated stock keeps the parent's original cost, which can mean a larger capital gains tax bill later than if the child had inherited the stock.

## Can an agent gift money to avoid the Medicaid look-back?

Not in the way people hope. When someone applies for nursing home Medicaid, the state reviews every transfer for less than fair value made during the look-back period, 60 months in most states. A gift inside that window produces a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay for nursing home care. It does not matter whether the principal or the agent signed the check.

| Question | Gift tax answer | Medicaid answer |
|---|---|---|
| Is $19,000 to a child fine? | Yes, under the 2026 exclusion | Still a transfer; counts in the look-back |
| Is a gift to a grandchild treated differently? | Same annual exclusion per person | Same, a transfer for less than value |
| Does the agent's signature change anything? | No, the principal is the donor | No, the principal is the applicant |
| Is a gift to a disabled child or a spouse special? | Often not taxable | Some transfers to a spouse or disabled child are exempt |
| How far back is the review? | Not applicable | 60 months in most states |

Some transfers are exempt, such as certain transfers to a spouse or to a disabled child, and some trusts are built for this purpose. An agent can carry out a Medicaid plan only if the power of attorney allows it, the plan is in the principal's interest, and the numbers have been checked against state rules. A parent with a clear plan from an attorney is a very different case from an adult child who decides to "get ahead of it" by moving money. The second one risks a penalty period for the parent and a fight with the siblings. See [gifting to children before Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/gifting-to-children-before-medicaid) and the [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) for how the five-year window is counted.

## What if the document is silent on gifts?

Then treat gifts as not allowed. Do not assume a broad form covers them.

Steps for an agent in that position:

1. Re-read the document for words like "gift," "make gifts," "transfer without consideration" or "benefit the agent."
2. Check whether your state has a default rule. Some allow small gifts consistent with the principal's history.
3. Ask whether the gift is needed for the principal. Paying the principal's own medical bill is not a gift. A birthday check of the sort the principal always sent may be fine in states with a small default.
4. If a larger gift or a Medicaid plan is the goal, talk to an attorney before moving money. Some states allow a court to authorize a gift for an incapacitated person.
5. Keep a record of the reason, the date, the amount and who agreed.

An agent who goes outside the document can be ordered to return the money, and may be personally liable. A well-meant gift to a grandchild's college fund is still a problem if the principal's own care later costs more than expected.

## What if you are still able to decide?

Then put your wishes in writing now. If you would like your agent to keep up your yearly gifts to your children, say so, with a number. If you would rather no gifts ever be made, say that too, and consider adding that the agent cannot be a recipient. Our guide to [choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers the person, and the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) page covers the rest of the document. If your family has a history of disagreement, name someone besides the person who benefits most.
