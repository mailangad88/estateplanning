The two arrangements can look similar from the outside, since in both a person makes decisions for someone who cannot. Underneath, they are opposites. A power of attorney is a choice you make. A guardianship is something a court does to you.

This article explains how each works, how they compare in practice, and when a court process is still unavoidable. If you are still deciding whether to sign a power of attorney, our overview of [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) is the best place to start.

If a parent has dementia and no document, see [what to do when there is no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

## What is a guardianship or conservatorship?

Guardianship and conservatorship are court-supervised arrangements in which a judge declares that an adult cannot manage some or all of their affairs and appoints another person to do it. The adult is called the ward, the protected person or the respondent, depending on the state.

State terminology is not uniform:

- Some states use **guardian** for decisions about the person, such as medical care and living arrangements, and **conservator** for money and property.
- Other states use **guardian** for both, sometimes with "guardian of the person" and "guardian of the estate."
- A few states use words such as "committee" or "curator."

Our glossary entries on [guardian](/glossary/guardian) and [conservatorship](/glossary/conservatorship) have the basic definitions. If you are looking for guardianship for children, that is a different subject, covered in our [guardianship for children guide](/learn/guardianship).

## How does the court process work?

Details differ by state, but the pattern is similar.

1. **A petition is filed** by a relative, friend, facility or government agency, claiming the person is incapacitated and needs help.
2. **Notice is served** on the person and close relatives. The person usually has a right to attend and to contest.
3. **Evaluation.** A physician or other professional examines the person and reports on their abilities. Some courts appoint a visitor or investigator.
4. **A lawyer may be appointed** for the person at the estate's expense.
5. **Hearing.** A judge decides whether the legal standard of incapacity is met and whether less restrictive options are available.
6. **Order and letters.** If granted, the court issues an order and a certificate of authority. The guardian may have to post a bond and complete training.
7. **Ongoing supervision.** Annual accountings and status reports, court approval for major actions such as selling a house, and eventual closing of the case.

An uncontested case can resolve in weeks to a few months. A contested case can take far longer.

## How do the two compare?

| Feature | Power of attorney | Guardianship or conservatorship |
|---|---|---|
| Who starts it | You, while you have capacity | A petitioner, after incapacity |
| Who picks the decision-maker | You | The judge, who may consider your wishes |
| Court involvement | None unless a dispute arises | Ongoing |
| Public record | Private | Court file; many states keep it open |
| Cost | Modest, one time | Higher up front plus ongoing |
| Speed | Immediate once signed | Weeks to months |
| Effect on your rights | You keep legal capacity and can revoke | May lose rights to contract, travel, vote or marry, depending on the order and state |
| Oversight | Little unless someone challenges | Reports, audits, court approvals |
| Flexibility | Broad or narrow as you choose | Limited to what the order and law permit |

The oversight in guardianship is both its cost and its benefit. A court can supervise a guardian in a way that no one supervises an agent under a power of attorney, which is why guardianship sometimes becomes necessary when abuse is suspected.

## Why do families end up in court?

Most guardianship cases arise from avoidable problems.

- **No documents at all.** An adult with dementia or a brain injury has signed nothing, and now cannot.
- **Documents exist but are not accepted.** A bank or hospital refuses the document, and the family gives up. See [what to do when a bank refuses](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) before concluding that a court is required.
- **Capacity was already gone** when the family tried to sign.
- **The named agent is unable or unwilling,** and no successor was named.
- **Family conflict** over who should decide, or accusations of misuse.
- **The person resists help** and refuses to sign anything, while genuinely unable to protect themselves.

Here is a typical case. Walter, 79, never signed a power of attorney. After a stroke he cannot communicate reliably. His wife Helen finds that his individually owned brokerage account, which holds most of their savings, is frozen to her. She must hire a lawyer, file a petition, pay for a medical evaluation, and wait for a hearing before she can touch the account. A power of attorney signed years earlier would have avoided all of it.

## Does a court have to consider a power of attorney?

In most states a judge is directed to consider less restrictive alternatives before taking away someone's rights. Valid powers of attorney, healthcare directives, trusts and similar tools are those alternatives. If they are working, a petition is often dismissed or narrowed. If the agent is abusing the role, the court can suspend the agent's authority and appoint a conservator instead.

Your documents also tell the court what you wanted. Many states honor a person's earlier nomination of a guardian, found in a healthcare power of attorney or similar document, unless there is good reason to appoint someone else. So even if the court process cannot be avoided, naming people in advance usually affects who is chosen.

## What are the other alternatives to guardianship?

Besides powers of attorney, families have several tools that may avoid the need for a court:

- **A revocable living trust,** so that a successor trustee can manage trust assets without court involvement. See [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).
- **Joint accounts or convenience accounts,** with their own risks, since the other owner has full rights.
- **Representative payees** for Social Security and VA benefits.
- **Healthcare surrogate statutes,** which in many states let a family member make medical decisions even without a signed document.
- **Supported decision-making agreements,** recognized in a growing number of states, used mostly for adults with developmental disabilities. Our article on [alternatives for adult children with disabilities](/learn/special-needs/guardianship-alternatives-for-adult-children) covers this.

Planning for cognitive decline specifically requires timing and capacity checks; see [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

## When is guardianship actually the right tool?

Honest advice should acknowledge that guardianship is sometimes right. It may be appropriate when:

- A person lacks capacity and has no documents, and decisions cannot wait
- An agent has abused authority and a neutral protector is needed
- A person is being financially exploited and needs court-ordered protection
- A young adult with significant intellectual disability turns 18 and no lesser arrangement fits, although limited or partial guardianships are worth asking about
- Family members disagree and need a neutral decision-maker

Even then, ask whether a limited guardianship is possible. Many states allow the court to restrict the guardian's authority to specific areas and leave the rest with the person.

## How do you avoid it?

The practical steps are not complicated.

1. Sign a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) and a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) while you have capacity.
2. Name successors so a single unavailable person does not force a court case.
3. Fund a living trust if you own a house or substantial assets.
4. Share copies with your agents and the institutions that matter.
5. Update the documents every few years and after major life events.

Our [documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) lists what a complete plan includes. State rules and terminology differ, so a guide for your own state, such as our [California estate planning guide](/estate-planning/california) or [Florida guide](/estate-planning/florida), can show how local law approaches these questions.

## What questions should you ask an attorney?

Bring these to a planning meeting:

- Should my power of attorney take effect now, or only if I lose capacity?
- What powers should my agent have, such as making gifts or changing beneficiaries?
- Who should be my agent, and who should be the backup?
- Can I nominate a guardian in case one is ever needed?
- Will banks in my state accept my form, or do they require their own?
- If a parent already lacks capacity, what are the options short of full guardianship?
