A power of attorney is only as useful as the person who accepts it. You may have signed it perfectly well, and the bank still says it is not good enough. Signing formalities are usually why.

This page, one of several in our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), explains what states ask for, why a notary is the safe route even where it is not strictly required, who should and should not witness, and what to do when the principal physically cannot sign.

## The short version

- Plan on a notary. It satisfies the law in most states and the practical test almost everywhere.
- If your state also requires witnesses, bring two neutral adults.
- Do not use the agent, the agent's spouse or a beneficiary as a witness.
- Sign while the principal has capacity. An unsigned or late document is the root of most disputes.
- Healthcare directives have separate rules. See [whether a living will needs a notary](/learn/healthcare-directives/does-a-living-will-need-a-notary).

## When is notarization required?

It depends on the state, and the trend is toward requiring it. Broadly:

| Approach | What it means | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Notary required | Signature must be acknowledged before a notary | Most common for financial powers |
| Two witnesses required | Signed before two adult witnesses, no notary needed | A few states, but institutions may still want a notary |
| Notary and witnesses | Both formalities | A few states |
| Neither is required by statute | Valid when signed by the principal | Rare, and a practical risk |

The Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which many states have adopted, requires the principal's signature and either a notary's acknowledgment or two witnesses. Several states layer on their own variations. Check the state guide, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois), or ask an attorney.

Think of it from the receiving side. A teller or a county recorder does not want to decide whether a signature is genuine. The notary's stamp is proof of identity that they can rely on. If the document is not notarized, they often decline it, which is why [banks refuse powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) in the first place. A recorder will usually refuse any real estate deed signed by an agent unless the underlying power is acknowledged, as we explain in [power of attorney and real estate](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate).

## Who can serve as a witness or notary?

Rules vary, but the common pattern is:

1. The witness is an adult, 18 or older.
2. The witness is not the agent or a successor agent.
3. In many states, the witness is not the agent's spouse, the principal's spouse or someone named as a beneficiary.
4. The notary is commissioned in the state, does not benefit from the document and is not the agent.

Two co-workers at the attorney's office are a classic choice because they are neutral and easy to find. Avoid the daughter who holds the power and the son who sits in the corner. If a sibling later challenges the document, a conflicted witness makes it easier to attack.

## What happens at a proper signing?

Most signings are short.

1. The principal shows photo ID.
2. The principal confirms they understand what the document does and who the agent is.
3. The principal signs and dates it in front of the notary, and in front of witnesses if required.
4. The notary completes the acknowledgment, signs and stamps.
5. Copies are made, and the original goes to a safe place the agent can reach.

A good attorney will ask a few simple questions to confirm capacity, because that record may matter later. The notary can refuse to proceed if the person seems confused or is being pressed, but a notary is not a medical examiner and should not be your capacity check.

## What if the principal cannot sign?

The law has a workaround. In most states, someone else may sign for the principal in the principal's presence and at the principal's direction, usually with a notary present. Alternatively, the principal can make a mark, such as an X, which witnesses confirm. Weak grip, tremor, a hospital bed and a missing arm are all manageable if everyone handles it correctly. The point is that the principal clearly wants the document and understands it.

Suppose Frank, 76, has had a stroke and cannot hold a pen. His wife Joan cannot sign for him, since she would be the agent. His neighbor Priya signs his name at his request while the notary and two witnesses watch. The notary records what happened in the acknowledgment. This is much safer than a rushed signature at the bedside by a family member who benefits.

If capacity is the question rather than physical ability, a signed power of attorney may not be possible at all. Then you may be heading toward a court process, which we compare in [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship). The article on [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) explains how to judge whether your parent can still sign.

## Does the power of attorney need to be recorded or filed?

Not to be valid. A financial power of attorney is a private document unless it is used for something that must be recorded, such as a real estate transfer. Some states ask that a power of attorney used in a land transaction be recorded in the county. Others require nothing at all. Do not file it with a court or the county on your own unless an advisor tells you to. The original should stay with you or your attorney until it is needed.

## Is an out-of-state power of attorney valid?

Generally yes, if it was valid where it was signed. Many states say so by statute. In practice, a bank in a different state or a county recorder may be slower to accept it, so it can be worth updating if you move. We cover the reasons in [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## Should the signing happen at a lawyer's office?

It does not have to, but there are good reasons to choose it. An attorney's staff know your state's witness rules, can handle the acknowledgment form correctly and will keep a record of who was in the room. If a relative later questions whether the principal understood the document, the attorney's file notes and the neutral witnesses are the best evidence you can have. A notary at a bank branch is fine for a simple document when everyone is clear about the rules, but nobody there will notice that a required witness line is blank.

If the principal cannot travel, many attorneys and mobile notaries will come to a home, hospice or hospital. Plan for a quiet room, a clear head and time of day when the principal is at their best. People with early memory loss often do much better in the morning.

## Common mistakes

- Signing the document with no notary and assuming that is fine
- Using the agent as a witness
- Leaving the date blank or using a copy instead of the original
- Skipping the acknowledgment page when copying or scanning
- Using a form from another state with the wrong signing language
- Waiting until the principal has lost capacity
