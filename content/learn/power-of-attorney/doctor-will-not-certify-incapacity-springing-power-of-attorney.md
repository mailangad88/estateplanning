Linda is 74 and has early Alzheimer's. Two years ago she signed a springing power of attorney naming her daughter Renata as agent, and the document says it starts only when "two physicians certify in writing that I am unable to manage my affairs." Now Linda has missed three mortgage payments and given $4,000 to a caller. Renata walked into the family doctor's office with the document. The doctor said she could not sign anything without a recent exam and would not discuss Linda's health with anyone.

That is the central problem with a springing document, one of the choices covered in our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney). The power sits in a drawer until a doctor says so, and doctors have reasons to hesitate. This page explains why a doctor might refuse, who else can sign, and what to do in order if you are stuck. It applies in Illinois, New Jersey and most other states, because the trigger comes from the document itself.

## Why do doctors refuse to certify incapacity?

Most refusals are not about the patient. They are about privacy, process or fear of being wrong. The reasons our attorneys hear most often:

- **Privacy.** Without a signed [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization), the doctor cannot confirm that Linda is a patient, let alone discuss her capacity with Renata.
- **No recent exam.** A doctor who last saw the patient eight months ago will not sign a statement about today.
- **Liability worries.** Certifying that someone cannot manage money can lead to a complaint by the patient or a relative, so some offices treat it as a legal opinion they would rather not give.
- **Wrong person asked.** Capacity is a judgment about decision-making, not a lab result. A busy primary care doctor may defer to a neurologist or geriatrician.
- **A wording problem.** The document asks for something the doctor cannot truthfully say, such as "permanently incapacitated," when the person is confused only some days.
- **Office policy.** Some practices refuse every request for a letter that will be used outside treatment.

Knowing which reason applies tells you what to fix. A privacy refusal needs paperwork. A wording refusal needs a conversation or, later, a new document.

## What does the document say, and who can sign?

Read the trigger clause before you call anyone. Both Illinois and New Jersey let the person who signs the power of attorney decide when it starts, and the clause controls. Illinois's statutory form lets the principal choose a future date or event, and the New Jersey statute treats the document as the guide, with a default meaning of disability as being unable to manage property and affairs effectively. Our pages on the [Illinois power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property) and the [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) cover those rules.

| Wording in the document | Who can usually sign | Common trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "One licensed physician" | Any physician who has examined the person | Bank wants a specific form of letter |
| "Two physicians" | Two separate doctors, not two partners who share a chart | Scheduling the second doctor takes weeks |
| "My attending physician" | The doctor currently treating the person | Person has no regular doctor |
| "A physician or licensed psychologist" | Either kind of clinician | Easy to overlook the option |
| "My spouse and my doctor" | Both must agree | One of them is unavailable or disputes it |
| No method stated | Unclear; a bank may insist on a doctor's letter, or a court | Disagreements land in court |

If your document names an outdated person, such as a doctor who has retired, you cannot simply swap in someone new. Stay as close to the wording as you can.

## What should the agent do first when a doctor says no?

Work through these in order. Most families get a signature by step four.

1. **Collect the paperwork.** You need the signed power of attorney, a government ID for the agent, and a HIPAA release signed by the principal. If the principal can still sign a release, get that done now. It is the single most useful page you can have.
2. **Write the request.** Send a short letter or portal message that quotes the trigger sentence and asks one question: "Based on your examination, do you believe my mother is unable to manage her property and financial affairs?" Attach the document. A vague phone call produces a vague answer.
3. **Book an exam, not a favor.** Ask for an appointment whose purpose is a capacity assessment. Bring a list of what has gone wrong: missed bills, scam payments, repeated purchases. Specific facts help the doctor.
4. **Ask a different clinician.** A geriatrician, neurologist, geriatric psychiatrist or psychologist does capacity work routinely. A hospital social worker can usually point you to one, and a hospital stay often produces a documented finding of its own.
5. **Ask the letter to match the document.** The letter should repeat the trigger language and carry the doctor's license number, the date of the exam and a signature.
6. **Give the letter to the bank.** Bring the original document, the certification and your ID. Our guide on what to do when a [bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) covers the usual objections.
7. **If nobody will sign, talk to an attorney about court.** A judge can decide whether the trigger is met or appoint a guardian.

## What happens if no doctor will sign?

Then the document stays dormant and the agent has no authority. Bills keep coming and accounts stay frozen to the agent. At that point the options narrow to a court process.

In Illinois, a guardianship petition asks the court to find that the person cannot manage their affairs and to appoint someone, and our [Illinois guardianship page](/learn/illinois/illinois-guardianship) describes it. New Jersey runs a similar process, explained in [guardianship in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/guardianship-in-new-jersey). Courts also hear a request to confirm that a power of attorney is in effect. Either way, expect public filings, a medical report, a notice to the person and legal fees. Compare that with a one-page letter and you see why the earlier steps are worth the trouble. Our article on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) lays out the tradeoffs.

## What does a worked example look like?

Back to Linda. Renata took four steps over about three weeks.

- Day 1: Linda, who still had good hours, signed a HIPAA release at Renata's kitchen table.
- Day 3: Renata sent the family doctor a letter quoting the trigger sentence and asking for an exam.
- Day 9: The doctor saw Linda, but would only write that she had "memory impairment." That did not match "unable to manage my affairs."
- Day 12: A geriatrician Renata found through the hospital's memory clinic evaluated Linda and signed a letter using the document's exact words.
- Day 20: The second physician the document required, a neurologist, saw Linda and signed.

On day 21 Renata presented the two letters to the bank. The mortgage was brought current. The whole process cost her a few weeks, not a court case. It worked because she aimed at the document's words, not at the diagnosis.

## How can you avoid this problem next time?

The most reliable fix is to take the doctor out of the picture. An immediate power of attorney works the moment it is signed and needs no certification. People worry that it hands over control, but the principal keeps full authority and a trusted agent will not use it until asked. If that still feels uncomfortable, [springing and immediate documents](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) each have a place.

If you keep a springing form, improve it:

- Name one physician or licensed clinician, not two.
- State that the agent may rely on a letter that tracks the trigger sentence.
- Let a named person (a spouse or the agent) make the finding if no doctor will.
- Attach a HIPAA release effective immediately so the doctor can talk to the agent.
- Review the form every few years. A document that predates a move or a change of doctor often fails on small facts.
