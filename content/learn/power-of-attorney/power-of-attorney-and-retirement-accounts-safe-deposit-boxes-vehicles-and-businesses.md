When an agent steps in, the first list is easy: checking account, mortgage, utilities. The harder items come a few weeks later. There is a 401(k) with a former employer, a car in the garage that needs to be sold, a locked safe deposit box nobody has opened in years, and a small business that still has customers.

This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), covers four of those harder assets and tells you what an agent can do, what the institution will want and where the limits are.

## The short version

- A power of attorney is the starting point. Banks, plan administrators and agencies each add their own requirements.
- Retirement accounts and vehicles are usually workable. Safe deposit boxes and businesses take more preparation.
- Anything that changes who inherits is a separate power.
- Authority stops at death for every asset on this page.

## Can a power of attorney access a 401(k) or IRA?

Usually, yes, and with more friction than a bank account. An agent with general financial powers can ask the plan or custodian for account information, manage investments, take distributions the principal is entitled to and handle required minimum distributions once those start. But a 401(k) is governed by a plan document and federal rules, and the administrator decides what proof it will accept.

What an agent should expect:

- **Plan forms.** Some administrators insist on their own agent authorization form, in addition to the power of attorney.
- **Plan-specific limits.** Loans, hardship withdrawals and rollovers follow plan rules. An agent cannot override them.
- **Taxes.** A withdrawal is income to the principal. An agent who takes a large distribution without thinking about the tax bracket can create a bill. Check with the principal's tax preparer first.
- **Beneficiaries.** Changing the named beneficiary on a retirement account is a power many documents leave out on purpose. Our piece on [whether an agent can change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary) explains why.

Consider Joan, 74, whose husband Ed has dementia. Ed has an IRA, and required minimum distributions are due this year. Joan, as his agent, calls the custodian, sends a certified copy of the document and asks for the custodian's agent form. Two weeks later she can set up the annual withdrawal. If the document had no tax or retirement language, the custodian might have asked for more. Our guide to the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lists the powers worth including.

## Can a power of attorney access a safe deposit box?

Sometimes. The bank is the gatekeeper. The box contract controls who may enter, and many banks will not simply accept a power of attorney. Typical approaches:

1. The principal signed a bank form naming the agent as an authorized deputy while still capable.
2. The power of attorney expressly mentions safe deposit boxes and the bank accepts it.
3. The bank allows access only in the presence of an officer, with an inventory.
4. If the bank refuses, a court order may be needed.

The practical lesson is to ask the bank before you need the box. Fifteen minutes with a branch manager now saves weeks later. If your bank refuses a valid document in general, see [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).

After death, the power of attorney is over. The executor will need letters from the court, and some states allow limited access to look for a will or burial instructions. Our guide on [what a will covers, including safe deposit boxes](/learn/property-and-assets/what-your-will-covers-timeshares-storage-units-safe-deposit-boxes) discusses the post-death side.

## Can a power of attorney sign a vehicle title?

In most states, yes. The agent signs the title as the principal's attorney-in-fact, which means writing something like "Maria Lopez by Ken Lopez, agent." Many motor vehicle agencies have their own power of attorney form for vehicle transactions, and some accept a general document only if it is notarized and specific. Some require the odometer statement to be signed by the agent too.

What agents get wrong:

- Signing the principal's name alone, with no indication of agency. That can look like forgery.
- Selling to themselves or a relative at a low price. This is self-dealing unless the document and the principal's wishes plainly allow it.
- Not telling the insurer. The coverage must follow the sale.

If a car is going to be sold to pay for care, the agent should record the sale price, keep the bill of sale and deposit proceeds into the principal's account. For real estate, which is stricter, read our [real estate and power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate).

## Can a power of attorney run a business?

An agent can act for the owner. That is not the same as being the manager. The line depends on how the business is structured.

| Business type | What the agent can usually do | What controls the rest |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Run the day-to-day affairs, sign contracts, pay bills | The power of attorney itself |
| LLC | Vote the owner's interest, sign as member | The operating agreement |
| Corporation | Vote shares, sign stock transfers | Bylaws and board decisions |
| Partnership | Act for the owner within the partnership agreement | The partnership agreement |

Sole proprietors are the easiest case. A plumber who suffers a stroke can have an agent sign checks and keep invoices flowing. Co-owned businesses are harder, because partners or the company's documents may limit transfers or require consent for a new manager. A business owner should have a business-specific power in the document, a successor plan and, ideally, a continuity plan. Our pages on [business owners and planning](/learn/business-owners) and [what happens to a business when its owner cannot act](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) go into that side.

## What about the other things people ask about?

**Voting.** An agent cannot vote or sign a ballot for the principal. That is a personal right. An agent can, however, vote the principal's shares in a company if the document and company rules permit.

**Medical decisions.** A financial document does not reach them. A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) covers those.

**Wills and trusts.** An agent cannot sign a will. Whether an agent can create or change a trust depends on explicit language, as discussed in [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do).

**After death.** All authority ends. The executor takes over, as we explain in [power of attorney vs. executor](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-executor).

## Preparing the agent: a short checklist

1. List every account, with institution and contact name.
2. Check each one for its own agent form and complete it while the principal can sign.
3. Add the safe deposit box to a bank form, or name it in the document.
4. Add business and retirement powers if they apply.
5. Store the original and give copies to the agent and successor.
6. Review the list yearly.

If you are not sure what belongs in your plan, the [plan finder](/plan-finder) can point you to the right next step.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how vehicle titles are signed, whether a motor vehicle agency form is required, and how banks may treat safe deposit boxes. Business rules come from the state where the entity was formed. Retirement plans are mostly federal, but state law controls the validity of the power of attorney itself.
