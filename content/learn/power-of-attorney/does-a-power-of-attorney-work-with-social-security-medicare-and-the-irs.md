Many families discover this the hard way. They hold a notarized durable power of attorney, walk into the Social Security office or phone the IRS, and are told that the paper is not enough. They are not being difficult. Federal agencies run on federal rules, and a state document is only one input.

This page covers what the main agencies accept, which extra forms to collect, and how to order the work so it is not left until it is hardest. It sits inside our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), which covers the document itself.

## The short version

- A durable power of attorney is the foundation. It is not a substitute for agency-specific forms.
- Social Security uses a representative payee, not an agent.
- The IRS has separate forms for representation (2848), information access (8821) and fiduciary notice (56).
- Medicare has its own forms for appointing a representative and authorizing disclosure.
- Medicaid is run by the states, so the local agency decides what it will accept.

| Agency | What a power of attorney does | What else you usually need |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security | Supports an application, does not control benefits | Representative payee application if the person cannot manage money |
| IRS | Lets the agent sign a return in some circumstances | Form 2848 to be represented; Form 56 to report a fiduciary |
| Medicare | Little on its own | Appointment of representative form; written consent to release information |
| Medicaid | Helps the agent file | State authorized-representative form, plus financial records |
| Veterans benefits | Little on its own | Accredited representative or the VA fiduciary process |

## Why do agencies not simply honor a power of attorney?

A power of attorney is a creature of state law. It says what your agent may do with your property and accounts under the law of your state. A federal agency distributing federal benefits is under no duty to apply that state document to its own programs, and it has a legitimate worry about fraud against vulnerable people. So each program built its own process.

That is why a power of attorney that sails through a bank may stall at an agency. It also explains why a good document includes language authorizing the agent to deal with Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, the IRS and veterans programs, even though that language will not end the paperwork. Our article on the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lists the categories to include.

## How does Social Security treat a power of attorney?

Social Security does not recognize a power of attorney as authority to receive or manage someone's benefits. If a person cannot manage their own benefits, the agency can appoint a representative payee, usually a family member, who receives the payments and must spend them for the person's needs. The payee is chosen by the agency, not by the principal's document, and must account for how the money was used.

Take Gloria, 79, who has memory loss. Her son Raymond is her agent under a durable power of attorney, and he assumes he can redirect her Social Security deposit. The agency tells him it needs a payee application. He applies, brings the power of attorney and a doctor's statement about Gloria's condition, and is approved after a review. The document helped his case. It was not the authority.

What the agent can do without the payee process is handle Gloria's other money. Once benefits land in an account she owns, the agent can use that account under the power of attorney. For help with benefits after a death, see [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death).

## What does the IRS require?

The IRS draws a line between talking about a taxpayer's account and representing them. The forms differ:

1. **Form 2848, Power of Attorney and Declaration of Representative.** Authorizes someone to represent the taxpayer for specific tax matters and years. The person named generally has to be an eligible representative, such as an attorney, certified public accountant or enrolled agent, with narrow exceptions for some family members.
2. **Form 8821, Tax Information Authorization.** Lets a named person receive tax information. It does not allow the person to act for the taxpayer.
3. **Form 56, Notice Concerning Fiduciary Relationship.** Tells the IRS that someone is acting as a fiduciary, for example an agent or guardian handling a person's affairs, so notices are directed appropriately.

For signing a return, the instructions generally allow an agent to sign for a taxpayer who cannot because of disease or injury or because of continuous absence, with a copy of the power of attorney attached. Instructions change, so check the current ones at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov) or ask the preparer before filing. A typical practical arrangement is a family agent who gathers records and signs the return, while a CPA or enrolled agent holds the Form 2848 for audits and notices.

## How does Medicare handle it?

Medicare does not treat a state power of attorney as its own authority. For claims and appeals, a person can name a representative with Medicare's appointment form. For talking about someone's file, Medicare uses a signed consent to release information to a named person. Private plans, such as Medicare Advantage and drug plans, have their own forms.

The cleanest approach is simple. While the person can still speak, call 1-800-MEDICARE together and have them give verbal or written permission for you to discuss their account. If they cannot, bring the power of attorney and expect to be directed to the right form. A healthcare power of attorney and a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) help with the medical side, where privacy rules often decide who gets information.

## What about Medicaid and long-term care?

Medicaid is run by the states within federal rules, so the process varies. Most state agencies accept an authorized representative or an agent under a power of attorney to complete the application. They then ask for financial records covering the look-back period, which is 60 months in most states. Every transfer the agent made in that period will be reviewed, including gifts. An agent who handles money for a parent entering a nursing home should keep clean records and read [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) before moving assets.

If a nursing home or a hospital asks the agent to sign admission papers, the agent should read them carefully. Signing as the agent is different from signing as a guarantor, and a facility cannot require an agent to promise to pay personally from their own funds. Ask for the signature block to show the agent's capacity.

## How to get ready before there is a crisis

The easiest way to avoid a stall is to do the agency work while the principal can still sign.

1. Make sure the power of attorney lists dealings with federal and state agencies, taxes, Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid and veterans benefits.
2. Sign Form 2848 for a CPA or enrolled agent who already prepares the returns.
3. Add your agent or a family member to Medicare's consent to disclose information.
4. Keep copies of the power of attorney, the agent's contact information and the principal's key numbers in a single folder.
5. Ask the bank how it wants deposits redirected if benefits need to go into a different account.
6. Refresh the document every few years and after any move, since staff can reject an old one.

## How state law changes this

State law decides the document's validity and the scope of the agent's powers. Federal law and each agency's rules decide whether the agency will take instructions from the agent. Medicaid sits in the middle, because each state sets its own application process. If you are managing a parent's affairs from another state, ask the local agency which forms it wants before you travel. For how banks respond in practice, see [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).
