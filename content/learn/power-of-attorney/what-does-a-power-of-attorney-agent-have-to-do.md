Someone asked you to be their agent, or you found your name in a document your mother signed years ago and now it is time to use it. Either way the same question comes up: what exactly am I supposed to do, and what can go wrong? The honest answer is that the role is mostly quiet bookkeeping and good judgment, with a few firm rules that you cannot bend even when everyone in the family is friendly.

This guide walks through the legal duties of an agent under a [power of attorney](/glossary/power-of-attorney), what a sensible first week looks like, how to keep records a court or an auditor would accept, and the mistakes that most often lead to complaints. It is general education, and your state's version of the law controls.

## What is an agent's basic job?

An [agent](/glossary/agent) steps into the shoes of the [principal](/glossary/principal) only for the tasks the document lists, and only for the principal's benefit. You pay their bills, deal with their bank and insurer, manage their accounts and property, and file what needs filing. You do not become the owner of any of it.

The law treats this as a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary) relationship, which is the same standard that applies to trustees and executors. In plain terms, you owe the principal loyalty and care. If your interests and theirs ever pull in different directions, theirs win.

## What are the specific duties?

Most states, including those that have adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, describe the same short list. The wording differs, but the substance is consistent.

| Duty | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|
| Act in good faith | Make decisions the principal would reasonably want, using what you know of their wishes |
| Stay within your authority | Read the powers granted. If gifts, real estate or trust changes are not listed, do not do them |
| Act loyally | No self-dealing. Do not sell the principal's car to yourself at a friendly price |
| Keep funds separate | Principal's money stays in principal's accounts, never mixed with yours |
| Keep records | Receipts, statements, a running ledger of what you paid and why |
| Act with care | The skill and caution a reasonable person would use for someone else's money |
| Preserve the plan | Do not undo the principal's will or estate plan unless the document clearly lets you |
| Cooperate with the healthcare agent | Pay for the care the medical decision-maker arranges |

Two of these catch people off guard. The first is the duty to preserve the estate plan. Say Walter's will leaves the house equally to his three children, and his agent, a daughter, sells the house to herself at a discount "to simplify things." That is a conflict of interest even if the price looks fair. The second is record keeping. You are not required to be perfect, but you are required to be able to show what happened to the money.

## What should an agent do in the first week?

Do the quiet, protective things first, and hold off on anything dramatic.

1. **Read the document twice.** Find the date, the powers granted, any limits, and whether it takes effect now or only on incapacity. If it is [springing](/glossary/springing-power-of-attorney), find out what proof is required.
2. **Get several certified copies.** Banks and brokerages often want their own, and some want a recent date or their own form. See our guide on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).
3. **Make an inventory.** Accounts, property, insurance policies, debts, regular bills, pensions, benefits, and where the paper files live.
4. **Open a ledger.** A spreadsheet or a notebook is fine. Date, amount, payee, reason.
5. **Redirect mail if needed.** Statements arriving at one place make the rest of the job easier.
6. **Keep your own money away from it.** Pay yourself back only through documented reimbursements for real expenses.

### How should I sign documents?

Sign as the agent, in the way the document and state law expect, for example "Maria Lopez, as agent for Rosa Lopez." Signing in your own name on a contract can make you personally responsible for it. Use the same format every time so the institutions on the other end recognize it.

## What records do I actually have to keep?

Keep anything that shows where the principal's money came from and where it went, for as long as your state's accounting rules or tax rules would reasonably require. A good working rule is to keep records for the whole period you serve, and then for several years afterward.

In practice, that means saving the following.

- Monthly bank and brokerage statements.
- Receipts for anything over a small amount.
- Invoices and proof of payment for care, repairs and taxes.
- Copies of every letter you send to a bank, insurer or agency.
- A short note whenever you make a judgment call, such as why you sold a stock.


Think of it as writing for a skeptical sibling who will read it in two years. If a document would answer their question, you have kept enough.

## Where do agents get into trouble?

Almost always in the same four places.

**Gifts.** The agent gives money to themselves or their children. Gifts are only allowed if the document says so, and often only up to a limit such as the yearly [gift tax exclusion](/glossary/annual-exclusion), which is $19,000 per recipient in 2026. Our article on [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) goes through this in detail.

**Mixed money.** An agent who pays their own electric bill from the principal's account "to be fixed later" has crossed a line, even if they always intend to repay.

**Caregiver pay.** A family member who is both agent and caregiver is tempting to pay generously. Without a written care agreement made in advance, those payments look like self-dealing. See [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

**Silence.** Nobody wrote anything down, a sibling asks questions, and the agent gets defensive. Silence turns a suspicion into a petition to the court. The consequences are covered in [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

## What happens if I fail in my duties?

A court can order an agent to repay losses, remove the agent, require a formal accounting, and in serious cases refer the matter for criminal charges. Courts also tend to look past labels. A "loan" with no paperwork, or a "gift" the principal could not understand, will often be treated as an improper transfer.

You are generally not personally liable for honest mistakes made with reasonable care. That is why the records matter: they are what shows reasonable care.

## When do my duties end?

Your authority ends when the principal dies, when they revoke the document while still competent, when a court terminates it, or when you resign in the way your state requires. At death, the [executor](/glossary/executor) named in the will takes over, and you should hand over your records. The timing rules are explained in [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).

If the principal never signed a document, the family faces a different problem, covered in our scenario on [what happens without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney).
