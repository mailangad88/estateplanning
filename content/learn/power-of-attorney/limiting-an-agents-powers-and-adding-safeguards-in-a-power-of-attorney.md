Most people who ask this question are not distrustful by nature. They have one child who is good with money and one who is not, or they have a house they never want sold, or they simply sleep better knowing someone is looking over the agent's shoulder. The law allows all of that. A power of attorney is a custom document, and you set the terms.

This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), covers the five ways people shape an agent's authority: limiting the powers, requiring a co-signer, naming a monitor, setting a start date and using a single-purpose document. For each one we explain what it does and where it tends to go wrong.

## The short version

- Narrow the powers to what your agent will actually need.
- Add a second signature for transactions above a dollar amount you choose.
- Name a monitor who receives statements or an annual accounting.
- Delay the start only if you have a clear way to prove the trigger.
- Tell the people involved. A safeguard nobody knows about protects no one.

## Can I limit the powers I give my agent?

Yes, and it is the safeguard with the least downside. Statutory forms in many states list the usual powers, such as banking, real estate, taxes, benefits and insurance, and let you initial the ones you grant. You can also write a short custom document that grants only what you name.

Think about what the agent will really do. Marguerite, 71, wants her son Daniel to pay bills and talk to her bank if she has a stroke. She does not want him selling the family cabin, which she has promised to her daughter. Her attorney grants banking, bill paying, tax and benefits powers, and writes that the agent may not sell, mortgage or transfer the cabin. Daniel can do his job and the cabin stays put.

Two kinds of limits matter most:

1. **Subject limits.** Which accounts, which property or which category of power the agent may touch.
2. **Act limits.** Specific things the agent may not do, such as make gifts, change beneficiaries or create trusts. These are the "hot powers" discussed in [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do), and in most states they need to be spelled out before an agent can use them at all.

The trade-off is flexibility. If you list only three powers and your agent needs a fourth, the document does not stretch. Families then end up in court or signing a new document at a hospital bed. A reasonable middle path is broad financial powers with a short list of bright-line exclusions.

## Can I require a second signature on large transactions?

Yes. You can say that any single transaction over, for example, a stated dollar amount needs the written consent of a named second person. That second person is not a co-agent. They do not manage anything. They only sign off.

Be realistic about two things. First, pick the threshold with care. Set too low, it turns every home repair into a committee. Set too high, it protects nothing. Many families choose a figure around a few months of ordinary spending, then adjust for the size of the estate. Second, banks have their own process. A teller may not know how to apply a second-signature clause, so ask your attorney to include language the bank's legal department can follow, and ask the bank in advance how it handles such documents. Our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) covers the usual friction.

If you want true joint control rather than a check, you are describing co-agents, which are covered in [naming more than one agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-name-more-than-one-power-of-attorney-agent).

## Can I name someone to monitor my agent?

You can, and this is the safeguard that most reliably catches problems early. A monitor, sometimes called a third-party reviewer, is a person named in the document who receives copies of statements, an annual accounting or both. They usually have standing to ask a court to review the agent's conduct.

Who makes a good monitor? Someone who is not the agent, not in an obvious conflict with the agent, and willing to read a bank statement every few months. A sibling who was not chosen as agent is the classic pick, and it often calms resentment. A CPA or a long-time family friend also works.

Write down three things:

1. What the monitor receives, and how often.
2. How the agent must deliver it, for example within 30 days of a written request.
3. What the monitor may do if something looks wrong, such as ask for records or contact an attorney.

Some states set a default right for certain family members to request an accounting. Others leave it entirely to the document. If you are unsure, putting it in writing removes the doubt. The signs a monitor should look for are listed in our guide to [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

## Can I make a power of attorney effective only after a certain date?

Yes, in two ways. One is a fixed date, such as "effective January 1." The other is a trigger event. The second is called a springing power, and we compare the two in [springing vs. immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).

A fixed date is clean. Nobody has to prove anything. But it suits a planned absence better than a medical decline, since you cannot know in advance when you will need help.

A trigger such as "when two physicians certify that I cannot manage my affairs" is more protective and more fragile. It depends on a doctor being willing to write the letter, and on the bank accepting it. A doctor may hesitate, particularly when the patient is declining slowly and has good days. That is the situation where families stall. If you go this route, name more than one way to satisfy the trigger and say who may make the call.

Many attorneys now suggest a third option: sign a document that takes effect immediately, but keep the original in a safe place and tell your agent not to use it until needed. That keeps the safeguard of your own say-so without the delays of proof. It does depend on trust, so it is not for everyone.

## What is a special power of attorney?

A special, or limited, power of attorney covers one task. Examples are signing closing documents on a house sale while you are overseas, handling a single tax matter, or dealing with one financial institution. It ends when the task is done or on the date you set.

It is the narrowest safeguard there is. The cost is that it does nothing for you if you later become incapacitated, unless it is also written as durable. For the full picture of general, limited and durable documents, see [the three types of power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-are-the-three-types-of-power-of-attorney).

## How the safeguards compare

| Safeguard | What it protects | Main cost |
|---|---|---|
| Limited list of powers | Property and decisions you want left alone | Document may not cover the unexpected |
| Second signature | Large transfers and sales | Slower; banks may not know how to apply it |
| Named monitor | Early detection of misuse | Someone must read the statements |
| Delayed start date | Your independence while you are well | Proof of the trigger can stall everything |
| Special power of attorney | One transaction | Ends too soon for ongoing needs |

## Making the safeguards work

A short checklist helps:

1. Tell your agent, monitor and any co-signer what you have asked of them, in person, before you sign.
2. Keep the original where your agent can reach it, and give your monitor a copy.
3. Ask your attorney to prepare a one-page certification that banks can use to confirm the document is in force.
4. Review the document every few years, and after any divorce, death or move. Our [plan review reminder tool](/plan-finder) is a good prompt.
5. Match your other documents. If a trust holds your home, see [whether you still need a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/do-i-need-a-power-of-attorney-if-i-have-a-living-trust).

Safeguards also work best when the person you chose is trustworthy to start with. If you find yourself adding six layers of protection, ask whether a different agent would be the simpler fix. Our guide to [choosing your agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) walks through that decision.

## How state law changes this

States differ on which powers must be spelled out, whether a monitor has a statutory right to records, and whether a particular form must be used. Some, such as [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois), publish a statutory short form that is easy to modify with the options above. A document that works well in one state may not read correctly in another.
