Two brothers and a sister are standing in the kitchen. Their mother signed a power of attorney last year and named the sister. Now one brother says the sister is hiding something. The sister says he never visits. Everyone has an opinion and no one has seen a bank statement.

This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), lays out what families in that position can actually do: what counts as grounds, the sensible order of steps, and what a court will and will not do.

## The short version

- Being unhappy with the choice of agent is not a legal claim.
- Records settle more disputes than arguments do. Ask for them first.
- A court can review, restrict or remove an agent who has breached their duties.
- A court can also set aside a document that was signed under pressure or without capacity.
- If the parent can still decide, a new document signed by the parent solves the problem fastest.

## What are valid reasons to challenge or remove an agent?

Courts look for specific problems, not family friction. The common grounds fall into two groups.

**Problems with the agent:**

1. Taking money or property for themselves, or "borrowing" without authority.
2. Failing to pay bills or care for the principal's property, so that utilities lapse or taxes go unpaid.
3. Mixing the principal's money with their own.
4. Refusing to give information to someone entitled to it.
5. Acting outside the document, for example making gifts the document does not allow.
6. Becoming unable to serve, because of illness, distance or a conflict of interest.

**Problems with the document:**

1. The principal lacked capacity when signing.
2. The principal was pressured or tricked, which the law calls [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence).
3. The signing formalities were skipped, such as the notary.
4. The document is a forgery.

The agent owes a legal [fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty) to the principal, which means acting in the principal's interest. Our article on [what an agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do) describes those duties in detail, and [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) lists the warning signs.

## What if siblings just disagree with the agent?

Then the law usually sides with the parent's choice. Say Helen, 82, names her daughter Priya, who lives twenty minutes away, and not her son Ravi, who lives in another country. Ravi thinks Priya is too frugal with Helen's money. That is a disagreement about judgment, not a breach. A court will not substitute Ravi's views for Priya's decisions when Priya acts honestly and within the document.

What can Ravi do? He can ask Priya for periodic updates, offer to review statements with her, or ask Helen to add a monitor to the document while Helen can still sign. Many family fights end there. If a family is stuck, a neutral mediator can sit down with everyone. Our guide to [siblings who fight over a parent's estate](/learn/what-if/siblings-fight-over-a-parents-estate) covers mediation and how to talk about money without trading accusations.

## What should you do first if you suspect a problem?

Work from the least costly step to the most.

1. **Write down what you have noticed.** Dates, amounts, missing bills, changes in the parent's living conditions.
2. **Ask the agent for records, politely and in writing.** A request for statements and a list of payments made is reasonable. A cooperative agent will usually comply.
3. **Talk to the parent if the parent can understand.** If Helen has capacity, she can revoke, change agents, or tell the bank to send duplicate statements to a child. That is better than any lawsuit.
4. **Talk to the bank.** Banks can sometimes flag unusual activity and may share concerns with their fraud teams.
5. **Report suspected abuse.** Adult protective services will investigate neglect and financial exploitation.
6. **Consult an attorney about a court petition** if records are refused or the numbers do not add up.

Keep copies of everything. Do not take money back yourself or move your parent's accounts without authority, because that can create the problem you are trying to prevent.

## What can a court do?

The details differ by state, but courts commonly have these options:

| Remedy | What it does | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Order an accounting | Agent must list and explain transactions | Records refused or unclear |
| Restrict the agent | Limits powers or requires court approval for sales | Concern but no proof of theft |
| Remove the agent | Ends authority; successor or court appointee steps in | Misconduct, neglect, inability |
| Order repayment | Agent must return money taken or lost | Proven self-dealing |
| Void the document | Treated as never valid | No capacity, fraud or undue influence |
| Appoint a guardian or conservator | Court-supervised substitute | No workable agent left |

Removal does not erase the problem, because someone must take over. If the document names a successor agent, that person steps in. If it does not, the court may appoint a guardian or conservator, which is slower and more public. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) and [guardian vs. conservator](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate).

## Who can bring the case?

This is called standing. In most states, the principal, the principal's spouse, adult children, other close relatives, and sometimes agencies or a person with a financial interest may ask the court to review an agent. Some states give a longer list; some are narrower. A friend or neighbor may have to go through adult protective services instead.

Timing matters too. Evidence disappears, and the principal's memory may fade or fail. A parent who can describe what happened is the best witness there is.

## Can the principal just fix it?

Yes, as long as the principal has capacity. A principal can revoke, name a new agent and notify the bank in writing. Banks are told in writing so that old copies of the document stop working. If your parent is willing, an attorney can prepare the new document and a revocation the same day. Our guide to [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) lists how revocation works and why old copies keep circulating.

If the parent has dementia, the question becomes whether they still have capacity to sign. Capacity can come and go, and a doctor's note taken near the time of signing helps. See [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) for the harder case.

## How to avoid the fight in the first place

Most of these battles are planned in advance by silence. A few steps help:

- Parents should explain their choice and their reason to all the children, ideally at one table.
- The document can name a monitor or require an annual summary to every child.
- Name a successor so no one wonders what happens next.
- Keep the agent's records tidy from day one. A shared spreadsheet is enough.

Our article on [choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers how to make the choice in a way that holds the family together.

## How state law changes this

Removal procedures, who has standing, how long you have to bring a claim and whether the principal's relatives can demand an accounting all vary. Some states give courts broad authority to review powers of attorney. Others limit petitions to specific people. A local attorney can tell you what the court in your county expects.
