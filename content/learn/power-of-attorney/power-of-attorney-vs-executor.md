People mix these two roles up all the time, and the confusion has real costs. A daughter holds her father's power of attorney for six years, pays every bill, knows every account, and the day he dies she walks into the bank and finds that none of it works anymore. She is surprised. She should not be, because the law set it up that way.

This article lays the two jobs side by side so you can decide who should hold each and what happens in the gap between them.

## What is the main difference?

The difference is timing and source of authority. A power of attorney is a document you sign while alive. It lets your agent manage your finances if you cannot or prefer not to. An executor is named in your will and is appointed by a court after you die to settle your estate.

| | Power of attorney agent | Executor |
|---|---|---|
| When it starts | Immediately or when you become incapacitated | At your death, once the court appoints |
| When it ends | At your death, or earlier if revoked | When the estate is closed |
| Where named | Power of attorney document | Will |
| Court involvement | Usually none unless there is a dispute | Almost always, through probate |
| Duty owed to | You, the principal | Beneficiaries and creditors |
| Typical tasks | Pay bills, manage accounts, deal with benefits | Inventory assets, pay debts and taxes, distribute property |
| Main paper proof | Signed power of attorney | Letters testamentary from the court |

## Why does the agent's power stop at death?

Because the agent acts for you, and after death there is no "you" to act for. Property then belongs to the estate, and the will or state law decides who gets it. Authority to deal with the estate comes from the court. This rule is the reason banks stop honoring a power of attorney once they learn of a death, and why an agent who keeps using the document afterward can face serious trouble. Our article on [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) walks through the details.

## What happens in the gap after death?

There is a stretch, sometimes a few days and sometimes months, when nobody has legal authority over the estate. The agent's power is gone. The executor has not yet been appointed.

During that gap:

1. Someone usually has to arrange the funeral and often pays for it from their own pocket.
2. The original will must be filed with the probate court. The [executor's first steps](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) begin there.
3. The executor applies for [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary), the court document banks and title offices ask for.
4. Bills keep arriving. Insurance, utilities and a mortgage cannot simply stop. The executor can address them once appointed.

An executor who has the original will and a death certificate can often get letters in a few weeks, though it varies widely by county. See [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) for realistic ranges.

## Who should hold each role?

Many families choose the same trusted person for both jobs, particularly when there is one obvious child. That makes the handover smooth because the person already knows where the records are.

Others split them. Consider Ruth, a widow with two children. Her son Daniel is good with money and lives nearby, so he is her agent. Her daughter Carla is organized, patient with paperwork and a former teacher, so she is executor. Ruth explains the arrangement to both children. Each knows what the other does, and nobody feels passed over.

Whichever way you decide, ask three questions about each candidate:

- Will they keep records and communicate?
- Can they handle conflict with relatives?
- Do they live close enough, or can they travel when needed?

More guidance is in [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) and [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).

## What can an agent still do right after the principal dies?

Very little, and the line is worth knowing. An agent may keep a few things from falling apart in the first hours or days, such as notifying the family, locating the will, and securing a house. What an agent may not do is use the power of attorney to move money, sell property or close accounts. A bank that discovers a death will usually freeze the principal's accounts, and an agent who withdraws funds afterward may have to return them and may face claims of misconduct.

Take Gloria, who held her mother's power of attorney. Her mother died on a Friday. On Monday, Gloria went to the bank to pay the nursing home's final invoice using the old document. The teller declined. Gloria paid the bill from her own funds, kept the receipt, and was reimbursed by the estate months later once her sister, the executor, was appointed. That is exactly how it should go. Keep the receipt and wait for the court.

## What records does the agent hand to the executor?

The agent should turn over a simple package: a list of accounts and balances at the date of death, the ledger of transactions during the agency, copies of statements and receipts, and a list of bills still unpaid. The [executor](/glossary/executor) uses this to build the inventory the court requires, a task described in [probate inventory and estate accounting](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting).

If the same person served in both roles, they are handing records to themselves. That still deserves a clean break. Close the ledger at the date of death, start a new one for the estate, and be ready to show beneficiaries what happened under each role.

## What if I have a living trust?

A [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) adds a third role: the trustee. While you live, you are usually the trustee. If you become incapacitated, a successor trustee takes over trust property. At death, that successor administers the trust, often without probate. The trustee does not replace the agent, because the trustee only controls assets that were actually transferred into the trust. The agent still handles everything outside it, such as a retirement account or a car. And a pour-over will still needs an executor. Our guide to [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) shows how the pieces fit.

## What if I have no power of attorney or no will?

Without a power of attorney, your family may need a court to appoint a guardian or conservator if you become incapacitated, which is slower, public and more expensive. The scenario is described in [what happens without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney). Without a will, the court appoints an administrator under state law, usually a close relative, and the same job is described in [what happens if there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor).

## How do I make sure both roles are covered?

A short checklist:

1. Sign a durable power of attorney naming an agent and at least one successor.
2. Sign a will naming an executor and at least one alternate.
3. Sign a healthcare power of attorney for medical decisions.
4. Tell each person where the documents are, and what you expect.
5. Review everything after a marriage, divorce, death or move.
