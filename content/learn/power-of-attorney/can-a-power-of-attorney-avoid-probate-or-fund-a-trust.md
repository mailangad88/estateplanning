Mario, 81, set up a living trust with his daughter Gina six years ago, then had a stroke before he moved his house into it. Gina holds his power of attorney. She asks the question we hear most from adult children: can she use that document to move the house into the trust, so the family can skip probate?

The honest answer has two halves, and it sits within the wider set of tools in our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney). A power of attorney by itself cannot avoid probate. But an agent with the right written powers can sometimes finish a plan that a trust owner started. Which half applies depends on exactly what the two documents say.

## Can a power of attorney avoid probate?

No, and the reason is timing. A power of attorney works only while the person who signed it is alive. At death, the agent's authority stops, and the assets go wherever the will, trust, beneficiary forms or state law direct. Our guide to [what a power of attorney agent cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) covers the death rule in more detail.

Probate is the court process that handles assets titled in the dead person's name alone. A power of attorney leaves the title exactly as it was. If your mother owns a house and a $90,000 account in her own name when she dies, both go through probate unless something else, such as a trust, a beneficiary form or joint ownership, already moved them.

What the agent can do is change how things are titled while the person is alive. That is a different act, with real limits.

## When can an agent move assets into a trust?

An agent can fund a trust when three conditions line up.

1. **The trust already exists.** The person created and signed it while having capacity. An agent generally cannot invent a new trust that the person never planned.
2. **The power of attorney reaches it.** The document must give the agent authority over trusts, real estate transactions or "all matters," in words a title company will accept. In Illinois, the statute says an agent has no power to make gifts, to appoint property to others or to change a beneficiary, and cannot revoke or amend a trust, unless the form gives specific authority (755 ILCS 45/3-4). That is the rule that surprises families.
3. **The transfer matches the plan.** A deed into the trust must benefit the same people the person already chose. Moving property into a revocable trust the person controls leaves ownership essentially unchanged, which is why it is the easiest trust-related act to defend.

New Jersey's Power of Attorney Act treats the agent as a fiduciary who acts only for the principal's benefit (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.13), which is the standard a court would apply to any funding transfer. Our page on the [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) describes the agent's duties.

## What can an agent do about a trust, step by step?

| Action | Usually allowed? | What it takes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Move an account or house into the person's existing revocable trust | Often yes | Trust exists; document gives real estate, banking or trust powers |
| Create a new trust | Rarely | Express written authority, consistent with the person's known plan |
| Change who receives the trust property | No, in most cases | Express power in the power of attorney or the trust itself |
| Revoke or amend the trust | Not in Illinois unless the form says so | Specific wording in the form or the trust |
| Pay bills out of the trust | Not as agent | Only the trustee can; the agent has no authority over trust property |
| Name the agent as owner | Dangerous | Court scrutiny; may be undone |

The last two rows catch people off guard. If a trust is already funded, the agent has no power over what is inside it. That job belongs to the trustee, and the trustee may be a different person. Our guide on whether you [need a power of attorney if you have a living trust](/learn/power-of-attorney/do-i-need-a-power-of-attorney-if-i-have-a-living-trust) explains how the two fit together.

## What does a worked example look like?

Back to Mario. His trust document names Gina as successor trustee. His power of attorney, signed four years ago, names Gina as agent and lists "real estate transactions" and "banking" among her powers. It says nothing specific about trusts.

Gina gets a deed prepared that conveys the house from Mario to "Mario, trustee of the Mario Rossi Revocable Trust." The title company asks two questions. Does the power of attorney authorize transfers into a trust? Does the trust give Mario the right to add property?

Gina's attorney reads both documents. The trust has a funding clause, and the power of attorney's real estate language is broad. In New Jersey or Illinois a title company may still want an additional certification, but the deed is recorded. The house, assessed at $410,000 in this example, is now in the trust, so after Mario's death it avoids probate and passes under the trust. The attorney also checks that the transfer leaves the homeowner's insurance and property tax exemptions intact.

Change one fact: Mario has no trust at all. Then Gina cannot create one, because the document gives no trust powers. The house stays in his name, and probate is likely. Some families discover this only when a death forces the issue.

## What should the agent check before touching a deed?

Run through this list first. Skipping it is how a well-meant transfer turns into a dispute.

- Read the trust's funding clause and confirm the trust permits additions.
- Find the power of attorney's exact words on real estate, banking and trusts, and highlight them for the title company.
- Confirm the person's own wishes, in writing if possible, such as a letter or an old estate plan.
- Ask the lender whether moving the house into a trust triggers any due-on-sale concern. Most lenders accept a revocable trust, but confirm.
- Keep records of every transfer, with dates and reasons, because the agent may be asked to account later.
- Tell the other family members. Surprises, not the transfers themselves, are what start fights.

## What should you not do?

Our attorneys see the same mistakes every year:

- **Adding the agent to the deed.** It looks like probate avoidance and feels like protection. It can be a gift, can trigger a Medicaid penalty, and can expose the property to the agent's creditors and divorce.
- **Changing beneficiaries.** The agent cannot move a life insurance or retirement beneficiary without express authority, and trying invites a challenge. See [whether a power of attorney can change a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-a-power-of-attorney-or-guardian-change-a-beneficiary).
- **Using trust assets as if they were the agent's.** The trustee manages trust property under a separate document with separate duties.
- **Waiting.** Most of these problems vanish if the person funds their own trust. The fix takes a few signatures and a recorded deed.

## How state law changes this

Illinois spells out in the statute that gifts, beneficiary changes and trust changes need specific authority, and the statutory form includes places to grant those powers. New Jersey lets the document control and holds the agent to fiduciary duties, so wording and purpose both matter. Check the living trust rules for each state in our guides to [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) and [living trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey). Both states also expect real estate transfers to be recorded with the county.
