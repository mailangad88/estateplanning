A fall, a stroke or a diagnosis of dementia can arrive without warning, and the bills do not pause while you recover. Mortgage payments, Medicare premiums, property taxes and credit cards all need someone with legal authority to act. A durable financial power of attorney is the document that gives that authority to a person you picked in advance, instead of leaving the question to a judge.

This guide explains what the document covers, how "durable" changes it, which powers deserve extra attention, and what it cannot do. If you want the full picture of how powers of attorney fit together, start with our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## What is a durable financial power of attorney?

A durable financial power of attorney is a signed legal document in which you, the [principal](/glossary/principal), authorize another person, your [agent](/glossary/agent), to manage your finances and property. Your state's law supplies the default list of what an agent can and cannot do, and the document you sign adds to or limits that list.

Three terms come up constantly:

- **Principal**: you, the person granting the authority.
- **Agent**: the person who acts for you. Some states and older forms say "attorney-in-fact." The agent does not need to be a lawyer.
- **Successor agent**: the backup who steps in if your first choice cannot or will not serve.

The document is called a "power of attorney" because it gives power to act, not because an attorney is involved. See our glossary entries on [power of attorney](/glossary/power-of-attorney) and the [durable power of attorney](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney).

## What does "durable" actually mean?

"Durable" means the document survives your [incapacity](/glossary/incapacity). Under traditional common law, an agent's authority ended the moment the principal lost the mental ability to supervise. That rule defeated the point of the document for anyone worried about aging. Every state now allows a durable version, usually created by including language such as "this power of attorney shall not be affected by my subsequent disability or incapacity."

Many states have adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which flips the default so that a power of attorney is durable unless it says it is not. Other states still require the durability language. Because forms travel between states and old forms linger in drawers, a properly drafted document should state durability plainly regardless.

Durability does not mean the document is unconditional. It can still be revoked while you have capacity, and it still ends at death. We cover every ending point in [when does a power of attorney end](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).

## What can an agent do with it?

An agent can do whatever the document grants and state law permits, and most well-drafted forms cover the everyday financial tasks. A typical grant includes:

- Depositing, withdrawing and transferring funds in your bank and credit union accounts
- Paying bills, mortgage, rent, taxes and insurance premiums
- Managing brokerage accounts, buying and selling securities
- Handling retirement accounts, including required minimum distributions
- Dealing with the IRS and state tax agencies, usually with a separate IRS Form 2848 for representation
- Applying for and managing government benefits such as Social Security, Medicare and VA benefits
- Buying, selling, leasing or mortgaging real estate
- Running or winding down a sole proprietorship
- Hiring professionals such as accountants and caregivers
- Accessing digital accounts, to the extent allowed by service terms and state law

Here is a realistic example. Tom, 71, has a stroke and cannot sign checks. His daughter Alicia is his named agent. She shows his bank the power of attorney, takes over online bill pay, files his tax return, and arranges Medicare supplement payments. His house stays insured, the electricity stays on, and no court is involved.

## Which powers need to be spelled out?

Some powers can be used to shift money away from you or to rewrite your estate plan, so the law generally requires you to grant them expressly rather than assume them. These are often called "hot powers," and they vary by state. Common ones include:

| Power | Why it needs extra care |
|---|---|
| Making gifts, including to the agent | Could drain assets or favor the agent over other heirs |
| Creating, amending or funding a trust | Can change who ultimately inherits |
| Changing beneficiary designations or survivorship rights | Overrides what you planned for retirement accounts and insurance |
| Creating or changing joint accounts | Moves ownership and can bypass your will |
| Delegating the agent's authority | Puts someone you did not choose in charge |
| Waiving survivor benefits under a pension | Can reduce a spouse's lifetime income |

A good rule is to grant a hot power only when you have a reason, and to describe limits. For example, a gifting power may allow gifts up to the federal gift tax annual exclusion, currently $19,000 per recipient in 2026, only to your existing heirs, and only in amounts that do not endanger your own care. Gifts made for Medicaid planning are a special topic; see our [Medicaid planning guide](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## What a durable power of attorney cannot do

The document has firm edges. Knowing them prevents surprises.

1. **It ends at death.** After you die your executor or trustee takes over, not your agent. The agent cannot pay your funeral bill from your account on the strength of the power of attorney, or distribute your belongings.
2. **It cannot make or change your will.** Only you can sign a will, and only while you have testamentary capacity.
3. **It does not control assets in a trust.** Property owned by a [revocable trust](/glossary/revocable-trust) is managed by the trustee. The power of attorney only reaches assets in your own name, which is why a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) and funded trust belong in the same plan.
4. **It does not make health decisions.** Medical choices need a separate [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney). Some forms combine them, but legally they are different authorities.
5. **It cannot excuse the agent from the rules.** An agent is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary) and must act in your interest, keep your money separate from their own, and keep records.

## What happens if you have no power of attorney?

If you lose capacity without one, your family usually must petition a court to appoint a [guardian or conservator](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship). That process requires notice to relatives, often a medical evaluation, a court hearing and sometimes a court-appointed lawyer for you. It can take weeks or months, costs thousands of dollars, creates a public record and ongoing court reporting, and the judge, not you, decides who serves.

Spouses do not automatically have access to each other's individually owned accounts, and adult children have none. Even a joint account only helps for that one account. The power of attorney is the low-cost way to avoid all of this.

## How do you set one up?

The steps are straightforward, and most of the effort is in the thinking.

1. **Pick your agent and at least one backup.** Our guide to [choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) walks through the traits that matter.
2. **Decide when it takes effect.** Immediately, or only on incapacity. See [springing vs. immediate](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).
3. **Decide on the hot powers.** Gifts, trust funding, beneficiary changes.
4. **Sign with the formalities your state requires.** Most states require a notary; a number also require one or two witnesses. Real estate transactions typically require a notarized document that can be recorded.
5. **Give copies to the agent and your key institutions.** Some banks want to review the document before you need it.
6. **Store the original somewhere findable,** and tell your agent where.
7. **Review it every few years,** or after a move, divorce, death or major change in your assets. Documents signed long ago are more likely to be questioned by banks.

If your agent will need to use the document with a financial institution that has its own form, ask now whether it will accept yours. If it won't, see [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).

The power of attorney is the incapacity half of the plan, and the will or trust is the death half. A complete plan has both, plus healthcare documents.
