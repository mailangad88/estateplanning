You hold a signed power of attorney, the principal needs bills paid, and the teller says no. This is among the most common and frustrating problems agents report, and it often happens even when the document is perfectly valid.

The good news is that most refusals can be resolved without a court, and the law in many states is on the agent's side. This guide explains why banks refuse, gives a step-by-step response, and lists the steps that prevent the problem. For the document itself, see our overview of [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## Why do banks refuse a power of attorney?

Banks refuse for reasons that are partly legal and partly practical. Understanding which one you face tells you how to answer.

- **Unfamiliarity.** Front-line staff rarely see them and are trained to escalate or refuse when unsure.
- **Their own form.** Many institutions prefer a bank-specific power of attorney form and treat others as a nuisance.
- **Age of the document.** A power of attorney signed 15 years ago may be treated as stale, although the law usually does not impose an expiration.
- **Missing powers.** The document may not mention a particular account type, or the power to open or close accounts, or may lack express authority such as gifting.
- **Springing triggers.** If the document is springing, the bank may want proof of incapacity in a specific form. See [springing vs. immediate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).
- **Formalities.** Missing notarization, a missing witness, or the document came from another state.
- **Fraud concerns.** Banks are under pressure to prevent elder financial exploitation, and may refuse a transaction that looks unusual, such as a large transfer to the agent.
- **Liability concerns.** Compliance departments may want a legal review before acting.

## What should you do first?

Stay calm and keep records. The first conversation is usually with a branch employee who cannot decide, so aim to reach someone who can.

1. **Ask exactly why.** "Can you tell me the specific reason and the policy that requires it?" Write down the name, date and answer.
2. **Ask for the refusal in writing.** Many agents never request it, and the request alone often changes the response.
3. **Ask for the legal or compliance department,** or the bank's "power of attorney desk." Large banks have a team that reviews these documents centrally.
4. **Ask what the bank will accept.** Some banks will honor the document after review, or will accept their own form signed by the principal, if the principal still has capacity.
5. **Provide helpful extras.** A certified copy, a notarized copy, the agent's certification, an ID, and a letter from the principal's attorney can resolve doubts.
6. **Cite the law.** A short note that references your state's statute on acceptance of powers of attorney, if there is one, shows you know the rules. An attorney can supply the right citation.
7. **Escalate.** If compliance refuses, ask for the manager's name and contact the bank's executive or customer relations office.
8. **Send a demand letter** through an attorney, if needed.
9. **Consider a petition** to the court as the next step.

## What does state law say?

Many states protect agents through statutes that limit when a bank can refuse. The Uniform Power of Attorney Act, adopted in a large number of states in some form, is a common model. Its key features, which vary from state to state, include:

- A person must generally accept an acknowledged (notarized) power of attorney within a set number of business days after receiving it, or must request a certification, a translation or a legal opinion within that period.
- A person may refuse for specific listed reasons, such as the agent being unable to provide a requested certification, a good-faith belief the power is invalid or the agent lacks authority, or actual knowledge of termination.
- A person may not refuse simply because the document is not on the bank's own form, or because it was not signed recently.
- A person who refuses in violation of the statute can be ordered by a court to accept, and may owe the agent's attorney fees.
- A person who accepts in good faith without actual knowledge of a problem is protected from liability.

States that have not adopted the uniform act have their own rules. Some impose penalties for unreasonable refusal, while others provide only weaker protection. Because the details matter, check with a local attorney before you cite a rule to a bank, and see whether your state's guide, such as our [Texas estate planning guide](/estate-planning/texas), mentions local acceptance rules.

## What documents help overcome a refusal?

A small package of paperwork often changes the outcome.

| Document | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Original or certified copy of the power of attorney | Proves the grant; some banks reject photocopies |
| Agent's certification or affidavit | Confirms the power is valid and unrevoked, under penalty of perjury |
| Notarized copy | Satisfies a bank that doubts authenticity |
| Government photo ID for agent | Proves the agent is the named person |
| Letter from the drafting attorney | Confirms the document is valid and gives a contact for questions |
| Principal's letter or call, if capable | Confirms they want the agent to act |
| Medical certificate, for springing powers | Proves the trigger occurred |

Take Gloria, 74, whose husband Ray has Alzheimer's disease. Ray signed a durable power of attorney naming her seven years ago. The bank's branch manager refuses it as "too old." Gloria asks for the legal department, sends a notarized certification, and attaches a short letter from the lawyer who prepared it. Within two weeks, the bank adds her to Ray's accounts as agent. She did not need the court.

## When should you involve a lawyer?

Bring in a lawyer if any of the following is true:

- The bank has refused in writing after receiving the full package
- The principal's money is tied up and bills are going unpaid
- The refusal cites suspected abuse, and you need help responding
- The bank wants a court order or a guardianship
- Several institutions are refusing at once

A lawyer can write a demand letter, explain which statutory deadlines the bank has missed, and, if needed, file a petition asking a court to order acceptance. These petitions are generally faster and cheaper than a guardianship or conservatorship, and in some states can result in an award of fees against the institution. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) for why you do not want to jump to the court-appointed route.

## What if the bank thinks the agent is abusing the document?

Banks have both a right and, in many states, a duty to report suspected elder financial exploitation. A bank may freeze a transaction while it looks into a concern. If you are an honest agent, cooperate: provide your records, explain the purpose of the transaction, and keep payments clearly tied to the principal's needs. Never mix the principal's money with your own. If you are a relative worried that an agent is stealing, read our [elder financial abuse guide](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) for what to do.

## How can you prevent a refusal in the first place?

Prevention costs a little time now and saves a great deal later.

1. **Ask the bank in advance.** Call each institution, ask whether it accepts outside powers of attorney, and ask whether it prefers its own form. Some will let you sign theirs in addition to yours.
2. **Choose an immediate rather than springing power** if friction worries you.
3. **Use a recent date.** Refresh the document every five to ten years.
4. **Have the agent present the document early,** while the principal is healthy, so that the bank can verify it and note it on file.
5. **Include the powers institutions commonly ask about:** banking, retirement accounts, real estate, taxes, benefits, digital assets and, if intended, gifting.
6. **Use a notary,** and for real estate transactions, make sure the document is in recordable form.
7. **Keep a certified copy or two** and a short agent's certification form.
8. **Consider a revocable trust** that holds the principal's major accounts, so the successor trustee, not an agent, is the contact. See [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).
