You may be reading this after a bank refused to talk to you, a bill went unpaid, or a doctor said something that made the situation real. Your parent has dementia, nobody signed a power of attorney, and you are not sure what you are allowed to do. That is a hard place to be, and it is more common than most families admit.

There are still real options. Which one fits depends on what your parent can understand now. This page explains how to tell, what you can and cannot do, and what to do in the first week. Nothing here replaces advice from a lawyer who can meet your parent, but it should help you walk into that meeting prepared. For the bigger picture, see our [guide to power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## What are your three situations?

Most families fall into one of three situations, and the path differs for each.

| Where you are | What it usually means | Likely path |
|---|---|---|
| Your parent can still follow a conversation, explain what they own and who they trust | They may have capacity to sign a power of attorney today | Meet an attorney soon; sign documents while it is possible |
| You are not sure; some days are good, some are not | Capacity may exist at certain times and for simpler documents | Ask an attorney to assess, possibly with a physician note |
| Your parent cannot understand what documents do or who they are choosing | They likely cannot sign a power of attorney | Guardianship or conservatorship, plus limited alternatives |

Do not decide which row you are in by yourself, and do not decide it by the diagnosis. A lawyer's judgment, not yours or the bank's, will matter.

## What does capacity actually mean?

Capacity is the ability to understand a specific decision at the moment you make it. It is not one switch that turns off with a diagnosis. A person with early or mid-stage dementia may be able to name a trusted child as their agent, even if they cannot manage a checkbook. Capacity can also fluctuate, with many people clearer in the morning than in the evening.

For signing a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), the person generally needs to understand roughly the following:

- That they are giving someone authority to handle their money and property
- Who that person is, and that they trust them
- The general scope of what they own
- That they can revoke the document while they have capacity

The bar is usually lower than for a complicated contract and is set by state law. It differs from capacity to make a will, which has its own test, covered in the glossary on [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity).

## If your parent may still have capacity, what should you do now?

Act quickly and do it properly. Delay is the main risk, because capacity can decline between a visit and a signing.

1. **Book an attorney who will meet your parent.** Ask in advance whether the firm has experience signing documents for older clients with memory loss.
2. **Let the attorney meet your parent alone.** This protects your parent and protects the document from a later claim of [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence). You can brief the attorney beforehand.
3. **Choose the time of day carefully.** Pick when your parent is usually at their best.
4. **Consider a physician note.** A short statement from the treating doctor, written close to the signing, that your parent understands the decision can help. It supports the attorney's own assessment but does not replace it.
5. **Sign the full set.** Besides the financial power of attorney, sign a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization). The attorney should document what they observed.

Our article on [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) explains the order of signing and how to document capacity.

## What if capacity is gone: when is guardianship the route?

If your parent cannot understand the document, nobody can sign it for them, and a court proceeding is generally the only way to gain broad legal authority. The process is called guardianship in some states and conservatorship in others, and some states use the first for personal decisions and the second for money. The glossary entries on [guardian](/glossary/guardian) and [conservatorship](/glossary/conservatorship) explain the terms.

The usual steps look like this:

1. A family member or another interested person files a petition in the county court where your parent lives.
2. A physician or other professional evaluates your parent, and the court may appoint a lawyer or visitor to represent their interests.
3. Close relatives receive notice and may object or support the petition.
4. A judge holds a hearing and decides whether your parent is incapacitated and who should serve.
5. The guardian or conservator receives court papers and reports regularly, often with inventories, accountings and approval for large transactions.

Compared with a power of attorney, this route costs more, takes longer, is a matter of public record and involves court supervision for as long as it lasts. It can also be a protection, especially when relatives disagree or someone may be exploiting your parent. Our comparison of [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) goes through the differences and how courts weigh less restrictive options. Many states have also adopted reforms that require courts to consider alternatives first.

## What less restrictive options might exist without a court?

Depending on the situation, a few narrower tools may handle specific problems.

- **Representative payee.** The Social Security Administration can appoint a payee to receive and manage a person's Social Security benefits. This covers only those benefits, not the rest of their money.
- **Authorized signer or a bank's own process.** Some banks let an account holder add a signer, but only while the holder can consent. Others have internal procedures for incapacitated customers, usually requiring court documents.
- **Joint account.** This lets a child pay bills, but it carries real risks. The money can be exposed to the child's creditors, may count as a gift for Medicaid purposes, and can pass outside the will. Ask a lawyer before using one.
- **Existing documents.** Check whether your parent already has a trust, a healthcare directive or a power of attorney from years ago that no one remembers. Search the safe, the filing cabinet and their old attorney's records.
- **Supported decision-making.** Some states recognize agreements in which a person chooses helpers and keeps legal control. These work only when the person still has enough capacity.
- **Benefit-specific processes.** Pension plans, insurers and agencies like the VA have their own processes for people who cannot act for themselves.

If a bank has already refused to talk to you, our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) may be helpful, though it assumes a document exists.

## What can you not do?

A few shortcuts feel natural and cause trouble.

- **Do not sign your parent's name.** Signing for someone without legal authority can be forgery or fraud, even with good intentions.
- **Do not rely on informal use of their debit card as a plan.** Occasional help may be tolerated, but long-term use leaves you without legal protection and invites questions from siblings and banks.
- **Do not retitle assets without advice.** Putting your name on a house or account can create gift tax filings, Medicaid penalties, creditor exposure and sibling disputes.
- **Do not have your parent sign something you wrote.** Online forms signed without a capacity check are easy to challenge.

## What is the first-week checklist?

When things feel urgent, a short list helps.

1. Gather what you can find: the will or trust, prior powers of attorney, insurance policies, deeds, recent statements and the name of your parent's old attorney.
2. List accounts, debts and monthly bills so nothing lapses.
3. Ask the doctor for a copy of the diagnosis and any capacity evaluation.
4. Call an estate planning or elder law attorney and say plainly that capacity is a question.
5. Watch for fraud: scam calls, new credit lines, unusual withdrawals. Consider a credit freeze if your parent is able to authorize it or if you become legally authorized.
6. Keep a written log of conversations, dates, expenses and decisions.
7. Tell siblings what you are doing. Our article on [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) has ideas for family conversations.

## How do you handle siblings and the burden of caregiving?

Caregiving for a parent with dementia is a heavy load, and families often split under it. The person with legal authority makes the decisions, and a court or a power of attorney defines who that is. Writing things down, sharing basic updates and keeping receipts protects everyone. If you are a paid or unpaid caregiver, see our article on [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver).

## How does paying for care fit in?

Care for dementia can cost a great deal, and Medicaid may eventually be part of the plan. Most states look back 60 months for transfers, so moving a parent's money to children in a panic can cause penalties. Our article on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains what counts, and our [guide to Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) covers the options. Do not transfer assets before talking to an attorney.

## Do state rules matter?

Yes. Capacity standards, guardianship names, court procedures and available alternatives vary widely. Check your parent's state, which may not be where you live, through our state guides such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [New York](/estate-planning/new-york).
