You are holding a pen over a form and the person who needs to sign it cannot. Maybe your father's hand shakes too hard after a stroke. Maybe your mother is foggy by the afternoon. Maybe she is perfectly clear and simply says no. Each of those is a different problem with a different answer.

This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), sorts them out. The first question is never "who can sign" but "does this person understand what they are signing." If the answer is yes, a workaround almost always exists.

## The short version

- A person who understands the document but cannot hold a pen can direct someone else to sign for them, or make a mark.
- A person who cannot understand the document cannot grant power of attorney, no matter who holds the pen.
- A person who understands and refuses has the legal right to refuse.
- When a signature is impossible, look at benefit-specific forms before you consider court, and see a lawyer before you pick either.

## Which situation are you in?

| Situation | Can a power of attorney be signed? | Usual path |
|---|---|---|
| Understands, but cannot physically sign (stroke, tremor, arthritis) | Yes | Signature at direction or a mark, with a notary |
| Understands on good days, confused on others | Yes, on a good day | Sign when lucid, document the circumstances |
| Understands, but refuses | No | Conversation, compromise, patience |
| Cannot understand what the document does | No | Narrower tools or a guardianship |
| Unconscious or in a coma | No | Surrogate rules for medical decisions, court for money |

## What if the person understands but cannot sign?

State laws on this are practical, because legislatures know that stroke survivors and people with severe arthritis still make sound decisions. In most states, another adult may sign the principal's name for them, as long as it happens in the principal's conscious presence and at the principal's direction. A notary then records that this is what occurred. Alternatively, the principal can sign with an "X" or another mark, and witnesses confirm it. Check your state's rule on which method is allowed and how many witnesses are needed. Our article on [whether a power of attorney needs to be notarized](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-a-power-of-attorney-need-to-be-notarized) covers the formalities.

The person who signs for the principal matters. They should not be the named agent, and should not stand to gain from the document. A hospital chaplain, a neighbor or a notary's assistant works better than a child who is also the agent. Picture Frank, 76, whose stroke left his right hand useless. His daughter Ana is his agent. His neighbor Priya signs his name while Frank watches and nods, the notary checks his ID and asks him to state aloud that this is what he wants, and two witnesses sign. Nobody could later say Ana signed her own authority.

## What if the person has good days and bad days?

Capacity is judged at the time of signing. A person with early dementia may be fully able to understand a power of attorney at ten in the morning and unable to do so by dinner. If a lucid window exists, use it, and use it soon.

Steps that make a lucid-day signing hold up:

1. Schedule it for the time of day the person is usually sharpest, and keep the visitors few.
2. Ask the attorney to meet the person alone for part of the meeting, so the conversation is theirs.
3. Ask the person's physician for a short letter or note about their mental status around that date.
4. Have the document read aloud, and ask the person to explain in their own words who the agent is and what the agent can do.
5. Keep the agent out of the room during the explanation and the signing.

The legal test is not demanding. The person needs to understand what a power of attorney is, who they are choosing, roughly what property is involved, and that they can revoke it. Many people with a dementia diagnosis can still meet it for a time. A diagnosis alone does not remove the right to sign. We look at that stage in [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

## What if the person refuses?

You cannot sign for a competent adult who says no, and you should not try. Pressure also poisons the paperwork. If a document is signed after weeks of pushing from the person who benefits, a sibling can later argue [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) and ask a court to throw it out.

Refusal is rarely about the document. It is usually fear: that the child will take over, that the parent is being declared old, or that a sibling will be favored. Some approaches that work in practice:

- Ask what worries them, and then listen.
- Offer limits. A springing document, a short list of powers, or a requirement that the agent report to a sibling can ease the fear.
- Suggest naming a different agent, or an agent and a successor. Sometimes the objection is to you, and that is useful to know.
- Bring in a neutral voice such as a physician, a faith leader, or an attorney who can explain it in a one-hour visit.
- Present it as something they are doing for themselves, since bills do not stop while they are in a hospital.

If after all that the answer is still no, accept it. Competent people are allowed to make choices their children dislike. You can still help with what they allow, such as a joint account that you only watch or a bank's own authorization form for one account.

## What if the person cannot understand the document?

Then no one can create a power of attorney for them, and a forged or rushed signature is worse than nothing. A bank will reject it, and it can expose the signer to claims of fraud. At this point the options are narrower.

**Institution-specific tools.** Many banks have their own agent or "convenience" forms that need less capacity than a full power of attorney, and some let a person be added to an account as a signer. These are limited and can create their own problems, so read the fine print.

**Benefit-specific representatives.** Social Security can appoint a representative payee to receive someone's benefits, and Medicare and other programs have their own authorized-representative processes. These do not give general authority, but they handle the monthly money. Our article on [what a power of attorney agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do) shows how limited an agent's reach is with federal agencies even when a document exists.

**Medical surrogates.** For healthcare, most states have a list of relatives who may decide when no agent is named. We describe the order in [what happens if you have no advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive).

**Guardianship or conservatorship.** When someone must manage property and nothing else works, a family member petitions a court, which holds a hearing and appoints a guardian or conservator. It is slower, public and supervised, and the court may not choose who you would have chosen. Compare the two paths in [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship), and see [what happens if someone is incapacitated without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney) for a plain-language walk through the court route.

## How state law changes this

The core principles are consistent, but the mechanics vary. States differ on how many witnesses are required, whether a notary must be present, whether remote online notarization works for powers of attorney, and what a guardianship petition requires. Some states give more weight to a capacity letter from a doctor. If the principal lives in a different state from you, the rules of the state where the signing occurs usually govern the formalities. Start with the state guides, such as [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) or [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey), and ask an attorney before the signing day.

## What to do this week

1. Decide which situation in the table fits the person.
2. If they can understand but not sign, book a notary who can come to the hospital, home or care facility, and line up two witnesses.
3. Ask the physician for a note about alertness on the signing day.
4. Pick a signer who is neither the agent nor a beneficiary.
5. If capacity is gone, stop and talk to an attorney about narrower tools or a guardianship before anyone signs anything.
