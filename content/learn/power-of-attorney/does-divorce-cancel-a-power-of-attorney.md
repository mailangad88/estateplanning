You signed a power of attorney years ago, and the person you trusted with your bank accounts and your house was your husband or wife. Now the marriage is ending. Does the document disappear on its own, or can your spouse still walk into the bank?

In Illinois, the statute answers it for the final judgment. In New Jersey and in the months before the judgment anywhere, the answer is less comfortable. This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), explains what each state's law says and the practical steps that protect you.

## What happens to a power of attorney when you divorce?

If your spouse is the agent, Illinois ends the role when the court enters a judgment of dissolution of marriage or legal separation. The Power of Attorney Act treats the former spouse as having died on the date of the judgment, for all purposes of the agency (755 ILCS 45/2-6). The next named successor then takes over. If none is named, nobody holds the authority.

The rule does not cancel the whole document, only the spouse's role, and the principal's own wishes in the document stay as written. It also says nothing about what the bank will do if no one tells it.

For health care, New Jersey spells out a rule in its advance directive statute. A spouse's designation as health care representative is revoked on divorce or legal separation, unless the directive says otherwise, and a domestic partner's ends when the partnership ends (N.J.S.A. 26:2H-57). Our page on [New Jersey advance directives](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives) covers the rest of that rule.

## What about New Jersey financial powers of attorney?

Do not assume. New Jersey's Revised Durable Power of Attorney Act asks for a signed and acknowledged writing and lets you revoke it by destroying the originals or signing a written revocation (see [New Jersey power of attorney](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney)). A reader should treat the divorce question as unsettled for that document and act as if the old power remains alive until it is revoked. That is the cautious course, and it costs a signature.

## What is the risky period?

The months between filing and the final judgment. During that time your spouse is still your spouse and still your agent, in every state. An agent owes fiduciary duties, but a spouse in a bitter divorce may be tempted to move money, change contact information or sell something. The agent could also pledge your credit.

Courts can restrict spouses from transferring assets while a case is pending, and divorce lawyers often ask for that. The power of attorney is still a separate source of authority. If you have any worry, do not wait for the judgment. Our article on [estate planning during separation or a pending divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-during-separation-or-a-pending-divorce) walks through the documents to fix first.

## How do you cancel it yourself?

1. **Write a revocation.** Say which power of attorney you are revoking by date and agent. Sign it and have it notarized. Illinois lets you revoke in any manner communicated to the agent or others involved (755 ILCS 45/2-5), but a signed, notarized paper is easier for a bank to accept.
2. **Deliver it.** Hand or mail a copy to your spouse. Use a method that shows the date.
3. **Notify every institution.** Banks, brokers, insurers, your employer's retirement plan and the county recorder if a copy was recorded. Many of them can keep honoring a document until they receive notice.
4. **Take back the originals.** Ask your spouse to return them, and destroy the ones you hold.
5. **Sign a new power of attorney.** Name a person you trust and a backup. Say in it that all earlier powers are revoked, since a new document does not always cancel the old.
6. **Replace the health care form.** Do not leave a former spouse as your medical decision-maker by oversight.

See [revoking an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney) for more on the Illinois mechanics.

## An example

Tom and Rhonda, both in their fifties, live in Joliet, Illinois. Rhonda is Tom's agent under a property power of attorney from 2019, with Tom's brother Ed as successor. They file for divorce in March. The judgment enters in November.

In April, Tom does nothing. In May, Rhonda is still a legal agent, and she could in theory have moved money out of his accounts. Tom, on his lawyer's advice, signs a revocation in April and sends it to the two banks. By the judgment date in November, the statute would have ended Rhonda's role anyway, and Ed would have been next in line. Tom's real protection came from the April letter, not from the November judgment.

## What if the agent is not your spouse but your spouse is a problem?

Sometimes the agent is your adult child, and the trouble is that your spouse is hostile to that child. Divorce does not change that arrangement, but it can change the pressure on it. A spouse who loses the agent role may ask a court to oversee your finances, and a pending divorce may bring accounting demands. Keep records. If your agent is asked to account, an Illinois agent must keep a record of receipts, disbursements and significant actions (755 ILCS 45/2-7), and a New Jersey agent has the same record-keeping duty (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.13). Tell the agent in plain words that the divorce does not change their job, and ask them to keep your money in your name, separate from theirs.

It also helps to confirm what the divorce settlement says about retirement accounts and the house. An agent should not be the person who discovers, after the fact, that an account has been retitled.

## What else does divorce leave in place?

The power of attorney is one piece. A divorce does not change by itself the forms that are not governed by a divorce statute. Look at:

- **Life insurance and retirement beneficiaries.** The ex-spouse may still be named, and some states and plans treat this differently. See [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).
- **Your will and any trust.** An ex-spouse may be named as executor or trustee.
- **Joint accounts and the house title.** These need to be separated by the settlement.
- **Payable-on-death accounts.** The beneficiary line does not update itself.

Our article on [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) has the full list. For the general topic of how the power ends, see [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).

## What if you are the ex-spouse who holds a power of attorney?

Stop using it. In Illinois your authority ended at the judgment, and acting after that could expose you to a claim. In any state, if the marriage is ending, using authority your spouse may be trying to revoke leads to disputes. Return the original, keep records of what you did, and let the principal sign a new one.
