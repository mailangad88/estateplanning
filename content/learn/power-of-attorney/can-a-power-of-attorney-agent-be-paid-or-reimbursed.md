Being someone's agent takes time. You pay their bills, drive to the bank, talk to the insurance company and keep the records. At some point most agents ask the same thing: am I allowed to be paid for this? And the other question lurks right behind it. If I cover a bill from my own pocket, or borrow from the account until payday, who will care?

The answers sit between two extremes. An agent is not meant to work for free at a loss. An agent is also not meant to treat the principal's money as their own. This page shows where the line is and how to stay on the right side of it. For the full list of duties, see our guide to [what an agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do).

## Can a power of attorney agent be paid?

Often yes, if the amount is reasonable and the document or state law allows it. Many states permit an agent to receive reasonable compensation unless the power of attorney says otherwise. Others are silent, and a few expect family agents to serve without pay. Because the rule varies, read your document first and your state's law second.

Pay is easier to defend when it follows these ground rules.

1. It is written down before the work starts, in the document or in a separate signed agreement.
2. The rate is tied to something real: an hourly figure, a monthly fee or a rate a professional would charge.
3. The principal, or a second person such as a co-agent, a trustee or an attorney, knows and agrees.
4. The agent logs the time and the tasks.
5. The pay is not larger than the work.

Consider Carol, 72, who names her son Dev as agent. Dev spends about five hours a month on her bills and taxes. A flat monthly fee for that work would be easy to justify. Dev paying himself a lump sum of several thousand dollars after a hard year, with nothing written, would be easy for his sister to challenge.

## Can an agent be reimbursed for expenses?

Yes, and reimbursement is the least controversial form of payment. Postage, a notary, a plane ticket to sign a document, a locksmith, a repair bill: if the agent paid it for the principal and has the receipt, reimbursement from the principal's funds is ordinary. Keep a folder, and reimburse yourself by a check from the principal's account, with a note of what it covers. Do not take cash.

The better habit is to avoid fronting money at all. Pay from the principal's own account whenever you can. Fronting money invites mistakes and makes records harder to read.

## Can an agent pay their own bills or borrow from the account?

No. This is the line that most often leads to a claim. An agent has a duty of loyalty, and the duty is broken when the agent benefits at the principal's expense. Here are the forms it usually takes.

- Paying the agent's mortgage, rent or credit card from the principal's account.
- Moving money into the agent's name "for safekeeping".
- Taking a loan from the principal without a written, fair-terms agreement.
- Putting the agent's name on the principal's house or accounts.
- Gifts to the agent, unless the document clearly authorizes them.

Gifts need particular care. Our article on [whether a power of attorney can make gifts](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts) explains why most documents restrict them and what a safe gifting clause looks like. If the principal wants to give the agent something, a lawyer should draft it while the principal still has capacity, in a document the agent did not prepare.

## What if the agent is also a caregiver?

Pay for care is different from pay for being an agent. A child who moves in and helps with bathing, meals and medication provides services that a stranger would charge for. The safest route is a written personal care agreement, signed while the parent has capacity, that sets a fair rate and the services covered. Without it, payments look like gifts, which creates trouble with siblings and, for a parent who may need Medicaid, with the look-back rules.

Our articles on [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) and [taxes when you pay for a parent's care](/learn/elder-care/tax-breaks-when-you-pay-for-a-parents-care) cover the agreement and the tax side. The agent role and the caregiver role should be kept separate on paper, even when the same person does both.

## When is an agent personally liable?

An agent who acts honestly and carefully is generally not responsible for a bad outcome. If the stock market falls after a reasonable decision, the agent does not owe the difference. Liability usually follows a breach of duty. These are the usual triggers.

| Conduct | Typical consequence |
|---|---|
| Self-dealing, such as paying personal bills | Repayment, removal, sometimes fees |
| Mixing the principal's money with the agent's | Burden on the agent to prove the money was spent properly |
| No records or accounting | Courts may assume the worst and order an accounting |
| Ignoring the principal's written wishes | Liability for the loss that followed |
| Acting after the authority ended | Personal liability to the third party and the estate |

A court can order an accounting, reverse a transaction, remove the agent and award damages. In serious cases, the agent can face criminal charges. [Power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) lists the signs families see first.

## Is an agent's pay taxable, and does it affect the principal?

Pay an agent receives for services is generally income to the agent, so it belongs on their tax return. For the principal, the amount is a cost of managing their affairs, and it can matter for benefits. A parent who applies for Medicaid will have their bank statements reviewed, and a large payment to a child without a written agreement can be treated as a transfer for less than fair value. A tax adviser can say how your own situation is treated.

The practical lesson is to decide on pay before any money moves. A modest, documented arrangement made early is far easier to explain than a large payment made later. Our article on [Medicaid's look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains why those payments attract attention.

## What should a principal put in the document?

Put the arrangement in writing at the start. A short clause can say whether the agent is paid, the rate, who approves it, and that reasonable expenses are reimbursed from the principal's funds. Other clauses can bar loans to the agent, require a yearly report to a named relative, and say what happens if the agent and the principal disagree.

A professional agent such as an attorney or trust company will bring a fee schedule. A family agent often brings none, and that is the person for whom a short, clear clause does the most good. The clause protects the agent from accusations as much as it protects the principal from loss.

## How should an agent protect themselves?

Follow a short routine.

1. Open a separate account in the principal's name for everything the agent handles.
2. Keep every statement, receipt and invoice, and write a monthly one-page summary.
3. Never pay yourself without written authority.
4. Send a summary to a second family member each quarter.
5. Ask an attorney before any gift, loan, sale or transfer involving the agent's own interests.

Principals can help, too. The document can set pay, bar gifts, require reports and name a monitor. Our article on [limiting an agent's powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/limiting-an-agents-powers-and-adding-safeguards-in-a-power-of-attorney) shows the wording.
