Usually the first sign is small. A sister mentions that Dad's electric bill went unpaid. A bank letter turns up with a name nobody recognizes. The brother who holds the power of attorney says everything is fine and changes the subject.

A power of attorney hands one person a lot of access with very little supervision, and most of the time that trust is well placed. This article explains how abuse happens, what it looks like from the outside, what an agent is and is not allowed to do, and what you can do if you suspect it. For the basics of the document itself, start with our overview of [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## What counts as power of attorney abuse?

Abuse is any use of the agent's authority for the agent's benefit rather than the principal's. The agent is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary), which means the law holds them to a strict standard: act in the principal's interest, keep the principal's money separate from their own, and keep records. Broad wording in the document does not change that.

In practice abuse falls into a few patterns.

- **Outright taking.** Withdrawals, transfers or checks made out to the agent with no tie to the principal's care.
- **Self-dealing.** Selling the principal's house to the agent at a low price, or lending the agent money from the principal's account.
- **Unauthorized gifts.** Many documents limit or forbid gifting. An agent who gives themselves large "gifts" may be going well beyond the document.
- **Neglect.** Not paying for care, or letting insurance and taxes lapse, while the money sits unused or goes elsewhere.
- **Isolation and pressure.** Cutting off phone calls and visits, or pressing the principal to sign new documents. This often overlaps with [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence).

Not every mistake is abuse. A new agent who commingles funds by accident or forgets to keep receipts has made an error that can usually be fixed with better records. The difference is intent, the amount and whether the agent will open the books when asked.

## What are the warning signs?

The signs are usually financial, but some are about behavior. A short list that families actually run into:

| Sign | What it may mean |
|---|---|
| Bills go unpaid despite plenty of money | Funds are being diverted |
| New joint accounts or the agent added as owner | Assets are being moved into the agent's reach |
| Changes to beneficiaries, deeds or the will | The agent may be rewriting the plan |
| Agent refuses to share statements | Records would show the problem |
| Large cash withdrawals or ATM use by someone who cannot drive | The card is in the wrong hands |
| Principal is suddenly isolated | Control, not care |
| Agent's lifestyle changes quickly | Money is going to the agent |

Take Rosa, 79, who gave her son Daniel a durable power of attorney after a hip surgery. Her daughter Elena notices Rosa's phone was disconnected and her pharmacy sent a past-due notice. Daniel says he is "handling it." Neither fact proves theft, but together they justify a written request for statements and a call to the bank.

## What may an agent not do?

An agent must stay within what the document grants and what state law allows. Typical limits include:

1. Using the principal's money for the agent's own purposes.
2. Mixing the principal's funds with the agent's accounts.
3. Making gifts the document does not clearly authorize, including to themselves.
4. Changing beneficiary designations or the principal's will. Our article on [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) goes through this list.
5. Refusing to keep records or to account when asked.
6. Acting after the document has ended. See [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).

Many states add an extra step for gifts and for changes to survivorship rights: the power must be spelled out in the document in so many words. That is a drafting protection, and it is worth reading your own document for it.

## What should you do if you suspect abuse?

Move in a sensible order. Do not start with an accusation.

1. **Write down what you have seen,** with dates, amounts and who told you what. Keep copies of statements you are legitimately entitled to see.
2. **Ask for records politely and in writing.** A request for "an accounting for the past 12 months" is easy for an honest agent to answer. A refusal tells you something.
3. **Talk to the principal if they can still understand,** privately. If they have capacity, they can revoke the power and sign a new one.
4. **Call adult protective services.** Every state has an agency that takes reports of financial exploitation of older or vulnerable adults. You can usually report in confidence.
5. **Contact the bank.** Banks can flag an account, review a pattern and in some cases pause suspicious transactions. They may be reluctant to talk about a customer, but they can listen.
6. **Report a crime to the police** when there is clear theft. Prosecutors can pursue exploitation, though they often wait for a records trail.
7. **Consult an attorney about a petition.** A court can order an accounting, freeze assets, suspend or remove the agent, and appoint a conservator. We compare that path to the alternatives in [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

Keep the family conversation separate from the legal one. The principal's safety comes first, and a loud argument at the kitchen table often leads the agent to hide records.

## Can the agent be held responsible?

Yes. A court can order an agent to restore money, pay interest and, in many states, pay the principal's attorney fees. Serious cases can bring criminal charges for theft or exploitation. An agent who is found to have taken money may also lose their right to inherit from the principal in some states, though the rules differ.

The principal's own rights matter too. A principal with capacity can revoke, can demand records and can ask the court for help. If the principal has died, the executor or successor trustee has the right to examine what the agent did, and can bring a claim for the estate. The [executor's duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) include collecting what the estate is owed.

## How do you prevent it when you draft the document?

You cannot make a power of attorney abuse-proof, but you can make it harder.

- Choose with care. Our guide to [choosing your agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers the qualities that matter more than family rank.
- Name a backup, and consider a second person who gets copies of statements.
- Limit gifting and self-dealing in plain words, or require the second person's written consent.
- Avoid giving authority over a trust or beneficiary designation unless you truly mean to.
- Think about a springing document if you worry about early misuse, with eyes open about its drawbacks. See [springing vs. immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).
- Make sure someone other than the agent knows where the document and the accounts are.

Our broader article on [protecting older adults from financial exploitation](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) covers scams and other threats that do not involve an agent at all.

## How state law changes this

Most states have a statute that lists an agent's duties and says who can ask a court to review the agent's conduct. Who counts as an interested person, how quickly a court must act and whether fees shift to the agent all differ. Some states' laws are close to the Uniform Power of Attorney Act and some are not. Your state guide, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), points to local rules, and an attorney in your state can tell you which court to use.
