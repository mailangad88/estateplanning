Before you pick an agent, you may wonder whether the person you have in mind is even allowed. Your closest friend lives three states away. Your partner is not your spouse. Your daughter is 17. Your brother served time a long time ago. These are the cases where people hesitate to name someone, or sign a document naming the wrong person because it felt like the only legal choice.

The law is more flexible than most people expect. This page covers who can serve, who cannot, and where the practical limits begin. For the qualities that make a good agent, see our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney) and our article on [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent).

## What does the law require of an agent?

In general, an agent must be a competent adult who is willing to serve. That is nearly the whole list. Most states do not require the agent to be a relative, a spouse, a citizen or a resident. Some states have a few extra rules, such as a limit on who may witness the document, and a handful have rules for corporate agents. The form you use may also ask the agent to sign an acknowledgment of their duties.

An agent is also a fiduciary, which means a legal duty to act in your interest. Not every person with the capacity to serve has the temperament to do it. Our article on [what an agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do) lists the specific duties, so the person you ask knows what they are agreeing to.

| Candidate | Allowed? | The practical question |
|---|---|---|
| Adult child | Yes | Can they stay neutral with their siblings? |
| Spouse | Yes | What if the spouse is also sick or older? |
| Unmarried partner | Yes | Is there a successor if the relationship ends? |
| Friend or neighbor | Yes | Will your family accept them? |
| Out-of-state relative | Yes | Who handles local signatures? |
| Attorney, accountant or trust company | Yes | What will it cost, and who supervises them? |
| Minor | Generally no | Wait until 18; name an adult backup |
| Person under guardianship | Generally no | Their own decisions are limited by a court |
| Someone with a fraud or theft record | Often allowed on paper | Institutions and courts may reject them |

## Can a friend or an unmarried partner be your agent?

Yes to both, and for many people they are the right choice. A friend who has known you for twenty years may understand your values better than a distant cousin does. A partner may be the person who is in your house when you fall.

Consider Hector, 66, who has been with his partner Gil for fourteen years. They never married. Hector's only relative is a niece in another state. If he has a stroke and has no power of attorney, Gil cannot talk to Hector's bank or sell a stock to pay the mortgage. The niece could ask a court to be named guardian, which takes weeks. With a signed document naming Gil, the problem goes away.

There is a catch in both cases. Family may resist a non-relative, and conflict is an expense of its own. Tell the relatives what you have done and why, ideally before the document is used. If you have strong feelings, put a short letter with the document. See also our article on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples), where we explain why the document matters even more without a marriage certificate.

## Can your agent live in another state?

Yes, with the practical limits that distance brings. A valid power of attorney does not care where the agent lives. A bank branch, a county recorder or a nursing home might care a great deal, because they want a person to appear, show identification and sign.

Think through the daily tasks. Can the agent get to your house in an emergency? Can they receive your mail? Will they work with your local accountant? If the answer is mostly no, a split can work: the out-of-state child as primary agent, a local friend as successor or co-agent for tasks that need a signature. Our article on [co-agents and successor agents](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-name-more-than-one-power-of-attorney-agent) explains how to define who does what. Another article covers whether [a power of attorney works in another state](/learn/power-of-attorney/is-a-power-of-attorney-valid-in-another-state-or-abroad) when you move or own property elsewhere.

## Can a minor or a person with a disability serve?

A minor generally cannot, because an agent has to be able to make binding contracts. A person with a disability can serve if they have legal capacity, but you should test whether they can carry out the work, with support if necessary.

If a person is under guardianship or conservatorship, a court has already limited their decisions, so they are a poor choice. For a young adult you trust, a better route is to name an adult now and plan to change it after their eighteenth birthday. The review habit matters, and our guide to [when to update your plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists the triggers.

## What about someone with a criminal record?

The law rarely bars them outright, but your attention should go to the kind of record. A person with a conviction from decades ago for a matter unrelated to money is in a different position from someone with a conviction for fraud, forgery, embezzlement or abusing an older adult.

A bank can refuse any agent for its own reasons, and a court can remove an agent who breaks the rules. If you name someone with a record involving money, ask whether the document can include safeguards: a requirement to report to a second person, a bar on gifts, or a co-agent who has to co-sign larger transactions. Our article on [limiting an agent's powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/limiting-an-agents-powers-and-adding-safeguards-in-a-power-of-attorney) shows how to draft them. If the concern is real, the simple answer is to choose someone else.

## What if the person you want is also an heir?

Naming a child or a sibling who will inherit is the most common arrangement, and it is allowed. The risk is a conflict of interest: the agent decides how the money is spent, and later receives what remains. Other heirs notice. Some families name a non-heir as agent for that reason, or require the agent to report to a second relative each quarter.

Take Beatrice, 80, who has three children. She names her eldest, Colin, as agent because he lives close by. She also tells the other two in writing what she asked of him and why, and she asks him to send a short yearly summary of her accounts to all three. Nothing about the arrangement is unusual, and it removes most of the suspicion before it starts. Our article on [what happens when siblings disagree about the agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-challenge-or-remove-a-power-of-attorney-agent-when-siblings-disagree) shows what the alternative looks like.

## How do you test the choice before you sign?

Use this short sequence.

1. Confirm the person is an adult and is willing to serve.
2. Ask whether they would accept the duty of keeping your money separate and recording what they spend.
3. Ask what would stop them from acting: distance, a demanding job, health, a conflict with your family.
4. Name a successor who is different from the first agent in at least one way, such as age or location.
5. Tell the family and give the agent a copy of the document or its location.

Choosing someone is a decision you can revisit. If the person later moves, falls ill or loses your trust, sign a new document and revoke the old one, a process we explain in [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).
