Many people assume a power of attorney is a permanent grant, or that it carries on after they die. Neither is quite right. The authority has a life cycle, and knowing where it stops protects both you and the person you name.

This article lists every common way a power of attorney ends, explains how to revoke one so the revocation actually works, and describes what an agent should do at each point. For the document's basic purpose, see our overview of [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) and the guide to the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

## What are the ways a power of attorney ends?

The law generally recognizes the following endings. The details differ by state, and the document's own terms can add to the list.

| Event | Effect |
|---|---|
| Principal dies | Ends immediately |
| Principal revokes while competent | Ends once revocation is effective |
| Stated expiration date or event occurs | Ends on that date or event |
| Purpose is accomplished (a limited power for one transaction) | Ends when the task is done |
| Agent dies, resigns or is incapacitated | Ends for that agent; a successor may step in |
| Court terminates or suspends the power | Ends or pauses as ordered |
| Principal's incapacity (non-durable power only) | Ends |
| Divorce, if the agent was the principal's spouse | Ends in many states |
| Principal creates a new document that replaces it | Ends if the new one says so |

## Does a power of attorney end at death?

Yes. Death of the principal terminates the power of attorney, durable or not, at once. This surprises families. After a parent dies, the child who held the power of attorney cannot continue using it to close accounts, sell the house or pay the funeral home from the parent's account.

At that point authority passes to the [executor](/glossary/executor) named in the will after a court appoints them, or to the successor trustee of a living trust. Until someone has that authority, the accounts generally stay frozen. The sequence is covered in [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) and our [after a death checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

The agent's duties do not vanish entirely. The agent still must account for what they did, may owe records to the executor, and can be asked to return any funds they had no right to keep. Many states protect an agent who acts in good faith before learning of the death.

## Can you revoke a power of attorney?

You can revoke a power of attorney at any time while you have capacity. Capacity is the key limit: once you lack it, you cannot revoke, and the agent's authority may continue. That is one reason to choose the agent carefully at the start.

To make a revocation effective, follow these steps.

1. **Put it in writing.** An oral revocation may be valid in theory but is hard to prove.
2. **Sign and, ideally, notarize it.** Notarization is not always required but helps banks accept it.
3. **Deliver it to the agent,** and keep proof of delivery, such as certified mail or a signed receipt.
4. **Notify every institution** that has the old document on file: banks, brokerages, insurers, the IRS if a Form 2848 was filed, and your doctors if the document touched benefits.
5. **Record it** in the county land records if the power of attorney was used to sell or mortgage real estate.
6. **Retrieve or destroy copies,** and consider asking a court to confirm revocation if the agent refuses to cooperate.
7. **Sign a new document** naming a different agent, and state that it revokes all earlier ones.

An agent or institution that acts in good faith without actual knowledge of the revocation is generally protected in many states. That makes notice the most important step.

## What happens if the principal becomes incapacitated?

It depends on whether the power is durable. A [durable power of attorney](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney) continues through [incapacity](/glossary/incapacity). That is its point. An old-fashioned or non-durable power ends when the principal loses capacity, which is when most families need it. If you have a form signed many years ago, check that it says it survives disability. Many states now treat powers as durable by default, but not all do.

A springing power activates only on incapacity; see [springing vs. immediate](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) for how that trigger operates, and whether it can turn back off if you recover.

## Does divorce end a power of attorney?

In many states, yes. Under the Uniform Power of Attorney Act and the laws of several states, a divorce or annulment automatically terminates a former spouse's authority as agent, unless the document says otherwise. Other states do not have a clear rule. Do not rely on automatic termination.

If you divorce, take these steps right away:

- Sign a new power of attorney and healthcare directive naming someone else
- Deliver written revocations to the former spouse and to your institutions
- Review beneficiary designations and your will, which may also be affected by divorce depending on state law
- Update joint accounts and any trust

Our [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) article walks through the whole checklist.

## What if the agent can no longer serve?

An agent's authority ends when they die, resign or become unable to serve, and if the agent was named as a specific person, no one else automatically takes the role. That is why naming successors in the document matters. Without a successor, your family is back to considering a court-appointed conservator. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

A resignation should also follow a process. In many states an agent who wants to quit must give written notice to the principal, or if the principal lacks capacity, to a co-agent, a successor agent or another interested person. An agent who simply walks away risks leaving the principal's bills unpaid and may still face claims.

## Can a court end a power of attorney?

Yes. A court can terminate or limit an agent's powers in response to a petition from an interested person, such as a relative, an adult protective services agency or the principal. Typical grounds include breach of fiduciary duty, abuse or exploitation, mismanagement, lack of capacity when the document was signed, or fraud. A court may also order accountings. Judges often appoint a conservator at the same time.

If you believe an agent is stealing or misusing funds, speak with an attorney quickly. Also see our [elder financial abuse guide](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) for warning signs and reporting options.

## What if the document has an expiration date?

Some people sign a power of attorney that expires on a date or after an event, such as "until I return from my overseas posting," or "until the sale of the property closes." The power ends exactly as written. Absent a stated date, a power of attorney does not expire from age alone. Banks may still balk at a document signed decades ago, so a refresh every several years is wise.

## What should the agent do when the power ends?

An agent should stop acting on the document, return control to the principal or successor, and keep records. Specific steps:

1. Stop using the document for any transaction.
2. Notify institutions and anyone who relied on it.
3. Prepare a final accounting of income, expenses and assets held.
4. Hand over property and records to the principal, successor agent, conservator or executor.
5. Keep your own records for several years in case of a challenge.

## What should you do now?

Check your own documents. Find the original, confirm the language about durability, read the successor-agent clause, and decide whether any of it should change after a move, divorce or death in the family.
