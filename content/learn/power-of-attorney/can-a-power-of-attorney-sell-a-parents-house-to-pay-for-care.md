A parent moves into memory care, the monthly bill arrives, and the house sits empty. If you hold the power of attorney, selling it can feel like the obvious step. Often it is. But the sale decision, the paperwork and what you do with the money are three separate problems, and each has a way to go wrong.

This page covers when an agent can sell, how the sale works, and the Medicaid and tax questions that should come before a listing agreement. It is general education for Illinois and New Jersey families, not legal advice.

## Can a power of attorney sell a house to pay for a parent's care?

Yes, when the document gives the agent authority over real estate and the sale is for the parent's benefit. Most financial powers of attorney include real estate, but read yours. In an Illinois statutory short form the real estate category is one of the powers the parent initials, so an uninitialed or crossed-out line means no authority. New Jersey documents are drafted individually, and the Revised Durable Power of Attorney Act leaves gifts and similar sensitive powers to what the document says.

The agent must also act as a fiduciary. That means the sale price should be fair, the reason should be the parent's needs, and the agent should avoid conflicts. Our overview of [power of attorney and real estate](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate) covers the closing mechanics, such as the title company's review and recording the document. This page is about the care decision.

## When does selling make sense, and when does it not?

Selling makes sense when the house will not be lived in again, the care costs exceed the parent's income, and the house is the main asset left to pay for them. It makes less sense in these situations:

- **A spouse still lives there.** Medicaid generally protects the home while a spouse lives in it, and forcing a sale can leave the healthy spouse without a home. See [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples).
- **The parent is likely to return.** Short rehab stays often end with the parent going home.
- **A child provided care for two years or more.** Some Medicaid rules allow a transfer of the home to a caregiver child without penalty, but the facts are strict. See [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).
- **The house is the only asset and Medicaid will pay anyway.** In that case a sale only raises the bill the parent pays privately before eligibility.

Medicaid may recover from the house after death through estate recovery, so "keeping it safe" is never certain. Our piece on [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) explains the limits.

## What are the steps if you decide to sell?

1. **Confirm the power is valid and still in effect.** The parent must be alive, not have revoked the document, and the agent's authority must not be limited to "bank matters." If a bank or title company wants a recent copy or a certificate, ask early.
2. **Check the title.** Whose names are on the deed? A house owned with a sibling, or held in a trust, changes who signs. If the house is in a living trust, the trustee may sell without the power of attorney at all.
3. **Order an appraisal.** An independent appraisal protects you if a sibling later says the price was too low.
4. **List with a broker and accept a fair offer.** Avoid a quick sale to an investor friend of the family at a discount.
5. **Sign as the parent's agent.** The signature block reads something like "Dana Reyes, as attorney-in-fact for Frank Reyes," and the title company will record the power of attorney with the deed.
6. **Deposit the proceeds in an account titled in the parent's name.** Not yours, not a shared family account.
7. **Keep records.** A ledger of every payment, plus the settlement statement, is what you hand over if anyone asks for an accounting.

The [agent's duties](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do) do not stop after closing. You are now managing a larger cash balance.

## What about Medicaid, gifts and the look-back?

The sale itself is not a gift, but what you do afterwards can be. Proceeds that go to the parent's care at fair rates are spent properly. Proceeds that go to the agent, or to children as "early inheritance," are a transfer. Most states look back five years for transfers made for less than fair value before a Medicaid application, and the penalty is a period of ineligibility. Our articles on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) and [half-a-loaf gifting](/learn/elder-care/half-a-loaf-gifting-and-how-long-the-medicaid-penalty-lasts) show how that penalty is counted.

An agent also cannot give gifts unless the document says so. Under the Illinois statute, gifts and changes of beneficiary are off the table without express authority, and the same prudent rule applies in New Jersey. See [can a power of attorney make gifts](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts).

## What about taxes on the sale?

Selling a home during life can produce a taxable gain, unlike keeping it until death, when most inherited assets receive a step-up in basis. A parent who has owned and lived in the house for at least two of the last five years can usually exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. Time in a care facility can affect the use test in some cases, so ask the tax preparer before assuming. Our guide to [capital gains when you sell a home to pay for nursing home care](/learn/elder-care/capital-gains-when-you-sell-a-home-to-pay-for-nursing-home-care) goes further.

## A worked example

This is an illustration with invented numbers. Dana is her father Frank's agent. Frank, 86, is in memory care at $9,500 a month, and his income covers about $3,900 of it. The shortfall is $5,600 a month. His house appraises at $340,000, and his mortgage is paid off. Frank has $45,000 in savings.

Dana sells the house for $335,000 after two months on the market. After commission and closing costs the net is around $310,000. She deposits it in an account in Frank's name. At $5,600 a month the cash and savings last roughly five years, a little over 63 months, before Frank would be Medicaid-eligible. Dana's brother Greg asks why they are not using the money to pay Greg back for the roof he fixed. Dana writes down the repair, collects the receipt, and gets a lawyer's advice. A reimbursement for a documented expense is different from a gift, but she is cautious and pays it only because the receipt exists.

Dana also sent Greg and their sister Joan copies of the closing statement and the first quarterly ledger. Nobody has asked for more.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- Selling to a relative at a "family price."
- Letting the sale proceeds sit in a joint account that also holds the agent's own money.
- Skipping the appraisal.
- Selling before checking whether a trust, a spouse or a caregiver child changes the answer.
- Ignoring the agent's own liability. If a sibling believes the sale was unfair, the first step is an accounting. See [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

If you are not sure whether to sell, speak with an elder law attorney before signing a listing agreement. The decision can usually wait a few weeks, and the cost of getting it wrong is large.
