You can find a power of attorney form in about thirty seconds. Some are free, some cost a small fee and some are drawn on a legal pad by a worried daughter at a kitchen table. The real question is whether the thing will hold up when a bank teller, a county recorder or a hospital billing office looks at it.

This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), explains what makes a power of attorney valid, where free and handwritten versions go wrong, and how to sign one remotely if you cannot get to a notary.

## The short version

- A power of attorney is valid because of how it is made, not where it came from.
- A good free form, filled in carefully and signed correctly, can do the job.
- A handwritten or verbal one is a poor bet, even in states that might allow it.
- A valid document can still be refused by an institution. Plan for that.
- If the stakes are high, such as a house or a large portfolio, pay for a review.

## What makes a power of attorney valid?

Four things, in plain terms.

1. **A capable signer.** You must understand what you are signing and who you are naming. Capacity matters most of all when the signer is older or ill.
2. **A clear grant of authority.** The document must name the principal and agent and say what the agent may do.
3. **Your state's execution rules.** That usually means a notary acknowledgment, and in some states also one or two witnesses. Our article on [notarization and witnesses](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-a-power-of-attorney-need-to-be-notarized) lists the common patterns.
4. **A durability statement,** if you want it to survive incapacity. Without it, the document may end at the moment you most need it. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

Nothing in that list requires an attorney. Nothing requires an expensive form either. That is why a free form is not automatically bad.

## Are free online forms valid?

They are valid when they are right for your state and you complete them correctly. In practice they fail in quiet ways.

**The form is out of date or from the wrong state.** Statutes change. A form written for one state may leave out a witness requirement that another demands, and the document is then defective.

**It does not cover what the agent needs.** Many generic forms are silent on gifts, trusts, digital accounts, retirement plans and tax matters. An agent who needs to move assets into a Medicaid-compliant arrangement, for example, will find the form gives them no authority to do so.

**It has no successor agent.** The form names one person. If that person dies, resigns or falls ill, you are back to square one, and perhaps a court.

**It is signed wrong.** Leaving off a witness, using a notary outside their commission, or signing in the wrong order are the common mistakes.

Consider Rick, 68, who downloads a free form and names his wife Carla as agent. They sign it at the kitchen table with no notary, because the website said "valid in all 50 states." Two years later Rick has a stroke. The bank will not honor the form and Carla ends up asking a court to appoint her guardian, a process that takes months and costs thousands. A fifteen-minute notary visit would have prevented it.

None of this means free forms are a trap. Some state bar associations and courts publish statutory forms, and those are usually sound. Our comparison of [lawyer, online and DIY costs](/learn/power-of-attorney/how-much-does-a-power-of-attorney-cost) goes through the price difference honestly.

## Is a handwritten power of attorney valid?

It can be, in states that do not insist on a particular format, as long as it is signed and acknowledged as the statute requires. A handwritten will gets special treatment in some states, as the [holographic will](/glossary/holographic-will) entry explains, but a power of attorney does not have a general equivalent. Treat any "handwritten exception" for powers of attorney as unlikely.

The practical problems are bigger than the legal ones:

- Banks tend to want their own forms or a professionally drafted document.
- A short handwritten page rarely includes durability language.
- It invites challenges. A relative who dislikes the choice of agent will point out every gap.
- Real estate documents require a recordable power of attorney, with a proper notary block, which a handwritten page almost never has.

If you must write something in an emergency, write it clearly, sign it before a notary and then replace it quickly with a proper document.

## Can I rely on a verbal power of attorney?

No. Spoken promises cannot be shown to a bank or a county recorder, and they give no one legal authority. Families sometimes believe that because Mom said "you can handle my accounts," the agent is covered. They are not. A joint account or a signed bank form may serve as a stopgap, but those carry their own risks, which we cover in [getting power of attorney when someone cannot sign](/learn/power-of-attorney/getting-power-of-attorney-when-someone-cannot-sign).

## Can I get a power of attorney online and sign remotely?

Yes, with conditions. There are really two steps. Completing the form online is easy. Signing it validly is the part to check.

| Signing method | Typical requirement | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| In person with a notary | Notary acknowledgment; sometimes witnesses | Notary must be commissioned in the state |
| Remote online notary (video) | Live video, identity checks, electronic record | Not available in every state; some limit document types |
| Mobile notary at your home | Same as in person | Fee for travel |
| Electronic signature with no notary | Rarely enough on its own | Banks and recorders may reject it |

If you live in a state that allows remote notarization, the process is usually: upload the unsigned document, verify your identity with ID and a few questions, join a video call and sign while the notary watches. Some states also require witnesses to be present on the call. Our broader guide to [signing estate planning documents electronically](/learn/basics/sign-estate-planning-documents-electronically) goes into the rules and when to avoid them.

If the power of attorney will be used for real estate in a different state, check that state's recording rules too. Our article on [real estate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate) explains why title companies are strict.

## How to check a form before you sign

1. Confirm the form is for your state and current.
2. Read every power. Strike or add what does not fit.
3. Name at least one successor agent.
4. Make sure it says it survives incapacity if you want that.
5. Sign with the exact notary or witnesses your state requires.
6. Call your bank and ask if it will accept the form before you need it.
7. Store the original and give your agent a copy.

If your situation includes a trust, a business, rental property or Medicaid planning, the form is probably not enough. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) can help you see what else belongs in your plan, and our piece on [estate plans without a lawyer](/learn/basics/making-an-estate-plan-without-a-lawyer) is blunt about where DIY works and where it does not.

## How state law changes this

State statutes control who must witness, whether a notary alone is sufficient, whether a statutory form is mandatory, and whether remote notarization is available. Some states want witnesses on top of the notary, while others need only the notary. Our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides cover those two states. Always check your own state's rule.
