A call comes in that your father collapsed at work and is unconscious. You have his power of attorney in a folder at home, so you drive to the hospital with it. A nurse reads the first page and says, kindly, that it covers his finances only. Someone else has to consent to the procedure.

That moment surprises many families, and it is why this question gets asked so often. This page explains what a financial power of attorney does and does not reach, which document handles medical choices in Illinois and New Jersey, and what to do right now if you hold the wrong one. Our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney) covers the wider picture.

## The short version

- A financial or "property" power of attorney covers money, accounts, bills and real estate.
- Medical consent needs a health care power of attorney, a proxy directive, or a statutory surrogate.
- A financial agent can pay the hospital, but cannot tell the hospital what to do.
- If a person is already unable to sign anything, you work with the surrogate rules and, if needed, a court.

## Can a power of attorney make medical decisions at all?

It can, but only if the document is written for health care. The words "power of attorney" describe a method, not a subject. You can give someone authority over your money, over your medical care, or over both, and the two jobs are created by different forms with different signing rules.

In Illinois the two are separate statutory forms under the same act. The property form is described in our piece on the [Illinois statutory power of attorney for property](/learn/illinois/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property): one witness plus a notary. The health care form is covered in [Illinois healthcare power of attorney, living will and POLST](/learn/illinois/illinois-healthcare-power-of-attorney): one adult witness who is not the agent or the attending physician, and no notary.

New Jersey draws the same line. Its Revised Durable Power of Attorney Act is about property, and the [New Jersey advance directive](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-advance-directives) is where you name a health care representative, with two adult witnesses or a notary. See [New Jersey power of attorney: signing, durability and agent duties](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-power-of-attorney) for the property side.

## What can a financial agent do around a medical crisis?

Plenty, and it matters. Hospital stays produce bills, insurance disputes and sometimes a need to move money fast. A financial agent with a durable document can:

- Pay hospital, rehab and pharmacy bills from the principal's accounts.
- Talk to the insurer or Medicare plan about claims and appeals.
- Handle the paperwork for disability benefits or long-term care insurance.
- Keep the mortgage, utilities and taxes current while the principal is in care.
- Apply for public benefits, which is a financial and not a medical act. Our guide to [applying for Medicaid for a parent](/learn/elder-care/how-to-apply-for-medicaid-for-a-parent) explains that process.

What the agent cannot do is consent to surgery, refuse a ventilator, sign a DNR, or approve hospice. Those are treatment choices, and they are not property.

## Who decides if there is no health care agent?

Your state's surrogate rules decide. Illinois sets a ranked list in the Health Care Surrogate Act (755 ILCS 40/25): guardian of the person, then spouse, adult child, parent, adult sibling, adult grandchild and close friend. The details, including how disagreements among adult children are settled, are in [who makes medical decisions in Illinois if you have no directive](/learn/illinois/illinois-health-care-surrogate-act-who-decides).

In New Jersey, hospitals generally work with the closest available family, and a dispute can end up in court. That is why our attorneys tell New Jersey clients to sign a proxy directive rather than rely on practice. Our explainer on [what happens without an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive) covers the general picture.

Notice what this means for the financial agent. Being the "power of attorney" gives you no rank on the surrogate list. A daughter who holds a property power of attorney but is estranged from the spouse may sit lower than the spouse for purposes of consent. A son who is a financial agent but has a sister who is the adult child present at the bedside may find the hospital turning to her. The financial form does not change that.

## What if the person is in a coma and the paperwork is wrong?

Start with the hospital's social worker or case manager, not the front desk. Ask three questions in this order.

1. **Is there a health care directive in the chart?** Sometimes a form was signed at a past admission or at a primary care office.
2. **Who does the hospital treat as the legal surrogate under state law?** In Illinois it is the first available person on the statutory list.
3. **Who has the financial power of attorney, and has the hospital seen it?** Hand over a copy so billing questions stop going to the wrong person.

If no one qualifies, or the family splits, a guardian of the person may be needed. That is a court process, and our comparison of [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains how long it takes and what it costs the family in control. The article on [getting power of attorney when someone cannot sign](/learn/power-of-attorney/getting-power-of-attorney-when-someone-cannot-sign) lays out the same options for a person who is unconscious.

### A short example

Marcus, 61, has a durable financial power of attorney naming his wife Dana. Nobody has ever asked him to sign a health care form. He has a stroke and cannot communicate. Dana pays the deductible and calls the insurer the same day, which her power of attorney supports. When the neurologist wants consent for a procedure, the hospital relies on Dana because she is Marcus's spouse and, in Illinois, ranks first among the available people on the statutory list.

Now change one fact. Marcus and Dana are separated, and his adult daughter Priya is at the bedside. Dana still holds the financial form. Priya may have no document at all. The consent question turns on state rules, not on who holds the financial paper. A health care power of attorney naming Priya would have settled it in an afternoon.

## How do you fix it before anything happens?

Sign the second document. It takes a short appointment, and it is the easiest repair in estate planning.

Many people name the same person for both roles, then list a backup for each. Some deliberately split the roles: a son who is an accountant handles money, a daughter who is a nurse handles medical choices. Either works, provided each agent knows the other exists. We cover the choice in [how to choose your agent under a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) and [who should be your healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/who-should-be-your-healthcare-agent).

Add a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) too. Privacy rules can block even a named agent from records until a doctor confirms incapacity, and a standing release removes that delay. Families who skip it often meet the problem described in [what to do when an adult child has a medical emergency and no HIPAA form](/learn/what-if/adult-child-medical-emergency-no-hipaa).

### A signing checklist

- Financial power of attorney signed, notarized and, in Illinois, witnessed.
- Health care power of attorney or proxy directive signed under the rules for your state.
- A living will, if you want to state end-of-life choices yourself.
- A HIPAA release naming both agents.
- Copies with each agent, the primary doctor and the hospital you use most.

Our article on [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) lists other limits that surprise families, including changes to a will.

## How state law changes this

States differ in the name of the health care document, the number of witnesses, whether a notary is allowed or required, and the default surrogate list. Some forms combine finances and health in one packet, and a few states allow a single instrument to carry both. Read the title and the scope paragraph rather than assuming.
