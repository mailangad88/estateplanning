Rosa signed a power of attorney in Ohio twenty years ago. Now she lives in Arizona, her daughter lives in Georgia, and she owns a small rental house in Michigan. If Rosa becomes unable to manage money, who will accept that Ohio form, and where?

This page explains how a power of attorney crosses state lines, what to do after a move, why real estate is the hard part, and how things change when a different country is involved.

## The short version

This is one of the narrower questions in our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), and the answers below assume an ordinary durable financial document.

- Legal validity and practical acceptance are two separate questions. The first is usually yes. The second depends on the person at the bank counter.
- A move does not cancel the document. It is still a good time to update it.
- Property in another state, especially land, calls for extra attention.
- Anything crossing a national border needs formalities and local advice.

## Is a power of attorney valid in another state?

In most cases, yes. A power of attorney is usually judged by the law of the place where it was signed. Many states have adopted some version of the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which says an out-of-state document is valid if it met the requirements where it was signed, and a number of other states reach the same result by their own statutes. Check your state's rule if the document is old or unusual.

That is the legal answer. The practical answer is messier. A bank teller in Arizona has probably never seen an Ohio form and may be unsure what it should say. Our page on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) walks through that standoff, including the legal pressure you can put on an institution that refuses without a good reason.

Three things make a document travel better:

1. It was signed before a notary, even where your home state does not require it.
2. It is clearly marked durable, so it survives incapacity.
3. It is not decades old. Banks distrust old paper for the simple reason that they cannot tell whether it has been revoked.

The notary point is covered at length in [does a power of attorney need to be notarized](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-a-power-of-attorney-need-to-be-notarized).

## What happens when you move to a new state?

Your old document does not expire. Most families do nothing at all, and the arrangement keeps working until someone tries to use it. The trouble is that no one tries to use it until a crisis, which is the wrong time to find out the new bank wants a particular certification.

A sensible plan after a move:

1. Find the original and confirm who your named agent and successor are.
2. Ask whether the agent is still the right person. Divorce, death and distance change that.
3. Have an attorney in your new state read the document for gaps, such as missing powers for a retirement account or for gifts.
4. If it needs changes, sign a new one and revoke the old one in writing, then tell the banks and brokerages that have the old copy.
5. Sign a new [healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states) too. Hospitals follow their own state's forms more comfortably.

Moves are one of the most common reasons a plan quietly goes out of date. We list the others in [moved states and the plan was not updated](/learn/what-if/moved-states-plan-not-updated).

## Can my agent live in another state?

Yes. Nothing requires the agent and principal to live in the same state or even the same country. Plenty of families name the one child who is best with money, even if that child lives a plane ride away.

The cost is logistics. An agent in Seattle may struggle to meet a branch manager in Tampa, collect mail, or inspect a house with a leaking roof. Online banking helps, and a good document lets the agent hire local help and pay for it. A named successor in the same city is a cheap fix. Our article on [choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers the trade-offs.

## Why is real estate in another state harder?

Because a deed is recorded in the county where the land sits, and the county follows its own state's rules. A power of attorney used to sign a deed generally has to be notarized in a form that qualifies for recording, and many counties want the power of attorney itself recorded along with the deed. A title company may ask for a lawyer's opinion that the document is valid.

| Situation | What can go wrong | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Agent signs a deed in another state | County refuses an out-of-state form | Notarized, recordable document; local attorney review |
| Agent refinances or sells a rental | Lender wants its own form | Ask early what the lender requires |
| Agent closes an account at a local bank | Teller does not know the form | Send the bank's legal department a copy before the day |
| Agent files taxes in two states | State agency wants its own form | Use each state's tax authorization |

Land in a second state can also mean a second probate after death. We explain that in [out of state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property). See also [power of attorney and real estate](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate).

## What if the power of attorney is for use in another country?

The rules are the other country's, so the first step is to ask a lawyer there. There is no single rule that makes a state-signed power of attorney valid abroad. In general, a foreign office will want:

- The document signed before a notary.
- An apostille from the secretary of state, for countries that belong to the Hague Apostille Convention. Other countries may want authentication through their consulate.
- A translation into the local language by a certified translator.
- Sometimes, a power of attorney in that country's own form, signed before a local notary.

The same steps run in reverse for a power of attorney signed overseas and used here. An American title company or brokerage will want to see that the document was valid where it was signed, translated into English, and authenticated. Many will still ask the principal to sign their own form. If you live abroad part of the year, have your attorney sign a US document and, if you own property abroad, a separate local one. The same idea applies to wills; see [moving states or living abroad](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad).

A practical note for families with a foreign-born parent: the parent's home-country document may be perfectly valid there and still be ignored by an American bank. Do not wait for a crisis to find out. Ask the institution what it accepts, and if the answer is its own form, have the parent sign it while able.

## What if the principal is already incapacitated and the form is the wrong one?

Then the options narrow. A person who cannot sign cannot make a new power of attorney. The family may have to ask a court for guardianship or conservatorship, which is slower and public. Some institutions will accept an out-of-state document after a letter from an attorney, and some will not. See [getting power of attorney when someone cannot sign](/learn/power-of-attorney/getting-power-of-attorney-when-someone-cannot-sign) and [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).
