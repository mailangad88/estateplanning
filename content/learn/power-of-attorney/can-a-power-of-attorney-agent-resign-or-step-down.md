Taking on a power of attorney sounds like a favor. A year or two in, it can feel like a second job. The paperwork is constant, a sibling is questioning every check, and your own health or family has changed. People ask whether they can simply walk away.

This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), explains how an agent resigns, what the resignation letter should say, what you still owe the person you served, and what happens next.

## The short version

- You can resign. Serving as an agent is voluntary.
- Do it in writing, and send it to the right people.
- Your duty to hand over records and property continues after you step down.
- A named successor usually steps in. Without one, a court may have to.
- Never quietly stop. Unpaid bills and missed deadlines can still be your problem.

## Can an agent resign?

Yes. Even after you accept the role by acting, you can generally end it. The Uniform Power of Attorney Act, which many states have adopted in some form, lets an agent resign by giving notice to the principal. Where the principal is incapacitated, notice goes to a guardian if one exists, or otherwise to a co-agent or the next successor agent. Some states have their own variation, and the document itself may add steps, such as notice to a named family member.

Before you write anything, read the document for any resignation clause. Some set a notice period or name who must receive notice. Our article on [what an agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do) describes the duties you are leaving behind.

## When does it make sense to resign?

Common reasons are:

- Your health has declined, or you are caring for someone else.
- You live too far away to manage the principal's affairs.
- A family conflict makes the role unworkable.
- You feel out of your depth, for example with a business or rental property.
- A conflict of interest has arisen, such as a dispute with the principal.

Say Tom, 61, is his mother's agent. Her finances were simple until she sold the family home and bought rental property. Tom is now filing tax paperwork he does not understand and her accountant is asking questions he cannot answer. He can resign, and name a successor if the document allows, or he can stay and hire professionals paid from his mother's funds. Many agents choose the second route. It is a reasonable alternative, and our attorneys often suggest it before a resignation.

## How do I resign as power of attorney?

Follow these steps.

1. **Read the document** for any resignation clause or named successor.
2. **Write a resignation letter.** Keep it short: the principal's name, your name, the date of the power of attorney, a statement that you resign effective on a date, and your signature.
3. **Deliver it properly.** Give it to the principal if they can understand it. Otherwise deliver to the guardian, co-agent or first successor. Keep proof, such as certified mail or a signed receipt.
4. **Notify third parties.** Tell banks, the brokerage, the pension administrator, the landlord and other places you have dealt with as agent. Ask them to note your resignation.
5. **Prepare an accounting.** List what came in, what went out and what remains, up to the resignation date.
6. **Hand over records and assets.** Give bank statements, receipts, keys, passwords you were given and any property you hold to the person taking over.
7. **Keep your own file** for several years, in case questions come up.

Notarizing the letter is not always required, but it makes it harder for anyone to say it was not signed. Banks also tend to trust a notarized notice more.

## What do you still owe after you resign?

Resigning ends your authority from that date. It does not erase your history as a fiduciary. You may still be asked to explain transactions you made, and if you made a mistake or took something improperly, resignation does not shield you. Our guide to [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) describes what happens when an agent is accused of misuse.

You also should not leave things half done. Before the effective date, pay any bills that are due, make sure automatic payments are in order, and note any upcoming tax deadlines for the successor. If you are a co-agent, the other agent continues unless the document says both must act.

## Who takes over when an agent resigns or dies?

| Situation | Who steps in | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| Agent resigns, successor named | The first successor | Notice and sometimes a certification |
| Agent resigns, co-agent remains | The co-agent, if the document allows | Check wording for "jointly" or "separately" |
| Agent dies, successor named | The first successor | Death certificate; successor's affidavit |
| No successor, principal capable | Principal signs a new document | A visit to an attorney and notary |
| No successor, principal incapacitated | Court-appointed guardian or conservator | Petition, hearing, cost and delay |

That last row is the worst outcome, and it is avoidable. The cheapest fix is naming a successor in the first place. Our article on [co-agents and successor agents](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-name-more-than-one-power-of-attorney-agent) shows how, and [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) lists the other events that bring a document to a close, such as divorce from a spouse named as agent.

If you are the person who needs a new agent right now, read [what happens when your agent is unavailable](/learn/what-if/power-of-attorney-agent-unavailable).

## How to choose a backup agent

If you are the principal, a thoughtful backup list prevents the mess above. Choose someone who:

1. Is likely to outlive and out-last the first agent, so not a person of the same age and health.
2. Will not clash with the first agent. Pick people who can hand off the job calmly.
3. Is comfortable with money, or willing to hire help.
4. Knows your wishes. Talk to them before you sign.

Some families name a trusted professional or a bank trust department as the final backup. Fees apply, but it ensures someone is available. Our guide to [choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers the qualities to look for.

## Common mistakes when stepping down

- Telling the family but not writing it down.
- Leaving the bank unaware, so the bank keeps honoring a signature that no longer works.
- Walking away with the original document or the principal's records.
- Resigning in anger, by text message, with no handover.
- Assuming that resigning as agent also ends a caregiving role, or the reverse.

## How state law changes this

Notice rules, who receives it and whether a court must approve a resignation differ by state. Some statutes are detailed; others say very little. If your document came from another state or you are unsure which law applies, ask before you send the letter. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) can help you find the right starting point.
