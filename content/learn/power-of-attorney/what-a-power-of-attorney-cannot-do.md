A common worry is that a person holding a power of attorney could quietly rewrite a will, give property to themselves or keep going after the principal dies. The law blocks most of that, though not everything, and it helps to know where the lines run.

This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), answers the question in plain terms: what a power of attorney cannot do, what it can do only with special wording, and what to do if an agent steps over the line.

## The short version

- An agent cannot sign, change or revoke a will for you.
- Authority ends at death. After that the executor or successor trustee is in charge.
- An agent cannot use your money for themselves, whatever the document says in broad terms.
- Healthcare decisions require a healthcare agent, a different document.
- Gifts, beneficiary changes and trust changes are the risky areas. Read your document for how it handles them.

## Can a power of attorney change a will?

No. A will is a personal act. In every state the person making the will, the testator, must sign it with the required witnesses, and must have the mental capacity to understand what they are signing. An agent has no authority to do that for them. The same is true of a codicil, which is an amendment to a will.

That is why the question becomes sharp when someone loses capacity. If Hal, 80, has advanced dementia and his will leaves everything to his three children equally, nobody can change it now. A court may allow limited changes in unusual cases, but ordinary agents cannot. Our article on [last-minute wills and capacity](/learn/what-if/too-late-for-a-will) describes the narrow window in which a person can still sign.

What an agent can affect indirectly is the amount of property that is left. If the agent spends, sells or gives away assets, there may be less to pass under the will. That is a breach if it is for the agent's benefit. See [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

## What else can an agent never do?

| Act | Why not |
|---|---|
| Make or amend a will | Only the person themselves can, with witnesses |
| Act after the principal dies | Authority ends at death |
| Vote, serve on a jury or testify for the principal | Personal civic acts |
| Marry, divorce or sign certain family agreements for the principal | Personal acts, with limited court exceptions |
| Make medical decisions under a financial document | Needs a healthcare agent |
| Override the principal's decisions while the principal has capacity | The principal still controls |
| Use the principal's money for the agent's own purposes | Breach of fiduciary duty |
| Delegate everything to someone else without authority | Many states limit delegation |

## What can an agent do only if the document says so?

Modern statutes, including the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, separate ordinary powers from "hot powers" that can change who inherits or lets the agent benefit. These usually need to be written out, not merely implied by broad language. Typical hot powers:

1. Make gifts above a small amount, or to the agent personally
2. Create, amend or revoke a trust
3. Change survivorship on joint accounts or real estate
4. Change a beneficiary designation on life insurance, an IRA or a 401(k)
5. Disclaim an inheritance
6. Create or change a payable-on-death arrangement

If you want an agent to do any of these, say so clearly and consider limiting it. If you do not, make sure the document does not hand it over by accident. This is one of the main reasons to have an attorney review a form from the internet. For the normal list of powers, see our guide to the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

## Can a power of attorney act after death?

No. The moment the principal dies, the document stops working, even a durable one. The executor named in the will takes over after being appointed, or the successor trustee takes over a trust. In the meantime accounts stay frozen. We walk through the aftermath in [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) and [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

A related point: a power of attorney does not make someone an executor. The roles are different, and one person can hold both. Our article on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) shows the other side of the picture, the assets a will does not control.

## What about healthcare decisions?

A financial agent usually cannot consent to surgery, refuse treatment or choose a nursing home, though they can pay for them. Those choices belong to a healthcare agent under a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney). Many people name the same person for both, which is fine if you trust them, but it needs two documents. Without the second one, a hospital may turn to a spouse or adult children under state rules, and that can mean delay and disagreement.

## What to do if an agent goes beyond the document

Take it step by step.

1. Read the document and identify the exact power that is in question.
2. Ask the agent, in writing, for an explanation and records.
3. If you are the principal and have capacity, revoke in writing and tell the banks. If not, consider a report to adult protective services.
4. Talk to an attorney about asking a court to review or remove the agent.

Acts beyond the agent's authority can often be unwound, and the agent may owe the principal personally. The first step is to stop further harm.

## What can an agent do that surprises people?

The limits get most of the attention, but families are often surprised in the other direction too. An agent can usually close accounts the principal no longer uses, cancel subscriptions and a gym contract, deal with the IRS if tax powers are granted, apply for benefits such as Social Security or veterans' programs, pay a caregiver and manage rental property. They can also hire professionals, such as an accountant or a home health agency, and pay them from the principal's funds.

What the agent cannot do is treat any of that as a license. Each action has to serve the principal. Take Denise, whose father Walt has dementia. She can pay the assisted living bill and sell his car because he no longer drives. She cannot sell the car to her husband for a low price, and she cannot move Walt's savings into her own account "for safekeeping." The test is always whose benefit the action serves and whether it matches what Walt would have wanted.

Keeping a simple ledger of every transaction makes the difference. A page of receipts, a monthly summary and a separate account titled in the principal's name will answer most questions before anyone asks them.

## How state law changes this

Details such as the list of hot powers, who can challenge an agent and how a gift is treated differ by state. Some states, like [New York](/estate-planning/new-york) and [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois), have their own statutory forms and rules on gifting. If your document came from another state, a local review is worth the time.
