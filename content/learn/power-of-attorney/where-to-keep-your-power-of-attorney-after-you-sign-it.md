You signed the power of attorney at your lawyer's office or kitchen table, the notary stamped it, and now you are holding a thick document. Where does it go? Who needs a copy today, who should not have one, and does anyone have to file it?

Most problems with a power of attorney show up after it is signed, not before. A perfectly drafted document that nobody can find at 2 a.m. in the emergency room is no better than no document. This page, part of our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney), walks through the steps to take in the first week.

## Where should you keep the original power of attorney?

Keep it in a place you can reach and your agent can reach, such as a fireproof document box at home, with a note in your files about where it is. The Illinois statutory form materials suggest keeping the original safe and giving copies to the agent and the bank. The principle holds in New Jersey too.

The common mistake we see is the safe deposit box. A box rented in your name alone can be opened by nobody else without a court order or a signature card, and a family in a crisis may have to petition a court to get to the document that was meant to avoid one. If you like a bank box, add your agent as a joint user, or keep a copy at home.

Other places that work:

- A home file labelled "Legal papers" that your agent has seen.
- With your attorney, who can send certified copies on request.
- A digital scan stored in a shared password manager or secure folder, as a back-up and not the only copy.

Our page on [organizing your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents) shows how to set up one place for all of it.

## Who should get a copy?

Think of three groups.

| Who | Copy? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Your agent | Yes, now (if effective immediately) | They cannot act without the paper |
| Your successor agent | Yes, or at least a notice of where it is | They step in if the first agent cannot serve |
| Your bank, broker, insurer | Yes, and ask them to put it on file | Many institutions review powers in advance |
| Your doctor | Only for a health care form | A financial power is not a medical document |
| Siblings and other family | Optional; tell them it exists | Transparency lowers later disputes |
| Your attorney | Yes | They hold the record of what you signed |

Banks differ. Some will accept the document and note it in your file. Others only react when the agent shows up, and then take days to review it. Asking in advance is the cheapest way to avoid the problems described in [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).

If your power is a springing one that begins on a future event, you may not want the agent to hold the original. Give your agent a copy and keep the original with someone neutral. Our comparison of [springing and immediate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) covers the tradeoff.

## Can a copy be used, or does the agent need the original?

In most dealings a photocopy works. New Jersey's Act says that third parties may rely on a photocopy of the signed original or a certified copy (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.11). Illinois practice is similar, but a financial institution has its own policy, and a title company or county recorder is the most likely to ask for an original or a certified copy.

A certified copy is a copy that your attorney or a notary certifies as true. Ask for two or three at the signing. They cost little then and are hard to get when the principal cannot attend.

## Does a power of attorney need to be recorded?

No, not as a general rule. A financial power of attorney is not filed with a court, and neither state keeps a registry for it. The exception is real estate. If your agent will sign a deed or a mortgage for you, the county recorder or title company often wants the power of attorney recorded with the deed in the county where the land sits. That is a function of the transaction, not of the document. For the closing steps see [power of attorney and real estate](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate).

Recording is public. A recorded power of attorney becomes a public record, so do it when it is needed for a sale, not as a habit.

## Should you name a successor agent?

Yes, almost always. If your agent dies, becomes ill, resigns or divorces you, the power ends in practice unless someone else is named. Without a backup, your family may need a court-appointed guardian, which is the situation you were trying to avoid. Illinois's statutory form has a line for successor agents, and its steps for signing recommend choosing at least one successor. New Jersey allows several agents and spells out what happens to the rest when one drops out, unless the document says otherwise.

How to choose:

1. Pick someone who does not share the first agent's risk. Two people in the same house can be hurt by the same event.
2. Ask them. A surprise appointment is a bad start.
3. Write them in order, so there is no argument about who goes next.
4. Do not name co-agents just to be fair. Sharing authority makes banks and families slower, as our page on [naming more than one agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-name-more-than-one-power-of-attorney-agent) explains.

## What should you tell the agent?

Hand over the document and have one conversation. Cover where your accounts are, who your accountant and insurance agent are, how bills get paid now, what you would never want sold and whether the agent is to be paid. Write the answers on a single page and attach it to your file. It is not legally binding, but it spares the agent from guessing.

## An example

Priya, 71, lives in Naperville, Illinois. She signs a property power of attorney naming her son Dev as agent and her daughter Asha as successor. She gives Dev a copy and tells him the original is in the gray box in her desk. She gives Asha a copy too. She gives her bank a copy and asks them to scan it into her file.

Eight months later, Dev is deployed overseas with the military and cannot be reached. Priya has a stroke. Asha walks into the bank with her copy, the original from the desk and her ID. The bank already has the document on file, and Asha is paying Priya's bills by that afternoon. Nobody filed anything in court.

## A checklist for the first week

1. Make five copies and two certified copies.
2. Put the original in a place the agent knows about.
3. Give the agent and successor their copies.
4. Send a copy to each bank and broker, and ask for confirmation in writing.
5. Note the date of the document in your calendar to review in three years.
6. Update it after a divorce, a death, a move or a major change in your assets.
