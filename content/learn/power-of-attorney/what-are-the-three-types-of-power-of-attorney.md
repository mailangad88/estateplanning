Search for "types of power of attorney" and you will find lists of five, seven or ten. They are not wrong, but they mix up three different questions. How much can the agent do? Does the authority survive your incapacity? When does it start? Once you separate those, the choice gets simple.

The answer most people are looking for is general, limited and durable. This page explains each, shows how they combine, and tells you which one fits common situations. Our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney) covers the wider picture.

## The short version

- **General** means broad financial authority. **Limited** (or special) means one task or one account.
- **Durable** means the authority continues if you become incapacitated. **Non-durable** means it stops.
- **Immediate** and **springing** describe the start date, and either can be paired with any of the above.
- Healthcare decisions belong in a separate [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).
- For most adults planning ahead, the right document is general, durable and effective on signing, with a successor agent named.

## What is a general power of attorney?

A general power of attorney gives your agent wide authority over your money and property. It usually lists categories: banking, investments, real estate, taxes, insurance, retirement accounts, government benefits, business interests, and claims or lawsuits. The agent can handle ordinary financial life on your behalf.

"General" does not mean unlimited. Under most state statutes, certain acts need express language in the document before an agent may do them. Making gifts, changing beneficiary designations, creating or changing a trust and creating survivorship rights in an account are the usual examples. If those powers are not written in, the agent does not have them. We walk through those boundaries in [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do).

Here is where a general document earns its keep. Carl, 68, has a checking account, a brokerage account, a rental condo and a pension. He breaks his hip and spends two months in rehab. His son Devon, as agent, pays the condo's property tax, collects rent and moves money to cover the rehab bills. No single limited document would have covered all of that.

## What is a limited power of attorney?

A limited power of attorney, also called a special power of attorney, hands over one defined job. The job might be signing closing documents on a sale, handling one vehicle title, dealing with one tax matter, or managing one account while you are overseas. The document says what the agent may do, and nothing else.

Limited powers are good for short, bounded tasks. They also tend to be easier for a third party to accept, because the person across the desk can see exactly what is being asked. A title company would rather read "sell the property at 14 Alder Lane" than a forty-page general document.

The weakness is coverage. Priya, 72, signs a limited power of attorney so her daughter can deal with a bank dispute. Six months later she has a stroke and her other accounts are untouched by that paper. Her daughter is back at square one. If you expect this person to be your standby for more than one thing, a limited document is not enough.

## What is a durable power of attorney?

Durable describes a power of attorney that remains effective after you become incapacitated. That is the single most important feature for estate planning. The whole point is to keep working at the moment you cannot sign anything new.

Under the older common-law rule, an agent's authority ended when the principal lost capacity, because an agent's power was only as good as the principal's ability to give instructions. Every state has now changed that by statute, but the details differ. In states that follow the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, a document is durable unless it says otherwise. In some other states, the document must contain specific language such as "this power of attorney is not affected by subsequent incapacity." Using a form from the wrong state, or an old one, can cost you the durability without you noticing. Our article on the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) covers the wording in detail.

A non-durable power of attorney is still useful for one narrow case: a task that will be finished while you are fully competent, like authorizing someone to sign for you at a closing next Tuesday.

## How do the types combine?

The labels are independent switches. Here is how they line up.

| Question | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| How much can the agent do? | General: broad financial authority | Limited: one task or account |
| Does it survive incapacity? | Durable: continues | Non-durable: ends |
| When does it start? | Immediate: on signing | Springing: when a trigger occurs |
| What subject does it cover? | Financial: money and property | Healthcare: medical decisions (separate document) |

So a "general durable immediate power of attorney" is the workhorse, and a "limited non-durable power of attorney for a car sale" is the one-day tool. Between them are combinations like a limited durable power for a parent's Medicaid application, which survives if the parent declines during the process. If the start date is your sticking point, read [springing vs. immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) before you decide.

## What about the healthcare power of attorney?

People sometimes count healthcare as a fourth type, and some lists make it the third. It is better to treat it as a separate document altogether. A healthcare power of attorney, sometimes called a healthcare proxy, names someone to make medical decisions when you cannot. It has its own signing rules, its own default powers, and its own companion paperwork, such as a living will and a [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization).

A financial agent cannot make medical decisions, and a healthcare agent cannot move money. Many families use the same person for both, but the two documents should each name a successor. Our guide to the [advance directive vs. living will vs. healthcare proxy](/learn/healthcare-directives/advance-directive-vs-living-will-vs-healthcare-proxy) explains how the pieces fit.

## Which type should you choose?

Match the document to the job. This walk-through works for most people.

1. **Are you planning ahead with no particular transaction in mind?** Sign a general, durable power of attorney that takes effect now, plus a healthcare power of attorney.
2. **Do you need someone to handle one transaction?** Use a limited power of attorney, and write the end date into it. Examples include a house closing while you are traveling and a vehicle sale for a relative abroad.
3. **Do you want the agent to wait until you are ill?** A springing version is possible, but accept that proving the trigger can slow things down.
4. **Are you managing a parent who is slipping?** If they can still understand what they are signing, get a general durable document now. If they cannot, the options change, as we explain in [a parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).
5. **Do you own real estate or a business?** Ask whether the document names those assets and whether the recorder or a business partner will accept it. See [power of attorney and real estate](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate).

A rule of thumb from practice: when in doubt, choose the broader document and control risk through the choice of agent and through written limits, not by making the document so narrow that it fails.

## How does state law change the picture?

States disagree on the details even though the categories are common. Some publish a statutory short form with set wording. Some require two witnesses in addition to a notary, some require only a notary. Some treat a power of attorney as durable by default and others do not. A few place extra conditions on gifting powers. A document you signed in another state is usually honored when you move, but the receiving institution may still ask questions, so a refresh after a move is sensible.
