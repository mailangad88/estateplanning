Maybe a mother is in a memory care unit and the house needs to be sold to pay for it. Or a father is overseas and the closing is Friday. In both cases someone holds a power of attorney and asks whether that piece of paper is enough to deal with real property.

Often it is (our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney) covers the basics), but real estate is where powers of attorney meet the most scrutiny. A deed is permanent, a title company has its own rules, and a county recorder will not take a document that looks off. This article explains how an agent actually handles a house, what slows a closing down, and where the limits are.

## What can an agent do with real estate?

An agent can do whatever the document grants, within state law and the duties of an agent. Most durable financial powers of attorney list real estate transactions among the agent's powers, and many states supply a default list. The powers usually include:

- Selling, buying or exchanging property
- Signing a deed, a listing agreement and closing documents
- Taking out, refinancing or paying off a mortgage
- Collecting rent and paying property taxes, insurance and repairs
- Dealing with tenants and contractors

Check the wording. Some older forms say only "manage my property" and are silent on selling. A few states require a specific grant, sometimes in the principal's own initials, before an agent can convey real estate. Our overview of the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) shows the usual list of powers.

## How does a closing with a power of attorney work?

The process is more paperwork than ordinary, and it helps to start early.

1. **Send the document to the title company first.** Do this before listing the house or accepting an offer. The company's underwriter decides whether it will insure the sale.
2. **Confirm the principal is alive and the power is still valid.** The title company may ask for the agent's sworn statement to that effect.
3. **Resolve questions about capacity.** If the principal was impaired when the document was signed, buyers' lawyers sometimes ask for medical proof. A power signed with an attorney supervising is easier to defend.
4. **Record the power of attorney.** Many counties require the document to be recorded along with the deed so that the chain of title shows the agent's authority.
5. **Sign correctly.** The agent signs in a form like "Maria Lopez, by Daniel Lopez, her attorney-in-fact," before a notary.
6. **Deposit the proceeds into the principal's account,** not the agent's. Keep a record.

Here is a typical day. Gerald, 71, has Parkinson's and lives with his daughter Anne. He signed a durable power of attorney two years ago after a fall. The house must be sold to cover an assisted living deposit. The title company asks for the recorded power, a certification that Gerald is living, and a letter from his neurologist confirming he understood the document when he signed it two years earlier. Anne gets them in three days, the closing proceeds, and the money goes into Gerald's account.

## What do title companies and recorders look for?

They look for risks that could later cost them money. The table below sets out what is common.

| What they check | Why |
|---|---|
| Notary acknowledgment | Most recorders accept only notarized documents |
| Explicit real estate power | Prevents a later claim that the agent exceeded authority |
| Original or certified copy | Guards against revoked or forged copies |
| Date of signing | Very old documents draw questions |
| Agent's identity and capacity to act | Confirms who signs |
| Spousal signature or consent | Required in some states where a spouse has homestead rights |

There is no shortcut. If the title company rejects a document, you can ask for the specific reason in writing, as you would with a bank. Our piece on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) applies the same logic to real estate.

## Can an agent give the house to themselves or a relative?

This is the biggest risk. A deed to the agent, a sibling or a child is a gift, and the law is wary of gifts by agents. Under most statutes an agent may make gifts only if the document says so, and often only up to the annual exclusion amount. For 2026 the federal gift tax [annual exclusion](/glossary/annual-exclusion) is $19,000 per recipient, which a house far exceeds.

Even where the document allows large gifts, there are three problems.

- **Medicaid.** A transfer for less than fair value can trigger a penalty period under the look-back rule, which is 60 months in most states. See [the Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).
- **Taxes.** Property given during life generally keeps the donor's original cost basis. Property left at death usually gets a stepped-up basis. The [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) can be worth tens of thousands to the recipient.
- **Family fairness.** A deed to one child can make the others sure that undue influence was involved, which leads to a lawsuit after the principal dies.

If an agent is also the person who would benefit, a court will look hard at the transaction. A sale at a fair, documented price is rarely questioned. A deed for "one dollar" nearly always is. Our article on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) explains why that move often backfires even when everyone means well.

## What if the power of attorney does not cover the house?

You have a few options, depending on capacity.

- **If the principal can still sign,** a short limited power of attorney for the specific property, or a deed signed by the principal, fixes it.
- **If the principal's home is already in a revocable trust,** the successor trustee can sell it and the power of attorney is not needed. The trust article [transferring your house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) covers how that works.
- **If neither is possible,** a court-appointed conservator may be the only way to sell. We lay out that path in [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

Do not backdate or alter a document. The cost of that mistake is far larger than the cost of a new one.

## What if the principal dies before closing?

The power of attorney ends at death, and the agent can no longer sign anything. The house passes under the will, the trust or state law, and the executor or successor trustee must act. If a contract was already signed, the buyer's rights are not erased, but the closing now depends on the executor. We cover the timeline in [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end).

## How state law changes this

Each state has its own recording rules, its own acknowledgment requirements and, in some places, special rules for homestead property and spouses. Community property states such as [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) add a spouse's interest to the analysis. Whether the agent can create a trust, whether a gift must be spelled out and whether the document must be recorded before the deed all differ. A local title company and an attorney will know what the county expects.
