Most estate planning is about what happens after you die. A power of attorney is about the harder question: what happens if you are alive but cannot act. A stroke, a car accident, advancing dementia or even a long hospital stay can leave you unable to sign a check, talk to your bank or sell a house, while your family watches the bills pile up.

This guide covers what a power of attorney is, the kinds that exist, how to choose and limit an agent, how the document ends, and what to do when institutions resist it. Each section links to a deeper article. If you are building a full plan, our overview of [what estate planning is](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning) shows where this document fits.

## What is a power of attorney?

A power of attorney is a signed legal document that gives another person authority to act on your behalf. You are the [principal](/glossary/principal). The person you pick is the [agent](/glossary/agent), called an attorney-in-fact in some states. The agent does not need to be a lawyer.

The agent acts as a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary): they must put your interests first, keep records, and stay within the powers you granted. The document is governed by state law, and nearly every state has a statute spelling out what agents can and must do. Many states have adopted some form of the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, and a few provide official statutory forms.

## What are the types of power of attorney?

Powers of attorney come in several types, distinguished by scope, timing and subject.

| Type | What it does | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| General | Broad authority over financial affairs | Long-term planning |
| Limited (special) | One task or time frame | Closing on a home while you are abroad |
| Durable | Survives your incapacity | Almost all estate planning |
| Non-durable | Ends if you become incapacitated | Short-term, specific tasks |
| Springing | Starts on a trigger such as incapacity | People who want a delay |
| Immediate | Effective on signing | Convenience and avoiding delay |
| Healthcare | Medical decisions | Companion to a living will |

Most people need two documents. The first is a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney). The second is a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney), covered in our pillar on [advance healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives).

### Why durability matters

Without durability language, authority ended when the principal lost capacity. Modern forms and many state defaults fix that, but you should still make sure your document says it survives incapacity. Our article on the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) explains the language, the powers an agent typically has, and the limits on those powers, including why gifting authority should be granted with care.

## Why does a power of attorney matter?

Without one, nobody has automatic authority over your individually owned accounts and property. Spouses and adult children often assume otherwise. Banks, brokerages, the IRS and the Social Security Administration will not simply accept a family member's word.

The alternative is a court proceeding. A judge appoints a guardian or conservator after notice, evaluation and a hearing. This is slower, more costly, public, and puts a stranger or a court-chosen relative in charge. We compare the options in [power of attorney vs. guardianship or conservatorship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

Consider Linda, 63, who is single, owns a home and has two adult children. She has a sudden brain bleed. With no power of attorney, her children need a court order to pay her mortgage from her savings account. If she had signed a durable power of attorney naming her daughter, the daughter could have started the same week.

## When should it take effect: now or later?

A power of attorney can be effective immediately or only after a trigger, usually a physician's determination that you are incapacitated. The second is a [springing power of attorney](/glossary/springing-power-of-attorney).

People like springing powers because they delay the agent's authority until it is needed. The cost is practical: someone must prove the trigger, doctors can be reluctant to sign, and banks may resist. An immediate document, with the original held back until needed, is often easier. We lay out the comparison in [springing vs. immediate power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney), including drafting tips for the trigger and a HIPAA authorization to support it.

If a parent already has dementia and never signed anything, read [my parent has dementia and no power of attorney: what now?](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney) for the options.

## How do you choose an agent?

Choosing an agent is the most important decision in the process. The best agent is honest, organized, willing, available and able to stand up to pressure. The best title is not necessarily the oldest child, and not necessarily the child who lives closest or who asks for the job.

Key points to consider:

1. Pick someone you would trust with your bank account and your secrets.
2. Name at least one successor.
3. Be careful with co-agents, who can deadlock.
4. Consider a professional fiduciary if no relative fits.
5. Talk to the person before you name them, and tell your family why.
6. Build safeguards, such as accountings, limits on gifts and a ban on self-dealing.

Our guide to [choosing an agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) includes a comparison of common choices and a list of red flags. Because elder financial abuse is often committed by someone with access, the article also points to our [elder financial abuse guide](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse).

## What powers should the document include?

A well-drafted document lists the categories of authority and then addresses special powers one by one. Common categories include banking, investments, real estate, taxes, retirement accounts, benefits, insurance, business operations, claims and litigation, and digital assets.

Some powers are risky and, under most state statutes, must be granted expressly. These include making gifts, creating or funding trusts, changing beneficiary designations, creating joint accounts and delegating authority. Consider each deliberately. For example, a person planning for long-term care might allow limited gifts consistent with a [Medicaid plan](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning); a person without such plans may prefer to limit gifts to the annual exclusion, which is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, or to bar them altogether.

A trust changes the picture. Assets held in a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) are managed by the trustee, so the power of attorney matters mainly for assets outside the trust. If you do have a trust, your power of attorney should let your agent fund it, so a forgotten account can be moved in. See our [guide to funding a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## How does a power of attorney end?

A power of attorney ends when you die, when you revoke it while competent, when a stated date or event occurs, when a court terminates it, when the agent cannot continue and no successor is named, or in many states when a spouse-agent is divorced from you. Death ends it for everyone: afterwards, the [executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) or trustee has authority, not the agent. Our article on [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) has a full table, the steps for a proper revocation and what the agent should do when the authority stops.

## What if a bank or institution refuses it?

Refusals are common, and often unjustified. Banks may cite their own forms, the document's age or their internal policies. Many states limit when an institution may refuse, and some allow courts to compel acceptance and award fees. The practical response is to ask for the reason in writing, escalate to the legal department, send an agent's certification and, if needed, have an attorney write a demand letter. Prevention is cheaper: check with your key institutions in advance and refresh the document periodically. The details are in [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney).

## What is the agent's job in practice?

The agent's duties are practical and recurring. A good agent will:

- Keep a copy of the document and present it with a short certification
- Open a separate record, such as a ledger or spreadsheet, of every transaction
- Keep the principal's money separate from their own, never commingling
- Pay bills and taxes on time and protect assets, including insurance
- Act in the principal's interest, following known wishes, such as not selling a cherished family home unless necessary
- Communicate with other family members, especially siblings
- Keep receipts, and be ready to give an accounting when asked
- Seek professional advice for taxes, investments and legal questions

Agents who ignore these duties can be personally liable and removed by a court.

## How does it fit with the rest of your plan?

A power of attorney handles incapacity. Other tools handle death and other questions. A typical complete plan includes:

- A [will](/learn/wills) or [trust](/learn/trusts) for distribution at death
- A durable financial power of attorney
- A healthcare power of attorney and living will, with a HIPAA authorization
- Up-to-date [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) on retirement accounts and insurance
- Guardian nominations for minor children, covered in [naming a guardian for your children](/learn/guardianship)
- Instructions for [digital accounts](/learn/digital-assets)

## Is a power of attorney useful for someone with a disabled family member?

It can help, but special needs planning is more complex. A parent of an adult child with disabilities may want that child to sign a power of attorney or other supported decision-making agreement at 18, if the child has capacity, to avoid guardianship. Planning can also involve special needs trusts. Our [special needs planning guide](/learn/special-needs) covers these options.

## What common mistakes should you avoid?

Families run into the same problems repeatedly.

1. **Waiting too long.** Capacity can be lost suddenly, and after that, the only route may be court.
2. **Using a generic form** that does not meet the formalities of your state.
3. **Naming only one agent.** Without a successor, the document may fail when needed.
4. **Naming the wrong person** because of birth order or guilt.
5. **Forgetting to tell anyone** where the original is.
6. **Granting broad gifting powers** with no limits.
7. **Never checking with the bank** that the document will actually be accepted.
8. **Assuming the document works after death.** It does not.
9. **Keeping an old form** after a divorce, move or death in the family.
10. **Skipping the healthcare side,** leaving medical decisions unaddressed.

## How does state law affect your document?

State law governs the formalities, such as the number of witnesses and whether a notary is needed, the default powers, how courts review agents, and whether a statutory form exists. A document signed in one state is generally honored in another, but details may not match and institutions may hesitate. If you move, have the document reviewed. For state-specific context, see our guides for [California](/estate-planning/california), [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), [New York](/estate-planning/new-york) and [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), and browse the rest of the state pages from there.

## Step-by-step: how to put a power of attorney in place

1. List your accounts, property and institutions so you know what the agent will manage.
2. Choose an agent and one or two successors, and talk to each of them.
3. Decide on the effective date: immediate or springing.
4. Decide which special powers to grant and which limits to impose.
5. Have an attorney prepare the document for your state's rules.
6. Sign it in front of a notary and any required witnesses.
7. Give copies to your agent and successors, and, if possible, to key institutions.
8. Store the original safely, and tell your agent where it is.
9. Sign your healthcare directives at the same time.
10. Review the whole package every few years and after major life events. Our guide on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists the triggers.
