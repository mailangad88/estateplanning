Dolores is 84 and has started to miss bills. Her son Paul has her power of attorney and wants to move her savings into a new account, set up automatic mortgage payments and close two old accounts she no longer uses. He asks the obvious question: does the document allow all of that?

Mostly yes. But banks, lenders and the law each add rules about how it is done, and a few moves that look harmless, such as adding yourself to her account, are exactly what the document does not allow. This page walks through the ordinary tasks first, then the lines an agent should not cross. For the wider picture, see our [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## Can a power of attorney open or close a bank account?

Yes, when the document gives banking powers. Illinois's statutory form for property and a typical New Jersey durable power both cover banking, so opening, using and closing accounts is within the agent's job. Check the power list in your copy. If the form is a limited one for a single purpose, such as one real estate closing, banking may not be included.

What the agent should do is open any new account in the principal's name only. The signature line reads something like "Dolores Reyes, by Paul Reyes, her agent." The social security number on the account is Dolores's. The money is hers. It is never part of Paul's finances, and Paul's own creditors, divorce or death cannot reach it if the paperwork is clean.

Closing an account follows the same rule. The funds go into another account in the principal's name, not into the agent's. If you are closing a joint account the principal shares with someone else, take care: closing it can affect the other owner, and the agent should not be deciding that without understanding who owns what. Our article on [joint accounts and beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations/joint-ownership-vs-beneficiary-designation) shows how ownership works.

## What does a bank need before it accepts the document?

Each bank has its own intake process, and most want the same four things.

1. A copy of the signed and notarized power of attorney, sometimes the original.
2. Photo identification for the agent.
3. A signature card for the agent as agent.
4. For some banks, a letter or internal form confirming the principal is alive and the document is in force.

New Jersey law lets a third party rely on a photocopy or certified copy of the signed original (N.J.S.A. 46:2B-8.11) and keep relying on it until the bank has actual notice of revocation or death. Illinois statute gives similar comfort to those who accept the form in good faith. Even so, some banks insist on their own form. When one refuses, our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) gives the escalation path, including how to ask for the refusal in writing.

## Can an agent pay a parent's mortgage and bills?

Yes. Paying the mortgage, taxes, insurance and utilities from the principal's accounts is the core of the job. A few practical points help.

- **Set up automatic payments** from the principal's account so a late fee does not follow a hospital stay.
- **Update the lender's contact** once it accepts your document, and keep a copy of every notice.
- **Keep insurance current.** A lapsed homeowner's policy can put the loan in default, and the lender may buy costly coverage and add it to the balance.
- **Track every payment** in a simple ledger or spreadsheet. The act of paying is easy. Explaining it later is where agents get into trouble.

Here is an example with invented numbers, for illustration only. Dolores's mortgage payment is $1,150 a month, taxes and insurance are paid through escrow, and her Social Security and a small pension total $2,900 a month. Paul sets the mortgage to draft from her checking account on the 5th, moves $3,000 to cover a three-month cushion, and writes the three figures in a ledger. When he later needs to show a sibling or a court what happened, the record takes him ten minutes.

What an agent should not do is use their own money and "settle up later" informally. Reimbursement is allowed in many cases, but it must be documented. Our guide to [whether an agent can be paid or reimbursed](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-agent-be-paid-or-reimbursed) explains the rules.

## What about refinancing, selling or a reverse mortgage?

Those are bigger moves and need explicit authority. A general power of attorney can cover real estate, but lenders and title companies read the document line by line before a new loan or deed. A reverse mortgage in particular requires counseling for the borrower and a document that names the power clearly. Our article on [power of attorney and real estate](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-real-estate) covers selling, buying and deeding a house.

If the plan is to pay for care out of the house, speak with an attorney before anything is signed, because a sale or transfer can affect Medicaid eligibility. The [Medicaid look-back](/glossary/look-back-period) is 60 months in most states.

## What can an agent not do with bank accounts?

Four limits come up again and again.

| Move | Allowed? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Put the agent's name on the account as joint owner | Usually no | It is a transfer of ownership to the agent |
| Change a payable-on-death beneficiary | Not unless the document says so | Illinois's statute says beneficiary changes need express power (755 ILCS 45/3-4) |
| Use the account for the agent's own bills | No | Self-dealing breaches the agent's duty |
| Keep using the account after the principal dies | No | The power ends at death |

Our piece on [whether a power of attorney can make gifts](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts) goes further into gift limits, and [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse) explains how courts view an agent who crosses these lines.

## How do you keep a record that protects you?

Agents who get questioned are almost always agents without records. Keep a folder, paper or digital, with these items.

- A copy of the power of attorney and a log of who received it.
- A list of the principal's accounts with account numbers masked.
- Monthly statements and receipts for each withdrawal.
- A short note for any large payment, with the reason and who approved it.
- Messages with siblings or the principal's doctor about financial choices.

If siblings are likely to ask questions, send a short summary every quarter. It costs little and prevents a lawsuit. Our article on [what a power of attorney agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do) lists the duties in order.

## What happens to the accounts when the principal dies?

The agent's authority ends. The bank may freeze the account as soon as it learns of the death. The executor named in the will, or the court-appointed administrator, takes over the probate accounts, while accounts with a named beneficiary pass directly. Our piece on [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) walks through who can access what.

If you have already used the power of attorney after death, even for a bill, stop and talk to an attorney. In New Jersey a third party who acts without notice is protected, but an agent who knew of the death is not.

## How state law changes this

Illinois and New Jersey both let the principal say how much the agent may do, and both hold the agent to a fiduciary standard. Illinois offers a statutory short form with a standard power list. New Jersey's Revised Durable Power of Attorney Act leaves more to the document's own words, so a vague form can give a bank reason to hesitate.
