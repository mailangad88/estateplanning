The document is only as good as the person named in it. A power of attorney can be beautifully drafted and still fail if the agent is overwhelmed, unreachable or tempted. Most of the legal protection for you lies in the choice made before you sign, not in remedies after something goes wrong.

This article gives you a practical way to choose, with traits to look for, warning signs to heed, and how to handle the conversation. For what the agent will actually be able to do, see our article on the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), or return to the [power of attorney overview](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## What does an agent actually do?

An [agent](/glossary/agent) steps into your financial shoes only for the purposes the document allows, and must act as a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary). In practice the work is often ordinary and sometimes tedious:

- Collect your mail and sort bills
- Pay expenses from your accounts and keep records
- Talk with banks, insurers, the IRS and Social Security
- File tax returns or hire an accountant
- Manage or sell a house, or arrange repairs
- Oversee investments or hand them to an adviser
- Coordinate with your care team about paying for care

The duties include [acting in your best interest](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), staying within the powers given, keeping your money separate, and keeping records. A mistake can create personal liability, so a good agent is someone who takes the role seriously.

## What qualities matter most?

Look for character first and skills second. You can hire an accountant, but you cannot hire integrity.

1. **Honesty.** Would you trust this person with an envelope of cash and no receipt?
2. **Reliability.** Do they return calls and meet deadlines?
3. **Organization.** They will keep a ledger and handle paperwork for years.
4. **Calm under pressure.** Agents often act during a family crisis.
5. **Willingness.** Some people say yes to avoid hurting you. Ask whether they truly want the job.
6. **Availability.** Distance matters less than it did, but signing in person, visiting a bank branch or checking on a house can be hard from across the country.
7. **Ability to say no.** An agent must stand firm when siblings or friends ask for money.
8. **Financial maturity.** Not wealth, but sound habits. An agent with their own debts, gambling, addiction, pending divorce or lawsuits is a risk.
9. **Respect for your wishes.** Pick someone who will carry out your decisions even if they would choose differently.

## Who do people usually choose?

Each type of agent has typical strengths and weaknesses, and there is no universal right answer.

| Choice | Strengths | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse | Knows your finances; shared interests | Same age and health, so may also be incapacitated; may be unfamiliar with investments |
| Adult child | Often nearby and committed | Sibling conflict; may be overwhelmed; potential conflict over inheritance |
| Sibling or other relative | Long-standing trust | Often close to your age |
| Close friend | Neutral between children | Family may resent or distrust a non-relative |
| Professional fiduciary, lawyer or trust company | Skilled, accountable, neutral | Fees; less personal; varying oversight |

Consider Diane, 66, who has three children. Her oldest, Paul, is a surgeon in another state with little free time. Her middle child, Jen, lives ten minutes away, keeps meticulous books for a small business, and gets along with the others. Diane names Jen as agent and Paul as first successor. She tells all three why. That choice shows the principle: pick the person best suited, and explain it, rather than defaulting to birth order or avoiding hurt feelings.

## Should you name co-agents?

Co-agents are two or more people authorized to act, and the document must say whether each can act alone or whether they must act together. Many families like the apparent fairness. In practice joint authority often creates deadlock, delay and bank complaints, because every transaction needs agreement or two signatures.

Better options usually include:

- One primary agent with ordered successors
- A primary agent who must give periodic accountings to another child, which spreads awareness without splitting authority
- Co-agents with authority to act separately in a small, defined area, for example one for real estate and one for investments

If you want shared decisions, ask your lawyer to build the rules into the document: how disputes resolve, what happens if one co-agent quits, and whether each can act alone for routine matters.

## Why name a successor agent?

You should name at least one successor agent because the primary agent may die, become ill, divorce you, move away, or simply decline. If no one is left, your family is back to petitioning a court for a [conservator](/glossary/conservatorship). Name two successors if you can. Specify what ends a person's turn: death, resignation, incapacity, or, if the agent is a spouse, divorce or legal separation. In many states divorce automatically revokes a former spouse's authority, but you should say so clearly anyway.

## What are the red flags?

Pause if any of these apply to the person you are considering.

- They pressure you to sign, or ask to be named
- They have a history of financial trouble, bankruptcies or addiction
- They isolate you from other relatives or keep information from siblings
- They are controlling about your money now
- They are dishonest with others or have been caught in lies
- They do not take your wishes seriously
- They are a new acquaintance, caregiver or romantic partner of short duration

Elder financial abuse is often committed by someone who held power of attorney. Our [elder financial abuse guide](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) describes common patterns and safeguards.

## What safeguards can you build in?

Even with a trusted agent, a few protections cost little.

1. **Limit gifting powers** or list a ceiling and allowed recipients.
2. **Require accountings** to a named person, such as another child or your attorney, annually or on request.
3. **Say that the agent cannot pay themselves** beyond stated compensation.
4. **Prohibit self-dealing** such as selling your house to the agent below market value, unless you intend to allow it.
5. **Name a monitor** who may demand records and, in many states, ask a court to review the agent's conduct.
6. **Keep a copy of the document with your attorney** so a revoked or replaced version can be tracked.

Agents who breach their duties can be forced to repay and removed. The remedy exists, but it is costly and slow, and a court rarely gets the money back in full. See [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end) for how to revoke.

## Should the financial agent also be the healthcare agent or executor?

Not necessarily. The same person can serve in all three roles, which simplifies communication. But the jobs differ. A [healthcare agent](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) needs to honor your values under emotional strain, and an [executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) manages the estate after you die. Some people split the roles so one child holds the checkbook and another holds the medical authority, which also gives each a check on the other. If your children disagree about caregiving, splitting may reduce conflict.

## How do you ask someone to serve?

Have the conversation before the lawyer drafts anything. Explain what the job involves, ask if they are willing, and give them an easy way to decline. Share where documents are kept and what your general wishes are about care, spending and gifts. Tell your other children what you chose and why, ideally together.
