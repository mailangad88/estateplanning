People who spend money on a living trust often assume it covers everything, including the day they can no longer manage their affairs. That is half right. A trust is a very good incapacity tool for the assets inside it. For everything else, it does nothing, and the family finds that out at a bank counter.

This page explains what each document covers, which gaps catch trust owners, and how to set the two up so they work together.

## The short version

- Your trust is a container. Your power of attorney is a person with authority. You usually want both.
- Even carefully funded trusts leave things outside: retirement accounts, newly bought assets, refunds, benefit checks, and a lot of paperwork.
- Name the trustee and the agent on purpose, and write the power of attorney so it can feed the trust.
- The trust does not cover healthcare. That takes a separate [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## What does a living trust do if I become incapacitated?

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) names a successor trustee who steps in if you cannot serve, typically after a doctor, or two, confirm incapacity as the trust describes. The successor then manages the trust's assets for your benefit without court involvement. That avoids the public conservatorship process that families face when no planning exists.

It works well, with one condition. Only assets titled in the trust's name come under the trustee's control. If the house was deeded to the trust but the brokerage account was not, the trustee can sell the house and cannot touch the brokerage account.

Our page on [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) shows how assets get in. The unfunded-trust problem is common enough that we gave it its own scenario: [what happens with an unfunded trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust).

## What stays outside the trust?

Plenty. Some things are left out by design and some by accident.

| Item | Usually in the trust? | Who handles it if you are incapacitated |
|---|---|---|
| Family home | Yes, if deeded to the trust | Successor trustee |
| Brokerage account | If retitled | Trustee if retitled; agent if not |
| IRA or 401(k) | Rarely; retirement accounts are generally not retitled | Agent, within plan rules |
| Checking account for daily bills | Often left in your name | Agent |
| Paychecks, pension, Social Security | Paid to you personally | Agent, or representative payee for Social Security |
| Tax returns and IRS matters | No | Agent, if the document gives tax powers |
| Car | Often left in your name | Agent |
| Asset bought after the trust was signed | Only if someone retitled it | Agent |
| Medical decisions | No | Healthcare agent |

The IRA row deserves a comment. Moving a retirement account into a trust by retitling it can be treated as a withdrawal and taxed. The usual approach is to keep it in your name and name beneficiaries, which means an incapacity plan for it is a power of attorney. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) for the other side of that choice.

## Why do I still need a power of attorney with a trust?

Four reasons come up repeatedly.

1. **The leftovers.** Every trust owner has some assets outside the trust, even a careful one. A power of attorney gives someone legal authority to deal with them.
2. **Government and agency dealings.** The IRS, the Social Security Administration and Medicare deal with you individually. A trustee is not your authorized representative before those agencies. See [how a power of attorney works with Social Security, Medicare and the IRS](/learn/power-of-attorney/does-a-power-of-attorney-work-with-social-security-medicare-and-the-irs).
3. **Moving assets in.** A good power of attorney lets the agent transfer stray assets to the trust after the trust is already in place, which finishes the job you meant to do.
4. **Claims and lawsuits.** If a claim or a court filing is made in your name, an agent can respond. A trustee only deals with trust property.

Take Walter, 79, who set up a trust five years ago and put the house into it. Last spring he had a stroke. His daughter, the successor trustee, can sell the house and pay care costs from it. But Walter's pension check, his checking account and the tax refund still arrive in his own name. Without a power of attorney she cannot deposit them, cancel his phone, or file his return. She would have to ask a court for a conservatorship, the very thing the trust was meant to prevent.

## Should the trustee and the agent be the same person?

Often yes, and sometimes no. A single person reduces confusion: one family member understands the whole picture, and the power of attorney can direct that person to transfer assets into the trust. But the roles carry different duties, and the combined power invites suspicion in a blended family or where siblings disagree.

Reasons to split the jobs:

- A professional trustee for a large trust, with a family member as agent for daily bills.
- Two children, each with a role, so one does not control everything.
- An agent who lives near the principal and a trustee who is better with investments.

If you split them, tell both people what the other does and give them each a copy of both documents. Our guide to [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) and [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) cover the qualities to look for.

## What should the power of attorney say when a trust exists?

Ask your attorney to include these points.

1. **Authority to fund the trust.** The agent may transfer your assets into your existing revocable trust, which follows your plan rather than changing it.
2. **No power to change the trust,** unless you chose otherwise on purpose.
3. **Tax authority.** The agent can sign returns and communicate with the IRS and your state.
4. **Retirement account authority.** Required distributions, rollovers and beneficiary-form records.
5. **Instructions about gifts.** A trust owner usually does not want the agent making gifts that undo the trust's plan. See [can a power of attorney make gifts](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts).
6. **A successor agent,** in case the first choice cannot serve.

The [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) page lists the other powers worth including, and the pillar [power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney) has the full set of articles.

## What about a pour-over will?

The usual partner to a trust is a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will), which catches whatever you forgot to retitle and sends it to the trust after death. It does its work through probate, and only after you die. The power of attorney works during your life. Together with the trust and a healthcare directive, you get four documents each covering a different time and a different set of assets. The question of which document controls what is one we address in [which controls: will, trust, joint account or beneficiary form](/learn/wills/which-controls-will-trust-joint-account-or-beneficiary-form).

## A short checklist for trust owners

- Sign a durable power of attorney that was written with your trust in mind.
- Name a successor agent.
- Give the agent and the successor trustee copies, or tell them where the originals are.
- Review your account titles each year. If you open a new account, ask whether it belongs in the trust.
- Add a healthcare power of attorney and HIPAA authorization.
- Revisit all documents after a move, a marriage or a divorce.
