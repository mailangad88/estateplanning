Probate is one of those words that sounds more frightening than it needs to be. If you have been told your family "will have to go through probate," or you are an executor trying to work out what that means, this guide gives you the whole map. It covers what probate is, how it works, what it costs and how long it takes, how to reduce it and what to do about debts, small estates and property in other states.

The sections below summarize each topic and link to a full article. Read straight through, or jump to the question you have. Everything here is general education, and the details differ from state to state, so treat it as a starting point for a conversation with an attorney who works in your state.

## What is probate?

Probate is the court-supervised process of settling someone's estate after death. A judge confirms the will is valid, appoints a person to be in charge, oversees payment of debts and taxes, and authorizes the transfer of property to the people entitled to it.

The person in charge is the [executor](/glossary/executor) named in the will, or, if there is no will, an administrator the court appoints. Many states call either one a [personal representative](/glossary/personal-representative). The court gives that person a document, [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary), that proves their authority to banks and title companies.

Our article [What is probate? The process step by step](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) walks through the seven stages in order: filing, notice, appointment, inventory, creditor claims, tax returns and final distribution. It also explains the difference between informal and formal probate, why probate records are public and what the court does and does not do.

## Does everything go through probate?

No, and this is the single most useful thing to understand. Probate only reaches the [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate), the assets titled in the decedent's name alone with no beneficiary and no co-owner. Many families find that their largest assets, such as retirement accounts and life insurance, skip probate completely.

The assets that skip it pass in one of four ways: a beneficiary designation, a payable-on-death or transfer-on-death registration, survivorship ownership, or trust ownership. The table below shows the sorting at a glance.

| Passes through probate | Passes outside probate |
|---|---|
| House in the decedent's sole name | House held in joint tenancy with survivorship |
| Bank account with no beneficiary | Bank account with a payable-on-death designation |
| Vehicle titled to one person | Retirement account with a living beneficiary |
| Personal belongings | Life insurance with a named beneficiary |
| Anything naming "my estate" | Assets in a funded living trust |

The full sorting guide, with a worksheet and a list of the mistakes that send assets back to court, is in [Probate vs. non-probate assets: what goes through court](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets). A will controls probate assets only, which is why a will and a set of beneficiary forms must be read together. Our [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers what a will does and the many things it cannot do.

## How long does probate take?

A simple probate commonly takes six to twelve months. A complicated one, with a business, a house that must be sold, estate tax, property in other states or a family dispute, can take well over a year and sometimes several years.

A large share of the timeline is built in. Most states give creditors a set period, often a few months, to file claims, and an executor generally should not make final distributions until it ends. Appraisals, tax returns and the court's own calendar add more. The usual causes of delay are will contests, missing heirs, real estate, taxes and an overwhelmed or disorganized executor.

## How much does probate cost?

A common planning figure is 3% to 7% of the probate estate, though real costs range from a few thousand dollars for a simple estate to six figures for a contested or multi-state one. The estate pays, so the cost reduces what heirs receive.

The main expenses are attorney fees, court fees, executor compensation, appraisals, publication costs and sometimes a bond. Some states, including California and Florida, set attorney and executor fees by a statutory percentage of the estate. Many others use hourly or flat fees subject to court review for reasonableness. Family executors often waive pay, which can be a smart move because compensation is taxable income, while an inheritance generally is not.

Fees are usually based on the probate estate, not the whole estate, so assets that pass by beneficiary form do not add to them. The details, a cost table and a worked example are in [How much does probate cost? Court fees, attorney fees and executor pay](/learn/probate/probate-costs). ## How can you avoid probate?

You avoid probate by giving each asset a direct route to a named person. Seven tools do most of the work: beneficiary designations, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, transfer-on-death deeds, joint ownership, a funded living trust, lifetime gifts and, for small estates, statutory shortcuts.

None of these is free of tradeoffs. Joint ownership with an adult child exposes the property to that child's creditors and divorce. Gifts lose the step-up in basis that inherited property gets. A trust only works if assets are actually retitled into it. A beneficiary form that is out of date can override your will in ways you did not intend.

Our article [How to avoid probate: seven tools that pass assets directly](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) compares the tools in a table and shows how one widow combined several to keep nearly her whole estate out of court. If you are considering a trust, read our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) and the [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations), which cover the two biggest levers.

### Is a living trust always the answer?

Not always. A living trust is a flexible way to avoid probate and plan for incapacity, and it suits people with real estate, a blended family, young children or property in several states. It costs more to set up than a will and it requires diligence in retitling assets. In states where probate is inexpensive, or for people with modest assets and well-kept beneficiary forms, a simpler plan can work well. We cover the decision in more depth in the trusts guide.

## Is there a shortcut for small estates?

Yes. Nearly every state lets heirs collect a small estate using a sworn affidavit or a short court filing instead of full probate. The estate must fall below a dollar limit set by the state, and a waiting period usually has to pass first.

The limits differ widely, from tens of thousands of dollars in some states to several hundred thousand in others. Real estate is frequently excluded or treated differently, and the person who signs the affidavit takes on personal responsibility for debts and for distributing assets correctly. The process often costs far less than a full probate and can finish in weeks. Our article [Small estate procedures: skipping full probate for modest estates](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains eligibility, the steps and the risks. ## What if you own property in another state?

If a person owns real estate in a state other than the one where they live, and the property is in their sole name, the family typically needs a second probate case in that state. This is called ancillary probate. It exists because each state controls title to land inside its borders.

The second case means another court, a local attorney, separate fees and often a separate creditor period. It can add months. It is also one of the most avoidable parts of probate. A revocable living trust, a transfer-on-death deed where available, joint ownership or an entity like an LLC can each take the property outside the court. Bank and brokerage accounts generally do not trigger ancillary probate. It pairs with the broader property guide at [owning property in more than one state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

## What happens to debts?

Debts are paid from the estate, not by the family. Family members are generally not personally responsible for a relative's debts unless they co-signed, held a joint account or live in a community property state where the law reaches the surviving spouse.

The executor notifies creditors, reviews claims and pays valid debts in an order set by state law, typically administration costs and funeral expenses first, then taxes, last illness medical expenses and secured debts, then unsecured debts like credit cards. If the estate cannot cover everything, the unpaid debts generally go unpaid and are not collected from heirs. Secured debts such as mortgages follow the property, so an heir who keeps a house takes on the loan.

An executor who pays heirs before settling valid claims can be personally liable, which is why timing and records matter. ## What does an executor do during probate?

The executor is the person who makes probate happen. The job includes filing the will, getting appointed, securing property, opening an estate bank account, inventorying assets, notifying creditors and beneficiaries, paying bills and taxes, selling or transferring property and delivering what remains to heirs. Executors owe a [fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty) to the beneficiaries, which means acting honestly and in their interest, keeping records and avoiding conflicts.

The work is manageable for a careful, organized person, especially with an attorney's guidance. It becomes harder when the estate is large, the family is in conflict or the executor lives far away. Many people pick the wrong executor by default, such as the oldest child, rather than the most capable. ## What about taxes?

Most estates owe no federal estate tax, because the federal basic exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026, and married couples can combine exclusions for $30 million with portability. A few states have their own estate or inheritance taxes with much lower thresholds. Separately, the executor has to file the decedent's final income tax return and, if the estate earns income, an estate income tax return.

Heirs typically do not pay income tax on an inheritance of cash or property, though income from inherited retirement accounts is taxable when withdrawn, and most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years. Inherited property generally gets a step-up in basis to its value at death, which can erase capital gains on appreciation during the owner's life. Our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) covers the federal and state rules, and [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) explains what a beneficiary should expect.

## How do you plan to keep your own family out of probate trouble?

A short, honest plan goes a long way. The steps below are the ones that matter most.

1. **Inventory your assets** and write down how each is titled.
2. **Name beneficiaries and contingents** on every account that allows them, and review them after major life events.
3. **Decide how to handle your house.** Options include a transfer-on-death deed where available, a living trust, or a plan that accepts probate for that one asset.
4. **Sign a will** that names an executor and a guardian for any minor children, and catches whatever else falls into probate.
5. **Sign a durable power of attorney and healthcare directive**, since probate does not help if you are alive but unable to act.
6. **Tell your executor where documents are** and keep originals in a findable place.
7. **Review every few years,** or after a birth, death, divorce, move or major purchase.

## Common probate myths

Several widely repeated ideas about probate are partly right and partly misleading.

- **"A will avoids probate."** It does the opposite. A will is the document the court validates.
- **"Probate always takes years."** Many uncontested estates finish in under a year.
- **"Probate eats half the estate."** Typical costs are a few percent, not half, though contested estates can cost more.
- **"A trust avoids all taxes."** A revocable living trust generally does not reduce estate or income tax. Its main benefits are probate avoidance, privacy and incapacity planning.
- **"My family will inherit my debts."** Debts are paid from the estate, not by family members, in most situations.
- **"Joint ownership is a free fix."** It avoids probate but can create creditor, tax and control problems.
