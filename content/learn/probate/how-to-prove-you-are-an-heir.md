When someone dies with a will, the will tells you who gets what. When someone dies without one, state law makes a list of heirs, and the court, the bank or the title company wants you to show that you belong on it. A daughter who has lived with her father for twenty years may find she needs paperwork to prove she is his daughter. A nephew may be asked to show how he is related at all.

This page explains what proof is accepted, when a court has to name the heirs formally, and what happens when a relative cannot be found. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) shows where this step fits into the whole process.

## The short version

- You prove a family link with certified records, not with family stories.
- Start with the death certificate and a birth certificate for each generation between you and the person who died.
- A court order naming the heirs is the strongest proof and is the one title companies prefer.
- An affidavit helps but is weaker than an order.
- Missing heirs slow things down but do not freeze the estate indefinitely.

## What is the difference between an heir and a beneficiary?

An [heir](/glossary/heir) is a person the law says inherits when there is no valid will. A beneficiary is a person a will or trust names. The same person can be both, and plenty of people are one without the other. Heirs are decided by the statute on [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession), which usually puts a spouse and children first, then parents, siblings and more distant relatives. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) gives the usual order.

If there is a will, the beneficiaries get the property, but the heirs still often receive notice of the case and have the right to object. So a niece who is not in the will may still be asked to sign off, which is why this question appears even in estates with a will.

## What documents prove I am an heir?

You are building a paper chain from the person who died to yourself. Each link needs a record.

| Relationship you must show | Typical proof |
|---|---|
| The person died | Certified death certificate |
| You are their child | Your birth certificate listing them as a parent |
| You are their spouse | Marriage certificate, and proof no divorce ended it |
| You are a grandchild | Your birth certificate and your parent's birth certificate, plus your parent's death certificate if the parent died first |
| You are a sibling | Birth certificates showing at least one common parent |
| You are adopted | Final adoption decree |
| Child born outside marriage | Birth certificate, acknowledgment of paternity or court order, as state law requires |

Order certified copies from the vital records office in the state where each event happened. This can take weeks, so start early. If an old record has been lost, you may need a court order or a delayed certificate, and a lawyer can often move that along.

Take Marcus, whose uncle died without a will, no spouse and no children. Marcus is one of five nieces and nephews. To share in the estate he needs his own birth certificate, his late mother's birth certificate showing the same parents as his uncle, and her death certificate. That is three records, not a stack. An organized set of three beats a box of photographs.

## When does a court have to name the heirs?

For a small bank account, a form and the death certificate are usually enough. A court gets involved when there is real estate, when the family is unclear or when someone needs an order they can show a third party. This is called a determination of heirship in some states, and it appears in others as part of a formal intestate probate case.

The proceeding runs along these lines:

1. **Someone files a petition** with the probate court in the county where the person lived, listing everything known about the family.
2. **Notice goes to known heirs,** and often by published notice to unknown ones.
3. **The court may appoint a representative** for anyone who cannot be found or who is a minor.
4. **Witnesses or affidavits** from people who knew the family are filed, along with the records above.
5. **The judge decides at a hearing** and signs an order naming each heir and the share.
6. **The order is recorded or certified,** and that copy goes to the title company, the bank or the county.

The order is valuable because it binds everyone who had notice. Without it, a buyer's title company may refuse to insure a sale of an inherited house, as our article on [fixing title problems on inherited property](/learn/property-and-assets/fixing-title-problems-on-inherited-property) explains.

## What does an affidavit of heirship do?

In some states, a person who knew the family can sign a sworn statement about who the heirs are, and it can be recorded in the land records. This is common for older deaths where nothing was ever filed. A title company may accept it after a number of years. It is less reliable than a court order, because the people named in it are not bound if they were never given notice. The article on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) discusses where this approach appears.

## Who gets notified in probate, and why does that matter?

Notice is how the law makes sure the right people had a chance to speak. In most states the surviving spouse, the heirs and the beneficiaries get a notice or a copy of the petition, and known creditors get a separate notice. A published notice in a newspaper covers creditors and heirs nobody knew about. If the court never notified someone who should have received notice, the case can be reopened later. That is why courts take the family tree seriously, even in estates the family thinks of as simple. Our article on [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) covers the first filings.

## What happens when an heir cannot be found?

An absent heir does not freeze the estate. The person who petitions has to show a good-faith search: the last known address, relatives and old neighbors, public records, social media and an internet search. The court may appoint a guardian ad litem, a lawyer who protects the interest of the person who cannot be found, and may order notice by publication.

If no one appears, the missing heir's share is held. After a period set by state law, it is typically paid to the state's unclaimed property program, where the heir or the heir's own descendants can still claim it. The rest of the estate can generally be distributed meanwhile, though the court may order the executor to hold back the missing share.

If no heirs exist at all, the property goes to the state. That is rare, since the law looks out to distant cousins, but it is why a lawyer sometimes hires a genealogist.

## What about adopted children, half-siblings and stepchildren?

Adopted children are generally treated as children of the adoptive parents. Half-siblings usually inherit the same as full siblings from a shared parent, though some states differ. Children born outside marriage can inherit from the mother in every state, and from the father if paternity was established during life or after death by the methods the state allows. Stepchildren usually do not inherit from a stepparent unless named, except in a few states as a last resort. See our article on [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance).

## How state law changes this

States use different names, different time frames and different thresholds for what requires a formal heirship order. Some allow a simple affidavit for small estates and for older real property, others expect a court proceeding each time. The order of heirs also differs, especially for a spouse when there are children from another relationship. Check your state's guide, such as the [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas), where determination of heirship is a well-known proceeding, or ask an attorney in the county where the person lived.
