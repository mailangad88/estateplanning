You are likely reading this because a family member has died and you want to know how long you will be tied up, or how long heirs will wait. The honest answer is a range, and the reasons behind it matter more than the average. This page breaks the timeline into stages and shows what usually causes delays. For an overview of the whole subject, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

A first point worth knowing: the executor does not control most of the clock. Some waiting periods are set by law, and the rest depend on the court and on the estate itself.

## How long does probate usually take?

Probate for a typical uncontested estate takes about six to twelve months from filing to closing. Smaller or simpler estates, especially in states with streamlined procedures, can finish in three to six months. Complex estates take eighteen months to several years.

These are rules of thumb, not promises. A county court with a light calendar may move quickly, and a single unexpected issue can double the timeline. Think of the range as a planning estimate for budgeting time and cash flow, not a deadline.

## What is the timeline stage by stage?

The table below shows rough durations for an ordinary estate. Your case may run faster or slower at each point.

| Stage | Typical time | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Locate will, gather documents, hire attorney | 1 to 4 weeks | Death certificates, will, account statements, deeds |
| File petition and get appointed | 2 to 8 weeks | Hearing may be needed; letters issued after appointment |
| Notice and inventory | 1 to 3 months | Estate account opened, assets valued, notices published and mailed |
| Creditor claim period | 3 to 6 months in many states | Creditors file claims; the executor reviews and pays or disputes them |
| Tax returns and paying bills | Overlaps the above | Final income tax return, estate return, possibly estate tax |
| Selling assets (if needed) | 1 to 6 months or more | Real estate, vehicles, business interests |
| Distribution and closing | 1 to 3 months | Final accounting, distributions, receipts, court order closing the estate |

Several stages overlap, which is why the total is less than the sum of the parts.

### The waiting period you cannot skip

State law gives creditors a set time to come forward. In many states it is three to six months after the executor publishes notice or the court opens the estate, and some states set a longer outer limit. During this period the executor generally should not distribute everything, because an unpaid valid claim could become the executor's problem. This waiting period is the single biggest reason probate rarely finishes in less than four or five months. Our article on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains how claims work.

## What slows probate down?

The most common causes of delay are disputes, assets that are hard to value or sell, tax issues and procedural gaps. Here they are in order of how often they cause real trouble.

### A will contest or family dispute

When an heir challenges the will's validity, claims undue influence or disputes the executor's actions, the case moves from paperwork to litigation. Discovery, depositions and hearings can add a year or more. The risk is higher when the will is old, was changed late in life, or leaves out a child or spouse. Our article on [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains who can challenge a will and why.

### Real estate

A house must usually be appraised, maintained, insured and often sold before the estate can close. A slow market, a house that needs repairs, or heirs who disagree about selling can add many months. Property in another state may require a separate court case in that state, called [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate), which runs on its own clock.

### Missing or unknown heirs, or a missing will

If the court cannot find an heir, it may require additional notice, publication or the appointment of a guardian ad litem to represent that person. A lost original will can require a petition to prove a copy, which adds hearings and evidence. Telling people where your will is kept makes a real difference; see [where to keep your original will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

### Taxes

Federal estate tax applies only to estates above the exemption, which is $15 million per person in 2026, so most families never file one. A few states have their own estate or inheritance tax with much lower thresholds. Estates that must file an estate tax return generally face a filing deadline measured in months after death and often wait for tax clearance before closing. The final income tax return and any estate income tax return also have to be completed, which can take a while if records are scattered. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

### Businesses and unusual assets

An operating business, a farm, collectibles, digital assets or intellectual property takes time to value and transfer. If the business needs to keep running, the executor may need court permission to continue operating it.

### Creditor problems

Large medical bills, tax debts, disputed claims or an insolvent estate (where debts exceed assets) require careful sorting and can lengthen the case. In an insolvent estate, state law sets a priority order for paying claims, and the executor must follow it.

### The executor

Delay often comes from the person in charge: slow filing, poor record keeping, missed court deadlines or reluctance to make decisions. Sometimes an executor is grieving and overwhelmed. A professional executor or an attorney can help, and in serious cases beneficiaries can ask the court to remove an executor, covered in [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

## Is there a faster path for small estates?

Often yes. Many states let heirs collect small estates using an affidavit rather than a full probate case, and the whole process may take a few weeks. Some states have summary or informal probate for simple cases, with fewer hearings. The dollar thresholds and eligibility rules differ a lot by state, so check your state's court website or ask an attorney. We explain the options in [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

## A realistic example

Priya, 44, was named executor for her father Anil, who died in March. His estate had a house, two bank accounts and a car, all in his name, and a clear will. She filed in April and was appointed in May. The state's creditor period ran four months from publication, ending in September. In the meantime she listed the house, which sold in August. She filed Anil's final tax return in October, paid the last bills and distributed the proceeds to her and her sister in November. The case closed that December, about nine months from the death.

Now change one fact. Suppose the sister disputed the will, claiming Anil lacked capacity when he signed it. The same estate could easily have taken two years, with much of the delay spent on litigation rather than administration.

## How can you speed things up?

Executors cannot skip legal waiting periods, but they can avoid adding to them. These practices help:

1. Order several certified death certificates early; banks and agencies each want one.
2. Find the original will and take it to the court right away.
3. Get appointed quickly and ask for multiple copies of the letters of authority.
4. Open the estate account and start the inventory at once, gathering statements, deeds and titles.
5. Get appraisals for real estate and unusual assets early.
6. Send creditor notices promptly so the claim period starts as soon as possible.
7. Keep beneficiaries updated so they do not escalate to lawyers out of frustration.

Our [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) goes into each of these in more detail.

## Can planning shorten the process for your own family?

Yes. Assets that pass by beneficiary designation, joint ownership or a funded trust do not wait for probate at all. A family whose main assets pass this way may only need the court for a handful of items, or not at all. See [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) and the [probate versus non-probate breakdown](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets). The right mix depends on your state and your goals, and a trust is not automatically better for every family.
