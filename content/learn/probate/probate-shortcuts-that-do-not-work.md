Most people who want to avoid probate have heard of the standard moves: put a child on the account, name a beneficiary, sign a power of attorney, record a deed. Each is a real tool, and each also has a failure point that nobody mentions until the death. This page goes through them one at a time and says plainly when each works and when it quietly does not.

If you want the full menu of tools and how they compare, the guide on [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) covers it. This page is the checking step: you have done something already, or you are about to, and you want to know whether it holds. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) gives the broader context.

## Which common moves avoid probate, and which do not?

| Move | Avoids probate? | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Joint account with survivorship | Yes, at the first death | Convenience accounts; a second death with no update |
| Payable-on-death (POD/TOD) account | Yes | Beneficiary died first; estate named; minor named |
| Beneficiary designation (IRA, 401(k), life insurance) | Yes | Estate named; no backup; ex-spouse still listed |
| Transfer-on-death deed | Yes, in states that allow it | Not recorded properly; state does not allow it; beneficiary dies first |
| Joint tenancy on the house | Yes, at the first death | Second death; co-owner's creditors and divorce |
| Life estate deed | Yes for the remainder | Loss of control; tax and Medicaid effects |
| Power of attorney | No | Ends at death |

The table is a quick screen. The details below are where the trouble hides.

## Does a joint account avoid probate?

At the first death, usually yes. If both names are on the account with right of survivorship, the survivor owns it and the bank needs only a death certificate. That is why couples hold most checking accounts jointly.

It fails in two ways. First, a parent adds a child to an account to help with bills, with no intent to give the child ownership. Depending on state law and the bank's paperwork, that money can be treated as the parent's estate property at death, which means the other children have a claim. Second, the account avoids probate only once. When the survivor dies, it is that person's account alone, and unless the survivor has added someone or named a beneficiary, it goes through the estate.

Ruth added her son Marcus to her checking account in 2019. She had three children and meant for the balance to be shared. At her death, the bank paid Marcus directly. His sisters disagreed that she meant it as a gift. The argument was not about probate. It was about intent, which is what joint accounts leave unanswered. The [guide on joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) covers the same issue for real estate.

## Does a payable-on-death or TOD account avoid probate?

Yes, if a living beneficiary is named. The account is yours until you die, and then it belongs to the person you named, with no court involved. These accounts are among the simplest tools there are.

They fail when the beneficiary dies first and no backup is named, when you name your estate (which defeats the point) or when the beneficiary is a minor. A minor cannot collect directly, so a court-supervised guardianship or a custodian may be needed. Our guide on [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) explains how to avoid each.

## Does a beneficiary designation avoid probate?

Retirement accounts and life insurance pass by the form, not by the will, and they avoid probate when a living person is named. The failure modes are the same: the estate is named, nobody is named, or the person named has died. An ex-spouse who was never removed can still collect in many situations. Review the forms every few years, and use the [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) to check what you have.

## Does a transfer-on-death deed avoid probate?

In states that authorize them, a TOD deed lets the owner name who gets the house at death without transferring ownership now. It must be signed, notarized and recorded before the owner's death, according to the state's formalities. A deed kept in a drawer does nothing. The deed also fails if the named person dies first and there is no alternate. The [TOD deed guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) goes through the steps.

## Does a life estate avoid probate?

The remainder interest passes automatically, so the house avoids probate. The cost is control. The owner who keeps only a life estate generally cannot sell or borrow against the whole property without the remainder owner's signature, and a gift of the remainder can affect Medicaid eligibility. The enhanced life estate deed, sometimes called a Lady Bird deed, tries to fix that; see [Lady Bird deeds explained](/learn/probate/lady-bird-deed-explained).

## Does a power of attorney avoid probate?

No. A power of attorney lets your agent manage your finances while you are alive, and it ends at death. The agent has no authority to pay bills, sell the house or distribute anything afterward. That authority belongs to the executor or administrator, appointed by the court, and the [power of attorney versus executor](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-executor) guide explains the handoff.

A power of attorney is still worth having, because it can prevent a guardianship while you are alive. It just has nothing to do with probate.

## Why do the shortcuts fail at the second death?

Couples tend to do the first half of the job. They title the house jointly, add each other to the accounts and name each other on the IRAs. That works well when the first spouse dies. Then the survivor, now 80, owns everything alone, with beneficiary forms that still say "spouse" and a deed that now has one living name. Nothing passes outside the court unless the survivor updates it.

This is where families get surprised. Tom's mother had kept the house in joint names with his father, who died in 2016. She never recorded anything after that and never named a beneficiary on her savings. When she died, the house and the savings both needed a probate case, even though the family believed the earlier planning had taken care of it. A short review after the first death, with new designations and a transfer-on-death deed or trust, is the cheapest fix. The [what-if page on the estate as beneficiary](/learn/what-if/estate-as-beneficiary) shows a similar outcome.

## What should you check on your own plan?

1. List every account and ask: does it have a living owner, a beneficiary or a payable-on-death name?
2. For each beneficiary, confirm there is a backup and that the person is an adult.
3. Check the title to the house: is a deed recorded, and does it say what you think it says?
4. Ask whether a shortcut creates a tax, creditor or benefits problem. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) helps with the first pass.
5. Look at the second death, not just the first. Many plans work once and fail the second time.
