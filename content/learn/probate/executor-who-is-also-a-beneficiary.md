Most executors are also family. A mother names her oldest daughter to settle her estate, and the same will leaves that daughter and her brother a half share each. There is nothing unusual or improper about this, and a will that tried to prevent it would be rare.

The trouble is in the details. An executor who is also an heir is on both sides of many decisions: what the house sells for, when money is paid, whether a fee is charged. This page walks through those moments, so you know where the line is. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) places this in the wider process.

## The short version

- You can hold both roles, and your gift under the will is unaffected.
- Your duty as executor is to everyone with an interest, including creditors, not just to yourself.
- Anything that benefits you personally needs disclosure, and often consent or court approval.
- Equal, documented treatment of beneficiaries is the best defense.
- When in doubt, ask the estate's lawyer before you act, not after.

## What does the law say about the dual role?

An executor is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary). That means a legal duty of loyalty to the estate and the people who are entitled to it. You must put their interests first and avoid transactions where yours conflict. When you are also a beneficiary, the law does not shift the duty. You are still required to act for all the beneficiaries equally.

Courts accept the dual role because it is practical. A family member often knows the assets and costs the estate less than a stranger would. They also watch for situations that are easy to abuse, which is why the rules about self-dealing are firm. Our article on [what an executor can take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate) covers fees and reimbursement; this one focuses on the conflicts.

## Can the executor buy the house or other estate property?

This is the most common problem. A brother wants to keep the family home, he is the executor, and he knows the house better than anyone. A sale to him is a transaction in which he sits on both sides of the table.

Most states treat it as presumptively improper. It may be allowed in any of these situations:

1. **The will authorizes it,** for instance by letting the executor purchase property at an appraised value.
2. **Every beneficiary consents in writing** after seeing the appraisal and the terms.
3. **The court approves** the sale after notice and a hearing.

In each case, you need an independent appraisal, not your own estimate, and a price at or above fair value. Don't take a loan from the estate to finance the purchase. Better still, step aside from decisions on this one asset and let the attorney or a co-executor handle the sale. Our article on [heirs selling the house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) explains who has the authority to sign, and [inheriting a house with siblings](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) covers buyouts.

A realistic case: Priya, executor for her father, wants the house. Her sister Anita is happy to sell to her. They order an appraisal at $360,000, Priya buys out Anita's half through a refinance and both sign a written consent that the estate attorney files. Nobody can later claim the price was rigged. Without the paper, the same deal can be reopened for years.

## Can the executor take their inheritance early?

Only in step with everyone else, and not before debts, taxes and expenses are covered. An estate has to pay creditors, final taxes, funeral costs and administration expenses first. If the executor takes a distribution early and the estate then runs short, they may have to put the money back from their own pocket.

A safer pattern is a partial distribution to all beneficiaries once the creditor claims period has ended, with enough held back for what is still owed. Our article on [how long creditors have to come forward](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) explains the timing, and [how long it takes to receive an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-long-does-it-take-to-receive-an-inheritance) shows what to tell beneficiaries who are impatient. Keep a written record of every payment and have each beneficiary sign a receipt.

## Can the executor use estate money for personal costs?

No. Not for rent, not for a car repair, not as a temporary loan. Estate money is not yours until it has been distributed to you. The executor may reimburse themselves for estate expenses they paid personally, such as a plumber for the empty house, with a receipt. That is different from reaching into the account when your own bills are due.

Keep the estate account separate. A payment from the estate to you should carry a clear label, either "reimbursement for [item]," "executor fee" or "distribution," and each should appear in the accounting.

## What about executor fees on top of an inheritance?

You can usually take both, as long as the fee is reasonable and the law or the will allows it. The fee is taxable income to you, and an inheritance is not. Many family executors who are also beneficiaries waive the fee for that reason and to avoid a quarrel. Others take it because the work was heavy and the other heirs agree. Either way, say so openly at the start, and in writing.

## Can an executor be sued, and for what?

Yes. A beneficiary, a creditor or a co-executor can bring a claim. Typical grounds are:

| Claim | What it means | What a court can do |
|---|---|---|
| Self-dealing | Buying property or making deals that favor the executor | Undo the deal, order repayment |
| Misuse of funds | Paying personal costs from the estate | Order repayment, remove the executor |
| Unequal treatment | Favoring one beneficiary, often themselves | Order equalizing payments |
| Failure to account | Refusing to share records or an accounting | Compel an accounting, deny fees |
| Neglect | Letting property lose value, missing deadlines | Surcharge the executor for the loss |

Remedies include repayment out of the executor's own money, loss of fees, payment of the other side's legal costs and removal from the job. Our article on [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) goes through how that works, and the guide on [staying protected as an executor](/learn/after-a-death/executor-hiring-help-and-staying-protected) covers prevention.

## What should a beneficiary-executor do to stay safe?

- **Disclose first.** Tell the other beneficiaries about any decision where you stand to gain.
- **Get written consent** or court approval for anything that benefits you directly.
- **Use outside valuations** for real property and valuable items.
- **Treat everyone alike** on timing and on communication.
- **Keep your own money separate** from the estate's.
- **Let the lawyer carry the weight** on conflicted decisions.

If you are the beneficiary dealing with an executor who is breaking these rules, our article on an [executor who is not doing their job](/learn/probate/executor-not-doing-their-job) lays out the steps to take.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether a sale to the executor is void or only voidable, on the form of consent required, on when a partial distribution is safe and on how courts review fees. Some give the executor wide powers under the will with little oversight; others require court approval for the sale of real estate. Your state's guide, such as the [Ohio guide](/estate-planning/ohio) or the [Pennsylvania guide](/estate-planning/pennsylvania), is a useful start, and the estate's attorney can tell you how your county handles conflicts.
