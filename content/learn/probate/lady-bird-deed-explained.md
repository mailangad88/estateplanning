You may have heard the name from a financial planner or a neighbor: a lady bird deed. The name sounds like a joke, and its origin is murky. What matters is that it solves a real problem. It lets you decide who gets your house without handing that person any power over it today.

This article explains what the deed does, which states recognize it, how it treats Medicaid and taxes, and where it falls short. For the wider picture of keeping a house out of court, start with our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## What is a lady bird deed?

A lady bird deed is an enhanced life estate deed. In an ordinary life estate, you transfer the house to someone else (the remainderman) and keep the right to live there for life. The catch is that the remainderman now has an interest, so you cannot sell or mortgage without them. Our article on the [life estate for a surviving spouse](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse) covers that ordinary version.

The enhanced version adds powers. You keep the right to sell, mortgage, lease or give away the property, and to change your mind about the beneficiary, all without that person's signature. The beneficiary's interest does not become solid until you die. Until then it is a hope.

Think of it as a transfer-on-death deed with a different legal structure underneath. The result looks similar, which is why it is mentioned in the same breath as the [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

## Which states allow it?

Only a few states clearly recognize the enhanced life estate. Michigan, Florida and Texas are the places it is most commonly discussed, with a small number of others also using it. In most of the country the deed either is not recognized or is not tested, and an attorney should confirm before anyone signs one.

If you live in a state that has a statutory TOD deed, that is usually the tool to look at first, since the statute spells out the effect. If you live in a state with neither, a funded [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is the dependable route.

## How does it work, step by step?

1. **A lawyer drafts the deed** with specific language reserving your powers. A form that simply says "life estate" will not do it.
2. **You sign before a notary** and record the deed with the county, in the same way as any deed.
3. **Nothing changes in your daily life.** You pay the taxes, insurance and repairs, and you keep any homestead exemption you qualify for.
4. **If you sell,** the title company deals with the deed, and the buyer receives full title.
5. **At your death,** the beneficiary records a certified death certificate and, in many counties, a short affidavit. Title moves without a court.

## Does it avoid probate?

Yes, for the home. Because the remainder interest is already assigned, the house is not part of the probate estate. That is the same logic as any [non-probate asset](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets). But the deed covers one property only. Your bank accounts, car and anything else without a beneficiary still need their own paths, and our guide to [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) lists them.

## How is it different from adding a child to the deed?

The difference is who owns what today. A joint deed makes the child an owner now, with a share that their creditors, their divorce and their signature on a sale all touch. Our article on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) walks through those costs. With a lady bird deed the child owns nothing you cannot take back. Walter could sell the house tomorrow and his daughter would not need to know.

It also differs from a will. A will is a set of instructions that a court has to approve after death. The deed is a transfer that is already complete, subject only to your reserved powers.

## What happens after you die?

The beneficiary does not need a judge. The usual steps are to get certified copies of the death certificate, record one with the county along with any affidavit the county asks for, and confirm that property taxes and insurance continue in the new owner's name. If the home still has a mortgage, the beneficiary contacts the servicer and keeps the payments current. If the estate owes other debts, creditors may still have a claim against the property in some states, which our article on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains.

Families sometimes assume the deed settles everything about the house. It does not settle the furniture, the contents or the car, and it does not tell the beneficiary what to do if several heirs are involved. When more than one person is named, they own the house together afterward and need to agree on whether to keep it or sell.

## What about Medicaid?

This is why many people ask about the deed. In states that recognize it, you generally keep the power to sell the home, so for some eligibility purposes the home remains your asset rather than a gift. That can avoid the penalty period that follows a gift within the look-back window, which is 60 months in most states. See [the Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

The deed does not make the house safe from a state's claim after death. Estate recovery rules differ, and some states reach assets that pass outside probate. Our article on [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) explains the variation. Some states have written specific rules for this kind of deed, so the answer for you depends on where you live.

## What are the tax effects?

For income tax, you generally keep the house as your own, so the property can receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) at your death. That is better than a gift now. Property tax treatment varies: some counties treat a recorded deed as a change in ownership and some do not. Ask your county assessor before you record.

## What are the risks?

| Risk | What happens | What helps. |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not recognized in your state | The deed may be treated as an ordinary life estate or fail | Confirm with a local attorney. |
| Beneficiary dies first | Gift lapses or goes to their heirs | Name a contingent beneficiary. |
| Minor or disabled beneficiary | Needs a guardian or loses benefits | Use a trust instead. |
| You become incapacitated | No one can sell or manage the home | Sign a power of attorney. |
| Lender or title company hesitates | Refinance or sale is slowed | Use an attorney familiar with the deed. |
| Estate recovery or debts | Claims may reach the house after death | Ask about your state's rules. |

## When would someone choose it?

Consider Walter, 78, who lives in Florida. His wife died, he owns a modest home, and his daughter Carol lives out of state. Walter wants Carol to receive the house without a court case, but he does not want her name on the title today, because her marriage is rocky and her husband has a business debt. A joint deed would expose the home to both. A lady bird deed leaves Walter in charge. He can still sell and move to assisted living next year if he has to, and Carol gets the house if he stays. It is a simple answer to a specific worry.

Compare that with a family where the heir is a ten-year-old or a person with a disability. There the deed is a poor fit, and the trust is the better answer.
