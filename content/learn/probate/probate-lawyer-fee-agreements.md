Most executors hire a lawyer once, and few have ever read a fee agreement for one. You may be about to sign a document that decides how thousands of dollars of your family's inheritance are spent. It is worth knowing how the pricing works before you ask a lawyer to take the case.

This page is about the lawyer's side of the bill: the three ways probate lawyers charge, what a good agreement says, and how to keep the number down. The full list of probate expenses is in [how much probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs), and whether you need a lawyer at all is covered in [do I need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer). Start with the [probate guide](/learn/probate) if you want the process first.

## How do probate lawyers charge?

| Method | How it works | Common in | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Rate per hour for each lawyer, paralegal and assistant | Most states | Open-ended bills, minimum billing units |
| Flat fee | One price for a defined list of tasks, often paid in stages | Simple, uncontested estates | What falls outside the list |
| Statutory percentage | Presumed fee set by law as a sliding percentage of the estate | A few states, including California and Florida | Extra fees for extraordinary work; the base may include the whole house |
| Hybrid | Flat fee for the basic case, hourly for extras | Many firms | A vague line between basic and extra |
| Contingency | Rare in ordinary probate; seen in disputes over money owed to the estate | Litigation | Not a way to pay for routine administration |

Percentage fees are not a national standard. They exist in some states' laws, and elsewhere a court looks at whether an hourly or flat fee is reasonable. Do not assume "5%" applies to you.

## What should a good agreement say?

Ask for these items in writing before you hand over a retainer.

1. **Who the client is.** In probate, it is usually the executor in that role, not the heirs. That affects whom the lawyer owes duties to.
2. **The method and the numbers.** Hourly rates for each person, flat fee amount, or the statutory schedule.
3. **What is included.** Opening the case, notices, inventory, accounting, tax returns, sale of a house, closing.
4. **What costs extra.** A will contest, a sale, a tax audit, appearing in court for a hearing, a second state.
5. **Costs apart from fees.** Filing fees, copies, publication, appraisals, travel.
6. **Billing practice.** Monthly itemized bills, how time is rounded, and whether you can review them.
7. **A retainer and how it is used.** Whether it is held in a client trust account.
8. **How the lawyer is paid.** From estate funds only after the court's approval, or as bills arrive.
9. **How to end the relationship.** What happens if you switch lawyers.

Nothing here is unusual to ask. A firm that avoids a clear answer to any of these has told you something.

## What affects the price most?

- **The size and mix of the estate.** A house, a business or property in two states adds work. Our article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains the second state.
- **Disputes.** One angry heir can double the bill.
- **Whether the will is clear and valid.** A properly signed will with a self-proving affidavit is easier than a handwritten one.
- **How organized you are.** Hours spent finding accounts are billable.
- **The state and county.** Rates and local practice vary.
- **Who does the work.** Paralegals charge less than partners, and a good firm delegates.

## How can you lower the legal bill?

You do not have to hand off everything. Gather death certificates, account numbers, statements, deeds and insurance policies yourself. Make the phone calls to banks and the post office. Keep a running list of questions and send them in one email instead of ten. Ask for monthly bills and read them. Consider a limited-scope arrangement, where the lawyer handles the court filings and the legal questions and you do the legwork.

If the estate is small, the first question is whether it needs probate at all. Many states have a simple process for small estates, described in our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit). And if a trust holds the assets, [settling a trust may not need a lawyer at all](/learn/after-a-death/do-you-need-an-attorney-to-settle-a-trust), or may need less.

## Who pays the lawyer, and when?

The estate does, once it has money. Early in a case there may be nothing in the estate account yet, so some firms ask the executor for a retainer and reimburse it later. Others wait to be paid from estate funds. Do not use your own savings without a written note that the estate will repay you, and keep the receipts. If an estate is thin, say so at the first meeting. Lawyers can often stage the work so the largest bills come after the house sells or the accounts are collected.

## Can the fee be challenged?

In many states, yes. The court that supervises the estate can review fees, especially when an heir objects. A lawyer who bills reasonably and documents the work rarely has to defend it. A fee that looks high next to the work done can be reduced. If you are an heir and not the executor, ask the executor for a copy of the fee agreement and the bills. You usually have a right to see an accounting; our article on the [probate inventory and accounting](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting) explains what that includes.

## Should I use an online service instead?

Services that prepare probate forms can save money on a straightforward case. They cannot advise you, and mistakes cost more to fix than they would have cost to avoid. They tend to make sense where the estate is small, the heirs agree, and a court clerk or self-help center is available. If you have real estate, a business, creditors fighting or a will that may be challenged, hire a lawyer. If you are not sure, pay for a one-hour consultation first.

## A realistic example

Teresa, 52, is the executor for her father's estate: a house, a checking account, and a car, with a will leaving everything to her and her brother. She interviews two lawyers. One quotes an hourly rate and a rough hours estimate. The other quotes a flat fee for opening the case, giving notices, preparing the inventory, and closing, with an hourly rate for anything else, such as a sale of the house.

Teresa picks the flat fee, then asks what happens if her brother objects. The lawyer explains that a dispute would be billed hourly. She signs, gathers the paperwork herself, and uses the lawyer for the filings and two questions about the house. When the estate closes, the lawyer's bill matches the quote, and Teresa reports it in the accounting her brother receives.

## What questions should you ask before hiring?

- How do you charge for probate, and what does that include?
- What is your rate for each person who will work on this?
- What are the likely extra charges in my situation?
- How many estates like this have you handled recently in this county?
- Who will actually do the work, and who will I speak to?
- Will you give me an estimate in writing, and tell me if it changes?
