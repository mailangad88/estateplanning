Couples who buy a house in a community property state are often asked how they want to "take title." The escrow officer rattles off options: community property, joint tenants, tenants in common. One of them, community property with right of survivorship, sounds like a mix of two things you have heard of. It is, and for married couples it is often the best of the two.

This page explains what it is, what it does at a death, and where it can go wrong. For the larger question of which assets skip court, see the [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## What is community property with right of survivorship?

It is a way of holding title. In a community property state, most of what spouses earn and buy during the marriage belongs to both of them equally. That is the default. Each spouse owns half of the community. If a spouse dies, the default is that the deceased spouse's half goes under the will, or under intestacy rules, and often through probate. Our glossary entry for [community property](/glossary/community-property) has the basics.

Add the words "with right of survivorship," and the half that belonged to the spouse who died passes by title instead of by will. The survivor ends up with the whole asset, with no probate for it.

Texas works in a slightly different way. Spouses there can sign a written survivorship agreement, which can be done for a bank account or other property, and which has to meet the formal requirements of the law. Arizona, California and Nevada expressly allow a form of title that uses these words for real estate. Some other community property states, and states with similar marital property systems, have their own versions. Check yours.

## How does it compare with joint tenancy?

| Feature | Community property with survivorship | Joint tenancy with survivorship |
|---|---|---|
| Who can hold it | Married couples in community property states | Anyone, married or not |
| Passes at death | To the surviving spouse, outside probate | To the surviving joint owner, outside probate |
| Tax basis at first death | Both halves can step up to date-of-death value | Usually only the deceased owner's half steps up |
| Overrides a will | Yes, for that asset | Yes, for that asset |
| Creditors | Community debts can reach it; rules vary | Co-owner's creditors may reach their share while alive |
| Easy to sever | Needs a spouse's agreement | One joint tenant can often sever alone |

The basis difference is the reason lawyers like this form for married couples. If the house bought for a modest price is now worth a great deal, the survivor who sells after the first death may owe far less tax. Federal tax law gives community property a full step-up for both halves at the first death, where ordinary joint tenancy generally steps up only half. Our article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains why that matters.

## What happens at death?

1. The surviving spouse gets certified copies of the death certificate.
2. For a house, the survivor records a document with the county. Often it is a simple affidavit of death or a survivor's declaration, which refers to the deed and the death certificate. The title company may require more.
3. For a bank or brokerage account, the survivor gives the institution the certificate and the account paperwork, and the account is retitled.
4. Vehicles and other titled property may need a separate title transfer.
5. A professional may be needed to check whether the property really was held in this form. Many deeds say "community property" and nothing more.

That last step is where problems appear. Some couples believe they hold survivorship title and do not. If the deed reads only "husband and wife as community property," the half belonging to the one who dies goes through the will, and the survivor may face a probate for what they thought was theirs. Look at your deed, not at what you remember signing.

## When does it fit, and when does it not?

It fits couples who want everything to go to the survivor and who do not have competing obligations. Elena and Marcus, married for 30 years with two adult children together, hold their home as community property with right of survivorship. When Marcus dies, Elena records the paperwork, and the house is hers within weeks. She later sells it, and her basis is the value at Marcus's death.

It does not fit when one spouse wants to protect children from an earlier marriage. Take Gary, 64, who has two children from his first marriage and now owns a house with his second wife, Beth. If the house is held with survivorship, Beth takes the whole house at his death and Gary's children receive nothing from it, whatever his will says. Gary may want a different arrangement, such as a trust that lets Beth live there and then passes the house to his children. Our articles on [the family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) and [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) cover that.

It can also be a poor fit when a surviving spouse may need long-term care and Medicaid planning is a concern, or when there is a special needs child who needs a share. The survivorship title takes the asset out of the trust or the will, and the plan has to account for that.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Assuming the deed says what you think.** Pull a copy and read the vesting language.
- **Forgetting about a second asset.** The house may pass by survivorship while the investment account passes by will, leaving things unevenly distributed.
- **Ignoring the second death.** Survivorship only helps for the first spouse to die. Unless the survivor makes a new plan, the house will go through probate at the second death, which is why a [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) often follows.
- **Not updating after a move.** Property rights can change when a couple relocates between states.
- **Treating it as a replacement for a will.** A will still handles everything else, including guardians for minor children.

## Does it avoid probate completely?

For the asset itself, yes, as long as the survivor is alive and the title is correct. Probate can still appear in three ways. First, the other half of your estate, such as a separately owned account or a car in one name, may still need a court process. Second, if both spouses die close together, or the survivor dies before moving the title, the asset can land in the survivor's estate and go through court then. Third, a creditor with a valid claim against the community may look to the property, which is why [debts after a death](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) are worth a look before you distribute anything.

Marriage ending changes things, too. A divorce usually cancels a spouse's survivorship right, though the deed itself stays as recorded until someone changes it. Do not rely on the law to clean that up for you. Record a new deed as part of the divorce.

## How does state law change this?

States differ on whether survivorship is available, how the deed must read, and whether the form can be used for accounts as well as property. Texas, California and Arizona each have their own approach. Our state guides for [California](/estate-planning/california), [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) and [Arizona](/estate-planning/arizona) point to what to check locally.
