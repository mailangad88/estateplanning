You have been named executor, the court has approved you, and you are standing at a bank counter with a folder. The teller asks what you need. It helps to know the answer before you go, because the bank will not guess or bend.

This page explains how an executor gets access to a dead person's money, how to open a separate estate account, and what that account should and should not be used for. The overall process is in our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## How does an executor get access to a dead person's bank account?

You get access by proving the court has given you authority. A will alone does not do that. The document banks want is letters, called letters testamentary when there is a will and letters of administration when there is not. The [difference between them](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) is explained in a separate article. In New Jersey the Surrogate issues them; in Illinois the circuit court issues letters of office. Our pages on [executor duties in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/executor-duties-in-new-jersey-step-by-step) and [Illinois letters and the surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) show how that stage works.

With letters in hand, bring:

1. The letters, certified copies, issued recently.
2. A certified death certificate.
3. Your photo ID.
4. The estate's EIN confirmation letter from the IRS.
5. The name of each account you have found, with a statement if you have one.

The bank will then either retitle the account to the estate or close it and issue a cashier's check to the new estate account. Ask which approach it uses for each account. Closing is usually simpler.

## Can you touch the accounts before letters are issued?

Mostly not, and this frustrates families. An account held only in the dead person's name is frozen once the bank learns of the death. You, even as the named executor, have no authority until the court says so. There are a few exceptions worth knowing:

- Some funeral homes accept payment from an account the bank will release directly for that purpose. Ask the bank before assuming.
- Accounts with a joint owner or payable-on-death beneficiary do not need letters at all. The survivor or beneficiary claims them with a death certificate. See [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account).
- Small balances may qualify for a small estate affidavit instead of a full case. Our [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) article describes the limits.

If you used your own money for the funeral, keep the receipt. You can usually reimburse yourself once the account is open.

## How do you open an estate account, step by step?

1. **Get the letters.** Order several certified copies, since each institution wants its own.
2. **Apply for the EIN.** Use the IRS online application as the responsible party for the estate. The [IRS paperwork for an executor](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810) article covers this and Form 56.
3. **Choose a bank.** Any bank or credit union that offers estate accounts works. Many executors use the bank where the dead person already had accounts, which simplifies the transfers.
4. **Name the account correctly.** A common title reads "Estate of Jane Doe, Raymond Doe, Executor." Do not use your own Social Security number.
5. **Ask what you need.** Ask whether there is a monthly fee and whether checks are included. A checking account is typical because you will write many checks.
6. **Deposit and record.** Move in each asset as you collect it, and note the source of every deposit.

Do not close accounts you have not located yet. Keep a running list; the article on [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) will help you track down the missing ones.

## What goes in, and what comes out?

Treat the estate account as the only pipe. Money comes in through it, and money goes out through it.

| Goes in | Comes out |
|---|---|
| Balances from the dead person's sole-name accounts | Funeral and burial costs |
| Final paychecks, tax refunds, pension payments owed | Court fees and publication costs |
| Proceeds from selling the house, car or other property | Valid creditor claims once the claim period allows |
| Rent or dividends earned after death | Property tax, insurance and utilities on estate property |
| Insurance refunds | Attorney, appraiser and accountant bills |
| | Executor's fee, if you take one, and distributions to beneficiaries at the end |

What does not go in: life insurance and retirement accounts with named beneficiaries, joint accounts that pass to the survivor, and trust assets. Those pass outside the estate. The article on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) shows where to draw the line.

## What mistakes do executors make with estate accounts?

The ones our attorneys see most often are small and avoidable.

- **Paying from a personal account and forgetting.** You then wait for reimbursement with no record. Always note it.
- **Depositing estate checks into a personal account.** Even for a day, this mixes funds, and banks may refuse the check unless it was made payable to you personally.
- **Paying heirs early.** A distribution before the creditor period closes can leave the executor personally on the hook. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).
- **Taking a fee with no paper trail.** The rule is covered in [what an executor can take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate).
- **Letting the account sit.** Idle money earns little, and you will be asked to account for it later.

## A worked example

Example only: Helen dies in Essex County, New Jersey, and leaves a will naming her son Tomas as executor. She had a checking account with $11,400, a savings account of $26,900 in her name alone, and an IRA with her daughter named as beneficiary.

Tomas receives letters from the Surrogate, gets an EIN online and opens "Estate of Helen Alvarez, Tomas Alvarez, Executor" at her bank. He moves both accounts in: $38,300. He pays the funeral home $9,800, pays the final utility bills of $640, and holds the rest while the nine-month creditor period runs. The IRA never touches the account because the daughter claims it directly. At the end Tomas has one statement trail that shows every dollar.

## Recordkeeping that makes the accounting easy

Estates are eventually reviewed, by a court, by beneficiaries or both. Keep a simple ledger: date, payee, purpose, amount, check number. Keep the statements, the receipts and the correspondence. In Illinois you may need to produce an inventory and accounting for interested persons. In New Jersey, a court accounting or informal releases from beneficiaries close things out; see the [New Jersey court accounting or informal releases](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-court-accounting-or-informal-releases-for-an-estate) article. The page on [probate inventory and estate accounting](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting) explains what goes into the report.

## Which bank should the estate account be at?

Convenience decides it. If the person kept their money at a local branch near you, using that bank means the transfers are internal and the staff may already have the death noted on file. If you live far away, choose a bank near the executor, because the executor will be writing checks and depositing sale proceeds for a year or more.

Ask three practical questions before you sign anything. Does the bank charge a monthly fee on estate accounts? Will it give you check copies or online images, since you will need them for the accounting? And does it require the court's letters to be recent? Some banks refuse letters older than 60 or 90 days, so keep the court's ordering process in mind if the estate runs long and you need fresh certified copies.

Interest is a smaller point than people expect. A large estate holding cash for many months may justify moving part of the money into an interest-bearing account or a short-term certificate, but only if the dates line up with when bills and distributions will come due. Park the money where it is safe and reachable, not where it earns a bit more but takes weeks to release.
