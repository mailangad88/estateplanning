Someone has died, there is a house and a pile of accounts, and no one has done anything yet. The person who feels responsible asks the same question: am I even allowed to start this? Or the reverse: my brother is sitting on everything, so can I do it instead?

The answer turns on three facts. Was there a will? Who does it name? And how close are you to the person who died? This page explains who can start a probate case, who gets appointed, and what happens when everyone with a right to act stays quiet. For the filing steps, see [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate). The wider topic is in our [probate hub](/learn/probate).

## Filing a petition is not the same as being appointed

People with an interest in the estate can usually file a petition. Only the court can appoint someone to serve. That distinction clears up most confusion.

When you file, you ask the judge to admit the will, if there is one, and to appoint a representative. The court checks that notice goes to the heirs and that the person asked for is eligible. If nobody objects, the appointment is routine. If someone does, the judge sorts it out at a hearing. Our article on [what happens at a probate hearing](/learn/probate/what-happens-at-a-probate-hearing) describes that moment.

## Who goes first when there is a will?

The executor named in the will. The person who made the will chose that individual, and courts give the choice real weight. An executor can accept by filing the petition, or can decline in writing. If the first choice declines, dies or is found unfit, the court turns to the backup named in the will.

The named executor is not required to serve. Someone who does not want the job can say no, and in many states can simply never file. Our guide on what happens [if there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) covers the situation where the will names no one or every named person refuses.

Anyone else may also offer the will to the court. The person holding the original will has a legal duty to deliver it to the clerk soon after the death, whether or not they want to be executor. In Illinois, the statute asks for the original to be filed with the clerk immediately. In New Jersey, the county Surrogate handles the original, and probate cannot be completed until the tenth day after death. Our overview of [Illinois and New Jersey probate](/learn/probate/illinois-and-new-jersey-probate-compared) lays out the timing in each state.

## Who goes first when there is no will?

State law sets an order of priority for the person appointed as administrator. The order varies, but this is the usual shape:

| Priority | Who | Why they rank here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Surviving spouse or registered partner | Closest legal tie and often the main heir |
| 2 | Adult children | Next in line to inherit in most states |
| 3 | Grandchildren or parents | Depends on who survives |
| 4 | Brothers and sisters | Inherit when there is no spouse, child or parent |
| 5 | Other relatives | Distant heirs |
| 6 | Public administrator or a creditor | Last resort when the family does not act |

Someone higher on the list can ask the court to appoint a person lower down, or one of their own choosing. They simply sign a written nomination. This is how a widow who does not want paperwork asks her daughter to serve. The article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains how the same ranking decides who inherits, and the comparison of [executor, administrator and personal representative](/learn/after-a-death/executor-vs-administrator-vs-personal-representative) clears up the titles.

## Can a creditor open probate?

In many states, yes, but only in a narrow situation. A creditor who is owed money can usually ask the court to appoint an administrator if the family has done nothing and no one with a higher priority comes forward. The creditor does not become the administrator in most cases. Typically a neutral person, such as a public administrator or a local attorney, is appointed.

Why would a creditor bother? Because the estate is the only place to collect. A hospital owed $40,000 has no one to bill until the estate has a representative. If the house is falling into disrepair and the family is silent, a creditor may decide that opening a case is cheaper than waiting.

For heirs, the lesson is practical. Delay does not keep creditors away. It can hand control of the process to a stranger. Read our article on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) and on the [creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) to see how bills are handled once a case is open.

## Who is not eligible to serve?

Courts commonly bar or question a few categories:

- People under 18 or found legally incapacitated.
- People convicted of a felony or a crime of dishonesty, in many states.
- Nonresidents, in some states, unless they post a bond or name a local agent.
- Anyone the judge finds unsuitable, for example because of a clear conflict of interest or a history of mishandling money.

An unsuitable executor can also be removed later. See [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

## An example with a family that disagrees

Ruth dies in Joliet with a will naming her eldest son, Paul, as executor. Her younger daughter, Elena, lives next door and has been handling Ruth's bills for two years. Paul lives out of state and says he is too busy.

Paul can decline in writing. The will may name a backup. If it does not, Elena, as an adult child with priority, can ask to be appointed as administrator with the will annexed. Paul would receive notice but would not need to approve. If Paul instead files and Elena objects, the judge decides after hearing both. A family that talks it through usually avoids that hearing, and the cost of a contested appointment comes out of the estate.

## What does the petition need to say?

The details differ by court, but the petition generally names the person who died, the date and county of death, the heirs and beneficiaries with their addresses, and a rough value of the estate. It asks the court to appoint a specific person, and it attaches the original will and a death certificate. Courts publish blank forms, and most clerks will tell you which one applies, though they cannot give legal advice.

One practical tip: before you file, call every heir you can find. A short phone call that says "I plan to ask to be appointed, and here is why" prevents more objections than any form. People rarely fight a plan they heard about first from the person doing the work.

## What if nobody does anything?

Then the estate sits. Accounts stay frozen, the house cannot be sold, and bills continue to run. Someone with an interest, whether a family member, a creditor or the county, can eventually petition. Our article on [what happens if you ignore probate](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-you-ignore-probate) explains the costs of waiting.

If you are not sure whether a case is needed at all, check first. Some estates, especially small ones, need only an affidavit. See [is probate always required](/learn/probate/is-probate-always-required).
