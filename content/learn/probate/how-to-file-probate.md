A person has died, and the bank has told you it needs "papers from the court." You want to know what filing probate actually involves, in what order, and how much of it you can do yourself.

Probate is a series of filings and notices, not a single form. This article walks through the first filings and the steps that follow them, in the order most courts expect, and shows where rules differ by state.

## What should you gather before you file?

Before you go to the court, assemble the documents the clerk will ask for. Filing with missing pieces is the most common reason a first visit is wasted.

- **Certified copies of the death certificate.** Order more than you think you need, often 10 or more, because banks, insurers and agencies each keep one.
- **The original will and any codicils.** A photocopy is generally not enough. If you cannot find the original, see [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) for where people usually look, and tell the court clerk what you have tried.
- **A list of heirs and beneficiaries** with addresses, relationships and dates of birth where known.
- **A rough list of assets and debts**, with approximate values. Courts often ask for an estimated value to set the filing fee or bond.
- **Your own ID and contact details**, and a copy of the person's Social Security number.

Also confirm that probate is needed. If most assets pass by beneficiary form, joint ownership or a trust, there may be little or nothing to probate. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets), and check whether the estate qualifies for [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

## Step 1: How do you find the right court and forms?

File in the court that handles probate for the county where the person lived when they died. Its name varies: probate court, surrogate's court, orphans' court, or a division of the circuit or superior court. Most court websites post the petition forms and a fee schedule, and many allow electronic filing. If the person owned real estate in another state, that state's court usually has authority over that land, so a second filing there may be needed. See [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

## Step 2: How do you file the will and the petition?

Deliver the original will to the clerk and file a petition asking the court to admit the will and appoint the executor. If there is no will, the petition asks for appointment of an administrator instead, and the court follows the state's order of priority for who serves. Many states set a deadline for filing the will itself: a person who has the original is legally required to deliver it within a short period after death, whether or not anyone plans to open probate.

The petition lists the person's name, date and place of death, the heirs and beneficiaries, and an estimate of the estate's value. You pay a filing fee, which varies by county and sometimes by estate size. Expect a few hundred dollars in many courts.

## Step 3: Who must be notified, and is there a hearing?

Heirs and beneficiaries are entitled to notice that probate has been opened. The rules differ a lot. Some states let the executor mail notice and proceed informally. Others schedule a hearing where the judge confirms the will and any person may object. In a formal or supervised proceeding the court reviews more of the executor's actions. In an informal or unsupervised proceeding, the executor acts with less oversight but with the same duties. Ask the clerk which type your county uses, and whether the will names a "waiver of bond" or "independent administration" if your state has that concept.

## Step 4: What are letters, and is a bond required?

After the judge or registrar approves the petition, the executor takes an oath and receives **letters testamentary**. When there is no will, the equivalent document is **letters of administration**. Letters are the proof of authority. Banks and title companies usually want a certified copy dated within a recent period, so order several.

Some courts require the executor to post a bond, an insurance policy that protects the estate if the executor steals or mismanages. Many wills waive the bond, and many courts then waive it unless an heir objects. If it is required, the premium is generally paid by the estate.

## Step 5: How do you get an EIN and open an estate account?

With letters in hand, apply for an Employer Identification Number for the estate. It is free on the IRS website and takes minutes. Use the EIN to open an estate checking account, which keeps the estate's money separate from yours. Do not deposit estate funds in your own account. Send IRS Form 56 to notify the IRS that you are acting as the fiduciary. See [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) and the [what to do when someone dies checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) for the agencies and accounts to notify, from Social Security to credit bureaus.

## Step 6: How do you notify creditors and handle claims?

Most states require the executor to give notice to known creditors and to publish a notice for unknown ones, which starts a claims deadline. The deadline length varies by state, commonly a few months. Valid claims must be reviewed and paid in the legal order of priority, and disputed claims can be rejected. The executor generally should not distribute to heirs until the creditor period has run. The order and the traps are covered in [what happens to debts when someone dies](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

## Step 7: How do you inventory and appraise the assets?

The executor lists everything the estate owns, with its value on the date of death. Real estate and unusual items such as art, a business or collectibles should be appraised by a professional. Bank and brokerage statements show date-of-death balances. Many courts require the inventory to be filed within a set period after appointment, often a couple of months. A date-of-death appraisal also supports the stepped-up tax basis that heirs use later. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## Step 8: What taxes and expenses must be paid?

The executor pays valid debts, funeral costs, administration expenses and taxes. Three returns come up most often: the person's final Form 1040, the estate's income tax return on Form 1041 if the estate earns enough income, and, for large estates, the federal estate tax return on Form 706. When an estate files Form 706, the executor also reports asset values to heirs on Form 8971. The federal estate tax applies only above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, but some states have lower thresholds. Details are in [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## Step 9: How do you distribute assets and close the estate?

When debts, taxes and expenses are paid and the creditor period has ended, the executor distributes what remains according to the will. Heirs sign receipts, the executor files a final accounting if the court requires one, and the court closes the estate. Do not distribute early. An executor who pays heirs and then learns of a valid debt can be personally responsible for it.

## What if there is no will?

If there is no will, the steps are similar, but the court appoints an administrator, and state intestacy law decides who inherits. A surviving spouse and children usually come first. The administrator often posts a bond, because there is no will to waive it. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Should you do it yourself or hire a lawyer?

Many executors of simple estates file without a lawyer. Think carefully if there is real estate, a business, family conflict or possible debts above the assets. Our article on [whether you need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer) gives a quick test and a list of red flags. Many people use a lawyer for the petition and any hearing, then handle the routine tasks themselves.

Rules differ in each state. Our state guides for [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), [California](/estate-planning/california) and [New York](/estate-planning/new-york) show how three very different systems handle filing, notice and the court's role.
