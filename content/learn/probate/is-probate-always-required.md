When a parent or spouse dies, one of the first things people ask is whether the family now has to go to court. The answer surprises many of them. Sometimes the whole estate is settled with a handful of phone calls and a stack of death certificates. Other times a single bank account or a house forces a full case.

This page explains what decides it, with a few examples of each outcome. For the full background on the court process itself, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## The short version

- Probate is required for assets that sit in the decedent's name alone and have no other legal path to a new owner.
- A will does not avoid probate. It tells the court who gets the probate assets.
- Beneficiary designations, joint ownership with survivorship and trust ownership move assets outside court.
- Small estates in most states can use a sworn affidavit or a short court filing.
- If you are unsure, list each asset and how it is titled. That list answers the question.

## What actually triggers probate?

Probate is triggered by an asset that nobody can claim on paper alone. A bank needs a document that says who may take the money. If the account has a payable-on-death beneficiary, the beneficiary brings a death certificate and ID, and that is the document. If the account is in the decedent's name alone, the bank wants a court order, usually [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary) or letters of administration, naming a person with authority.

The same logic applies to land. A county recorder will not move a deed from a dead person to anyone without paper that shows legal title passed. A surviving co-owner with survivorship rights has that paper in the form of the original deed plus a death certificate. A child inheriting from a parent's sole name does not.

So the real question is never "do we have a will?" The question is "what does each holder of an asset need to see before it will release it?"

## When the answer is no: examples that skip court

Consider Hector, 71, a widower. His house is held in a revocable trust. His checking account names his daughter as payable-on-death. His IRA names both children as beneficiaries, and his life insurance names them equally. He owns a 2016 sedan and some furniture. When Hector dies, his successor trustee handles the house, the bank and the insurer receive paperwork, and the car is retitled at the motor vehicle office. No judge is involved. The will he signed is a short backup, and the family may only file it.

Or consider Linda and Paul, married 40 years, who own their home as joint tenants and share every account. When Paul dies, Linda records a death certificate against the deed and updates the bank accounts. She does not open a case. The [joint ownership article](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) explains why that arrangement is convenient for a surviving spouse and often risky for anyone else.

In both stories the people did their planning with titles and forms. Not one of them needed a lawyer's help to avoid court after the death, because the work was done earlier.

## When the answer is yes: examples that need a case

Now Maria, 80, a widow, who lived in the house she bought in her own name in 1987. She had a will leaving everything to her three children equally. She also had a checking account with no beneficiary and a few thousand dollars in a brokerage account with no transfer instructions. When Maria dies, her children cannot sign the deed over to themselves, and the brokerage firm will not talk to them without court papers. The will is real and valid, and it still has to be admitted to probate so the court can name an executor and authorize the transfer.

Dying without a will makes the same point from the other side. If Maria had left no will at all, the court would appoint an administrator and apply [intestate succession](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will). Probate happens either way. The will only changes who steers it and who receives the property.

| Situation | Probate usually needed? |
|---|---|
| House in one person's name, no deed beneficiary, no trust | Yes |
| House held jointly with survivorship, one owner dies | No, the survivor takes it |
| Bank account with a payable-on-death beneficiary | No |
| Bank account in one name, no beneficiary, large balance | Yes |
| Bank account in one name, small balance, state allows affidavit | Often no |
| 401(k) or IRA with a living beneficiary | No |
| Anything payable to "my estate" | Yes |
| Assets owned by a funded living trust | No |

## What if the estate is small?

Nearly every state offers a shortcut for modest estates, though the dollar limit and waiting period vary a great deal. The shortcut might be a sworn statement given to the bank, or a simplified filing with the court, sometimes called summary probate. Our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) covers the design and the traps.

There are two cautions worth repeating. First, many states handle real estate differently, so a modest house can lose the shortcut even if the estate's dollar total looks small. Second, the person who signs the affidavit takes on responsibility for debts and for paying the right people, which is why we suggest reading the rules before signing anything.

## How to tell whether your family needs it: a short checklist

1. **List every asset.** Include accounts, real estate, vehicles, brokerage accounts, life insurance, retirement plans, business interests and valuable personal items.
2. **Write down the title or beneficiary for each.** Look at statements, deeds and the last beneficiary form on file.
3. **Mark anything in the decedent's name alone with no beneficiary.** That is the probate pile.
4. **Add up the pile.** Compare the total to your state's small estate limit and note whether real estate is excluded.
5. **Check for debts.** An estate with large bills may call for the formal creditor notice that a court case provides. Our article on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains why.
6. **Ask the holders.** A short call to each institution tells you what paperwork it requires.

If the pile is empty or below the limit, you may be able to skip court. If it holds a house, you should assume you need a plan for the title.

## How state law changes this

State rules differ in three ways that matter here. The small estate limit ranges from a few tens of thousands to several hundred thousand dollars. Some states allow a transfer-on-death deed for real estate, which lets a house skip probate with a form recorded before death. And a few states treat married couples' property differently, such as community property states, where a simplified spousal route may be available.

The best way to see local rules is your state's guide. Our [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas) and [California guide](/estate-planning/california) show how much two large states can differ. When property is in more than one state, the [ancillary probate article](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains why one case can turn into two.

## What happens if no one opens a case but one was needed?

Assets stay frozen. Bank accounts cannot be accessed, a house cannot be sold, and a deed stays in a dead person's name. Property taxes and insurance still come due. Our article on [what happens if you ignore probate](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-you-ignore-probate) describes the consequences in more detail.
