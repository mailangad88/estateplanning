If you are trying to spare your family a trip to court, you have more options than you may think. Probate only reaches assets in your name alone, so the task is to give each important asset a direct route to the person you want to receive it. This page covers seven tools, what each does best and where each can go wrong. For the broader subject, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

One caution first: avoiding probate is a means, not the goal. The goal is a plan that works if you are alive but unable to act, that treats your family the way you intend, and that accounts for taxes and creditors. Some of the cheapest probate-avoidance tricks create problems later.

## Why would you want to avoid probate?

People usually want to avoid probate for four reasons: cost, delay, privacy and control. Probate fees and waiting periods are covered in [what probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) and [how long it takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take). Probate files are public. And, for people who own property in more than one state, probate may happen twice (see [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate)).

Not everyone needs to avoid it. In states with a simple process and an estate with few assets, probate may be quick and inexpensive. Weigh the benefits against the cost and effort of each tool.

## Tool 1: Beneficiary designations

Beneficiary designations are the simplest, most common way to pass assets without probate. Life insurance policies, annuities, IRAs, 401(k)s and many other retirement accounts let you name a [beneficiary](/glossary/beneficiary) and a [contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary). At death, the institution pays the named person after receiving a death certificate and a claim form.

Strengths: free, fast, and often covers the largest assets. Risks: the form overrides your will, so an outdated ex-spouse designation can send money to the wrong person; naming a minor child can force a court-supervised guardianship of the money; and naming "my estate" sends the asset right back into probate. Review forms after every major life event. See our [beneficiary designations guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations).

## Tool 2: Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts

Bank accounts and certificates of deposit can carry a [payable-on-death](/glossary/payable-on-death) (POD) designation, and brokerage accounts can be registered as [transfer-on-death](/glossary/transfer-on-death) (TOD). The owner keeps full control during life. At death, the named person claims the account directly.

Strengths: low cost and no loss of control while you are alive. Risks: if the beneficiary dies first and there is no backup, the account goes to probate; a minor beneficiary may need a custodian; and splitting a single account among children can be uneven if values change. Details are in [POD and TOD accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).

## Tool 3: Transfer-on-death deed

Many states let you sign and record a [transfer-on-death deed](/glossary/transfer-on-death-deed), sometimes called a beneficiary deed, that names who receives your real estate at death. You remain the owner during life and can change or revoke the deed.

Strengths: cheap, and keeps the house out of probate while preserving the tax step-up in basis for the heir (see [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis)). Risks: not every state allows it, the deed must be recorded before death, and it does not help if you become incapacitated. Rules differ by state; see [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

## Tool 4: Joint ownership with right of survivorship

When property is held in [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy) with right of survivorship, or by a married couple as [tenants by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety) in states that allow it, the surviving owner takes the whole property automatically. Married couples commonly hold their home and bank accounts this way.

Strengths: simple and familiar; works well between spouses. Risks are larger for parents adding children. The child's creditors, divorce or lawsuit can reach the property, the parent may lose control, adding a child to a deed can be treated as a gift with tax consequences, and a child may not get the favorable step-up in basis on the part gifted. Our article on [joint ownership risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) covers these in detail. Joint ownership with one child can also leave other children out unintentionally. If the asset is your house, see [adding a child to your deed to avoid probate](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) for a worked tax example and safer alternatives.

## Tool 5: A funded revocable living trust

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) holds assets under your control while you are alive, then passes them to named beneficiaries through a successor trustee, with no court involvement. You can change or cancel it at any time.

Strengths: flexible, avoids probate for everything it owns, handles incapacity because a successor trustee can step in, and can set conditions for heirs such as ages or milestones. Risks: it only works for assets titled in the trust's name (see [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust)), costs more to set up than a will, and does not by itself reduce estate taxes. A trust does not shield assets from creditors during your life. Most trust plans also include a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) to catch stray assets.

## Tool 6: Retitling assets and lifetime gifts

You can avoid probate on an asset by giving it away before death, for example by gifting cash to children or deeding property to them. Gifts are not part of your estate once complete.

Strengths: reduces the probate estate and, for some families, future estate taxes. Risks: you lose control and access; gifts of appreciated property carry over your original tax basis instead of getting a step-up at death; gifts above the annual exclusion ($19,000 per recipient in 2026) require a gift tax return, though most people owe no tax thanks to the lifetime exemption; and gifts made for Medicaid planning trigger a look-back period (see [the Medicaid look-back](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period)). Gifting is a significant decision and not a casual shortcut.

## Tool 7: Small estate procedures and other statutory shortcuts

This is not a tool you use before death, but it is worth knowing: many states let heirs collect modest estates with an affidavit rather than a full probate case. Some states also allow surviving spouses to transfer a house or vehicles with a simple filing. If your assets are modest, your family may be able to rely on these, with no planning cost. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

## How do the seven tools compare?

| Tool | Cost to set up | Keeps control while alive | Covers incapacity | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beneficiary designation | Free | Yes | No | Outdated or missing forms |
| POD/TOD account | Free | Yes | No | No backup beneficiary |
| TOD deed | Low | Yes | No | Not allowed in every state |
| Joint ownership | Low | Shared | Partly | Co-owner's creditors, lost control |
| Living trust | Moderate to high | Yes | Yes | Assets not retitled |
| Lifetime gifts | Low | No | No | Loss of control, basis, Medicaid |
| Statutory shortcuts | None | n/a | n/a | Thresholds and eligibility |

## A realistic example

Elena, 66, is widowed with two adult children. She owns a house, a $90,000 checking and savings balance, a $450,000 IRA and a $200,000 term life policy. Her plan: she names both children equally, plus contingent beneficiaries, on the IRA and the life policy. She adds POD designations to her bank accounts. For the house, she considers three options: a transfer-on-death deed, joint ownership with one child, or a living trust. She rejects joint ownership because one child has a pending divorce and she does not want that exposure. A transfer-on-death deed is allowed in her state, so she records one naming both children. She also signs a simple will and a durable power of attorney. Nearly her entire estate passes without court involvement, and her will only catches stray belongings.

## What do people miss?

The most common errors involve things that look done but are not:

- A trust that was signed but never funded, so the house is still in the owner's name.
- Beneficiary forms naming a deceased person or a former spouse.
- Naming the estate as beneficiary.
- Adding a child to an account for convenience, creating unintended ownership.
- Forgetting vehicles, small accounts or digital property, which fall into probate if titled solely to you.

A short audit once every few years catches most of these. Our article on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) offers a framework for checking.
