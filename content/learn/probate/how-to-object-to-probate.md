Rosa's father died in March. In April she learned that a will signed eleven weeks before his death left the house and nearly everything else to her brother's wife, a woman who had moved in to help with his care. An earlier will, from years before, split everything between Rosa and her brother. Rosa wants to say something to the court. She does not know whether she can, or how.

This article covers the practical side of objecting during probate: who qualifies, what grounds work, how a caveat fits, and what the timeline looks like. The deeper law of will challenges is in [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will), and the process around it is in our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## The short version

- Check your standing first. If your financial position would not change, the court will not hear you.
- Objections need legal grounds and some evidence, not just a sense of unfairness.
- Look for the notice date in your papers and count forward to the deadline.
- A written objection, filed on time, is what protects you. Talking to the executor does not.
- Settlement is common, and most disputes end before a trial.

## Who can object to probate?

An "interested person" can object. In most states that means someone whose inheritance would be different if the court ruled their way. Three groups usually qualify:

- **Legal heirs** who would inherit under state law if there were no will, such as children or a spouse.
- **Beneficiaries under an earlier will** who would receive more if the new will were set aside.
- **Beneficiaries under the current will** who object to the executor, the bond, or a mistake in the accounting.

A creditor can object to certain things too, like the sale of property or the handling of claims. A stranger to the family cannot object to a will because they think it is wrong.

Rosa qualifies twice over. She is a child, so she is an heir, and she is named in the older will.

## What are valid grounds for objecting?

The court is not asking whether the will is fair. It is asking whether the document is legally good. These are the usual grounds.

| Ground | What you would need to show | Typical evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Improper execution | The signing did not meet state rules for signatures and witnesses | Witness statements, the document itself |
| Lack of capacity | The maker did not understand what they owned or who their family was | Medical records, caregiver notes, a doctor's testimony |
| Undue influence | Someone overpowered the maker's own judgment, often a caregiver or a new beneficiary | Isolation, sudden changes, the helper arranging the lawyer |
| Fraud or forgery | The maker was tricked, or the signature is not theirs | Handwriting examiner, financial records |
| Revocation | The will was cancelled by a later will or by physical act | The later document, witnesses |
| Unfit executor | The person named should not serve | Convictions, conflicts, history of mismanagement |

The terms [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) and [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) are legal standards with their own definitions. A parent who was forgetful or sick is not automatically incapacitated in the law's eyes. Capacity is judged at the moment of signing, which is why a note from the signing lawyer or a witness who watched can matter more than a diagnosis from a year earlier.

Feeling that a sibling got too much is not a ground. A parent can generally leave unequal shares, as we discuss in [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).

## What is a caveat?

Some states let a person file a caveat. It is a notice placed in the court file, before or soon after a will is offered, saying: do not admit this will or appoint anyone until I have been heard. Its purpose is timing. It prevents a quiet admission to probate while you are still gathering your evidence.

A caveat has limits. It generally lasts only for a set time, it must usually be followed by a real objection, and filing one without a basis can bring costs against you. In states that do not use the word, the same job is done by an early objection, a petition to contest, or a request for formal rather than informal probate. See [formal vs informal probate](/learn/probate/formal-vs-informal-probate).

## How to object, step by step

1. **Read every paper you were sent.** Note the filing date, the hearing date, and any stated deadline.
2. **Collect the key documents.** The will in question, any earlier wills, and anything showing the maker's health and habits around the signing.
3. **See an attorney early.** Many deadlines are measured in weeks or a few months from notice or from admission, and the cost of waiting is permanent.
4. **File the objection with the probate clerk.** Use the form or format the court demands, and serve a copy on the executor and other interested people.
5. **Attend the hearing or have your lawyer attend.** What a hearing involves is covered in [what happens at a probate hearing](/learn/probate/what-happens-at-a-probate-hearing).
6. **Expect discovery or mediation.** Many courts push families toward mediation before a trial date.
7. **Settle or try the case.** Most end in settlement. A trial usually follows only when the sides are far apart.

## What happens to the estate while you object?

The court may limit what the executor can do. In many cases a temporary or special administrator is appointed to preserve assets, pay urgent bills and keep the house insured. Sales and distributions generally wait. The delay, along with legal fees, can eat into the estate. That cost falls on everyone, including you if you win.

Meanwhile, bills do not stop. Property taxes, insurance and utilities run on. A fight that lasts a year can feel longer to the family living with an unsettled house, which is a reason to weigh settlement early.

## Does a no-contest clause matter?

If the will contains a [no-contest clause](/glossary/no-contest-clause), a beneficiary who contests and loses may forfeit what the will gave them. Whether this is enforced depends on the state, and many exempt challenges made in good faith with probable cause. Someone who would get nothing anyway, like Rosa under the new will, has little to lose. Someone with a decent gift needs advice first. Our article on the [no-contest clause](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) goes through it.

## Can you object to something other than the will?

Yes. Objecting to probate does not always mean attacking the will. Beneficiaries object to a proposed sale of the family home, to unusually high executor or attorney fees, to a missing inventory, or to a plan to distribute before debts are cleared. If the problem is how the executor is behaving rather than the will itself, the remedy may be an accounting, a surcharge or removal. We cover that in [executor not doing their job](/learn/probate/executor-not-doing-their-job) and [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

## Is objecting worth it?

Ask three questions. What would I receive if I win, compared with what I receive now? How strong is my evidence, and who are my witnesses? What will it cost in money and in the relationship? A strong case that moves a large share is worth pursuing. A thin case that risks a no-contest forfeiture and a family split is often not. A lawyer who reads the documents can tell you which one you have. Disputes of this kind are one reason many families choose a trust, a point made in [siblings fighting over a parent's estate](/learn/what-if/siblings-fight-over-a-parents-estate).

## How state law changes this

States differ on the deadline to contest, whether a caveat exists, how much proof is required, whether a jury is allowed, and how no-contest clauses are enforced. Some set the deadline from the date of notice, some from the date the will is admitted. Because the dates are strict, check the court's own notice and talk to a local attorney.
