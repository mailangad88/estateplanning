Someone you love has died, you have been named executor or you are the closest relative, and the first practical question is whether to pay a lawyer. You have probably seen advice at both extremes: "never hire a lawyer, the court gives you the forms" and "never open probate without one." Neither is right for everyone.

This article gives you a short self-check, a list of situations where doing it yourself is realistic and a list of red flags where it is not. It also explains what a probate lawyer does, how they are paid and how to get partial help if you want to keep costs down.

## What is the short answer, and the quick test?

The short answer is that a simple estate with cooperative heirs can often be handled by a careful executor, and a complicated or contested one should not be. Answer these six questions. Each "yes" in the second column moves you toward hiring a lawyer.

| Question | Leans DIY | Leans lawyer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is there a valid, signed will that names an executor who will serve? | Yes | No will, or the will is old, handwritten or unclear |
| Do all the heirs get along and agree on the plan? | Yes | Someone has threatened to object |
| Is real estate involved? | None, or one house that is being kept or sold simply | Property in another state, or a house nobody agrees about |
| Is there a business, LLC interest or farm? | No | Yes |
| Do debts look small compared with assets? | Yes | Debts may exceed assets, or there are tax problems |
| Could the estate owe federal estate tax or a state estate tax? | Clearly no | Possibly, or you are not sure |

Two or more answers in the right-hand column are a good reason to at least have a consultation. If your estate is below your state's small-estate limit, read [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) first, because you may not need full probate at all. To learn which assets go through court in the first place, see [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## When can you often handle probate yourself?

You can often handle probate yourself when the estate is simple, the paperwork is routine and nobody is fighting. These are the situations where individual executors most often manage without a lawyer:

- **The estate is under the small-estate limit.** Many states let heirs collect assets with a sworn affidavit and no court case. Each bank or other holder reviews your affidavit, which makes it a paperwork task more than a legal one.
- **There is a clear will and one main heir.** A surviving spouse or a single adult child who inherits everything has no one to disagree with.
- **The assets are simple.** A checking account, a car and a few personal belongings are very different from a rental property portfolio.
- **Your state has an informal or unsupervised process.** In many states an executor can be appointed with little court involvement, which keeps hearings and judge approvals to a minimum.

Even in this group, doing it yourself means doing the real work: filing the will, getting [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary), notifying creditors, keeping records and paying valid debts before distributing anything. Our article on [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) lays out each step so you can judge how much of it you are comfortable doing.

Consider Daniel, 61, who is executor for his mother's estate. She left a will naming him and his sister as equal heirs, owned a condo outright, and had about $40,000 in a checking account and a small brokerage account. His sister agrees with the plan, there are no big debts, and the county court website has all the forms. Daniel might reasonably file the paperwork himself and pay a lawyer for an hour to review the petition and the plan to sell the condo.

## What red flags mean you should hire a lawyer?

Hire a lawyer when a mistake would be expensive, when someone may sue, or when the rules are outside what a court's self-help center can explain. These are the most common red flags.

- **A contested will.** If a relative says the will is forged, that the person lacked capacity or that someone pressured them, you need a lawyer. See [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) for what courts look at.
- **No will and unclear or missing heirs.** Finding every heir, including half-siblings and children of a dead relative, and proving who they are can be harder than it sounds. An overlooked heir can reopen the estate later.
- **Real estate in another state.** Each state where land sits may need its own proceeding. See [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).
- **A business or LLC.** Someone has to keep the business running, value it and follow the operating agreement. Delay can destroy value.
- **An insolvent estate.** If debts may exceed assets, the order of payment is set by law and paying the wrong creditor first can make you personally responsible. See [what happens to debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).
- **Possible estate tax or complex income tax.** The federal estate tax applies only above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, but some states tax much smaller estates. A large retirement account or an unfiled prior-year return also needs attention.
- **A trust plus a will.** Settling both together means coordinating the executor and the successor trustee. See [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).
- **An executor under pressure.** If an heir is demanding money now, or you feel unsure you are being fair, a lawyer is a buffer and a source of clear answers.

## What does a probate lawyer actually do?

A probate lawyer does the legal work of opening and closing the estate and tells the executor what the law allows. The work usually includes:

1. Preparing and filing the petition, the original will and any required notices.
2. Getting the court to appoint the executor and issue letters.
3. Publishing or mailing creditor notices and reviewing claims against the estate.
4. Advising on which debts to pay, in what order, and when it is safe to distribute.
5. Coordinating with the CPA on the final income tax return and the estate's own return. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).
6. Preparing the final accounting, the closing papers and the distribution receipts.

The lawyer also protects the executor. An executor who distributes the money too early, ignores a creditor or sells a house without proper authority can be personally liable. A lawyer who has seen these problems can spot them before they happen. For the executor's full job, see [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

## What does a probate lawyer cost, and how are fees structured?

Fees are usually paid from the estate, and the structure varies by state and by lawyer. The common models are:

| Fee model | How it works | Best fit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hourly | You pay for time spent, often with a retainer deposit | Contested or unpredictable matters |
| Flat fee | A fixed price for defined tasks, such as opening a simple estate | Simple estates with a clear scope |
| Statutory or percentage | A schedule set by law, or a percentage of the estate, used in some states | States that set or guide fees by estate size |
| Hybrid | A flat fee for the basics, hourly for surprises | Estates that are mostly simple |

Our article on [how much probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) breaks down court fees, attorney fees and executor pay with a worked example. Whatever the model, get the fee agreement in writing, and ask what costs such as filing fees, appraisals and publication are billed on top.

## Is there a middle option between doing everything and hiring everything?

Yes. Many families use a lawyer for part of the work. Two arrangements are common:

- **A one-hour consultation.** You bring the will, a list of assets and your questions, and leave knowing whether your estate is simple, what your state requires and what to watch for. For many executors this is the best use of a small budget.
- **Limited-scope help.** The American Bar Association's model rules allow a lawyer to limit the scope of the work if the client agrees and the limit is reasonable. In practice, the lawyer handles the petition and the court hearing, or reviews your accounting before you file it, and you do routine tasks such as gathering statements and keeping the estate account. Ask for the list of tasks in writing.

## How do you choose a probate lawyer?

Choose a lawyer who handles probate regularly in your county and explains things in plain language. Ask these questions at the first call:

- How many probate matters do you handle in a typical year, and in which court?
- Who in your office will do the day-to-day work, you or an assistant?
- How do you charge, and what is likely to be billed on top of the fee?
- How often will I hear from you, and how fast do you return calls?
- What would make this estate more complicated than it looks?

Our guide to [choosing an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) has a longer list of questions that works for probate as well. Be wary of anyone who promises a result or a timeline you cannot verify.

## What is the difference between a probate lawyer and an estate planning lawyer?

An estate planning lawyer helps you make a will, trust and related documents while you are alive. A probate lawyer helps settle an estate after a death. Many attorneys do both, and the skills overlap, but not every estate planning attorney takes probate cases and not every probate litigator drafts wills. If a will contest or a dispute is likely, ask specifically about litigation experience. If there is a trust involved, ask about trust administration as well.

State rules and court practices differ, so your county court's website is a useful reference. The [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) guides show how much the process can vary.
