Hollis's mother died fourteen months ago. His older sister is the executor. Since the funeral she has said "it's in process" and nothing else. He has not seen an inventory, nobody has mentioned the house, and the checking account that was supposed to go to the three children is still, as far as he knows, sitting where it was.

Hollis is not sure whether his sister is overwhelmed, hiding something, or simply doing what executors do at a slow pace. All three happen. This article helps you tell them apart and gives you a sequence of steps that gets results without starting a war. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) has the wider picture of how an estate normally moves.

## First, is it actually too slow?

Estates run slower than families expect. Before you decide anything is wrong, compare the timeline to what is normal. A typical sequence looks like this:

| Stage | Common range | What it is waiting for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening the estate | A few weeks | Court scheduling and paperwork |
| Creditor claims window | Often 3 to 6 months | A legal waiting period many states require |
| Inventory and valuation | 1 to 3 months | Appraisals, statements, finding assets |
| Selling or transferring property | 1 to 6 months | Buyers, titles, repairs |
| Tax returns and final accounting | 2 to 6 months | Tax calendars, refunds, IRS letters |
| Distribution and closing | 1 to 2 months | Releases and receipts |

If you are seven months in and the house is on the market, that is a working estate. If you are fourteen months in with no inventory, no communication and no filings, that is a different thing. Our article on [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) goes deeper on realistic timelines.

## What are you entitled to see?

The executor is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), which means a duty to act honestly for the people the estate belongs to. Within that duty, beneficiaries generally have a right to:

- A copy of the will, which is also in the court file in most places.
- Notice that the estate has been opened.
- An inventory of what the estate owns, in states that require one to be filed or shared.
- An accounting of money received and spent, on request or at the end.
- Information about when and how they will be paid.

You are usually not entitled to every private email or a say in every decision. But basic information is a right. Details on the inventory and accounting are in [probate inventory and estate accounting](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting).

## Why do executors go quiet?

Most of the time, it is not theft. It is one of these:

- **Overwhelm.** The executor works full-time, is grieving, and has never done this before. See our situation page on an [executor who is overwhelmed or unable to serve](/learn/what-if/executor-overwhelmed-or-unable-to-serve).
- **Waiting.** The creditor window, a title company, or the IRS may be the hold-up.
- **Avoiding conflict.** They fear an argument about the house or the jewelry.
- **A hidden problem.** The estate owes more than expected, or a mistake has been made.
- **Misuse.** Less common, but real. Money has been spent that should not have been.

You cannot tell which from the outside. That is why the first steps are written requests, not accusations.

## What to do, step by step

1. **Ask politely, in writing.** Email or letter, dated, asking for a status update, a copy of the inventory, and an expected timeline. Keep it short and friendly. Save a copy.
2. **Look up the court file.** If the estate is in probate, you can find the case number from the probate clerk and read what has been filed. Check for the petition, letters, inventory and any accounting.
3. **Send a formal demand.** If two or three weeks pass with no answer, send a letter by a method that proves delivery. State what you want, cite the date of your first request, and set a fair deadline, such as 30 days. Have an attorney send it if you can; it changes the tone.
4. **Ask a lawyer about the options.** This is where you find out if your state requires an inventory or annual accounting, and what the court can do.
5. **Petition the probate court.** The petition may ask the court to compel an inventory, order an accounting, set a deadline, restrict the executor's powers, or begin removal. Judges generally dislike having to do this, and executors generally respond once a hearing date is set.
6. **Keep the family informed.** If other beneficiaries share your concern, a joint letter carries weight.

For what a hearing involves, see [what happens at a probate hearing](/learn/probate/what-happens-at-a-probate-hearing).

## What can the court actually do?

A probate judge has several tools short of removal, and most are used first.

- **Order an accounting** by a date certain, with the records attached.
- **Set a status hearing** so the executor must appear and explain.
- **Limit powers**, for example barring a sale without approval.
- **Require a bond** if none was posted.
- **Surcharge** the executor for losses the neglect caused, so they repay from personal funds.
- **Reduce or deny fees** for poor performance.
- **Remove the executor** and appoint a successor, which is the heavy tool. Our page on [whether an executor can be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) lays out the grounds.

Courts want estates finished. A judge who sees a year of silence and no filings is usually willing to push.

## What if the executor won't release my inheritance?

There are legitimate reasons to hold money back. The executor has to cover debts, final taxes and administration costs, and cannot safely distribute until the creditor claims window ends. An executor who pays heirs early and then discovers a large bill can be personally liable. So "not yet" is often correct. Our article on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains why.

What is not legitimate is an open-ended no. If the creditor period is over, the debts are paid, and the executor still sits on the funds, ask for a written reason and a distribution date. Many courts will allow a partial distribution if the estate is clearly solvent. Where it is not moving, a petition to order distribution is the remedy.

## When the problem is money

If your concern is not delay but spending, treat it more carefully. Request bank statements and receipts. Compare them to what an executor may pay under [what an executor can take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate). A mistake, like paying the funeral from the wrong account, can be fixed with a record. A pattern of withdrawals, self-purchases, or an estate account that does not exist points to something more serious, and you should speak to a lawyer before confronting anyone. Evidence preserved early is hard to dispute later.

## What does this cost, and is it worth it?

A letter from a lawyer is inexpensive compared with a court case. A contested petition costs more, and the legal fees may come out of the estate, which shrinks what everyone inherits, including you. Judges may shift costs to the executor when the complaint is justified. Weigh the size of your share against the likely fees. When the amount is modest, a firm letter and a hearing date often do the work. When the estate is large or the conduct is bad, the court route is worth it.

## How state law changes this

States differ on when an inventory is due, whether annual accountings are required, how beneficiaries request information, what standard applies to removal, and whether the executor must post a bond. A few require a formal closing by a set time. See our [Georgia guide](/estate-planning/georgia) or your own state's guide, and confirm with a local attorney.
