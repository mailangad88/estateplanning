Marcus was named executor for his aunt, who lived alone and kept her papers in four shoeboxes. A month in, he had a pile of statements and no idea what the court meant when it asked for an inventory. He worried about listing something wrong, or leaving something out.

The inventory and the accounting are the two reports an executor makes. One is a snapshot at the start. The other is a movie of everything that happened afterward. Neither needs a finance degree, and both are far easier if you set up the records on the first day. This guide covers how to build each, what courts and beneficiaries expect, and where people get into trouble. For the overall process, see our [probate hub](/learn/probate).

## What is a probate inventory?

A probate inventory is a list of the probate assets with a value for each, as of the date the person died. Probate assets are the things the decedent owned alone, with no beneficiary and no co-owner. Our article on [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the sorting, and the [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) helps you do it.

The inventory is the executor's baseline. It tells the court and the heirs what the estate holds, and it later becomes the starting line for the accounting. Some states require the executor to file it with the court. Others require only that it be delivered to interested persons on request. Either way, expect the clock to start when the court issues [letters](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration), and expect a deadline measured in months.

## What goes on the inventory?

List what the decedent owned alone on the date of death.

- Bank and credit union accounts in the decedent's sole name, with no beneficiary.
- Real estate titled to the decedent alone, with legal description and address.
- Vehicles, boats and trailers.
- Brokerage accounts and stock without a transfer-on-death designation.
- Business interests, such as a sole proprietorship or LLC share.
- Tangible items of value: jewelry, art, tools, collections, furniture.
- Money owed to the decedent, such as a final paycheck, a tax refund or a personal loan.
- Accounts, policies or retirement plans that name the estate as beneficiary.

What stays off: retirement accounts and life insurance with a living beneficiary, joint accounts with survivorship, payable-on-death accounts, and assets already in a living trust. Keep a second list of them anyway. You will need it for tax filings and for telling the family what exists.

## How do you value each item?

You value everything at what it was worth on the date of death, not on the day you list it. The sources are ordinary documents:

| Asset | Usual source of value |
| --- | --- |
| Bank accounts | Statement balance on the date of death, plus interest accrued. |
| Brokerage and stocks | Average of the high and low price on the date of death, per the broker's statement. |
| Real estate | Written appraisal, or a realtor's opinion in a simple case. |
| Vehicles | A published valuation guide for the make, model and mileage. |
| Business interest | Accountant's estimate, or a formal valuation for a large business. |
| Personal property | Executor's reasonable estimate; appraisal for items of real value. |

Write down where each number came from and save the document. Those date-of-death values also serve as the new tax basis for heirs who later sell, which is the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). An executor who guesses and loses the paper trail creates a problem for the heirs years later.

## How do you build the inventory step by step?

1. **Secure the property.** Change locks if needed, forward mail, and keep a list of what is in the home.
2. **Collect statements.** Request date-of-death statements from every institution, and look for unclaimed property in the state's database.
3. **Open an estate account** under the estate's own tax ID number, so estate money never mixes with yours.
4. **Order appraisals** for real estate and anything unusual.
5. **Write the list,** using your court's form if it has one, and sign it.
6. **File or deliver it** by the deadline, and keep a copy.
7. **Update it** if you find more assets. Most courts accept a supplemental inventory.

## What does an estate accounting cover?

An estate accounting is a running record of money in and money out. It usually shows the inventory total at the start, income the estate earned, such as interest and sale proceeds, payments the executor made for debts, taxes, funeral costs and professional fees, any payments or distributions to beneficiaries, and the balance on hand.

A formal accounting is often a court filing with a set format. An informal accounting may just be a clear summary sent to the beneficiaries with copies of statements. Which one applies depends on the state, whether the estate is supervised, and whether anyone asks. For a trust version of the same duty, see how a [trustee reports to beneficiaries](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account).

## When can beneficiaries waive the accounting?

In many states, adult beneficiaries can sign a written waiver that accepts the executor's report without a full accounting, or a receipt and release when they get their share. Executors often ask for this because it saves time and cost. But a waiver only protects you when the beneficiaries understood what they were signing, so share the numbers first. A court may still require an accounting if there is a minor or incapacitated beneficiary, if someone objects, or if the estate is under supervision.

A beneficiary who suspects a problem can usually demand a full accounting, even after earlier informal reports. Our article on [executor pay and estate funds](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate) covers what an executor may and may not charge, and the [executor removal guide](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) shows what happens when records do not add up.

## What records should you keep?

Keep these from day one:

- A single estate bank account, with every deposit and payment going through it.
- A simple ledger, even a spreadsheet, with date, payee, amount and purpose.
- Receipts, invoices and cancelled checks for every expense.
- Copies of every filed tax return and court document.
- A log of your own time and mileage, if you plan to take executor compensation.
- Notes on every conversation with a beneficiary, creditor or institution.

The most common mistake is paying a bill from a personal account and forgetting to reimburse it. The next most common is selling something for less than it was worth to a friend or relative, without telling the heirs. Both are easy to avoid with a paper trail.

## A realistic example

Marcus opens an estate account the week letters arrive. His aunt's checking account has $12,400, her car is worth about $6,000 from a valuation guide, and her house is appraised at $265,000. Her IRA names two nieces, so it stays off the inventory. He lists the three probate assets, files the inventory with the court, and sends a copy to the two nieces and a cousin. Eight months later he pays the last bill, sells the car, and sends a one-page accounting with bank statements. The nieces sign a receipt and release. The court closes the estate. Nothing about it was complicated. It was just documented.
