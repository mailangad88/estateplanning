Most people picture probate as a line of creditors waiting at the estate's door, with the family at the back. In many states the line works differently. A husband dies with a mortgage, credit card balances and a will that leaves his farm to a son from his first marriage. His widow is not in the will. She still has rights that come with the law, not with the document.

Those rights go by a few names: family allowance, homestead and exempt property. This page explains each one, how they rank against creditors and what a spouse or parent should do to claim them. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) shows where these claims fit in the process. For a wider look at what happens to debts, read our article on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

## The short version

- State law protects a surviving spouse and young children in probate no matter what the will says.
- There are usually three protections: an allowance for support, a homestead right and exempt personal property.
- They typically come ahead of ordinary creditors, though after funeral and administration costs in many states.
- You normally have to request them from the court, within a deadline.
- The numbers differ a lot from state to state.

## What is a family allowance?

A family allowance is money for living costs while the estate is open. Probate can last six months to more than a year, and bank accounts are frozen in the meantime. The law does not expect a widow to wait with an empty account. The court lets her draw a reasonable sum from the estate, usually for a limited period, and in some states a fixed dollar amount.

Who qualifies varies. Many states give it to the surviving spouse and to minor children, or to children the dead person supported, and some include a dependent adult. In some states it must be requested by petition. In others the personal representative can pay it without a hearing if all the interested people agree.

Here is how it might look. Ruth, 61, is the widow of Dale, who owned a small trucking business. His will leaves most of the business to his sons. Ruth has a modest pension but her household budget relied on Dale's pay. The estate will take at least eight months to settle. She files for a family allowance, the court sets a monthly figure and the estate pays it until the case closes. It ranks ahead of the sons' gifts and ahead of Dale's unsecured debts.

## What is homestead in probate?

Homestead is a set of protections for the family home. It can take several forms, and a single state may use more than one.

- **A creditor shield.** A certain amount of the home's value, or in some states the whole home, cannot be reached by ordinary creditors after the owner dies.
- **A right to stay.** The surviving spouse, and sometimes minor children, may live in the home for life or for a period, even if the will gives it to someone else.
- **A dollar allowance.** In some states a surviving spouse can claim a fixed sum as a homestead allowance in addition to the family allowance.

States that protect the home most strongly, such as Texas and Florida, are known for it, though the details are intricate and sometimes depend on whether there are minor children. Other states give a fixed dollar amount. Our guide on [staying in the house after a death](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-stay-in-the-house-after-a-parent-or-spouse-dies) covers the practical side of living there while the estate is settled, and the page on [a house still in a late spouse's name](/learn/what-if/house-still-in-late-spouses-name) covers the title.

## What is exempt property?

Exempt property, also called a personal property set-aside, lets the surviving spouse and children claim items of ordinary household use free of most creditor claims. The list usually includes furniture, appliances, clothing, books, and often one or two vehicles, up to a dollar cap. A few states also protect a set amount of cash or tools of the trade.

The point is simple. Nobody should have to sell a bed and a kitchen table to pay a credit card issuer. The set-aside is claimed by the family, taken out of the estate first and not counted against what they inherit under the will.

## How do these claims rank against creditors?

The usual order in a state with these protections looks like this:

| Priority | What gets paid |
|---|---|
| 1 | Costs of administration, such as court costs and the personal representative's reasonable expenses |
| 2 | Funeral and burial costs, often capped |
| 3 | Family allowance, homestead and exempt property for the spouse and children |
| 4 | Taxes and certain government claims |
| 5 | Secured and unsecured debts in the order state law sets |
| 6 | Gifts under the will |

States do not agree on the order, and in some the allowance outranks funeral costs or is capped at a fixed amount. If the estate is insolvent, a spouse's claim can be the difference between receiving something and nothing. The idea is spelled out further in our article on the [creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period).

These claims reach assets in the probate estate. They do not give a spouse a claim against property that already passes outside probate, like a joint account or life insurance with a named beneficiary. That property is the spouse's through survivorship or the policy, subject to its own rules.

## How are these different from the elective share?

The [elective share](/glossary/elective-share) is a different protection. It lets a surviving spouse who is left little or nothing in the will choose a set fraction of the estate instead, often one third or one half depending on the state and the length of the marriage. Allowances and homestead are about immediate support and the home. The elective share is about a fair portion of the whole. A spouse can usually claim both, and our article on [elective share and disinheriting a spouse](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) explains the timing and the tradeoffs.

## How do I claim these rights?

1. **Tell the personal representative or the court** that you intend to claim, ideally in writing and soon after the estate is opened. Our guide on [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) shows where things start.
2. **Ask which protections your state offers** and what the dollar limits are. The probate court clerk or a lawyer can tell you.
3. **File the petition or form** the court uses. Some states have a short form for the allowance and a separate one for exempt property.
4. **Describe what you need and why,** such as monthly expenses and the household items you want to keep.
5. **Watch the deadline.** Some states give a few months from the date of appointment. Missing it can end the claim.
6. **Keep records** of what you receive, since the personal representative will need to report it in the accounting.

## What if the estate has a mortgage on the house?

Homestead protections shield the house from unsecured creditors, such as credit cards. They do not erase a mortgage. A lender with a lien can still foreclose if the loan goes unpaid. Federal law generally prevents a lender from calling the whole loan due when a spouse or certain relatives inherit the home, and the loan can often be kept going, as the article on [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) explains.

## How state law changes this

Almost everything about this topic is a matter of state law: who qualifies, how much, for how long and what it outranks. Some states offer little beyond a short allowance, and others give strong protection to the family home. A few states do not use the words "family allowance" at all but offer similar relief by another name. Check your state's page, such as the [Florida guide](/estate-planning/florida) or [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas), or ask a lawyer who handles probate in the county where the estate is open.
