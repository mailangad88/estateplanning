If a relative died with a modest amount of property, the good news is that your family may not need full probate at all. Nearly every state has a faster, cheaper route for small estates. This page explains how those procedures work, who qualifies, and where they stop working. The wider topic is covered in our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

The details turn heavily on your state. We describe the general design and point out where states differ. Always confirm the current limit and form with your state's probate court or an attorney, because dollar thresholds are updated.

## What is a small estate affidavit?

A [small estate affidavit](/glossary/small-estate-affidavit) is a sworn statement, signed by an heir or beneficiary, that says the decedent's estate is under the state's limit, the required waiting period has passed, and the person signing is entitled to the property. It is given to the bank, brokerage, motor vehicle agency or other holder, which then releases the asset.

The idea is simple. Full probate protects creditors and heirs through court oversight, but for a small estate the cost of that oversight can swallow the estate. States trade a little oversight for speed.

## Who can use it?

Most states allow the procedure when all of the following are true:

- The probate estate is below the state limit.
- A waiting period after death has passed.
- No probate case has been opened, or none is pending.
- The person signing is the rightful successor, such as the spouse, an adult child, or a beneficiary under the will.
- The signer promises to pay valid debts and expenses and to distribute assets to the people entitled.

Some states also require a certified death certificate attached to the affidavit, a copy of the will if there is one, or a list of the assets.

## How much can a small estate be worth?

The limit is set by each state and varies widely. Some states set the limit as low as the tens of thousands of dollars. Others allow estates worth several hundred thousand dollars to use the process, and a few have separate, lower limits for real estate. Because these numbers change as legislatures update them, search your state court's self-help page or ask an attorney instead of relying on a figure from an article.

### What counts toward the limit?

In most states, only the probate estate counts, meaning assets titled in the decedent's name alone. Life insurance, retirement accounts with a named beneficiary, jointly owned property and trust assets generally do not count because they never needed probate. Some states subtract liens, funeral expenses or the value of a vehicle. Our article on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) shows how to separate the two groups.

## How does the process work, step by step?

The exact sequence differs, but most affidavit procedures look like this.

1. **Confirm eligibility.** List every asset in the decedent's sole name and total its value on the date of death. Compare to the state limit and the waiting period.
2. **Gather documents.** Get certified copies of the death certificate, your photo ID, the will if there is one, and proof of your relationship.
3. **Complete the form.** Many states publish a standard small estate affidavit. Some require notarization.
4. **Deliver it to each holder.** Present the form to each bank, brokerage or agency with the death certificate. They check it and release the asset to you.
5. **Pay debts and distribute.** Use funds first for final expenses and valid debts, then share the rest with the people entitled.
6. **Keep records.** Save copies of what you submitted, what you paid and what you distributed, in case a creditor or heir asks.

A few states require filing the affidavit with the court, or use a "summary probate" process that involves a short hearing. These are still far quicker than full probate.

## What about real estate and vehicles?

Real estate is often the catch. Many states do not allow a house to be transferred by a general small estate affidavit, or they apply a separate lower limit, or they require recording the affidavit in the land records with additional steps. Some states have a separate summary procedure for a home of modest value, or allow a surviving spouse to claim a house using a simple filing. If a house is the main asset, check carefully before assuming a quick route. Our guide to [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) covers the options.

Vehicles often have their own streamlined process through the state motor vehicle agency, with a lower paperwork burden than the general affidavit.

## What are the risks of signing?

The person who signs the affidavit usually takes on personal responsibility. If you collect assets and later a legitimate creditor appears, or another heir turns out to have a better claim, you may be liable to pay them. That is why a careful list of debts and heirs matters before you sign.

Another risk is using the affidavit when the estate is really over the limit, which can invalidate the transfer or create liability. If you are not sure of the estate's value, particularly with property like a home, a business interest or a brokerage account, seek advice.

## A realistic example

Gloria, 83, died with a checking account of $14,000, a car worth $6,000 and a small amount in a credit union savings account. She had no will and no house. Her daughter Rosa checked the state's limit, found the estate comfortably under it, and waited out the 30-day period her state required. She completed the court's affidavit form, attached the death certificate and presented it to the bank and the credit union. After paying Gloria's funeral bill and a final medical bill, she split the remainder with her brother in line with state [intestacy rules](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will). Total legal cost: the notary fee and a few certified copies.

Compare that to Gloria's neighbor Sam, whose $70,000 in a brokerage account and a $210,000 house put him over the limit in the same state. His family needed regular probate.

## What if a creditor or heir objects later?

An affidavit does not end creditor rights. Creditors can still pursue the estate, and in many states they can pursue the people who collected the assets up to what they received. Heirs who were left out can also claim their share. For that reason, treat the affidavit as a legal commitment. Our article on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains how to handle claims and the order in which debts are paid.

## What if the estate is just over the limit?

Sometimes an estate hovers near the limit. A few things can help. Check whether non-probate assets are being counted correctly, whether the state excludes a vehicle or household goods, and whether the date of death value is the right one. If the estate is truly over, regular probate, often in its simplified informal form, is the next step. Our overview of [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) lays out how the full process works.

## Can you plan to stay under the limit?

You can plan to make the process easier, but be careful about trying to game a threshold. The more dependable approach is to have beneficiary designations and, where it makes sense, a trust or transfer-on-death registration so that little or nothing needs a court. See [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate). Thresholds also change, so a plan built around an exact number can fail.

## What should you do right after a death if the estate may be small?

Start with the basics: secure the property, order certified death certificates, and make a list of every account and debt. Do not pay creditors out of your own pocket without keeping receipts. Do not move or spend the decedent's money before you know whether the estate is small and who is entitled.
