Your house is probably the biggest thing you own, and it is the asset most likely to land in front of a probate judge. Money in a retirement account passes by a form. A house passes by whatever the deed at the county says, and many people have not looked at theirs in thirty years.

This page works backward from your situation. Instead of describing every tool, it asks who you are, what you own and who you want to inherit, and then points to the tool that usually fits. For the full list of tools across all assets, see our [guide to avoiding probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate), and for the whole subject start at the [probate hub](/learn/probate).

## Why does a house end up in probate?

A house goes through probate when it is titled in one person's name and the deed names no one to take it afterward. A will does not change this. It tells the court who should receive the house, but only after the court has opened a case, appointed an executor and signed off. Our article on [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) shows the stages, and [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) shows why families want to skip them.

The fix is to give the house its own path. There are four common paths, and a fifth, the small estate procedure, that sometimes fits after the fact.

## Which tool fits which situation?

| Your situation | Tool that usually fits | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Widowed, adult children who get along, state has TOD deeds | Transfer-on-death deed | Backup beneficiary, no help if you lose capacity. |
| Married, house is joint | Survivorship title (joint tenancy, or tenancy by the entirety where available) | What happens after the second death. |
| Remarried, children from a first marriage | Trust, or life estate with clear remainder | Spouse's rights under state law |
| Minor, disabled or financially fragile heir | Living trust | Must actually be funded. |
| Homes in two states | Trust, or a deed in each state | Ancillary probate on the second home. |
| State has no TOD deed | Trust, or lady bird or life estate deed where recognized | Local recording rules. |
| Medicaid long-term care is a real risk | Attorney-designed plan before any transfer | The 60-month look-back in most states. |

## What if you are single or widowed with adult children?

This is the simplest case and the one where a transfer-on-death deed does the most good. Rosa, 71, owns a house outright and has three grown children. Her accounts name them. A recorded TOD deed naming all three, with a backup for any who die first, covers the house for the cost of drafting and recording.

Two cautions. First, a deed gives your children equal ownership with no rules about running it, so decide who handles the sale. Second, a deed does not help while you are alive, so pair it with a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney). Our [comparison with a trust](/learn/probate/is-a-tod-deed-better-than-a-trust) goes through the failures in detail.

## What if you are married?

Many married couples already avoid probate at the first death without knowing it. If the deed reads as joint tenants with right of survivorship, or as tenants by the entirety in the states that offer that form, the survivor takes the house automatically. In community property states, some couples hold title as community property with right of survivorship, which does the same job.

The gap is the second death. The survivor now owns the house alone, and it is exposed to probate again. Couples who care about this usually add a TOD deed or retitle into a trust after the first spouse dies. Our guide to [updating your plan after a spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies) lays out the sequence.

## What if you are remarried and have children from before?

Joint title can disinherit your own children without your meaning to. If you add your second spouse and die first, the house goes to your spouse, and when your spouse dies it goes wherever the spouse's plan says. Your children may receive nothing. A trust or a life estate lets you give your spouse the right to live there while naming your children as the final owners. Our article on the [family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) goes through the options.

## What if you own property in another state?

A house in a state where you do not live can force a second probate case there, called [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate). One fix is a TOD deed recorded in that state, if that state allows it. A better fix for people with several properties is a living trust that holds each deed. Our guide on [owning property in more than one state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) covers it.

## What if there is a mortgage?

A mortgage does not stop any of these tools. The loan stays attached to the house, and the person who receives it has to keep paying or refinance. Federal rules generally prevent a lender from demanding the full balance only because a home passes to a family member. A reverse mortgage is different, since the loan usually becomes due when the borrower dies. Ask the servicer before you plan around one.

## What about Medicaid and nursing home costs?

If a long stay in a nursing home is a real possibility, do not retitle the house on a friend's advice. A transfer of the home itself can be a gift for eligibility purposes, which in most states can cause a penalty if it falls within the 60-month window. Some deeds that preserve your control are treated more gently, and some are not, depending on the state. Start with [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).

## What steps should you take?

1. **Pull your current deed** from the county recorder or your closing file. Check whose names are on it and how title is held.
2. **List who should get the house and who is next.** Include a backup for every beneficiary.
3. **Check your state's options.** Not every state allows TOD deeds or enhanced life estates.
4. **Look for a mortgage, reverse mortgage or lien.**
5. **Decide whether a trust is worth the extra cost** because of minors, blended family or other property.
6. **Have the document drafted, signed and recorded,** then keep the recorded copy with your papers.
7. **Revisit after any marriage, divorce, death or move.**

## A realistic example

Greg and Alma bought their house in 1994 and held it as joint tenants. Alma died in 2021, and Greg took title without probate. Greg, now 80, assumed that the arrangement continued. It did not. The house is now in his sole name. His two children, Tom and Beth, would need to open probate when he dies unless he records a TOD deed or moves the house into a trust. A one-page deed and a recording fee would solve it. Doing nothing means an executor, a court date and several months.
