Some families are surprised to learn that the details of a parent's finances can sit in a courthouse file for anyone to read. Others know it already and worry about it: an adult child who does not want an ex, a business rival or a salesperson to see the size of an inheritance. If that is you, the short answer matters, and so do the exceptions.

This page explains what probate puts on the record, what it leaves out and what you can do before or after a death to limit it. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) has the wider picture.

## The short version

- Probate is a court case, and court files are open to the public unless a judge orders otherwise.
- The will, petition, notices and in many states the asset inventory are part of the file.
- Beneficiary forms, joint ownership and trusts keep assets off the record.
- Sealing is possible for narrow items, but it is not a plan.
- If privacy matters to you, decide while you are alive. After death the choices narrow.

## Why is probate public?

Courts in the United States operate in the open, and probate is a court process. The openness has a purpose. Creditors need to find out that an estate exists so they can file claims. Heirs who were not told need a way to learn that a case has started. The public record also makes it harder for someone to quietly transfer a dead person's property without oversight.

Those reasons make sense, but they come with side effects. A document that protects a creditor also informs a stranger.

## What can people see in a probate file?

The contents depend on the state and the court, and the stage of the case. Typically, the file includes:

- The death certificate or a petition stating the date and place of death.
- The will, if there is one, and the petition asking the court to admit it.
- Names and addresses of heirs and beneficiaries, so the court can notify them.
- Letters of appointment naming the executor or administrator.
- An inventory of assets and values, in states that require one to be filed.
- Creditor claims, and the executor's responses.
- Accountings, which list receipts and payments, in states or situations that require them.
- Court orders and the final petition to close the estate.

Many states let an executor file the inventory with the court only on request, or give it just to the heirs. Others require filing. If privacy is a concern, ask the court clerk or an attorney what is required where you live, because the difference is real.

## Can people look it up online?

More and more, yes. Many county courts publish a searchable index with the decedent's name, the case number and the date of filing. Some also scan documents and let anyone download them for a fee. Others require a visit to the clerk's office. A growing number of private data services scrape the indexes and send mail to heirs, which is why some families receive solicitations a few weeks after filing.

An example: Dennis filed his mother's estate in a mid-sized county. Within a month, his sister received a letter from a company offering to buy her inheritance for a fraction of its value. The letter listed her name and the case number. Nothing improper had occurred. The information was simply public.

## What stays private?

Anything that never enters the court file is invisible to the public. This includes:

- Property held in a funded [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).
- Bank and brokerage accounts with a payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designation.
- Retirement accounts and life insurance with a named beneficiary.
- Real estate held in joint tenancy with survivorship.
- Property passed by a transfer-on-death deed.

The sorting is the same as the one used to decide what passes through court at all. Our article on [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) lays it out in detail. Privacy follows probate: the less that passes through court, the less is on the record.

## What if some assets go through probate and others do not?

Only the probate assets appear in the file. That is how a family can have a $2 million estate and a public record that shows only a modest account and a car. People who set up a trust but forget to put an account into it end up with a thin probate filing for just that account. It is often a better outcome than a full case, though the gap in planning is usually accidental. A [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) catches those leftovers and sends them to the trust, and that will becomes public when filed.

## Can you keep a will private?

Not after death. Once the original is filed, it is public. Before death, a will is private, and nobody can compel you to show it. The practical danger is the opposite: a will that cannot be found. Where you keep it matters, and our guide to [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) covers safe options.

Some people try to avoid publicity by writing a short will that says "all to my trust" and putting the details in the trust. That is the design of a pour-over will, and it works. The will reveals the existence of a trust but not its terms or the assets in it.

## How to limit what the public sees: steps

1. **Take stock of your title.** List which assets are in your name alone. Those are the ones that would appear in a file.
2. **Name beneficiaries wherever the account allows it.** Our article on [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) explains the forms.
3. **Consider a living trust for the house and larger accounts.** A trust must be funded to work. Retitling is covered in our guide to [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
4. **Keep a pour-over will as a backstop.**
5. **Tell your executor or trustee what privacy matters to you.** If a probate case does occur, they can ask the court to seal items that qualify and can use a short inventory when the law allows it.

## When privacy is not worth the effort

Privacy has a price. A trust costs money to set up and takes attention to fund. For a modest estate in a state with a cheap, simple probate, many families decide the openness is a minor inconvenience, and the savings from skipping a trust are worth more. A person who owns a house, a pension and a small bank account, and who leaves everything to two adult children who get along, may reasonably accept a public file. The calculation changes for people with substantial wealth, a public profile, a blended family or heirs who could be targeted.

Our article on [whether you need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) works through those tradeoffs without a sales pitch.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether inventories are filed or only delivered to heirs, whether wills must be filed within a set time after death, and whether the court index is searchable online. A few states offer simplified or informal procedures with less filing. Your state guide, such as the [Florida guide](/estate-planning/florida) or [New York guide](/estate-planning/new-york), gives local detail. If your concern is specific, such as a family member who might misuse the information, ask an attorney about sealing options before filing.
