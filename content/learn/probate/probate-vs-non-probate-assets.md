When someone dies, the first practical question is often "does all of this have to go through court?" The answer depends on each asset separately. Some of what a person owns goes through probate, and some skips it entirely. Sorting them correctly tells you who must act, what the court controls and what your will can and cannot do. For the whole subject, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

This page gives you a way to sort assets, a table you can use as a worksheet and the common ways the sorting goes wrong.

## What is the difference between probate and non-probate assets?

A probate asset is one that is owned by the decedent alone, with no named beneficiary or surviving co-owner, so the court must authorize its transfer. Together these assets make up the [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate). A non-probate asset is one that moves to a new owner automatically at death by contract, by title or by trust terms, without a court order.

The distinction matters because the will only controls probate assets. A will can say "everything to my daughter," but if the IRA names the son, the son gets the IRA. Our article on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) goes further into this.

## Which assets are probate assets?

Probate assets usually include:

- A house, condo or land titled only in the decedent's name.
- Bank, savings and brokerage accounts with no joint owner and no payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designation.
- Vehicles, boats and trailers titled only to the decedent.
- Personal belongings, jewelry, furniture and collectibles with no other title.
- Business interests owned directly, depending on the entity's governing documents.
- Money owed to the decedent, such as a final paycheck, tax refund or a lawsuit claim.
- Any asset that names the estate as beneficiary, or names a beneficiary who has already died with no backup.

## Which assets are non-probate assets?

Non-probate assets pass outside the court in one of four ways.

### Beneficiary designation

Life insurance, annuities, IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s and pensions pass to the person named on the form. See [naming beneficiaries for retirement accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) and [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

### Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death designations

Bank accounts with a [payable-on-death](/glossary/payable-on-death) designation, brokerage accounts with [transfer-on-death](/glossary/transfer-on-death) registration and, in states that allow it, real estate with a [transfer-on-death deed](/glossary/transfer-on-death-deed) pass directly to the named person.

### Survivorship ownership

Property held in [joint tenancy with right of survivorship](/glossary/joint-tenancy), or in some states by spouses as [tenants by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety), goes to the surviving owner. Community property states have their own survivorship rules for married couples, sometimes called community property with right of survivorship. A joint account without survivorship rights, or a tenancy in common, does not work the same way; the decedent's share goes through probate.

### Trust ownership

Assets titled in a [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) are managed and distributed by the successor trustee under the trust's terms. A trust only holds what has been retitled into it, a step often called funding. See [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## How do the common assets sort?

Use this table as a worksheet.

| Asset | Probate or not? | What decides it |
|---|---|---|
| House in sole name | Probate | Deed names one owner |
| House held as joint tenants | Non-probate | Survivorship passes it to co-owner |
| House in a funded living trust | Non-probate | Trustee holds title |
| House with a transfer-on-death deed | Non-probate | Beneficiary deed recorded (where allowed) |
| Checking account, sole name, no POD | Probate | No beneficiary |
| Checking account with POD | Non-probate | Bank pays named person |
| Joint account with survivorship | Non-probate | Surviving owner takes it |
| IRA or 401(k) with living beneficiary | Non-probate | Beneficiary form controls |
| IRA with no living beneficiary | Often probate | Default rules or estate as beneficiary |
| Life insurance with named beneficiary | Non-probate | Contract controls |
| Life insurance naming "my estate" | Probate | Proceeds are payable to estate |
| Car, titled to decedent | Probate | Some states offer a quick DMV process |
| Personal belongings | Probate | No separate title |
| Business owned through LLC | Depends | Operating agreement and ownership interest |

## A realistic example

Joan, 68, a widow, dies with these assets: a house worth $400,000 in her name alone, a $120,000 IRA naming her son Mark, a $250,000 life insurance policy naming both children, a $60,000 checking and savings account in her name alone, and a $30,000 car and belongings. Her will leaves everything equally to Mark and his sister Alice.

Probate estate: the house, the bank accounts, the car and belongings, about $490,000. That is split between Mark and Alice under the will. Non-probate: the IRA ($120,000) goes entirely to Mark. The life insurance goes equally to both. The result: Mark gets $245,000 from the will plus $120,000 from the IRA plus $125,000 from insurance, or $490,000. Alice gets $245,000 plus $125,000, or $370,000. Joan thought she was splitting equally, but she overlooked that the IRA form named only Mark. The family might accept it, or it might cause a dispute.

## What mistakes send assets into probate?

Most of the avoidable probate comes from these errors.

1. **Naming "my estate" as beneficiary** on an account or policy, which sends it to probate and may remove creditor protection or retirement tax options.
2. **No contingent beneficiary.** If the primary beneficiary dies first and nobody is backup, the asset may fall into the estate.
3. **Naming a minor child directly.** The court may appoint a guardian of the property, which is a mini court process. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).
4. **Forgetting to fund the trust.** A trust that owns nothing does not avoid probate.
5. **Not updating after life events.** Divorce, remarriage, births and deaths all change who should be on a form. In some states divorce automatically cancels a former spouse's designation for some assets, but retirement plans governed by federal law often follow the form as written.
6. **Overlooking small items.** An old bank account, a brokerage account from a previous job or a vehicle can trigger a case by itself, though small estate procedures may handle them. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

## Is probate always a bad thing?

Not always. Probate gives court oversight, a set deadline for creditor claims and a clear process if family members disagree. Many states also offer a simpler process for small estates.

Most people keep a will even if nearly everything passes outside probate. The will catches assets you forgot, assets you received late, and assets whose beneficiary died first. It is also where parents name a guardian for minor children.

## Do non-probate assets still matter in the estate?

Yes, for several reasons. They count toward the federal estate tax total, which applies to estates above $15 million per person in 2026, and toward state estate tax in states that have one. They affect fairness among heirs, as in Joan's case. And in some states creditors may be able to reach certain non-probate assets if the probate estate cannot pay valid debts. See [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate). Our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) covers the tax side.

## How do you take inventory of your own assets?

A short exercise tells you most of what you need.

1. List every asset and its approximate value.
2. Next to each one, write how it is titled: sole name, joint with whom, trust, or beneficiary named.
3. For accounts with beneficiaries, write down the primary and contingent names and the date you last checked.
4. Total what falls into probate. This is your probate estate.
5. Compare the result with your will. Does the probate estate match what the will assumes? Do the beneficiary forms add up to the split you want?
6. Store the list with your documents, and tell your executor where it is.

Our article on [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) covers the tools you can use to shift an asset from one column to the other.
