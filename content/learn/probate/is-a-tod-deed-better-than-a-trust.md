Denise is 67, widowed, and owns a paid-off house that is most of what she has. Her friend at church says a transfer-on-death deed cost her $150 and a trip to the recorder's office. Her cousin says only a trust is safe. Both are partly right, and that is the problem: which one is better depends on who inherits and what could go wrong between now and then.

This page is about the choice itself. If you want the mechanics of recording a deed, our article on [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) covers them, and our [probate guide](/learn/probate) explains what you are trying to avoid.

## When is a TOD deed the better tool?

A TOD deed wins when the situation is simple and the cost of complexity is not worth paying. It tends to fit when every one of these is true:

- The house is the only significant asset without a beneficiary, because the bank and retirement accounts already name people.
- The beneficiaries are adults who can manage property and get along.
- You want the house split in plain shares, such as equally between two children.
- Your state recognizes the deed. A majority of states do, but not all.
- You are in reasonable health and have a durable power of attorney in place for everything else.

Denise fits most of that. Her two sons are in their forties, her accounts name them, and she wants the house sold and split. A deed does that job for the price of a recording fee and an attorney's drafting time. Putting her home into a trust would also work. It would cost more and give her little she needs.

## Where does a TOD deed break?

A deed is a single instruction that takes effect on one day, the day you die. Anything the instruction did not anticipate falls back into probate or into a dispute. These are the failures we see most often.

### The beneficiary dies first, and there is no backup

If Denise names her son Marcus alone and Marcus dies before she does, the gift usually lapses. Depending on the state and the deed, the house then goes through probate or passes to Marcus's descendants by default. Naming a contingent beneficiary on the deed fixes this. People forget it, and nobody notices until it is too late to change.

### A beneficiary is a minor or cannot manage property

A deed can name a child, but a child cannot hold title in a useful way. A court may need to appoint a guardian of the estate, which is the very court involvement you were trying to skip. The same problem arises with a beneficiary who is on SSI or Medicaid, because an outright inheritance can disrupt benefits. A trust, including a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust), solves this. A deed cannot.

### You lose capacity first

A deed does nothing while you are alive. If Denise develops dementia at 80, nobody can sell or manage the house unless she signed a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) that covers real estate. A funded trust lets a successor trustee step in without a court. The deed offers nothing here, which is why we pair it with a power of attorney so often.

### A mortgage or a debt follows the house

The deed passes the property, not a clean title. A mortgage stays attached, and the beneficiary takes over the house along with the lien. Federal law generally prevents a lender from calling the loan just because a home passes to a relative, but the payments still have to be made. Unpaid debts of the estate and, in some states, Medicaid recovery claims can also reach the property. See [what happens to debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

### The beneficiaries disagree about the house

A deed gives beneficiaries equal ownership with no rules about who pays taxes, who may live there, or how a sale is decided. Two siblings who each own half and want different things often end up in court. A trust can name a trustee, set a sale date and set rules for the carrying costs.

## When does a trust come out ahead?

A trust is the better tool when the plan has moving parts. The common triggers are a minor or vulnerable beneficiary, property in more than one state, a second marriage, or a wish to control timing, such as holding a share until a grandchild turns 25. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) also keeps the terms private, while a recorded deed is a public document.

The cost is real. A trust is a longer document, and it works only for assets actually retitled into it. A house left out of an [unfunded trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) goes through probate anyway. Our guide to [moving a house into a trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) shows the steps.

## How do the two tools compare?

| Question | TOD deed | Revocable trust. |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Avoids probate on the house | Yes, if recorded and not defeated | Yes, if the house is retitled into it. |
| Helps if you lose capacity | No | Yes, successor trustee takes over. |
| Handles a minor beneficiary | Poorly | Well. |
| Backup if a beneficiary dies | Only if you name one | Written into the document. |
| Covers other assets | Only the one property | Any asset titled in the trust. |
| Typical cost | Low | Higher. |
| Privacy | Deed is a public record | Terms usually private. |
| Available in every state | No | Yes. |

If the table points both ways for you, the [will-or-trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) is a quick way to see which side your answers favor.

## What about taxes?

Neither tool changes the tax result much. Property that passes at death generally receives a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) to its value on the date of death, whether it moves by deed or by trust. That is the main advantage both have over adding a child to the title today, which can cost the child that step-up on part of the house. Our article on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) shows the numbers.

## What should you do first?

Work through this list before you pick a side.

1. **Find out whether your state allows a TOD deed.** Some states call it a beneficiary deed or a TOD instrument. Your state guide is a starting point.
2. **Write down who gets the house, and who gets it if that person dies first.** If you cannot answer the second question in one sentence, the deed is incomplete.
3. **Check for minors and benefits.** Anyone under 18, or anyone receiving needs-based benefits, points toward a trust.
4. **Look at the mortgage and any reverse mortgage.** Ask the servicer what happens when the owner dies.
5. **Confirm you have a power of attorney.** A deed alone leaves a gap if you become incapacitated.
6. **Record the deed in the right office, in the right way.** In many states an unrecorded TOD deed is invalid, and a deed recorded after your death is too late.

## A realistic example

Denise has two sons, Marcus and Paul. Marcus has a young daughter, Lena, who is nine. Denise signs a TOD deed naming Marcus and Paul equally, with Lena as the backup for Marcus's half. Eight years later Marcus dies in a car accident. At Denise's death, Lena, now seventeen, would take her father's half outright. A guardian of her property would be needed until she turns 18, in most states.

Had Denise used a trust, she could have named a trustee to hold Lena's share until she was 25 and let the trustee pay for college. The deed cost less up front. It cost Lena more in the end. That is the pattern: the deed is cheap when nothing goes wrong, and expensive when something does.
