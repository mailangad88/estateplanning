Your mother lived in Naperville for forty years, spent her last eight months in a nursing home in a neighboring county, and owned a lake cabin two hours north. She died in a hospital in a third county. You are the executor, and you have a will and a death certificate. Which courthouse do you walk into?

This page answers that, and covers the odd situations that cause delays: nursing homes, second homes, snowbirds and property in another state. It is part of our [probate guide](/learn/probate), and the filing steps themselves are in [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate).

## What is the rule for choosing the county?

Probate goes in the county where the person was domiciled when they died. Domicile is a legal term for the place someone treats as their permanent home and means to return to. It is the same idea whether the estate has a will or not.

In Illinois, the circuit court of the county of residence handles the estate, and the filing is made with that county's circuit clerk. In New Jersey, the county Surrogate where the person was domiciled admits the will and issues letters. Middlesex County's Surrogate tells families to use the county on the death certificate, which is a practical shortcut when the address there is current. For people who lived in a different state, both states let the court of a county where they owned property take the case, which is the subject of a later section.

## Does a nursing home change the county?

Almost never. Moving to a nursing home, rehab center or assisted living facility, even a long way from home, is rarely a change of domicile. The person usually still counts the old house, or the family's hometown, as home. The exception is a person who sold their house, moved permanently to be near a daughter, registered to vote there and changed their driver's license. Then the new county can be the right one.

Consider Walter, 82, who lived in Bergen County, New Jersey, and spent his last year in a care facility in Morris County while the family kept his house. He had not sold it and still received mail there. The correct office was the Bergen County Surrogate, even though every bill came from Morris. A family that filed in Morris would have been redirected.

When the facts are fuzzy, collect the proof a court would ask for. Gather these.


- Driver's license or state ID address
- Voter registration
- Property tax bills or a deed showing the homestead
- Tax returns listing the home address
- Bank and Social Security mailing addresses
- Where the person told friends and doctors they lived

## What if there are two homes in the same state?

You file in one county only. A cabin in another Illinois county, or a shore house in another New Jersey county, is part of the estate, but the case is opened in the county of domicile. The executor then uses the letters and, where needed, a certified copy of the order to deal with the other county. The deed for the second property is recorded in that county's recorder or land records office.

The same holds for bank accounts and vehicles. They follow the domicile court, not the branch location. This is the central reason a single-state estate usually stays in a single case.

## What if the property is in another state?

Then you may need a second case. A court in the state of domicile has no power over land in a different state, so the estate often opens an **ancillary probate** in the state where the real estate sits. Our guide to [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) shows how that works, and the [Illinois and New Jersey comparison](/learn/probate/illinois-and-new-jersey-probate-compared) explains how each state treats a will probated elsewhere.

This is the reverse of the question above. A Floridian who owned a rental in New Jersey leaves a New Jersey property that needs a New Jersey case, in the county where the rental sits. A person who lived in Illinois and died owning land in Wisconsin needs a Wisconsin proceeding for that land, even though the Illinois court appoints the main executor. [Probating a will from another state](/learn/probate/probating-a-will-from-another-state) covers the other direction.

## How do you decide, step by step?

1. **Read the death certificate.** Note the residence address, which often matches the correct county.
2. **Check where the person's legal ties were.** Licenses, voter card, tax bills, homestead exemption.
3. **List all real estate.** Write down each parcel and its county and state.
4. **Separate in-state from out-of-state.** The in-state parcels ride with the main case. Out-of-state ones need their own.
5. **Call the clerk or Surrogate.** Ask which office handles estates for that address. Counties vary in whether they take walk-ins or require appointments.
6. **File the petition and original will in that county.** Bring the death certificate, the original will and the fee.
7. **Order letters and certificates.** You will need several copies for banks and title companies.

| Situation | Where the main case goes |
| --- | --- |
| Lived in one home in the state until death | County of that home |
| Nursing home in another county, house kept | County of the house |
| Moved permanently in with a child in another county | County of the new home, if domicile truly changed |
| Owned a second home in another county, same state | Still the county of domicile |
| Lived in another state, owned land here | County here where the land sits (ancillary) |
| Lived here, owned land in another state | Home county here, plus a case in the other state |

## What happens if you file in the wrong county?

The court will usually notice, because the petition lists the residence. It can dismiss the case, send it to the right county, or in some situations hear it anyway if no one objects and the facts support jurisdiction. In each of those, you lose time and sometimes a filing fee. An interested person can also object to venue, and that can slow a family that has already started to sell property.

If you are not certain, make a call before you file. County staff cannot give legal advice, but they can tell you what office handles an address. The [who can open a probate case](/learn/probate/who-can-open-a-probate-case) article covers who may file once you know where.

## What mistakes do our attorneys see most often?

Three come up again and again. The first is filing where the executor lives. An executor in Chicago who is settling an estate in Peoria County must go to Peoria County. The second is treating a hospital address as the home address, because the funeral director copied it onto a form. The third is assuming a cabin or condo needs its own case just because it sits in another county. In the same state it does not.

A fourth, quieter mistake is waiting. If the person lived in one county and the family cannot agree who should serve, an early petition in the right county gives the court someone to appoint. Letting weeks pass while relatives argue by text usually just gives creditors and utilities more time to send bills.

## Does the county affect cost and speed?

Yes, though not through the law. Filing fees, appointment systems and processing times differ by county. Illinois fee schedules are set county by county, and New Jersey court fees are set by statute and each county Surrogate publishes the schedule. Our articles on [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) and [New Jersey probate costs](/learn/new-jersey/probate-costs-in-new-jersey) show the line items. You cannot pick a cheaper county than the one the law assigns, so do not try to move the case.

You can look up a filed case later, as explained in [how to look up a probate case](/learn/probate/how-to-look-up-a-probate-case).
