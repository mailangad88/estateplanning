Losing someone is hard enough without wondering whether you are about to inherit their credit card balance. In most cases you are not. This page explains what happens to different kinds of debt after a death, the order in which an estate pays its bills and the situations where family members can become responsible. For the process that surrounds all this, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

The short version is that debts follow the estate, not the family. The details matter, though, particularly if you are the executor.

## Who is responsible for a dead person's debts?

The estate is responsible. The executor or administrator gathers the assets and uses them to pay valid debts and expenses before anything is distributed to heirs. If the estate has nothing, the creditors generally are not paid.

A relative is only personally liable when the law or a contract makes them so. These are the usual situations:

- **Co-signers and joint borrowers.** If you co-signed a loan or are a joint owner on a credit card account, you remain liable for it.
- **Joint accounts.** The debt on a joint account is generally the surviving holder's problem. An authorized user on a card, however, is normally not responsible.
- **Community property states.** In several states, community property law can make a surviving spouse responsible for debts incurred during the marriage, often limited to community assets. See [community property](/glossary/community-property).
- **Medical and related care.** A small number of states have "filial responsibility" laws that, in rare cases, have been used to pursue adult children for a parent's care costs, though they are seldom enforced. State Medicaid agencies can also seek reimbursement from the estate for care costs. See [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).
- **The executor's own mistakes.** An executor who distributes assets without paying valid claims may be personally liable.

If a collector says you owe a relative's debt, ask for written proof of why. You are not required to pay a debt you did not sign for.

## In what order are debts paid?

State law sets the order, and it generally follows this pattern. Check your state's statute, because details differ.

1. **Costs of administration.** Court fees, attorney fees, executor compensation and the cost of maintaining estate property.
2. **Funeral and burial expenses.** Often given high priority and sometimes capped in amount.
3. **Taxes and government claims.** Federal and state income taxes owed at death, and other government debts.
4. **Last illness medical expenses.** Often ranked above ordinary unsecured debt.
5. **Secured debts.** Mortgages and car loans, which are tied to their collateral.
6. **Other unsecured debts.** Credit cards, personal loans and utility bills, paid in whatever remains.

If the estate has enough money, everyone is paid in full and the order does not matter. If it does not, the order decides who gets paid and who goes without. An executor who pays a lower-priority creditor first may have to make up the difference. This is one reason an insolvent estate calls for legal advice.

## How do creditors make claims?

The executor must notify known creditors directly and often publish a notice in a local newspaper to reach unknown ones. Creditors then have a deadline to file a claim with the estate or the court. The window is generally some months, and a creditor who misses it is usually barred from collecting. Our article on [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) explains why that claim period sets a minimum timeline.

The executor reviews each claim and accepts or rejects it. If a claim looks wrong, such as a debt that has already been paid, one past the statute of limitations or a bill with errors, the executor can deny it. The creditor can then sue to have the claim allowed. Keep a log of every claim, what was decided and why.

## What happens to specific types of debt?

Each kind of debt follows slightly different rules.

### Credit cards and personal loans

These are unsecured and paid from the estate after higher-priority items. If there is no money, they are written off. Executors should usually stop using the card, close the account and send the death certificate to the issuer. Do not pay them in a rush.

### Mortgages and home equity loans

A mortgage is secured by the house, so it travels with it. If a family member inherits the house, the loan stays in place. Under federal law, lenders generally may not demand full payment merely because the borrower died when the house passes to certain family members, such as a spouse or child who will live in it. The heir can keep paying, refinance, sell or, in some cases, assume the loan. If the house is worth less than the mortgage, the heir may decline it. Our guide to [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) has the details.

### Car loans

These are also secured by the vehicle. The heir can keep paying, pay it off or return the vehicle to the lender.

### Student loans

Federal student loans are generally discharged on the borrower's death. Private student loans vary by lender, and a co-signer may remain responsible. Ask the lender about its policy and send proof of death.

### Medical bills and nursing home costs

These are claims against the estate. State Medicaid programs often have estate recovery rights for long-term care costs paid on the person's behalf, which can claim against the probate estate in many states.

### Taxes

Unpaid income tax and the final return are priorities. The executor must file the decedent's final tax return and pay any balance from the estate. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## Can creditors reach non-probate assets?

Usually not directly. Life insurance with a named beneficiary and retirement accounts such as IRAs and 401(k)s generally pass outside the probate estate and are protected from the decedent's creditors in many states, though protection can vary by state and account type. Jointly owned property goes to the survivor. A trust may or may not shield assets from claims, depending on its type and state law. Some states let creditors reach assets that passed outside probate if the probate estate is insufficient. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for a fuller sorting of assets.

## What if the estate is insolvent?

An insolvent estate owes more than it owns. The executor should stop distributing to heirs, pay claims in statutory priority and keep careful records. Heirs generally receive nothing and are not asked to make up the gap. The executor should also avoid paying any one creditor in full ahead of those with higher priority.

Some assets are exempt from creditors in many states, such as a homestead allowance for the surviving spouse or children, family allowances and certain personal property. These are set by state law and are usually claimed from the court.

## A realistic example

Denise, 62, died owning a house worth $280,000 with a $120,000 mortgage, a car, a checking account with $15,000, credit card debts of $22,000 and a medical bill of $9,000. Her son Marcus was executor. He paid the funeral home $8,000 and court costs from the checking account, then notified creditors. Her estate owed more than the cash on hand, but the house had equity. After consulting an attorney, he listed the house, paid off the mortgage from the sale proceeds, paid the medical bill and credit cards in priority order and distributed the remaining balance to Denise's two children. He never used his own money for her debts.

Now suppose Denise had also co-signed a loan for her friend. That debt would not be hers alone to pay, and the friend's obligation would continue.

## What should an executor do about debts?

The following steps keep an executor on safe ground.

1. Gather mail, statements and the decedent's credit report to find out what is owed.
2. Order certified death certificates and send them to creditors with a notice that the person has died and who the executor is.
3. Open an estate account and pay bills only from it, not personal funds.
4. Publish creditor notice where the state requires it, and note the claim deadline.
5. Review each claim, and deny the ones that are invalid or time-barred.
6. Pay in priority order once the claims period ends, or earlier only if the estate is clearly solvent.
7. Wait to make final distributions until debts and taxes are settled.

Our [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) goes into all of this in more depth, and [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explain how the affidavit route handles debts.

## Can you plan to protect your family from your debts?

Yes, to a degree. Keeping assets with named beneficiaries, and keeping life insurance and retirement accounts updated, gets money to your family without passing through the probate estate in the first place. Life insurance can be used to leave cash for a spouse or children whatever the estate owes. Planning cannot cancel a legitimate debt, and it should not be used to defraud creditors. A conversation with an attorney can help.
