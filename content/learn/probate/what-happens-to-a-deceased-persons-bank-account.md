Within a day or two of a death, most families have the same practical worry: the rent, the utilities and the funeral are due, and the money is sitting in an account nobody can touch. This page explains what the bank does, who has the right to the money, and when you can close the account without a court case.

The details depend on one thing, the way the account is titled. We start there. The wider process is in our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## The short version

- Tell the bank, but do not withdraw money or use the card.
- The account type decides who gets the money and what paper the bank needs.
- Joint and payable-on-death accounts are the easiest.
- A sole-name account might need letters from the court, or an affidavit if small.
- Final bills can be paid from the estate account once someone has authority.

## What does a bank do when someone dies?

It freezes the account after it learns of the death. Banks usually learn from a family member, a death notice or a Social Security report. Once frozen, automatic deposits and checks may bounce or be reversed, and the debit card stops working. The bank will not discuss balances with an unauthorized person.

A power of attorney does not help. It ends at death, as explained in our article on [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end). An agent who keeps using it can face serious consequences.

## Who gets the money, by account type?

| How the account is titled | Who can collect | Court needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Joint with right of survivorship | The surviving owner | No |
| Payable-on-death (POD) or transfer-on-death | The named beneficiary | No |
| Held in a living trust | The successor trustee | No |
| Sole name, small balance | Heir by affidavit, in states that allow it | Often no |
| Sole name, larger balance | Executor or administrator with letters | Yes |
| "Convenience" joint account | Treated as sole name of the deceased | Usually yes |

A "convenience" account is one where a child was added only to help pay bills. Many states presume the money still belongs to the original owner. Our article on the [hidden risks of joint accounts](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) explains why.

## How to close the account without probate

If the account has a surviving owner or a named beneficiary, the steps are short.

1. **Order certified death certificates.** Banks usually require an original or a certified copy. Order more than you think you need.
2. **Call the bank's estate or bereavement department.** The branch teller often cannot process it.
3. **Bring identification and the death certificate.** The bank will check its own records for the survivor or beneficiary.
4. **Decide whether to keep the account.** A surviving joint owner can usually just keep using it. A beneficiary can have the funds paid out or moved to a new account in their own name.
5. **Update autopays and direct deposits.** Cancel any that belong to the deceased.

Think of Teresa, whose husband Jim died. Their checking account was joint with survivorship, and the savings account named their son as POD. Teresa brought the death certificate to the bank, kept the checking account and got a new debit card. The son received the savings balance a week later. No attorney and no court were involved.

## What if the account is only in the deceased's name?

Then the choice depends on the balance and your state.

**A small balance.** Many states allow heirs to collect by signing a sworn statement that the estate is under the limit and that a waiting period has passed. Our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains the limits, and the form usually comes from the state court. Banks generally must honor a proper affidavit, but some ask for extras.

**A larger balance.** The bank will require letters from a probate court naming an executor or administrator. That person opens an estate account, deposits the funds and pays bills and distributions. Our guide to [filing probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) shows the first steps.

Either way, the decision turns on all of the person's probate assets, not just this one account. A $12,000 account could still force a full case if there is also a house in the same name.

## Can you close the account and just divide the money?

Not safely. Some families treat a parent's account as a family pot and split it among siblings. This can be risky for two reasons. First, creditors have a claim on the estate, and heirs who took money may have to give it back. Second, someone may have a better right to the funds, such as a person named in the will. Mixing the deceased's money with your own muddies the records and can look like conversion if a dispute arises.

If a real dollar need exists, such as the funeral bill, ask the bank about a limited release, or pay it yourself and keep the receipt. Reimbursement from the estate is a normal step.

## What about direct deposits and Social Security?

Social Security benefits are paid for the month after they are earned, so a payment received in the month of death, or later, generally has to be returned. The bank may return it automatically. Tell the payer of any pension or annuity as well. Do not spend those deposits, since the agency will pull them back, sometimes after you have already used the money.

## How to find accounts you do not know about

- Check the mail and email for statements and tax forms.
- Review the most recent income tax return, which lists interest and dividends.
- Look through checkbooks, deposit slips and a safe-deposit box key.
- Search your state's unclaimed property office. Dormant accounts, uncashed checks and old utility deposits end up there, and searching is free. If you find something, you submit proof that you are an heir or the executor. Many states list claims by the decedent's name.
- Ask the executor, if one is appointed, to request statements from banks in the area where the person lived.

Treat claim services that charge a percentage with caution. The state will return the money to you without a fee.

## What if the bank is difficult?

Bank staff sometimes give conflicting answers. Ask for the estate or legal department, and ask for requirements in writing. Quote the statute your state publishes for small estate affidavits. If the bank still refuses, an attorney can send a short letter, which usually resolves it. Our article on [what to do when a bank refuses a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) has similar approaches that apply here.

## How state law changes this

States set different small estate limits and waiting periods, differ in how they treat convenience joint accounts, and vary in how POD rules work. Some allow a spouse to claim a modest balance with a simple form. Your [state guide](/estate-planning) lists local detail, such as the [California guide](/estate-planning/california) or the [New York guide](/estate-planning/new-york).
