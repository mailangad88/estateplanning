You did what the courthouse website said. You found the form, filled it in, had it notarized and carried it to the branch where your mother banked for forty years. The teller scanned it, went to the back, came out and said the bank cannot accept it. The balance is $23,000 and the funeral bill is due.

This is common, and it does not always mean you did something wrong. This page explains why banks turn affidavits down, how to find out which reason applies, and how to decide between pushing back and opening probate. The general rules are in our [probate guide](/learn/probate) and the article on [small estate affidavits](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).

## Why do banks refuse a small estate affidavit?

Most refusals come down to one of seven reasons, and four of them can be fixed in a day.

| Reason the bank gives | What it usually means | Can you fix it? |
|---|---|---|
| "We need our own form" | The bank has an internal affidavit it prefers | Yes. Ask for it and complete it |
| "It is not notarized correctly" | Missing seal, wrong notary block or unsigned page | Yes. Re-sign before a notary |
| "The waiting period has not passed" | The bank holds accounts for a set time after death | Yes, by waiting, or ask for the policy in writing |
| "The name does not match" | The account name differs from the death certificate | Often. Provide a name-change or identity document |
| "The estate is too large" | The total you listed is over the legal limit | Only by probate or a different route |
| "There is a will" | The statute or the bank's policy requires probate when a will exists | Probate, usually |
| "We need a court order" | The bank's legal department has a stricter policy | Escalate, or probate |

Banks are cautious because they carry the risk. If they pay the wrong person, a creditor or another heir can come to them. The statute protects a bank that acts in good faith, but compliance staff are trained to avoid ever needing that protection. Our article on [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) explains what the bank is allowed to do from its side.

## Step by step: what to do when the bank says no

1. **Ask what exactly is wrong.** Get the answer in writing by email or letter if you can. "Our policy" is not an answer. A specific document or defect is.
2. **Check the basics yourself.** Is the death certificate certified? Does the account really belong to the decedent alone, with no payable on death beneficiary or joint owner? Is the estate really within the limit?
3. **Ask for the estate services department.** Branch staff rarely decide these cases. Most banks have a deceased-customer unit, and a phone call to it often works where the branch did not.
4. **Send a short letter.** State the statute, attach the affidavit and ask the bank to confirm in writing whether it will honor it and when.
5. **Add an attorney's letter if the bank still hesitates.** A letter on law firm letterhead citing the statute moves many files that a family could not.
6. **Set a deadline for yourself.** If you have no answer in about thirty days, begin probate instead of waiting.

### Illinois and New Jersey work differently

In Illinois the person signing hands the sworn affidavit to the bank under 755 ILCS 5/25-1. There is no court filing, so the bank decides on its own whether the document satisfies the statute. See the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) article for the contents and limit.

In New Jersey the affidavit is signed before the Surrogate and filed there. The bank can look at a Surrogate's record, which usually helps. A New Jersey bank may also ask for an inheritance tax waiver before releasing an account. The [New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) and [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) pages cover that paperwork.

## An example with real-looking numbers

This is an illustration. Marcus, 52, is the only child of his father, Leonard, who died in Joliet with $23,000 in a checking account in his name alone and a car. Marcus brings an affidavit and a death certificate to the branch. The bank refuses because the bank's own form was not used. Marcus calls the estate services number, gets the form by email, fills it in, has it notarized again and returns it. The bank releases the money in nine days.

Now change one fact. Leonard also had a $9,000 savings account at a second bank and a stock account worth $140,000 in his name alone. The total is $172,000, over the Illinois limit of $150,000 that applies as of 2026. The second bank is right to refuse, and so is the first if it learns of the total. Marcus would have to open a regular estate.

## When is a refusal correct?

Sometimes the bank sees something the family missed. These are the common cases:

- **Another account or asset pushes the total over the limit.** The bank may not know, but the affidavit swears the total is within the limit, and signing a false one is serious.
- **A will exists and a court should admit it.** In New Jersey the small estate affidavits are written for estates without a will.
- **Someone has asked for letters.** In Illinois the affidavit cannot be used if a petition for letters is pending or planned.
- **The account is jointly owned or has a beneficiary.** Then the money does not belong to the estate and the affidavit is not the right route. The [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) article shows how to sort accounts.

## What should you bring when you go back?

Bring the full set the first time and keep copies of everything you hand over. A typical packet includes:

- the original affidavit, signed and notarized, plus the bank's own form if it has one.
- a certified copy of the death certificate, since the bank usually keeps it.
- your photo ID and proof of your relationship, such as a birth certificate if you are a child.
- the most recent statement or account number.
- a list of all the decedent's assets with values, if the bank asks to see how you reached the total.

Write down the name of the person who accepts the packet and the date. If the bank loses a document, you will want that record.

## When should you give up on the affidavit and open probate?

Open probate when the bank has refused twice in writing, when you suspect the estate is over the limit, when heirs disagree or when other assets, such as a house, need a court appointment anyway. If you need letters for something else, the court's [letters testamentary or letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) will satisfy any bank, and the affidavit becomes unnecessary.

Probate costs more and takes longer, so it is a decision, not a punishment. The article on [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) lists the steps, and [is probate always required](/learn/probate/is-probate-always-required) helps you decide whether it is needed at all.

A final warning on a tempting shortcut. Do not take cash from the account with a debit card or a forged signature, and do not have a relative sign the decedent's name on a check. It can be treated as theft or fraud even when the money would have gone to you anyway.
