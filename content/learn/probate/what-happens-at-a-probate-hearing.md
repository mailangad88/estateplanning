Carla got a certified letter from a court in the mail. It said her late uncle's will had been filed for probate, and that a hearing was set for a Tuesday morning three weeks out. She is named in the will for a small amount. Does she need to take a day off work? Does she need a lawyer?

For most people in her position, no. This article walks through what really happens at a probate hearing, who has to be told about it, and the few situations where it is worth showing up. The general process is in our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## The short version

- A first hearing is often ten minutes long and mostly confirms paperwork.
- The judge is looking at validity, the right person to serve, and proper notice.
- You get notice so you can speak up, not because you must appear.
- Attend, or have a lawyer attend, when you have an objection or when your own rights are being decided.
- Some courts decide uncontested estates on written filings with no hearing.

## What does a probate court do, and what is a probate hearing?

A probate court is the part of the state court system that handles wills, estates, and in many states guardianships. In some places it is a separate court, and in others it is a division of the general trial court. The hearing is the moment a judge looks at the petition someone filed to open the estate and decides whether to grant it.

If a lawyer is handling the case, the executor often does not need to say much. The lawyer states the facts, the judge asks a few questions, and an order is signed. After that, the clerk issues [letters testamentary or letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration), the paper that lets the executor open accounts and deal with banks.

## What does the judge decide?

At a first hearing, the judge usually looks at four things.

1. **Is the death proven?** A certified death certificate is filed with the petition.
2. **Is the will valid, or is there none?** If there is a will, the court checks the signatures and witnesses, and a [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) makes that much simpler. With no will, the court applies the state's inheritance order.
3. **Who will serve?** The judge appoints the executor named in the will, or an administrator if there is no will or the named person cannot serve. Some states require a bond, which is an insurance policy protecting the estate.
4. **Was notice given?** The court wants to see that every heir and beneficiary who must be told was told, and that any needed newspaper notice ran.

Later hearings, if any, deal with specific requests: approving the sale of a house, settling a dispute, approving fees, or closing the estate. In states with supervised administration, the court is involved at more steps. In states with independent or informal administration, the executor does most things without coming back. Our companion article on [formal, informal and supervised probate](/learn/probate/formal-vs-informal-probate) explains the difference.

## Who has to be notified, and do beneficiaries have to be told?

Yes, in nearly every state the executor must tell people with a stake in the estate. The exact list and the timing vary, but it usually includes:

- Everyone named in the will, even for a small gift.
- The legal [heirs](/glossary/heir), meaning the people who would inherit if there were no will, even if the will leaves them nothing.
- A surviving spouse, and sometimes a person named as executor who is not serving.
- Known creditors, who get a separate notice (see [the creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period)).

Notice usually goes by mail, with a copy of the petition or a summary of it and the hearing date. Some states allow a waiver: you sign a form saying you have been told and do not need to be told again. Read a waiver before you sign. Signing one often gives up the right to receive notice of later steps, though not your right to later object.

Executors sometimes skip notice because they think the heirs are not important. That is a mistake. Missed notice can undo orders later, or give an unhappy relative an easy argument.

## When should you actually go?

Most people can stay home. These are the situations where attending makes sense.

| Your situation | Go to the hearing? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Named in the will, no concerns | Not needed | Nothing is being decided against you |
| Left out of the will but would be an heir | Consider it | You may want to raise a concern about validity |
| You think the will was forged or signed under pressure | Yes, with a lawyer | Deadlines to object can start running at this stage |
| You object to the person named as executor | Yes | The appointment is decided here |
| The petition asks to sell the house you live in | Yes | Your housing is affected |
| A waiver or a bond is being requested | Read it, maybe go | Waiving protections is hard to reverse |
| You are a creditor | Rarely | File your claim in writing instead |

Dennis was left out of his father's latest will, which cut him out after a falling out. He received notice, went to the hearing, and told the judge he wanted time to review the will with a lawyer. The judge set a date. That small step kept his options open. For the grounds and the deadlines, see [how to object to probate](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate) and [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## What if I cannot attend in person?

Call the clerk's office and ask three things: is the hearing in person or by video, can I appear remotely, and is a written objection accepted instead. Many courts now allow remote appearances, especially for out-of-town heirs. A written objection filed before the hearing is often treated as seriously as a spoken one, but only if it follows the court's format. If you plan to object, this is the moment to let a lawyer file it.

## Do you have to go to court for probate at all?

Not always. Many states let an uncontested estate run with paperwork only, or with one short hearing. Some use informal procedures in which the clerk or registrar, not a judge, approves the appointment. Very small estates may avoid court through a [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit). Estates that skip probate altogether, because everything has a beneficiary or sits in a trust, never see a hearing. Our article on [whether probate is always required](/learn/probate/is-probate-always-required) lists what decides it.

## How to find out what is happening in a case

Here is the sequence that works for most people.

1. Find the county where the person lived at death. That is almost always where the case was opened.
2. Call or search the probate clerk using the full name. Ask for the case number.
3. Ask whether there is a docket you can read online, or request copies of the petition and the order.
4. Read the notice. It will say the hearing date, the court address and what the court is being asked to do.
5. Write down the deadline for any response. Do not rely on memory.

Probate files are usually open to the public, with some exceptions. If privacy is a worry, [is probate public](/learn/probate/is-probate-public) explains what a stranger can see.

## What happens after the hearing?

If the judge grants the petition, the executor receives letters and can begin work: inventory, a bank account in the estate's name, notice to creditors, and the long middle stretch of paying debts. The executor's job list is in [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties). The timing depends on the state, the creditor window and whether anyone fights. See [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) for typical ranges.

If the judge does not grant it, the usual causes are fixable: a missing document, a notice defect, or a question about the will's signature. The petitioner corrects the problem and comes back.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether a hearing is required to open an estate, who must be served and how, whether beneficiaries may waive notice, and how soon after the hearing an objection must be filed. Some states have commissioners or referees who handle parts of the case. Check the court's own website for local forms, and see our [California guide](/estate-planning/california) or [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas) for how two large states treat the process differently.
