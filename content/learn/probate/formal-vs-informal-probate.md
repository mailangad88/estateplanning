When Priya went to the courthouse website to open her father's estate, she found a menu she did not expect. Informal. Formal. Supervised. Unsupervised. Summary. Her state offered all of them. The clerk's page did not say which she should pick, only that "your choice affects what the court will require."

It does, and the choice is often yours. This article explains what each label means, how to tell which fits your case, and where they overlap. The big picture is in the [probate guide](/learn/probate), and our decision map for [types of probate](/learn/probate) shows the options side by side.

## The short version

- Think of probate as a dial running from "court barely involved" to "court approves everything".
- Most states let families pick a lighter track if no one disputes the will.
- Heavier tracks exist to settle fights, uncertain heirs and problem executors.
- You can often start light and move up if a problem appears.
- A lawyer's advice matters most at the choice of track, not at each later step.

## Why are there different kinds of probate?

Courts want two things that pull against each other. They want estates closed cheaply and quickly, and they want protection for people who could be shortchanged. An estate where three siblings agree on a clear will does not need a judge watching every move. An estate where one heir suspects the will is forged does.

So many states built a menu. The Uniform Probate Code, which a number of states have adopted in whole or in part, splits proceedings into informal and formal. Other states, like Texas and Florida, use their own terms. The names change, but the questions the system asks are the same: is the will clear, are the heirs known, is anyone objecting, and is the estate big enough to need full process?

## Informal vs formal probate

| Feature | Informal probate | Formal probate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who approves | Clerk or registrar, on paper | A judge, after a hearing |
| Notice to heirs | Required, but simpler | Required, with a hearing date |
| Speed to appointment | Days to a few weeks | Weeks to months |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |
| Binding effect | Can be challenged later within the state's window | A judge's order that resolves the question |
| Best for | Clear will, cooperative family | Disputes, unclear heirs, validity questions |

Informal probate is the workhorse. The petitioner files the will, the death certificate and a form, swears the facts are true, and receives authority to act. Nobody holds a hearing unless someone objects. Formal probate puts the question to a judge, who rules on validity and appointment. That ruling is stronger against later attack, which is why a person who fears a challenge might choose it on purpose.

Our article on [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) walks the paperwork, and [what happens at a probate hearing](/learn/probate/what-happens-at-a-probate-hearing) covers the formal track.

## Supervised vs unsupervised administration

A second dial is about what happens after the executor is appointed.

- **Supervised administration.** The court stays involved. The executor files reports and asks permission to sell property, pay fees, or distribute. It is slower and costs more. It is used when the will requires it, when there are serious concerns about the executor, or when a beneficiary persuades the court it is needed.
- **Unsupervised or independent administration.** The executor acts on their own authority. They sell, pay and distribute without a judge reviewing each move. Texas calls its version independent administration, and many states treat unsupervised as the default.

Marcus served as his aunt's executor under an unsupervised track. He listed the house, accepted an offer, paid the claims and distributed the proceeds with a closing statement signed by each beneficiary. No hearing was needed. In a supervised case he would have asked the court's permission for the sale and the fees.

An unsupervised executor is still a fiduciary and still answers to the beneficiaries. See [what an executor can and cannot take](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate) for the limits.

## What is summary probate, and how is it different from a small estate affidavit?

Summary probate is a court procedure for estates that are small or simple. It cuts steps and notice periods. Florida uses it for estates under a statutory value limit, and other states have similar short forms under different names. A [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit), by contrast, skips the court altogether in many states. The heir signs a sworn statement, hands it to the bank or agency, and takes the asset. Which one you can use depends on the estate's value, what kinds of property it includes, and how long it has been since the death. Real estate often disqualifies the affidavit route, though some states have a separate process for it.

If you are not sure whether your estate qualifies, run the numbers in our [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker).

## What is a muniment of title?

This term is mostly Texas. A muniment of title is a court order that admits a will to probate as proof of who owns the property, without naming an executor. It is available when the estate has no unpaid debts apart from those secured by real estate, such as a mortgage. The will is recorded, and the heirs use the order to retitle the house and accounts. It is faster and cheaper than full administration, which is why Texas families with a clean will and no debts often choose it. See our [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas) for more.

## How do I choose?

1. **Is there a valid will?** A clear, self-proved will points toward informal or independent handling.
2. **Does everyone agree?** If an heir may object, expect formal proceedings, and consider [how to object to probate](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate) from the other side.
3. **Is the estate small?** If it fits your state's small-estate rules, you may skip the full process.
4. **Are there debts?** Large or uncertain debts raise the value of court oversight and a clear creditor window.
5. **Is real estate involved?** Title companies sometimes want a court order, which can push you toward a formal track.
6. **Is the executor trusted?** If beneficiaries distrust the executor, supervised administration adds a safeguard.
7. **Check local practice.** Courts differ in what they expect.

## What does it cost to switch tracks?

Starting light and moving up is allowed in many places. A contested will can turn informal probate into formal, and a worried heir can ask for supervision. The costs of the earlier steps are not wasted, but you will pay for hearings and likely a lawyer's time on top. If you expect a fight, going formal at the start often costs less overall. Our [probate cost guide](/learn/probate/probate-costs) gives the fee types, and [do I need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer) helps decide when to hire help.

## How state law changes this

Every state has its own vocabulary. Texas has independent administration and muniments of title. Florida has formal and summary administration. States that follow the Uniform Probate Code use informal and formal proceedings and supervised and unsupervised administration. California has full and limited authority under the Independent Administration of Estates Act. Illinois calls its main track independent administration as well. A few states still use older terms. Always search your state court's own self-help page, and treat any list of "types" as local.
