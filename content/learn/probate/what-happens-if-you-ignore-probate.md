Families sometimes put off probate for a good reason. The paperwork feels overwhelming, the money is tight, a sibling is dragging their feet, or someone assumed the house just goes to the kids. A year passes. Then the house will not sell, a tax bill arrives and a bank letter asks for documents nobody has.

This page explains what actually happens when probate is ignored, which consequences are real and which are myths. If you are behind, it also lays out how to start. The overall guide is in our [probate hub](/learn/probate).

## The short version

- Ignoring probate does not make an estate go away; it freezes it.
- Court-supervised assets such as a house, a sole-name bank account or a brokerage account cannot be moved without an appointed representative.
- Costs keep running: property tax, insurance, mortgage interest, utilities, repairs.
- Whoever holds the will may be legally required to file it.
- You can usually still open a case late, and it is better to do so sooner.

## Does the law require probate?

Whether the law requires a case depends on what the person owned. If everything passed by beneficiary form, joint title or trust, no case may be needed. Our article on [whether probate is always required](/learn/probate/is-probate-always-required) walks through that sorting. The problem arises when something titled in the decedent's name alone sits untouched.

Even when a full case is not needed, many states require the original will to be delivered to the court within a short period after the death. The person holding it, often a family member or the lawyer who drafted it, has the duty. In some states, willfully withholding a will is an offense and can lead to a fine or liability for losses.

## What happens to the assets?

They stay where they are, in the dead person's name. That has several effects.

**Bank and brokerage accounts stay frozen.** The institution will release them to a properly authorized person only. Meanwhile, checks bounce and automatic payments fail.

**The house cannot be sold or refinanced.** A buyer's title company needs a seller with authority. Heirs cannot sign. See our article on [selling a house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate).

**Vehicles cannot be retitled.** Registration may lapse, and insurance may be denied if no one owns the car on paper.

**Digital and business accounts drift.** Subscriptions renew, domain names lapse, and a business with one owner can stall without anyone empowered to sign.

## What costs grow while you wait?

The estate keeps paying, or failing to pay, even though nobody is in charge.

| Item | What can happen if ignored |
|---|---|
| Property taxes | Interest, penalties, a lien, and eventually a tax sale |
| Mortgage | Late fees, default, and foreclosure |
| Homeowner's insurance | Lapse or vacancy exclusion, leaving a loss uninsured |
| Credit cards and loans | Interest and fees add to the debt the estate owes |
| Income tax | A final return may be late, with penalties and interest |
| Utilities and upkeep | Frozen pipes, mold, vandalism and code violations |

An example: Rick's father died and left a house in his sole name worth about $240,000, with a $60,000 mortgage. Rick and his sister agreed to "deal with it in the spring." Nobody told the lender. Payments stopped, and the lender started foreclosure after about nine months. The siblings opened a case in a hurry, paid off the arrears from the sale and kept far less than they would have a year earlier.

## Can the family just split everything informally?

For furniture, jewelry and keepsakes, usually yes, if everyone agrees and nothing is owed. For titled assets, no. A bank will not accept a handwritten agreement among siblings. A county recorder will not record a deed from a person who is dead.

Informal division also carries a risk that people forget: the people who take property can be liable to creditors up to the value they received. That is true in many states. Our article on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains who pays what, and in what order.

## Does ignoring probate affect creditors and taxes?

Yes, but not in the way people hope. Creditors usually have a limited window to file claims once a case is opened and notice is given. If no case is opened, that window may never start, so claims can arrive months or years later. The estate also owes any final income tax and perhaps an estate tax return, and the clock for those runs regardless. Our [final tax returns guide](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) lists the returns that may be due.

## Could someone else open the case?

Yes. A creditor, a sibling, a person named in the will, or in some places the county itself can petition the court. If you are the person named as executor and do nothing, someone else can ask to be appointed instead, or can ask the court to compel you to act. The court may remove an executor who accepted the role and then neglected it. See our article on [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

## What if the case was needed years ago?

It is usually still possible to open one. Courts accept late petitions in most states, though some have limits for opening an estate long after death, particularly for creditor claims. The practical obstacles are different:

- Records and receipts are harder to find.
- Heirs may have died, moved or become unreachable, so descendants have to be traced.
- Property values must be reconstructed as of the date of death for tax basis and accounting.
- Taxes, penalties and interest have grown.
- The house may have fallen into disrepair, or the family may be living there in disputed circumstances.

Land passed down for generations without a case can end up held by dozens of heirs. The [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) article describes how that happens and how families clean up title.

## How to catch up: a step list

1. **Find the original will and any codicils.** If the will is in a safe-deposit box, ask the bank how to access it for filing.
2. **Order death certificates.** Get at least five certified copies.
3. **List the assets and how they are titled.** Our [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) can help.
4. **Check for urgent bills.** Pay or negotiate taxes, insurance and the mortgage first.
5. **Meet with an attorney in the county where the person lived.** Ask whether a late filing needs extra notice or an explanation.
6. **File the petition.** See [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) for the standard sequence.
7. **Open an estate account and keep records.**

## When ignoring probate is fine

If the estate truly falls under the small estate limit, a family can use an affidavit with no case at all. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit). If every asset has a beneficiary or co-owner, there may be nothing to probate. The point is to check, not to assume. A short call to the bank or the court clerk usually settles it.

## How state law changes this

States differ on deadlines for filing a will, penalties for withholding it, limits on opening an estate after a long time, and the way creditor claims are cut off. Some states are lenient, others strict. A resource like the [Florida guide](/estate-planning/florida) shows local timing. An attorney who works in your state can say what applies.
