A father dies, and the paperwork on his kitchen table shows a $9,000 hospital balance, three credit cards, a car loan and a house with a mortgage larger than its value. His checking account holds $2,400. His children want to know whether they now owe all of it. This page is for that moment. It explains what an insolvent estate is, who gets paid and who does not, what an executor must be careful about, and where family members really can be held responsible. The wider picture of how debts work is in our [probate guide](/learn/probate), and the general rules on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) are the foundation for what follows.

## What does "insolvent" mean for an estate?

An estate is insolvent when what the person owed at death is greater than what they owned. The test uses the probate estate. That is the property the executor controls: assets in the person's name alone, without a co-owner or beneficiary designation. Our article on [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the line.

So the arithmetic is not "all the debts against everything the family receives." It is the debts against the property that is actually in the estate. A $250,000 life insurance policy payable to a daughter is not in that pile, in most cases. Neither is a retirement account with a named beneficiary. Those pass by contract, and ordinary creditors generally cannot reach them, though there are exceptions in some states and for certain claims. Our piece on [whether creditors can take what a beneficiary inherits](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits) covers that boundary.

A very low estate is not the same as an insolvent one. An estate with $30,000 of assets and $12,000 of bills is merely modest, and everyone gets paid.

## Who is personally responsible for the shortfall?

Usually no one. The debts of a person who dies are claims against the estate, not against the children, and relatives do not inherit the debt along with the keepsakes. That is the rule in nearly every state, and it holds even if a collector sounds sure of itself on the phone.

There are exceptions that matter, and they are worth checking before anyone says "I'll take care of it."

- **Co-signers and joint borrowers.** If you co-signed a loan or share a credit card account as a joint holder, you owed it before the death and you still do.
- **Joint accounts.** Money in a joint bank account may belong to the survivor, but that does not make the survivor liable for the other person's separate debts.
- **A surviving spouse.** In some states, a spouse can be responsible for certain necessary expenses such as medical care. In community property states, debts taken on during the marriage can reach community property. Ask a local attorney how your state treats it.
- **The executor.** An executor is not liable for the estate's debts as such. An executor is liable for mistakes: paying in the wrong order, distributing early, or ignoring a claim that was properly filed.
- **Promises.** If a relative voluntarily agrees to pay, or pays part of a stale debt in a way that revives it, that person may have created a new obligation.

Two questions an adult child should ask before paying anything: did I sign this, and am I being asked to pay from my money or from the estate's? The answer decides almost everything.

## In what order does the executor pay claims?

When the estate cannot cover everyone, state law ranks the creditors. The order differs, but the pattern is similar nearly everywhere.

| Rank (typical) | Type of claim | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Costs of administration | Court fees, the executor's and attorney's fees, appraisal costs |
| Early | Funeral and burial expenses | A reasonable amount, in most states |
| Early | Family allowance or homestead | A surviving spouse or minor children may have a protected amount |
| Middle | Taxes and debts owed to the government | Federal claims have a priority of their own |
| Middle | Medical costs of the last illness | Ranking varies, and some states place them higher |
| Later | Ordinary unsecured debts | Credit cards, personal loans, store accounts, most medical bills |

Secured debts sit outside this ranking in a practical sense. A mortgage lender or a car lender can take the house or the car regardless of the order, and the unpaid remainder (if any) falls into the unsecured pile. Our article on [mortgages, car loans and student loans after death](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-car-loan-and-student-loans-after-death) walks through each of them.

Your state's ranking may differ from this table, and an executor should read the actual statute or have an attorney confirm it. If the estate is solvent, the order does not matter. It only bites when money runs short.

## What should an executor do first?

Slow down. The risk in an insolvent estate is not the debt. It is acting too quickly, because an executor who pays a favorite creditor, or hands something to an heir, can be asked to make the estate whole out of personal funds.

1. **List what the estate owns and what it owes.** Use statements, credit reports and the mail. Our guide on [the probate inventory and accounting](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting) shows how to keep the list.
2. **Do not pay any unsecured creditor yet.** Pay only the things needed to protect assets, such as insurance on the house, and keep receipts.
3. **Set aside the costs the law ranks first.** Funeral costs and administration costs usually come early. Do not pay yourself a fee without understanding the rules in [can an executor take money from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate).
4. **Open the case if one is needed and publish the notice to creditors.** The claims window cuts off late claims. How long it lasts and how notice works are in our article on the [creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period).
5. **Review claims in writing.** You can accept, reject or ask for proof. A rejected claim may make the creditor sue within a short window.
6. **Pay in the legal order, and stop when the money is gone.** Keep a record showing how each payment fit the ranking.
7. **Close the estate with the court's approval where required.** An accounting shows where every dollar went.

A short example. Priya, 41, is executor for her late uncle. His estate holds $18,000 in cash from selling his car. Court costs, a lawyer's fee and the funeral bill total $13,500, and claims from two credit card companies total $22,000. After the first group is paid, $4,500 remains, which is shared between the two card companies in proportion to their claims, assuming the state treats them as equal-ranked. Priya receives nothing as an heir, and she owes nothing from her own pocket because she paid the claims in order and kept the records.

## Do the heirs get anything?

Not from an insolvent probate estate. Creditors come before beneficiaries, so the heirs' share is zero until every valid claim in the estate is paid. That means a gift of a specific item in the will can be sold to pay debts if nothing else is available, with a few protections: certain family allowances, exempt property such as household goods, and sometimes the homestead.

What heirs can still receive is anything outside the probate estate. Accounts with a named beneficiary, a life insurance payout, a jointly held home that passes to the survivor and assets in a living trust usually pass directly. Creditors may have limited reach into a trust in some situations, so this is a place to get advice, not guess. The hard fact for families to hear is that the assets that protect them were set up before the death. See how a plan does that in our guide to [ways to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

## When should a family talk to a lawyer?

When the numbers are close, when a taxing authority or a long-term care program has a claim, when the house is the main asset and heirs want to keep it, or when the executor is a relative who could be blamed for a misstep. Medicaid may seek repayment from an estate for care it paid for. Our article on [whether you need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer) lists the situations in which an hour of advice pays for itself.

A related worry: should you take the job at all? If you are named executor of an estate you know is deeply in debt, you can decline in writing before you act. If you have already started paying bills, talk to an attorney before you decide to step back.

## How can you protect your own family from this?

By planning before you die. Name beneficiaries on retirement accounts and life insurance, keep an up-to-date list of debts and accounts, and tell the executor where things are. A family that finds one folder with every statement can deal with creditors in a week. A family that finds a shoebox of unopened mail spends months. If you carry large debts, an attorney can explain how your plan will hold up against them. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) is a good place to begin.
