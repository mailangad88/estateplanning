Frank, 74, owns a paid-off house worth $400,000. A friend told him he can skip probate by adding his daughter Alicia to the deed, and the clerk's office says the form is cheap. It sounds simple, and it often works as advertised. But the deed does more than avoid probate, and some of the side effects are hard or impossible to undo.

This article explains how the shortcut works, what it can cost you, and what to consider instead. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) covers the process you are trying to avoid.

## The short version

- Yes, a joint deed with a child usually skips probate on that property.
- The price is control, tax basis, exposure to the child's problems and possible Medicaid trouble.
- It treats children unequally unless every child is added or the property is shared another way.
- Alternatives such as a transfer-on-death deed or a trust can avoid probate without handing over ownership now.
- Check your state's rules before signing, because they vary widely.

## When the answer is yes: how it avoids probate

When you add a child as a joint tenant with right of survivorship, the two of you own the property together, and when one dies the survivor owns the whole. Because the property passes by the way it is titled, it does not go through probate. Our article on [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) covers the structure, and [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) puts it in context with other tools.

The catch is that the child becomes an owner on the day you sign, not on the day you die. That one fact drives nearly every risk below.

## When the answer is no: the risks

### It is a gift

Adding a child to a deed is a gift of a share of the property, usually half. If the value of that gift is above the annual gift tax exclusion, which is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, you may need to file a federal gift tax return (Form 709). Filing does not usually mean paying tax, because gifts reduce your lifetime exemption, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. But the filing is required, and it is often overlooked. Our article on the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) explains the rules.

### You lose part of the step-up in basis

Assets inherited at death generally get a new tax basis equal to value at death, which can wipe out the taxable gain. Lifetime gifts do not. The child takes your original basis on the gifted share.

Example: Frank bought his house for $100,000, and it is now worth $400,000. He adds Alicia. She receives half, with a basis of $50,000. When Frank dies, the house is worth $500,000. Only Frank's half gets a step-up, to $250,000. Alicia's basis is $50,000 plus $250,000, or $300,000. If she sells for $500,000, her taxable gain is $200,000. Had Frank left the house to her through a transfer-on-death deed or a trust, her basis would have been $500,000 and her gain close to zero. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

Some home sales qualify for an exclusion for a primary residence, but the child often does not live there, so do not count on it.

### Your child's problems become your problems

Because the child owns a share, their creditors can go after it. A lawsuit judgment, unpaid debt, bankruptcy or divorce can put your home in play. A child who is sued after a car accident, or who divorces and faces a property settlement, may find that half of Mom or Dad's house is counted as theirs.

### You need their signature

To sell or refinance, you generally need the child to sign. If they live far away, are difficult, or lose capacity, a sale can be delayed. If the child dies first, the house may come back to you, but the child's share may pass through their estate depending on how title was held.

### Medicaid and long-term care

Gifting a share of your home can count as a transfer for Medicaid. Most states look back 60 months before an application for long-term care, and a transfer within that window can cause a penalty period of ineligibility. See [the Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period). Some transfers to a caregiver child or in other special situations have exceptions, so ask before assuming.

### Unequal treatment of children

If Frank has three children and adds only Alicia, she takes the house at his death, no matter what his will says. The others may receive nothing from it. That is sometimes intended, but many parents do not realize the effect. If you want fairness, a will or trust is more flexible.

### Property tax and homestead effects

Some states and counties may treat the deed as a change of ownership that can lead to reassessment, or may limit homestead or senior exemptions based on who holds title. Some have exceptions for parent-to-child transfers. We cannot say what applies to you without your state and county, so check before filing.

## What are the alternatives?

| Option | Avoids probate? | You keep control while alive? | Step-up at death? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Joint deed with child | Yes | No, child co-owns now | Only on your half | Gift, creditor and Medicaid exposure |
| Transfer-on-death deed | Yes | Yes | Yes | Available in many states, not all |
| Revocable living trust | Yes | Yes | Yes | Needs funding; more setup cost |
| Life estate or lady bird deed | Yes | Mostly | Often yes | Available only in some states |
| Will only | No | Yes | Yes | Goes through probate |

A [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) lets you name who gets the home when you die while you keep full ownership. It is only available in some states, and rules about recording and who can be named differ. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) works everywhere and handles incapacity and multiple properties, at higher upfront cost. Our comparison of the [transfer-on-death deed versus a trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust) and of [joint ownership versus a trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust) goes through the trade-offs.

## What should you do before signing a deed?

1. **List your goals.** Avoid probate, treat children equally, protect against nursing home costs, or all three.
2. **Check your state options.** Ask whether a transfer-on-death deed or life estate deed is available.
3. **Look at the tax effect.** Estimate the child's basis and the possible capital gains.
4. **Consider Medicaid.** If long-term care is a realistic risk, get advice before transferring anything.
5. **Think about the child.** Marriage, debts, lawsuits, health and trustworthiness all matter.
6. **Have an attorney draft the deed.** Wording determines whether it creates a joint tenancy, a tenancy in common or something else.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether a transfer-on-death deed is available, whether life estate deeds that preserve control exist, how a joint tenancy is created, reassessment rules, and the look-back details. Our state guides are a place to start, and a local attorney can confirm the details.
