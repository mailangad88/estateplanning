After a death, one of the first questions an executor asks is: how long do I have to wait before I can safely pay the family? The answer lies in the creditor claims period, a window set by state law during which anyone owed money by the deceased can file a claim. It is also a major reason probate takes the time it does.

This page explains how the window opens, who has to be told, what happens at the end, and what an executor should do while it runs. The wider rules on paying debts are in [what happens to debts when someone dies](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate), and the whole process is in the [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## What is the creditor claims period?

It is a deadline. Once a probate case opens, the law gives creditors a set time to present their claims. If they miss it, the claim is usually cut off. The rule exists so that an estate can be closed in a reasonable time, and so that heirs are not threatened with old bills for years.

Two clocks are often at work. The first is the notice-based period, which starts when a creditor receives direct notice or when the executor's published notice runs. The second is an outside limit, counted from the date of death, after which most claims are barred however little notice anyone got. Many states use both. The lengths vary. A few months is common for the notice period, and a year or two is common for the outside limit, but some states differ, so check yours.

## When does the clock start?

| Event | What it does |
|---|---|
| Death | Starts the outside limit in many states |
| Court appoints the executor | Often starts the executor's duty to give notice |
| Executor publishes a notice to creditors | Starts the period for unknown creditors |
| Executor mails direct notice | Starts the period for that known creditor |
| Claim deadline passes | Late claims are generally barred |
| Executor reviews claims and pays | Distribution to heirs can follow |

A few states let the executor choose a faster process that shortens the wait. Others let claims be filed with the executor rather than the court. Read the notice forms the court gives you.

## Who has to be notified?

**Known or reasonably ascertainable creditors** have a constitutional right to actual notice. In practice, the executor goes through the decedent's mail, bank statements, credit reports and tax files, and sends written notice to each creditor found. The U.S. Supreme Court held decades ago that a creditor who is known or easy to find cannot be cut off by publication alone.

**Unknown creditors** are reached by publication. The executor places a notice in a newspaper of general circulation in the county for the period the statute requires. Courts usually supply the forms.

**Government agencies** need their own thought. The IRS and state tax agencies may have claims that are not barred by the state's rules. A state Medicaid agency may have an estate recovery claim if the person received long-term care benefits. Our article on [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) explains when this applies.

## What should an executor do during the window?

1. **Open an estate account** and use it for all payments. Keep receipts.
2. **Get letters.** Banks and creditors will not deal with you until the court issues them. See [letters testamentary and letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration).
3. **Collect and review the mail.** Every bill and collection letter is evidence of a creditor.
4. **Send direct notice to each known creditor** and publish where required. Keep copies and proof of mailing.
5. **Log every claim**, with the date, amount, what it is for and your decision.
6. **Pay funeral costs, administration expenses and taxes** as the law allows. Those usually come ahead of other debts.
7. **Hold distributions** until the claims period closes unless the estate is clearly able to pay everything.
8. **Pay valid claims in order** once the window closes.

## What happens to claims that arrive?

The executor can allow a claim, reject it, or negotiate it. A rejected claim is not the end: in most states the creditor then has a short time to sue to have the claim allowed. Common grounds for rejection include a debt already paid, a debt past the statute of limitations, a mistaken identity, or a debt that belongs to someone else, such as a co-signer.

Secured debts are different. A mortgage lender or a car lender holds collateral. It does not need to file a claim to foreclose or repossess, though it may file one if it wants the estate to pay a shortfall. A house with a mortgage is still the heir's to keep if the loan is paid, which is a topic for the [debts article](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

## What happens after the window closes?

Claims that were not filed in time are barred, and the executor can pay approved claims in priority order, then distribute to heirs. Executors who distribute earlier carry the risk. If a valid creditor appears and the money is gone, the creditor can pursue the estate's property in the hands of the heirs, and a careless executor can be held liable personally. This is a core part of the [executor's duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

Waiting costs little. Paying early can cost a lot. Many executors make a partial distribution once they are confident the estate has plenty of money, and hold a reserve until the final deadline.

## Can the wait be shortened?

Sometimes. If the estate is small, a state may let you skip the formal claims process through an affidavit, covered in our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit). If the debts are plainly smaller than the cash on hand, an executor can pay the obvious bills early, such as the funeral home and the utilities, and keep the rest in reserve. What an executor should not do is promise heirs a date. The statute, not the family's patience, sets the earliest safe one.

## A realistic example

Nathan, 45, is the executor for his mother, Joan, who died in March. Her estate is a house, a checking account and a car. Nathan is appointed in April and publishes the notice. The statute in his state sets a creditor window of a few months from first publication. He mails notice to the credit card company, the hospital and the electric company he found in her mail.

In the second month a collection agency files a claim for a store card Joan never used. Nathan rejects it, with a note that the account was not hers, and the agency lets it go. A dentist's bill arrives a week after the deadline. Nathan checks the dates and rejects it as late. Once the window closes, Nathan pays the allowed claims, the funeral bill and the attorney, and divides the rest between himself and his sister. He keeps his records for years.

## How does state law change this?

States differ on the length of the period, on whether publication is required, on whether claims go to the court or to the executor, and on whether a very small estate can skip the process. In some states a trust can run its own creditor process outside court, which is one reason trusts are used. Our state guides for [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) are a useful start, and the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) article describes one state's version in detail.
