A will is a piece of paper that was written somewhere, and probate is a court proceeding that happens somewhere. When those places differ, families worry that the document might not work. Usually it does. This page explains which state's court takes the case, what happens to a will that was signed in a different state, when a second case is unavoidable and how to handle the paperwork from a distance. For the basics of the process, read our [probate guide](/learn/probate) first.

## Which state's court opens the case?

The court of the person's domicile. Domicile means the place a person treated as their permanent home, the place they intended to come back to. It is not the same as where they died, where they were born or where the will was signed.

That is why a retired schoolteacher from Newark who dies in a hospital in Pennsylvania while visiting a daughter is probated in New Jersey, at the Surrogate's office in the county where she lived. Our article on [how probate works in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) walks through the first steps there. A man who lived in Peoria and passes away on a fishing trip is probated in Illinois, in the circuit court for his county.

The main case is called the primary or domiciliary probate. It covers everything that is personal property, wherever it sits: bank accounts, brokerage accounts, vehicles, jewelry. Real estate is the exception, and it comes up further down.

## Does a will from another state still count?

Usually yes. Most states have a rule that a will is valid if it met the signing requirements of the place where it was signed, the place where the person lived when it was signed, or the place where the person lived when they died. The details differ, and a few states are stricter, but the general direction is generous. Courts do not want a family to lose a will on a technicality when the person tried in good faith to do it right.

Where trouble arises is in the small differences. Suppose Dana signed her will in a state that requires two witnesses but no notary, then moved to a state that has a special self-proving affidavit form. Her will is probably still valid, but without a matching affidavit the court may ask one witness to confirm the signing. If the witnesses are dead or missing, that is a headache. Our articles on the [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) and on [moving states with a will](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) explain the pieces.

Other parts of a will can have different results under a different state's law, even if the will is valid. A spouse's right to take a share against the will, the treatment of a divorce, and how a lapsed gift is handled all follow the law of the home state. A will drafted for one state does not carry its law along on a move.

## What if the person died in a different state from where they lived?

Death certificates come from the state where the death happened. Probate follows the home. So the executor orders certified death certificates from the vital records office where the person died, then files the case in the home county.

If the death happened abroad or in a hospital far away, the paperwork may take longer to arrive. Our article on [what to do when someone dies away from home](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-in-a-hospital-hospice-nursing-home-or-out-of-state) covers the practical side, including transport and records.

## When do you need a second probate case?

When the person owned real estate in a state other than their home state, in their name alone. A court in the home state cannot transfer title to land in another state. That requires an ancillary case there. Our guide to [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains it in full, so here is the short version of how the pieces fit together.

| Situation | Where the main case is filed | Is a second case needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Person lived in one state, owned only bank accounts and investments elsewhere | Home state | Usually no |
| Lived in one state, owned a cabin in another in their own name | Home state | Yes, for the cabin |
| Cabin was in a living trust | Home state, if any case at all | No |
| Owned a cabin with a co-owner who takes by survivorship | Often none | No, once the survivor records the death |
| Moved states and never updated documents | Newest home state | Possibly, if old property remains |

A first case is often needed in the home state to give the executor authority. A second can be run from there by local counsel. Many states let an out-of-state executor ask the local court to recognize the home-state appointment, which is faster than starting from scratch. Some do not, and then the executor is appointed again.

## How do you use an out-of-state will in practice?

The mechanics are not hard, but the order matters.

1. **Locate the original will.** The home court wants the original. If it is in a lawyer's vault in the state where it was signed, ask that office to send it, and ask whether they will keep a copy.
2. **Find out where the person was domiciled.** Look at the last driver's license, voter registration, tax returns, the address on bank statements and where the person spent most nights. If the answer is obvious, move on.
3. **Contact the clerk in the home county.** Ask for the filing requirements for an executor who lives elsewhere. Our guide on [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) lists the common forms.
4. **File and receive letters.** With letters testamentary in hand, the executor can collect accounts, pay claims and manage property. See the difference between [letters testamentary and letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration).
5. **Identify any out-of-state land.** Search deeds in each state where the person might have owned property. A title company can run a search by name.
6. **Order certified copies.** For a second case, ask the home-state clerk for an authenticated copy of the will and the order admitting it.
7. **Hire local counsel in the second state.** The rules for foreign executors differ, and a local lawyer knows the court.

## Can the executor handle it from a distance?

Often. Many courts accept filings by mail or electronic filing, and hearings can sometimes be attended by video. Some require the out-of-state executor to appoint a local person to receive legal papers, post a bond, or both. A will that waives the bond for the executor can help in many states, but not in all.

A practical point: if the executor lives far from the home, the real work of securing the house, sorting belongings and meeting with the bank is hard to do by phone. Naming a co-executor who lives nearby, or hiring professional help, is common. Our article on [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) explains what the role covers, and [hiring help while staying protected](/learn/after-a-death/executor-hiring-help-and-staying-protected) explains how to delegate safely.

## What if there is a dispute about which state was home?

It does not happen often, but when it does, it is serious. A person with a house in New Jersey and a winter condo elsewhere might leave heirs who disagree about where the "real" home was, often because one state has a tax the heirs want to avoid or a favorable law they prefer. A court will weigh facts. Where did she vote? Which address was on her tax return? Where were her doctors, her church, her mail? How she described the place to friends can count too.

Two states may each claim the person, and each can demand taxes. That costs families a lot of money. People who split the year should settle the question while alive: file taxes consistently, register to vote in one place, get one driver's license, and say so in the will. A clear statement that the will is made by a resident of a particular state does not end the question, but it helps. A [plan review after any move](/learn/what-if/moved-states-plan-not-updated) is the simplest protection.

## How can you avoid the problem for your own estate?

Put out-of-state real estate into a revocable living trust, or use a beneficiary deed where your state allows it. Keep your will current with the law of the state where you live. Keep the original in one place and tell your executor where it is.
