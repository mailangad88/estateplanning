Grace's father died in September. He left a house with a mortgage, a truck with a loan on it, two credit cards, a hospital bill, and a student loan he had co-signed years ago for Grace's cousin. Her phone has already rung twice with a collector on the line. She keeps hearing conflicting advice: "you inherit the debt," "you don't owe a thing," "just pay it to be safe."

None of those is quite right. Each type of debt has its own rule, and the rule depends mostly on whether the debt is secured by an asset, who signed, and how much the estate holds. This page takes them one at a time. For the general order of payment, read [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate), and for the wider process see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## The short version

- You are not personally liable for a dead relative's debt just because you are related.
- The estate pays first. What is left, if anything, goes to heirs.
- Secured loans can be kept by paying them, or the collateral can be sold or given up.
- Never pay an old debt with your own money out of guilt, and never sign a collector's form without reading it.
- Keep a dated note of every call. The executor handles the claims.

## Who pays the debts?

The estate does. The executor collects what the person owned, notifies creditors, pays valid claims in the order the state sets, and then distributes the rest. If the money runs out, the unpaid creditors absorb the loss. Heirs are not asked to make up the difference. Joint debts are the exception, because the surviving co-owner was already on the hook.

Some debts get paid before others. A common order puts administration expenses, funeral costs, and taxes ahead of secured and unsecured creditors, with variations by state. That ranking is why credit card companies are often last in line, and why [the creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) matters: creditors who miss the deadline can lose their right to be paid.

## How each kind of debt behaves

| Debt | Secured? | What happens | What an heir can do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mortgage | Yes, by the house | Stays in place; payments must continue | Keep paying, refinance, sell, or let it go |
| Home equity loan | Yes, by the house | Same as a mortgage | Same |
| Reverse mortgage | Yes, by the house | Due after the last borrower dies | Pay, sell, or surrender within the allowed window |
| Car loan | Yes, by the car | Follows the car | Keep paying and retitle, or sell |
| Federal student loan | No | Discharged at the borrower's death | Send the death certificate to the servicer |
| Private student loan | No | Depends on contract and any co-signer | Ask the lender about discharge; co-signers may remain liable |
| Credit cards | No | Claim against the estate | Executor pays if funds remain |
| Medical bills | No | Claim against the estate | Executor pays by priority |
| Taxes | No, but high priority | Paid early by the estate | Executor files final returns |

## What happens to a mortgage after death?

The mortgage does not vanish and it does not move to the heir by name. It sits on the house. The lender wants payments to keep coming. If the house goes to a spouse, a child, or certain other relatives, federal law generally stops the lender from demanding the whole loan at once just because ownership changed. The heir can usually ask to be treated as the successor and continue payments, though lenders still require paperwork.

Stephen inherited his mother's house with a $90,000 balance and decided to keep it. He called the servicer, sent a death certificate and the letters from the court, and kept making the payments. The loan stayed exactly as it was. If he cannot afford it, he can sell, and the sale pays off the loan first. Any gain beyond the loan is his. For heirs of a home, our guide to [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) covers the next steps, and [what if the deed is still in the dead person's name](/learn/what-if/house-still-in-late-spouses-name) covers title.

If the loan is larger than the house is worth, the lender can foreclose and, depending on the state and the loan type, may or may not pursue the estate for a shortfall. Heirs are not forced to keep an underwater house.

## What happens to a reverse mortgage?

A reverse mortgage works differently, because it is meant to be repaid when the borrower dies or moves out. It usually becomes due when the last borrower dies. Heirs get a limited time to decide, often counted in months, and extensions are sometimes available if the heir is trying to sell or refinance. Many programs let heirs keep the house by paying the lesser of the loan balance or the appraised value, which protects them when the loan has grown past what the house is worth. Contact the servicer quickly, because deadlines start from the date of death or the date of the notice. See also [protecting your home](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) for how this loan type interacts with long-term care planning.

## What happens to a car loan?

The lender holds the title or a lien, so the car is the security. An heir who wants the car keeps paying and then retitles it. Our article on [transferring a car, boat or RV after death](/learn/property-and-assets/transfer-a-car-boat-or-rv-after-death) walks through the paperwork. If nobody wants the car, it can be sold. If the price falls short of the balance, the rest is an unsecured claim against the estate, not against the heir.

## What happens to student loans?

Federal student loans, including Direct loans and Parent PLUS loans, are generally discharged when the borrower dies, and a Parent PLUS loan is discharged if the student dies. The servicer will want a certified death certificate. Private student loans are governed by their contracts. Some lenders discharge them on death, many do not, and a co-signer may remain responsible. That is the situation in Grace's family: the cousin's loan her father co-signed may be a claim against his estate, depending on the lender's terms. Ask the lender for the contract language in writing.

## What happens to credit card and medical debt?

These are unsecured, which means no asset is attached to them. The issuer or provider files a claim with the estate. The executor pays valid claims after higher-priority ones if money is left. If the estate cannot cover them, they go unpaid. Authorized users on a credit card owe nothing. Joint holders do owe the balance. A related article, [can creditors take what a beneficiary inherits](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits), explains why accounts with a named beneficiary are usually out of a creditor's reach.

Medical debt also deserves a second look. Some states let hospitals and nursing homes claim against a spouse or file against an estate in specific ways, and Medicaid may recover costs after death. Read our overview of [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) before assuming a bill is simply gone.

## Is a surviving spouse responsible?

For debts in the dead spouse's own name, usually no. A spouse is responsible for joint accounts, for loans they co-signed, and for debts taken on for the family in some places. In community property states, debts incurred during the marriage may reach community assets, though not necessarily the survivor's separate property. A few states have a "necessaries" rule that can make a spouse liable for medical care. If a collector claims you owe, ask for proof of the debt in writing and speak with an attorney before paying or acknowledging anything.

## What should you do first?

1. **Tell the lender or issuer about the death** once someone has authority to act. Send a certified death certificate when asked.
2. **Stop automatic payments** on accounts the estate cannot afford, but keep secured loans current where you want the collateral.
3. **Do not pay from your own money.** Pay from estate funds, through the executor.
4. **Ask collectors for written validation** of any debt they say exists.
5. **List every debt with the balance and the collateral.** This feeds the inventory the executor files.
6. **Decide asset by asset** what you want to keep, and which loan you can carry.
7. **Watch the claims deadline.** It protects the estate from late surprises.

## How state law changes this

States differ on the priority of claims, the length of the creditor window, whether a spouse can be reached for a partner's medical debt, how community property is treated, and how a deficiency on a mortgage is handled. Our [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas) and [California guide](/estate-planning/california) show two community property approaches. A local attorney can confirm what applies.
