Someone has died and you want to know whether a probate case has been opened. Maybe you are an heir who has heard nothing. Maybe you are a neighbor, creditor or relative trying to find out who is handling the estate. Or you have been named executor and need to confirm that nobody else filed first.

This page covers the mechanics of finding a case: which court, how to search, what the results mean and what to do when the search comes back empty. The question of whether you are entitled to see the will is covered in [can I see the will after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-see-the-will-after-someone-dies). The broader privacy question is in [is probate public](/learn/probate/is-probate-public). The [probate guide](/learn/probate) has the rest.

## Which court should you search?

Search the court in the county where the person lived when they died. In most states that is a probate court, a surrogate's court, a circuit court's probate division or a superior court. The name varies, and the structure is almost always by county. A person who lived in one county for forty years and died in a nursing home across the state line is usually probated where the home was.

If the person owned real estate in another state, a second case may exist there. That is an ancillary case, and it is filed in the county where the land sits. See [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) for how the two relate.

## How do you search by name?

1. **Find the county's court website.** Search for the county name plus "probate court" or "clerk of court." Use the official county or state court site, not a third party that sells records.
2. **Look for "case search," "estate search" or "docket search."** Some counties require registering for a free account.
3. **Enter the last name first, then the first name.** Start broad. A search for "Hernandez, Maria" will return too much, and you can narrow by year.
4. **Filter by case type.** Choose estate, decedent or probate, not civil or family.
5. **Check variations.** Try nicknames, maiden names, a middle initial and common misspellings. Clerks key in the name from the petition, and a hyphen or a "Jr." can break an exact match.
6. **Note the case number and the filing date.** Everything else is requested using those.

If the court has no online search, call the clerk. Ask for a search of the estate index by name, and ask whether it covers wills that were deposited without a case.

## What will the search show?

A typical result lists the case number, the filing date, the name of the person who has died and the status of the case. The docket is a running list of what has been filed: the petition, the will, the order appointing the executor, notices to creditors, the inventory and eventually a petition to close. Some courts show only a summary, and the documents themselves must be requested.

The name of the executor or administrator tells you whom to contact. The appointment date matters too. A case where letters were issued years ago and nothing else has happened may be dormant, and that may be a problem for heirs. The [executor not doing their job](/learn/probate/executor-not-doing-their-job) guide covers what to do.

Keep in mind what the public docket usually does not show: a will that was never filed, assets held in a trust, accounts that pass by beneficiary and anything the court has ordered sealed.

## How do you get copies?

Once you have the case number, you can ask the clerk for copies. Most courts offer three ways: in person at the clerk's counter, by mail or email with a written request, and in some counties through the online portal. Courts usually charge a per-page fee for copies and an additional fee for a certified copy, which carries the clerk's stamp and is what banks and title companies ask for. Bring a case number and a photo ID if you go in person.

If you are not an interested person in the estate, you can generally still read and copy the file, since it is public. If you are an heir who was never notified, tell the clerk, and consider asking an attorney to review the notice requirements. The guide on [how to prove you are an heir](/learn/probate/how-to-prove-you-are-an-heir) is the next step if the family has to document the relationship.

## What if nothing comes up?

An empty result does not mean there is no will. Work through these possibilities in order.

- **Wrong county.** Try the county where the person lived most recently, then the one where they owned property or where they died.
- **Too early.** A case may not have been opened yet. Many families wait several weeks or months.
- **Different name.** Search under a former name or the way the person signed documents.
- **No case needed.** The estate may have been small, or the assets may have passed by beneficiary, joint ownership or a trust. See [is probate always required](/learn/probate/is-probate-always-required).
- **Out of state.** The person may have lived elsewhere in the last years of life.
- **Will deposited, not probated.** In some states a will can be on file with the clerk without a case.

Carla's uncle died in Arizona after living in Ohio for decades. She searched Ohio and found nothing, then Arizona and found nothing. The case turned out to be in the Arizona county where he had spent his last winters and, by then, claimed as his home. Searching three counties took twenty minutes once she thought of it.

If you still cannot find a will, work through the steps for locating one in [how to find a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) and the guide on [lost original wills](/learn/wills/lost-original-will).

## What if you are a creditor or a neighbor, not a family member?

Anyone can search. A creditor who finds a case should note the executor's name and the date letters were issued, because the deadline to file a claim usually runs from the notice or the appointment. The [creditor claims period guide](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) explains the clock. A neighbor or friend who only wants to know who is handling the house should contact the executor named in the docket, not the court, since the clerk cannot give advice or relay messages.

## Is it safe to hand over information when you search?

Searching a court index is free and does not require you to give any personal details beyond your own contact information, if you want copies. Be cautious about sites that charge for "probate records" or ask for the full Social Security number of the person who died. Official clerk sites do not. Estate solicitors also read filings to find heirs and mail offers, so a letter that mentions a recent case is not itself proof of a real inheritance.
