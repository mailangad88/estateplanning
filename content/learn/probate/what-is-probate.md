If someone close to you has died and you have heard the word "probate," you are probably wondering what it involves, how long it will take over your life, and whether you have to do it. This page explains the process from the first filing to the final closing, so you know what is coming. For the full picture of the topic, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

Probate is less mysterious than it sounds. It is a legal procedure with a predictable order of steps, run by a court, with a family member or professional doing most of the actual work.

## What is probate, exactly?

Probate is the court-supervised process of gathering a dead person's assets, paying their debts and delivering what remains to the people entitled to it. The court has three jobs: confirm who is in charge, give creditors a fair chance to be paid, and make sure property ends up with the right people.

The person who died is called the [decedent](/glossary/decedent). The property that goes through the court is the [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate). The person in charge is the [executor](/glossary/executor) if the will names one, or an [administrator](/glossary/administrator) if there is no will or the named executor cannot serve. Many states use the umbrella term [personal representative](/glossary/personal-representative) for both.

### Which assets go through probate?

Only assets titled in the decedent's name alone, with no beneficiary and no co-owner, go through probate. A house deeded only to the decedent, a bank account with no payable-on-death designation, a car, and personal belongings are typical examples.

Assets that pass by contract or by title skip the court entirely: life insurance and retirement accounts with a named beneficiary, a house held in [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy), accounts with a [payable-on-death](/glossary/payable-on-death) designation, and property in a [living trust](/glossary/living-trust). Our article on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) walks through how to sort each kind of asset.

## How does probate work, step by step?

Most probate cases follow the same seven stages, though names and forms vary by state and even by county.

1. **Locate the will and file a petition.** Someone, usually the named executor, files the original will and a petition with the probate court in the county where the decedent lived. Most states require a person holding a will to deliver it to the court after a death. If there is no will, a relative petitions to be appointed administrator.
2. **Notify heirs and beneficiaries.** The court requires notice to the surviving spouse, children and other heirs, and to everyone named in the will. This gives anyone who wants to object a chance to speak.
3. **Get appointed.** If the will is valid and no one contests it, the judge appoints the executor. The court issues a document, called [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary) when there is a will (letters of administration when there is not), that proves the executor's authority. Banks, title companies and brokerages ask to see it.
4. **Open an estate account and take inventory.** The executor gets a tax ID number for the estate, opens an estate bank account and lists everything the decedent owned, often with date-of-death values. Real estate and valuable personal property may need an appraisal.
5. **Notify creditors and pay debts.** The executor publishes a notice in a local newspaper in many states and sends direct notice to known creditors. Creditors have a limited window, often a few months, to file claims. Valid debts, final expenses, taxes and administration costs are paid before heirs receive anything. For more, see [what happens to debts when someone dies](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).
6. **File tax returns.** The executor files the decedent's final personal income tax return and, if the estate earns income, an estate income tax return. Larger estates may owe federal or state estate tax; see our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).
7. **Distribute and close.** After debts and taxes are paid and the claims period has ended, the executor distributes what is left according to the will or state law, files an accounting if the court requires one, and asks the judge to close the estate.

### Formal versus informal probate

Many states offer two tracks. Informal or "unsupervised" probate lets the executor act with minimal court involvement after being appointed, which is faster and cheaper. Formal or "supervised" probate involves hearings and court approval of major steps, and is used when there is a dispute, a complicated estate or a real concern about the executor's conduct. A few states, such as Florida, label their tracks "formal administration" and "summary administration." Ask the court clerk or an attorney which tracks your state offers.

## A realistic example

Ray, 71, died owning a house in his own name, a checking account, a brokerage account with no beneficiary and a 401(k) naming his daughter Dana. His will named Dana as executor and split everything equally between Dana and her brother Marcus.

The 401(k) went straight to Dana through the beneficiary form with no court involvement. The house, checking and brokerage accounts were probate assets. Dana filed Ray's will, was appointed, opened an estate account, sold the house after an appraisal, paid Ray's last credit card bill and his final income taxes, and split the remainder with Marcus. The 401(k) was never part of that process, which is why the beneficiary form mattered as much as the will.

## What does the court actually do?

The court does not run the estate day to day. A judge or clerk reviews filings, rules on disputes, approves certain actions and signs off at the end. The executor makes the decisions and carries them out. In an unsupervised estate the judge may see the case only twice: once to appoint and once to close.

## Is probate public?

Yes. Wills filed with the court and the inventory of assets are generally public records, and anyone can look them up. That is one reason some families prefer to keep assets out of probate. Public filings can invite unwanted attention, solicitations from strangers and, occasionally, disputes. A trust avoids this, though trust-based plans have their own costs; see [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).

## What does probate not do?

Probate does not reach assets that already have a destination, it does not resolve the family's grief or disagreements, and it does not by itself create any tax. It also does not require the executor to be a lawyer. It does require care, because an executor who distributes too early, ignores a creditor or mishandles estate funds can be held personally responsible. Our guide to [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) covers the responsibilities in detail.

## When is probate simple, and when is it hard?

Probate tends to go smoothly when there is a clear, valid will, an organized executor, a modest list of assets in one state and heirs who get along. It gets complicated with real estate in another state (see [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate)), a business, large debts, a missing or contested will, or heirs who cannot be found. If there is no will at all, state [intestacy](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) rules decide who inherits, which may not match what the person would have wanted.

Costs and timing depend on those factors. We cover them in [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) and [what probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs).

## What if the estate is small?

Many states let heirs collect modest estates without opening a full probate case. The family files a short sworn statement, a [small estate affidavit](/glossary/small-estate-affidavit), with the institution holding the asset. Thresholds vary widely from state to state and sometimes depend on whether real estate is involved. See our article on [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for how to find out whether your family qualifies.

## Can probate be avoided?

Often, yes, at least in large part. Beneficiary designations, joint ownership with the right kind of survivorship, transfer-on-death registrations and a funded living trust can all pass assets directly. They carry tradeoffs, which we explain in [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate). A plan should be built around your family and your state, not around avoiding probate for its own sake.
