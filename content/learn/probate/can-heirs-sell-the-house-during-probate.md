A parent dies, the house is in her name, and the family wants to sell it. The roof needs work, the taxes are due, and nobody wants to pay for a home no one lives in. Then someone says the house cannot be sold until probate is finished. That is not quite right, and the details matter.

This page explains who can sell a house that is going through probate, what the buyer and title company will ask for, and how to avoid the mistakes that stall a closing. For the general process, see our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## The short version

- Heirs do not own a probated house in a way that lets them sign a deed individually.
- The executor can sell once appointed, subject to the will and state law.
- Court approval is required in some states and not in others.
- The house usually sells faster if the executor is appointed early and the will gives a power of sale.
- Selling inside probate often saves family friction, and the tax treatment is generally favorable.

## Who can sell: heirs or the executor?

The executor, once the court issues letters. Until then, no one has authority. A house in a dead person's sole name does not automatically belong to the heirs the moment of death in a form they can sell. Title is held, in effect, for the estate while debts and expenses are sorted out. The [executor](/glossary/executor) becomes the person who can sign for the estate.

Consider Priya and her brother Anil. Their father died and left a will leaving the house equally to them. They found a buyer in two weeks and signed a contract. The title company then asked for letters. There were none, because no case had been opened. The buyer's financing expired while the court appointed Priya. The delay cost them the sale and a month of mortgage payments.

The lesson is plain. Contact an attorney or the court about opening the case as soon as the family decides to sell.

## What does the executor need to sell?

A buyer's title company will ask for several items:

- The death certificate.
- Letters testamentary (or letters of administration if no will).
- A certified copy of the will, in many states.
- Proof that the will grants a power of sale, or the court order approving the sale.
- Payoff information for any mortgage or lien.
- Sometimes a release of the state estate tax lien, where that applies.

Different states handle this in different ways. In states with "independent" or "unsupervised" administration, the executor can usually sell with little court involvement. In states with stricter oversight, the executor may need to petition the court, give notice to heirs, and sometimes accept the highest bid in a confirmation hearing. Our article on the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) shows one state's version of independent versus supervised administration.

## What if the will does not mention selling?

Courts and statutes fill the gap in different ways. Some states treat the executor as having an implied power to sell whatever is needed to pay debts and distribute the estate. Others limit the executor unless the will or the court says otherwise. In some places, if every heir agrees in writing, the court will approve a sale with no hearing.

When the will leaves the house to one person and the rest of the estate to others, it becomes a question of whether the sale is needed to pay debts. If there is plenty of cash, the executor may simply distribute the house as written. If the estate is short, the house may have to be sold.

## How the sale works: step by step

1. **Open the case and get letters.** Do this first. See [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) for the filings.
2. **Secure the house.** Change locks if needed, forward mail, keep utilities on and confirm insurance covers a vacant home.
3. **Get a date-of-death appraisal.** This sets the tax basis for the heirs and is often required by the court. The [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) usually means little or no capital gains tax if the sale is soon after.
4. **Check whether court approval is required.** Ask the court clerk or an attorney.
5. **Choose an agent and list.** The listing agreement is signed by the executor in that capacity.
6. **Accept an offer and, if needed, get confirmation.**
7. **Close.** The title company pays off the mortgage and costs, and the executor deposits the net into the estate account.
8. **Distribute after debts and claims.** Proceeds are not available to heirs until the estate is ready to distribute. Our article on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains the order.

## What if the heirs would rather keep the house?

Selling is not the only path. The executor can distribute the house to the heirs as the will says, and they take title as co-owners. That is often easier at first and harder later. If one sibling wants to keep it, a buyout with a refinance or a payment from other assets works well. If everyone wants to keep it but nobody agrees about costs, the shared ownership can become a dispute. Our article on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) describes the problems that follow when co-owners have no written agreement, and [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) lists the options.

## What about taxes on the sale?

Inherited property generally receives a stepped-up basis equal to its value at death. If the house sells for close to that value, there is little taxable gain. A sale for much more than the date-of-death value creates a gain on the difference. The estate or the heirs report it depending on who held title at the sale. See [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) for details on timing and reporting.

Property taxes continue until closing, and a prorated amount is usually settled at the closing table.

## Can the house be sold without probate?

Sometimes. If the house was held in a living trust, the successor trustee can sell without court. If it passed by a transfer-on-death deed, the named beneficiary owns it after the owner dies and can sell after recording the deed and death certificate. If it was held jointly with survivorship, the surviving owner can sell. Our article on [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) explains how these deeds work and where they fall short.

A small estate procedure in some states permits transfer of a house of modest value by affidavit, but many states exclude real estate. Check [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) before assuming it applies.

## What are the common traps?

- **Signing a contract before appointment.** A buyer can walk away, or the contract may be unenforceable against the estate.
- **Ignoring a mortgage.** Payments need to continue, or the lender can foreclose.
- **Letting insurance lapse.** Vacancy can void a standard homeowner's policy.
- **Selling too cheaply to a family member.** A below-market sale can look like self-dealing, which can lead to claims against the executor.
- **Spending sale money early.** Creditors have a claim until the notice period ends.

## How state law changes this

State rules differ on whether court approval is required, how long a sale takes, and whether a surviving spouse or minor children have rights in the home that must be respected before a sale. Homestead rights in some states can block or complicate a sale. If you are in a state with a strong homestead protection, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), speak with an attorney early.
