Marcus was named executor of his mother's will. He has paid for her funeral on his credit card, her checking account is still open, and her house taxes are coming due. He wonders where the line sits between getting his own money back and touching money that is not his. His sister, meanwhile, wonders the same thing from the other side.

The short answer is that an executor is a caretaker of the estate, not an owner of it. This article explains what an executor may pay, what they may be paid, what is off limits, and what beneficiaries can do. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) covers the process around it.

## The short version

- Estate money is held for creditors first, then for the people named in the will or the law.
- An executor can pay legitimate estate costs and be reimbursed with receipts.
- Pay is allowed in most states, by statute or by a reasonable standard, and sometimes needs court approval.
- Mixing funds, borrowing from the estate and early self-payment are the common mistakes.
- Beneficiaries have a right to see what has been done with the money.

## When the answer is yes

An executor is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), someone who must act for the benefit of others. Within that duty, three kinds of payments are generally fine.

### Paying estate expenses

The executor can pay the estate's bills from the estate's own account: funeral costs, last medical bills, utilities and insurance on the house, property taxes, court fees, appraisal fees and attorney fees. Our article on [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) lists these tasks in order, and [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) covers which creditors get paid first.

### Reimbursement for documented costs

When an executor pays for something personally, such as the funeral, the executor can usually be repaid from the estate. Keep the receipt, the proof of payment and a note of why it was an estate expense. Reimbursement is not pay. It simply returns money the executor advanced, and it is not income. Reimbursement without paperwork invites questions.

### Executor compensation

Most states let an executor be paid. Some set a percentage schedule by statute, based on the value of the estate. Others use a "reasonable compensation" standard that depends on the work and the estate's complexity. In some places the court must approve the amount, and in others the executor may pay themselves and report it. A will can set the fee or waive it. Compensation is taxable income to the executor. See [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) for how fees fit into the overall bill.

Some states also let the court cut an executor's fee when the work was poor, late or needlessly expensive. The reverse is true too: a court may allow extra pay for unusual work, such as running a business or selling several properties. Keeping a simple time log, even if the state uses a percentage schedule, helps justify the fee if anyone asks.

Family executors often waive pay, especially when they are also heirs, because an inheritance is generally not income tax while a fee is. Our blog post on [whether an executor can also be a beneficiary](/blog/can-an-executor-also-be-a-beneficiary) discusses that dual role.

## When the answer is no

These actions are not allowed, even with good intentions.

| Action | Why it is a problem | Safer approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commingling | Mixing estate money with personal money makes records unreliable and risks claims of conversion | Open a separate estate account |
| Loans to self | Borrowing from the estate is self-dealing, even if repaid | Do not borrow; if needed, get court or all-beneficiary approval |
| Buying estate assets cheaply | The executor owes the best price to beneficiaries | Use an appraisal, open sale and written consent |
| Paying yourself first | Creditors and taxes have priority over distributions and often over fees | Pay valid claims and reserve funds first |
| Using estate property personally | Free use of a house or car benefits the executor alone | Rent at fair value with written approval |
| Paying oneself an unapproved fee | Compensation must follow statute, court order or the will | Follow your state process and record it |

Elena, executor of her father's estate, bought his car for $2,000 because "nobody else wanted it." An appraisal later showed it was worth $9,000. Her brother could ask the court to charge her the difference, and the incident colored every other decision she made.

## What about an executor who is also a beneficiary?

Taking your own share early is a common trouble spot. An executor may be a beneficiary, but the estate must pay debts, taxes and expenses first, and the claims period must usually pass. If the executor distributes money to themselves and the estate later runs short, the executor can be personally liable. A careful executor waits until the estate is clearly solvent, then distributes equally and with written receipts.

## A worked example of doing it right

Marcus opens an estate checking account with the estate's tax ID and deposits his mother's final paychecks and a refund. He pays the funeral home from the estate account instead of his credit card going forward, and he sends his sister Dana a short list each month: what came in, what went out, and why. His $4,200 funeral advance is reimbursed with the invoice and card statement attached. He declines a fee, because he is also an heir, and he waits to distribute until the claims period has ended and the estate tax return, if any, is filed. Nothing here is complicated, and Dana has no reason to suspect a problem. Most executor disputes come from the opposite pattern: money moves in a personal account, no one sees a record, and silence fills the gap.

## What should beneficiaries do if the money looks wrong?

1. **Ask politely and in writing.** Request an inventory, bank statements and a list of payments.
2. **Request an accounting.** Many states require one, at least on request or at the end.
3. **Compare to the will and the law.** Check whether payments match the rules above.
4. **Talk to an attorney.** A letter from counsel often settles it.
5. **Ask the court.** A beneficiary can petition to compel an accounting, review fees, surcharge the executor or remove them. See [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

For comparison, our article on [trustee compensation](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation) explains the parallel rules for trusts.

## What does it look like when the estate is short on cash?

Sometimes the estate has more bills than money. In that case the executor cannot pick favorites or take fees ahead of the creditors the law puts first. States set an order of payment, usually putting administration costs, funeral expenses and taxes ahead of ordinary debts. An executor who pays a family loan or their own fee before higher-priority claims may be personally responsible for the shortfall. If the estate looks insolvent, pause distributions and talk to an attorney before spending more.

## What if you are the executor and unsure?

Keep it simple. Open an estate account. Pay only estate costs. Keep every receipt. Write down your reasons for each payment. Ask the beneficiaries before doing anything unusual. And get written approval or a court order before paying yourself anything beyond documented reimbursement. If your state requires court approval for fees, wait for it.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether executor pay follows a statutory schedule or a reasonableness standard, whether court approval is required, how and when accountings must be filed, and the order in which debts are paid.
