Families are often surprised to learn that settling an estate has a price tag, and that the bill comes out of the inheritance. If you are an executor, you may be wondering what you will owe and who pays. If you are planning ahead, you may be weighing probate cost against the cost of a trust. This page lays out each category of expense and how it is set. The [probate guide](/learn/probate) explains the process these costs attach to.

## What does probate cost?

A common planning range is 3% to 7% of the probate estate, but that range hides huge variation. A simple estate of $150,000 in a state with flat fees might cost a few thousand dollars. A $1 million estate in a state with statutory fee schedules might cost well over $50,000 once all fees are added up. Estates in litigation cost far more.

Because costs vary so much, treat any percentage as a starting point and ask for an estimate in your own state.

## What are the main probate expenses?

Probate costs fall into a handful of buckets. Here is a summary.

| Expense | How it is usually set | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| Court filing fees | Fixed by court, sometimes tied to estate value | Often a few hundred dollars; some counties charge more for larger estates |
| Attorney fees | Hourly, flat fee, or statutory percentage | Hourly rates often $250 to $500+; flat fees for simple estates; statutory schedules in some states |
| Executor compensation | Statute, court approval or the will | Waived, hourly, or a percentage in some states |
| Appraisals | Per appraisal | Hundreds of dollars for a house; more for businesses or collections |
| Newspaper publication | Local rates | Often under a few hundred dollars |
| Bond premium | Percentage of bond amount | Annual premium; may be waived by the will |
| Accounting and tax preparation | Hourly or flat | Varies |
| Property costs | Actual costs | Insurance, utilities, repairs, taxes while the house waits to sell |

### Court fees

Probate courts charge a filing fee to open the case, plus smaller fees for certified copies of letters, petitions to sell property and other motions. In some counties the opening fee is a flat amount. In others it rises with the value of the estate. These fees are modest compared with the other categories.

### Attorney fees

Attorney fees are usually the largest cost. They are set in one of three ways.

- **Statutory fees.** A few states, including California and Florida, set a presumptive fee by percentage of the estate, with the schedule written into law or a court rule. Under California's schedule, for example, the fee is a sliding percentage of the estate's value, and attorneys can also seek extra compensation for extraordinary work. Check the current rule in your state, because schedules change.
- **Hourly.** Many states let the attorney bill for time spent, subject to court review for reasonableness.
- **Flat fee.** Some firms quote a flat fee for routine, uncontested estates, often in stages.

Ask any attorney you interview for the fee method in writing and what it covers. A written fee agreement helps the executor, who is responsible for approving the bill.

### Executor pay

Executors are entitled to reasonable compensation in most states, either by a statutory formula or by what the court finds reasonable. A will may set the amount or waive it. Family members serving as executors often decline pay. Pay is taxable income to the executor, while an inheritance generally is not, so waiving compensation can leave the executor better off in tax terms. Professional executors, such as a bank trust department or a professional fiduciary, charge fees according to a published schedule. Our article on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) discusses when a professional makes sense.

### Bonds and other costs

Courts often require an executor to post a bond, an insurance policy that protects the estate if the executor misuses funds. A will can waive the bond requirement in many states, which saves the premium. Other costs include certified death certificates, storage fees for belongings, and real estate commissions if a house is sold, which are not strictly probate costs but come out of the same pot.

## Who pays for probate?

The estate pays. Expenses of administration come ahead of heirs in the priority order, so every dollar spent on probate is a dollar not distributed. Executors generally should not use personal funds without recording the advance, and should be reimbursed from the estate with proper records.

If the estate has few liquid assets, the executor may need to sell something to pay expenses, which is another reason probate can slow down and cost more.

## A realistic example

Walter, 79, died in a state that uses hourly attorney billing. His estate: a house worth $320,000, a checking account with $40,000 and a car worth $9,000, all in his name, plus a $200,000 IRA naming his daughter. Only the house, checking and car go through probate, about $369,000.

His daughter, serving as executor, waived her fee. Estimated costs: court fees, $500; attorney, $6,500 at hourly rates; appraisal, $450; publication, $200; bond premium, $400; real estate commission, 5% of the sale or $16,000. Excluding the sales commission, probate cost about $8,050, or roughly 2.2% of the probate estate. If the state used a statutory percentage schedule, the attorney fee alone could have been higher.

Notice what did not count: the $200,000 IRA, which passed by beneficiary form and did not raise the bill at all.

## How are costs different in a state with statutory fees?

States with statutory fee schedules, where fees climb with the estate, can make probate considerably more expensive for larger estates, especially those with a house. A $700,000 estate in such a state can produce attorney fees and executor fees that each reach five figures. Notably, the statutory fee is calculated on the gross value of the probate estate in some states, which means a mortgage does not reduce the base. If you own a home in California or Florida, see our state guides for [California](/estate-planning/california) and [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), and ask a local attorney how current fees are calculated.

## Can you lower probate costs?

Yes. These steps help.

1. **Keep assets out of probate.** Beneficiary designations, transfer-on-death registrations and a funded [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) shrink the probate estate, and fees are usually based on that. See [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).
2. **Use small estate procedures.** If the estate qualifies, an affidavit can cost a small fraction of full probate. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).
3. **Compare fee structures.** Get at least two quotes and ask for the method in writing.
4. **Be organized.** Hand the attorney a list of assets, account numbers and documents so hours are not spent searching.
5. **Do suitable tasks yourself.** An executor can often gather records, call banks and handle correspondence without paying attorney rates.
6. **Avoid fights.** Litigation is the biggest cost multiplier. Clear planning and honest family conversations reduce the chance.

## Is avoiding probate always cheaper?

Not always. A trust costs money to create and maintain, and assets must be retitled correctly. In states with simple probate or high small-estate limits, probate may cost less than a trust would. In states with expensive probate, a trust often pays for itself. For a comparison of planning costs, see [how much estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost).

## What does a probate bill look like for large estates?

Large estates have higher fees in dollars, though not always as a percentage. An estate with real estate in several states may owe fees in each state, because ancillary proceedings are separate cases. Our article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains why. Estates with taxes, business interests or disputes involve accountants, appraisers and sometimes litigators, and costs can run well into six figures.
