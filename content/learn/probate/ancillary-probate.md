If you own a vacation house, rental, cabin or piece of land in a state other than the one where you live, your family may face probate twice: once at home and once where the property is. This page explains why, what the second case involves and the usual ways to avoid it. The wider topic is in our [probate guide](/learn/probate), and our article on [owning property in more than one state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) covers other planning issues beyond probate.

## What is ancillary probate?

[Ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate) is a probate case in a state other than the decedent's home state. It exists because each state controls the title to real estate within its borders. A judge in Ohio cannot sign an order transferring a cabin in Michigan. The Michigan court has to do that, under Michigan law.

The home-state case is called the primary or domiciliary probate. The second case is "ancillary," meaning supporting. It runs alongside the main case. It can be handled by the same executor if the second state allows, or by a local representative.

## What property triggers ancillary probate?

Real estate is the main trigger. Land, houses, condos, cabins and mineral rights titled in the decedent's sole name in another state usually need a case there.

Not everything does. Bank accounts, brokerage accounts, retirement accounts and life insurance are usually tied to the owner's home state or pass by contract, so they do not need a second court. Tangible personal property, such as a boat, a car, furniture or art kept in the other state, is a mixed bag. Many states treat vehicles through a motor vehicle agency, and some will treat modest personal property under a simple affidavit. Whether you need ancillary probate for those depends on the state and the value.

The catch is that if the out-of-state property is titled in the decedent's name alone, then it needs the court. If it is titled in a trust, in joint names with survivorship, or has a transfer-on-death deed, it does not.

## How does ancillary probate work?

The general steps look like this, though each state sets its own forms and timing.

1. **Open or complete the home-state case.** The executor is appointed in the primary case, if one is needed.
2. **Hire a local attorney.** The second state typically requires a lawyer licensed there or at least local counsel to guide the process.
3. **File authenticated documents.** The executor files a certified copy of the will and the home-state letters with the ancillary court, or asks to be appointed locally.
4. **Local notice and claims.** The second state may require its own creditor notice, which creates a separate claims period.
5. **Manage or sell the property.** The ancillary representative can sell the property, collect rent or transfer title to the heirs, following local rules.
6. **Report and close.** After any debts, taxes and expenses connected to that property are handled, the ancillary case closes and any proceeds return to the primary estate or go to heirs.

Some states have simplified procedures for out-of-state executors, such as recognition of foreign letters or a short filing, which can cut the cost and time. Others require a full local appointment.

## How much does it cost and how long does it take?

Ancillary probate adds court fees, an attorney's fees in that state, possible publication costs and, depending on the state, an appraisal. It may also add several months, because the second case has its own creditor period and timeline, and an out-of-state executor may need to travel or give local counsel authority. Some states base fees on the value of the property, so a valuable lakeside house can generate a meaningful local bill. Our overview of [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) explains how fees are set, and [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) explains typical timing.

## A realistic example

Frank, 72, lives in Illinois and owns a condo in Florida in his name alone. He dies, leaving everything to his two children. His Illinois executor opens probate in Illinois to handle his house and accounts there. To transfer the Florida condo, the executor must also open an ancillary case in Florida, hire a Florida attorney and file Frank's will and letters there. The Florida case has its own timing and fees.

Had Frank put the condo into a revocable living trust during his life, or signed a deed form that Florida honors naming his children, the condo would have passed outside probate in both states. See the guide for [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) if you own property there.

## How can you avoid ancillary probate?

You have several options. Which one fits depends on the state where the property sits, your tax picture and your family.

### Use a revocable living trust

Retitling the out-of-state property into your [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is the most common solution. Title belongs to the trustee, so at death the successor trustee transfers or sells it without a court. The step that people skip is the one that matters: the deed must be signed, notarized and recorded in the county where the property is located. See [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) for the details. Check any local transfer-tax or reassessment effect before moving property, since a few places treat a transfer into a trust differently from a sale.

### Use a transfer-on-death deed

Where allowed, a transfer-on-death deed lets you name a beneficiary who receives the property at death. It is inexpensive. It works only if the state where the property is located permits it and you follow that state's recording rules. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

### Use joint ownership

Joint tenancy with right of survivorship also avoids probate on that property, but it has the usual drawbacks of control and creditor exposure. See [joint ownership risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

### Use an LLC or similar entity

Some owners put out-of-state rental property into a limited liability company, which turns the real estate into an ownership interest that is usually treated like personal property and can pass under the home-state will. This adds legal and tax complexity, so it deserves real attorney input and is more common for rental or investment property than for a family vacation home.

### Sell or gift the property

Selling the property before death turns it into cash. Gifting can work too, but lifetime gifts have tax and control consequences. Gifts of appreciated real estate do not get a basis step-up at death, which can cost heirs in capital gains tax later. Compare options with our overview of [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## What about the decedent who moved states?

Ancillary probate is about where property sits, and the decedent's home state is the one where they were domiciled at death. People who move often leave an old house behind, or inherit property in the old state from relatives. If you recently moved, review your documents. A will signed in your old state can usually still be valid in your new one, but formalities such as self-proving affidavits and trust terms are worth a check against the new state's law. A short review is much cheaper than a surprise later.

## What should an executor do about out-of-state property?

An executor who discovers real estate in another state should do the following:

1. Check how it is titled using the deed from the county recorder or an online search of county records.
2. Find out whether the property is in a trust, jointly held or covered by a transfer-on-death deed.
3. If it is in the decedent's name alone, look up the other state's procedure for foreign executors.
4. Hire a local attorney early, so the second court's timeline starts as soon as possible.
5. Keep the property insured, secure and maintained meanwhile.
