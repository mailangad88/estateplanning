Two families, two deaths. In Naperville, Illinois, a retired nurse leaves a house, a checking account and a will. In Princeton, New Jersey, a retired engineer leaves nearly the same list. The paperwork, the offices, the timing and even the tax questions will not match. Most guides to probate describe an average that does not exist, so this page puts two real systems side by side. It is meant for families who own property in both places, for people who might move between them, and for anyone who wants to see how much "probate" varies from one state to the next. The general process is explained in our [probate guide](/learn/probate).

## Where does a probate case begin in each state?

In Illinois, the case begins in the probate division of the circuit court in the county where the person lived. The clerk of the circuit court receives the will, and a judge admits it. Illinois statute asks anyone holding the original will to file it with the clerk immediately after the death, even if no estate is opened. Our walk-through of the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process) covers each step.

In New Jersey, the case begins with the Surrogate of the county where the person lived. Uncontested cases stay in that office. Contested ones move to the Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part. County Surrogates say probate and letters cannot be completed until the tenth day after death, though you can gather papers sooner. See the [New Jersey probate process](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-probate-process) for the sequence.

Neither state follows the Uniform Probate Code in the form some other states use, so the "informal" and "formal" labels you read about elsewhere are not the right search terms here. Our article on [formal and informal probate](/learn/probate/formal-vs-informal-probate) explains where those labels do apply.

## How do the main features line up?

| Feature | Illinois | New Jersey |
|---|---|---|
| First office | Circuit court clerk, probate division | County Surrogate |
| Court for disputes | Same circuit court | Superior Court, Chancery Division, Probate Part |
| Court papers for the executor | Letters of office | Letters testamentary or letters of administration |
| Main track | Independent administration is the usual choice | Executor acts after the Surrogate admits the will |
| Notice to heirs | Within 14 days of the order admitting the will | Within 60 days of probate |
| Time to contest a will | Six months after admission | A caveat filed with the Surrogate before probate can pause the case and force a hearing |
| Small estate shortcut | Up to $150,000, no real estate | Intestate estates only, $50,000 with a spouse or partner, $20,000 otherwise |
| Transfer-on-death for a house | Yes, by recorded instrument | No transfer-on-death deed currently |
| Estate tax | Yes, $4 million exclusion | None |
| Inheritance tax | None | Yes, depends on relationship |
| Handwritten will | Not recognized | Valid if material parts are in the testator's handwriting |

Rules change, and dollar limits move, so confirm a number with the court before relying on it.

## What does independent administration change in Illinois?

It lets the executor work with little supervision. Under independent administration, the executor can sell property, pay claims and make distributions without asking the judge for approval at each step. The court still admits the will, issues letters, and receives the final report. Our article on [independent and supervised administration in Illinois](/learn/illinois/independent-vs-supervised-administration-in-illinois) explains who can ask for supervision and why a family might.

New Jersey does not use that label. The executor still has duties, files notices, handles creditors and follows the Surrogate's procedures, and an administrator must typically post a bond. The practical feel can be similar for an uncontested estate: a first appointment, then months of work, then a closing. The difference is mostly in which forms, offices and deadlines apply.

## How do small estates compare?

Both states give small estates a shortcut, but the two shortcuts are unlike.

Illinois allows a small estate affidavit for estates up to $150,000 for deaths on or after August 15, 2025, as long as there is no real estate. A bank or transfer agent accepts the sworn affidavit in place of letters. See the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) article.

New Jersey's version is for estates without a will. The Surrogate can approve an affidavit process when the estate is $50,000 or less with a surviving spouse or partner, or $20,000 or less otherwise. If the person left a will, the will goes through regular probate, even on a modest estate. Our article on the [New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) has the detail.

Take two daughters, each handling a parent's $30,000 account. In Illinois, Carla can use the affidavit if there is no real estate, will or no will. In New Jersey, Jun-ho has the same $30,000 but only qualifies if his father had no will and the surviving spouse limit applies. If a will exists, he opens a case. The facts are the same and the route is not.

## Which state is more likely to lead to a tax bill?

It depends on the family. Illinois taxes estates above a $4 million exclusion, which does not follow the federal $15 million figure. A couple with a $5 million estate may owe Illinois estate tax and nothing federally. Our guide to [the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) explains the calculation.

New Jersey repealed its estate tax for deaths after 2017 but keeps an inheritance tax. Spouses, partners, children, grandchildren and parents are exempt. Siblings and children-in-law pay 11 to 16 percent above the first $25,000, and others pay 15 or 16 percent. A $400,000 estate left to three nephews can generate a larger tax bill in New Jersey than a $3 million estate left to children. See the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) article.

The point is not that one state is kinder. The question is which tax your heirs meet, and the answer changes your planning.

## What about passing a house without probate?

Illinois permits a recorded transfer on death instrument for residential property of one to four units. It must be recorded before death. See [Illinois transfer on death instruments](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument).

New Jersey does not currently authorize transfer-on-death deeds. Owners rely on joint ownership, tenancy by the entirety for spouses, beneficiary designations on accounts, or a funded living trust. Our article on [transfer-on-death options in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-on-death-options-in-new-jersey) explains the choices. A comparison of the instruments themselves is in [transfer-on-death deed versus trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust).

## What if you own property in both states?

Then expect two cases unless you plan around it. The home state opens the main case. A house in the other state, in the dead person's name alone, needs its own ancillary case there. New Jersey's own guidance is that a will probated only elsewhere has no effect there until it is probated in New Jersey too. Our articles on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) and on [probating a will from another state](/learn/probate/probating-a-will-from-another-state) show how to sequence the filings, and a living trust is the usual way to avoid the second case.

## What should you do with this comparison?

1. **Identify where the person lived.** That decides the main court.
2. **List the assets by type and location.** The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) shows what needs a case.
3. **Check whether a shortcut fits.** Use the small estate rules above, then confirm limits with the court.
4. **Look for a tax angle.** Illinois means estate tax, New Jersey means inheritance tax.
5. **Ask local counsel about real estate.** Titles are a state matter.

If you are planning for yourself, the comparison also shows how much a move changes. A plan built in one state can quietly stop working in the other, which is covered in our article on [moving states without updating your plan](/learn/what-if/moved-states-plan-not-updated).
