Sometimes the person a will names as executor says no. Sometimes the will names no one, the person is too ill, or the executor died years before the person who wrote the will. Sometimes there is no will at all. Families in this spot often assume the estate is stuck. It is not. A court has a standing procedure for filling the role, and the rest of this page describes it.

The [probate guide](/learn/probate) covers the full process once someone is in charge. Here we focus on the gap before that happens.

## The short version

- A named executor can say no, and the court then appoints someone else.
- If no will exists, the court appoints an administrator under a statutory order of priority.
- Until the appointment, nobody can close accounts, sell property or pay bills from the estate.
- The family can usually propose a person, and the court tends to approve a sensible choice.
- A professional appointed by the court costs money, so families often prefer to settle on one of their own.

## What happens when no executor is named?

When a person dies without a will, or with a will that names no executor, the estate is "intestate" as to who serves. A family member files a petition with the probate court asking to be appointed administrator. State law gives certain people priority, and the usual order is:

1. The surviving spouse or registered partner.
2. Adult children.
3. Grandchildren or other descendants.
4. Parents.
5. Siblings.
6. More distant relatives.
7. A creditor or a public administrator, if no relative applies.

The exact order differs by state, and some states let a majority of heirs choose. A person with priority can step aside and nominate someone else by filing a written waiver or nomination. Our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains who inherits, which is a separate question from who gets appointed.

An example: Walter, 77, died with no will. His wife had died earlier, and he had three adult children. The oldest, Beth, lived two states away and did not want the job. Her brother Karl lived in town. Beth signed a short waiver and nominated Karl, and the court appointed him. Nobody had to hold a hearing.

## What if the named executor says no?

A will names an executor, but a name is an offer, not an order. The person named can decline. The usual way is to file a written declination with the court, or simply not to petition and let the court notice that they have not stepped forward. After that, the will's backup executor is next. If none exists or the backup also declines, the court appoints an administrator with the will annexed. That person follows the will, but is chosen by the court.

A few reasons people say no:

- Living far away, with little time to travel for hearings and signings.
- Health or age.
- A complicated estate, such as a business or property in several states.
- Family conflict, where the executor expects to be criticized or sued.
- Discomfort with the legal exposure that comes with the job.

All of those are fair. Our [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) is useful for anyone trying to decide, because it is concrete about the workload. If the executor accepts and later wants out, the process is described in the article on [removing or replacing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

## What if the executor is dead, incapacitated or cannot be found?

The same fallback applies. If the will's named executor and backup are unavailable, the court appoints. If the person is alive but cannot act because of illness or disability, an interested person can ask the court to appoint someone else, and the court will usually hear from the person named if possible. If the named executor cannot be located, there may be a notice requirement before the court moves on.

## Who can ask the court to appoint someone?

Any interested person can petition. That includes a beneficiary, an heir, a creditor and in some states a funeral home that is owed money. Typically the person asks to serve themselves, or nominates someone. The court then issues notice to heirs and beneficiaries, waits for objections and holds a short hearing if needed. If all interested parties agree, many courts approve the appointment on paper.

## What does the court need from the new representative?

Expect these items:

- A sworn oath to carry out the duties faithfully.
- A bond in many cases, which is an insurance policy protecting the estate against loss. A will can waive the bond for a named executor, but an administrator without a will often has to post one. The cost is paid from the estate.
- Proof of residency or, in some states, a requirement to name an in-state agent to accept legal papers.
- Letters, which are the court document banks and agencies will accept as proof of authority.

Our article on [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) walks through those first filings.

## What can the family do while waiting?

Not very much, and that is the point. Without letters, no one has legal authority over the estate. Still, several things are normal and safe:

1. **Protect the property.** Lock the house, collect mail, keep the heat on, and photograph the contents.
2. **Keep insurance active.** Call the homeowner's and auto carriers, and tell them about the death. Vacancy clauses can end coverage.
3. **Gather documents.** Find the will, deeds, statements, and tax returns.
4. **Pay the funeral bill if you choose,** but keep receipts. Funeral costs are generally reimbursable from the estate with priority.
5. **Do not move or sell estate property** or take money from accounts. Doing so can create personal liability.
6. **Talk to the other heirs** about who will serve, before the hearing.

Our [after-a-death checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) goes further.

## Is a public administrator ever involved?

In some places, yes. When there is no known relative willing and able to serve, many counties have a public administrator or similar official who handles the estate. That office has statutory fees and takes over, and is more common for estates with real property or no identified heirs. For most families, this is the last resort.

## How does this compare with having the right executor in place?

Choosing a good executor in advance avoids all of it. A will that names a primary and one or two backups, who have each agreed in advance, can usually be filed and appointed within weeks. The [guide on choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) walks through who is a good fit, including what to do when no relative seems right. Families can also use a living trust, where a successor trustee takes over property without court appointment. See [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) for how that works.

## How state law changes this

States vary on the order of priority, the amount of notice required, whether a bond is needed, and whether an out-of-state person can serve. Some states require nonresident executors to appoint a local agent or post a bond. Check your state's guide, such as the [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas) or [Ohio guide](/estate-planning/ohio), or ask an attorney how your county handles it.
