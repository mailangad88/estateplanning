Priya's father died in March with a will naming her as executor. She walked into his bank with the will and a death certificate and asked for the balance of his checking account. The teller politely told her she needed letters. She had never heard the word used that way.

Letters are the single document that turns a name in a will into legal authority. This page explains what they are, who gets which kind, how to obtain them, what the court may require along the way, and what to do when an institution still refuses. If you are earlier in the process, our guide to [filing probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) covers the first steps, and the [probate hub](/learn/probate) shows the whole route.

## What are letters testamentary?

Letters testamentary are a certificate signed by the probate court that says a named person is the executor of a named estate and may act on its behalf. Despite the name, they are not correspondence. The court clerk issues them after the judge or registrar admits the will and accepts the executor's oath. Many courts use a different name, such as a certificate of appointment, a certificate of authority or letters of authority.

A will that names Priya as executor is a request. Letters are the court's yes. Until the court issues them, she holds a nomination and nothing more, which is why she cannot touch the account.

## What are letters of administration?

Letters of administration are the same certificate for an administrator. The court appoints an administrator when there is no will, when the will names no executor, or when the named executor has died, declined or been disqualified. Our article on [what happens if there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) explains how the court picks among the candidates.

Most states give priority to the surviving spouse, then adult children, then other relatives. The person asks the court to be appointed, notice goes to other heirs, and the judge issues the letters. Other terms you may see are "letters of administration with will annexed," which applies when there is a will but no executor able to serve, and "special administrator," a temporary role used to protect assets in an emergency.

## How do you get letters?

The route is similar in every state, though forms and names differ.

1. **File the petition.** With a will, the petition asks the court to admit it and appoint the executor. Without one, it asks to appoint an administrator and lists the heirs.
2. **Give notice.** Heirs and beneficiaries are told, and in contested cases the court holds a hearing. In many states with informal probate the registrar approves the filing without a hearing.
3. **Post a bond, if required.** See the section below.
4. **Take the oath.** You promise to carry out the duties honestly.
5. **Receive the letters.** The clerk issues them, and you pay for certified copies.
6. **Get an EIN and open an estate account.** These steps follow once you hold letters.

Timing ranges from days in a simple informal case to months if someone objects. Our article on [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) explains what drives the difference.

## Who is eligible to receive them?

Most states bar a few people from serving. The usual list is minors, people under guardianship, people convicted of certain felonies, and in some states people who do not live in the state unless they name a local agent. A court can also refuse someone who has a conflict of interest or a history of mismanaging money. See [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) if you are still deciding who should take the job.

## Do you need a bond?

A probate bond is an insurance policy, bought from a surety company, that pays the estate if the executor steals or mismanages assets. It does not protect the executor. The premium is generally paid from the estate and is a small yearly percentage of the bond amount, which a court sets based on the value of the personal property.

Many wills say the executor serves without bond, and courts usually go along with that. A judge may still require it if an heir objects, if the executor lives out of state, or if the executor has poor credit or debts. Some states waive it for a surviving spouse who is the sole beneficiary. If a bond is required and you cannot get one, the court may allow other security, such as restricting the estate's funds.

## What do letters let you do?

| With letters | Without letters. |
| --- | --- |
| Open an estate bank account | Pay a few bills from your own money and keep receipts. |
| Collect accounts titled to the decedent alone | Secure the home and its contents. |
| Sell estate property, including real estate | Order death certificates. |
| Sign tax returns for the estate | Gather documents and the will. |
| Deal with the title company on a sale. | Look up accounts and benefits. |
| Retitle a vehicle or transfer securities | Notify Social Security and employers. |

## How many copies should you order?

Order more than you think. Each bank, brokerage, insurer, title company and agency usually keeps one, and some will not accept a copy older than 60 to 90 days. A certified copy carries the clerk's seal or signature. Ten copies for a typical estate is not unusual, and the court charges a small fee each. Store the original with the will.

## What if a bank or title company still says no?

It happens. A bank may want its own form, ask for a recent date, or send you to a legal department. Ask for the written requirement, order new certified copies, and call the legal department. If the institution is still stuck, the executor can ask the court for a short order confirming the authority. Most institutions relent once they see that the letters are current. Our article on a [deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) walks through the usual hurdles.

## Can letters be limited or taken away?

Yes. A court can issue limited letters that allow only certain acts, such as selling a house or defending a lawsuit. It can also issue temporary letters in an urgent case, such as a business that needs to keep operating. Letters can be revoked if the executor stops doing the job, steals, or has a conflict. Our guide to [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) explains how that works. An executor can also resign, with the court's approval.

## A realistic example

Priya files a petition in the county court, with her father's original will and a death certificate. Her brother Anil signs a waiver of notice, which speeds things up. The will waives bond, and the court accepts it. Three weeks later the clerk issues her letters, and she orders eight certified copies. With one in hand, she opens an estate account, transfers the checking balance from her father's bank and starts the list of assets. The copy she brought to the title company six months later was too old, so she ordered another. That is the usual story, which is why we suggest ordering extra.
