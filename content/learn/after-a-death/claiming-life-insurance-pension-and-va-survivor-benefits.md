Within a few weeks of a death, most families discover that the money they were counting on sits with someone else: an insurance company, an employer's benefits office, a pension administrator, the VA. None of them calls you. Each wants a form, a certified death certificate and, sometimes, a good deal of patience.

This page covers the practical side of three kinds of claims: life insurance, pensions, and benefits for the survivors of a veteran. It does not cover retirement accounts such as IRAs, which have their own rules in our guide on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries), or [Social Security](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death). It belongs to our wider guide on [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## How do you claim life insurance after a death?

The beneficiary files the claim. It does not go through the estate, and it does not need a court.

1. **Find the policy.** Look in the person's files, bank statements (premiums paid), tax records and mail. Check employer benefits and credit card or mortgage insurance. If you cannot find one, our guide on [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) lists the free lookup tools.
2. **Call the claims department.** Have the policyholder's full name, date of birth, date of death and policy number if you have it. Ask for a claimant's statement and note the claim number.
3. **Gather proof.** A certified copy of the death certificate is the one item every insurer wants. Many require the original certified copy, not a photocopy. Our page on [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need) suggests how many to order.
4. **Return the claim form.** Name yourself exactly as the policy does, and give a tax ID. Choose a payout method. A lump sum is usual.
5. **Follow up in writing.** Ask for a written status after two weeks. Keep a log of who you spoke with.
6. **Review the payment.** Check the amount against the policy. Add any interest or accidental death rider that applies.

If the beneficiary named has died, the policy usually looks to the contingent beneficiary, then to the insured's estate. Our guide on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) covers those traps, such as an ex-spouse who is still listed.

## Why do some life insurance claims take longer?

Most claims on an older policy are quick. Four situations slow things down.

- **A death inside the first two years.** Most policies have a two-year contestability period, during which the insurer may check the application for material misstatements. It does not mean denial. It means the insurer reads the medical history.
- **Unclear beneficiary.** Two people claim the same policy, the form was not signed, or a trust or minor is the beneficiary. The insurer may hold the money until the question is settled or a court says who gets it.
- **The cause of death.** An accidental death rider requires a particular cause. A pending coroner's report can hold up payment.
- **A policy that lapsed.** The insurer will check the premium history, and some policies have grace periods. A reinstatement conversation sometimes helps.

The insurer must pay interest in many states if it delays beyond a set period. Ask about it. Interest is taxable income to the beneficiary, although the death benefit itself generally is not; see our guide on whether [beneficiaries pay taxes on what they receive](/learn/beneficiary-designations/do-beneficiaries-pay-taxes-on-what-they-receive).

### Employer and group policies

A group life policy comes through the employer, so call the human resources office, not the insurer first. Ask about the basic policy, any supplemental coverage the person bought, and any accidental death coverage. Ask also about unpaid wages, accrued vacation, a final bonus, and a 401(k). Federal employees, teachers and members of the military have their own offices. Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance and Veterans' Group Life Insurance are handled by a dedicated office, and they pay the person named on the form, whatever the will says.

## What happens to a pension when the retiree dies?

It depends on the option chosen at retirement, and the paperwork often sits in a drawer.

| Pension option | What happens at death |
|---|---|
| Single life | Payments stop. A final partial payment may be owed. |
| Joint and survivor | The surviving spouse continues to receive a stated percentage, such as half or all |
| Period certain | Payments continue to a beneficiary for the remainder of a set period |
| Lump sum already taken | Treated as an account that belongs to the estate or to a named beneficiary |

Notify the plan administrator by phone and in writing. Ask what survivor benefits exist, what forms are needed, and whether a death benefit is payable. If a retiree dies while still working, the plan may pay a pre-retirement survivor benefit to a spouse.

A common mistake is spending pension deposits that arrive after the death. Like other regular payments, they are often reclaimed from the bank account. If a payment lands, do not use it. Tell the administrator, so the correction is simple. For retired federal workers, the rules differ, and the agency that pays the annuity is the right first call.

Spouses have special rights in many employer plans. Federal law generally requires a spouse's written consent before a participant in a plan covered by it can name someone else for survivor benefits. A surviving spouse who finds a different beneficiary on file may have a claim.

## What survivor benefits does the VA offer?

If the person served in the military, call the Department of Veterans Affairs or a county veterans service officer early. Several separate programs exist, and the application for one does not file for the others.

- **Dependency and Indemnity Compensation (DIC).** A monthly tax-free payment to certain survivors, mainly of those who died of a service-connected condition or while on active duty. A spouse, child or parent may qualify.
- **Survivors Pension.** A needs-based benefit for some wartime veterans' surviving spouses and children with limited income and assets.
- **Accrued benefits.** Money the VA owed the veteran at the time of death but had not paid, which can go to eligible survivors.
- **Burial benefits.** Free burial in a national cemetery, a headstone or marker and a flag for eligible veterans. A burial allowance may be available toward private costs. See [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral).
- **Health coverage for dependents.** Some surviving spouses and children qualify for CHAMPVA.

Eligibility depends on dates of service, discharge status, cause of death and, for the pension, income and net worth. Do not self-screen out because you think the death was not service-connected. The VA decides that, and a veteran with a chronic condition often turns out to qualify. If the veteran was receiving the [Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance) benefit, a surviving spouse may be able to continue it under the survivor's pension.

Free help exists. County and state veterans service officers, and accredited representatives of veterans' organizations, prepare and file claims at no cost. Be wary of anyone who charges to file an initial claim.

## What is the order to follow?

A workable sequence for the first six weeks:

1. Order death certificates, at least one per claim.
2. Ask the employer's benefits office for a written summary of every benefit, living and dead.
3. Call each insurer and the pension administrator; note claim numbers.
4. Contact the VA or a veterans service officer if there was military service.
5. Send forms by a trackable method, and keep copies.
6. Revisit open claims every two weeks until each is paid or denied.

None of this needs a probate court, unless the estate is the beneficiary or no beneficiary was named. In that case the money joins the estate and goes through the executor.

## Example

Wendell died at 71 after a stroke. His wife, Rosa, found a life insurance statement from 1998, a pension letter from a steel company, and a discharge paper from the Army. She called the insurer, who paid $50,000 within three weeks. The pension turned out to be a joint and survivor option at half the amount, which began after she filed a form. A county veterans officer reviewed Wendell's service and told her the benefits for a survivor of a non-service-connected death were limited, but she qualified for a flag and burial in the national cemetery, and a pension review to follow later. She spent about two hours on the phone for each claim and had everything in place within two months.
