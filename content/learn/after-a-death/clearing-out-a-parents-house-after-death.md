A parent's house has forty or fifty years of living in it. After the funeral, someone has to open the closets, and that someone is usually you and your siblings, tired and sad, with a rental truck booked for the weekend. Most families do this too fast. They throw out a drawer of old papers that held the deed, or they take a ring that the will left to someone else, and the damage shows up months later.

This page walks through how to do it in a sensible order: who has the right to decide, what to protect first, how to handle the big categories of belongings, and what to do when the house is full beyond ordinary. The [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) covers the rest of the estate.

## The short version

- Find out who has authority before you remove anything of value.
- Photograph every room and every valuable item on day one.
- Sort in this order: documents, money and valuables, medications, firearms, then everything else.
- Divide keepsakes by a written method the heirs agree on, not by who shows up first.
- Hire help for volume, not for judgment. You still decide what is kept.

## Who is allowed to decide what happens to the belongings?

The person with legal authority is the executor named in the will once a court appoints them, or the successor trustee when the house and contents sit in a revocable trust. Everyone else, including a child who lives nearby and holds the key, is a helper. If there is no will, the court appoints an administrator, as our article on [what happens when there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) explains.

Before that appointment, you can protect things but not dispose of them. Locking up, collecting mail, keeping the heat on and taking jewelry to a safe place for the family are all reasonable. Giving away the furniture is not. A [personal representative](/glossary/personal-representative) who later learns that the contents were scattered can be left with an inventory they cannot complete.

Read the will first, and look for a separate list. Many wills say "my tangible personal property goes to my children in shares as they agree," and some refer to a written memorandum naming who gets the clock or the quilt. A trust may have its own schedule. If those exist, they control.

## What should I do in the first days?

Work through this list before anyone loads a box.

1. **Secure the house.** Change locks if people you do not trust have keys, and ask the insurer whether a vacant house changes the coverage. Our guide to a [vacant inherited house](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters) covers insurance and squatters.
2. **Take photographs or a video of every room,** including the insides of closets and drawers. This is your record if anyone later says something went missing.
3. **Find the papers.** Look for the will, deeds, insurance policies, tax returns, account statements, vehicle titles and a safe deposit box key. Do not shred anything yet, including junk mail with account numbers on it.
4. **Collect cash, jewelry and small valuables** and store them somewhere neutral, with a written list signed or emailed to all the heirs.
5. **Deal with medications and anything dangerous.** Pharmacies and police departments often take back old prescriptions. Gas cans, old paint and ammunition need proper disposal.
6. **Redirect the mail** so bills and statements reach the executor. The [notification guide](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) explains how.

## How do you handle valuables, firearms and sentimental items?

Valuables first. Check coat pockets, books, freezers, coffee cans and the back of picture frames, because older people hide money in odd places. Assume nothing is trash until someone has looked inside it.

Firearms need special care. Do not move, sell or give away a gun until you know your state's transfer rules, and keep them locked and unloaded in the meantime. Federal and state law can require a background check or a licensed dealer for the transfer, even between relatives. Our article on [passing on firearms](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning) explains the planning side, and local police will often tell you how to handle a gun you are not sure about.

For jewelry and heirlooms, the fair way is a process agreed in advance. Some families go around a table, each picking one item per round. Others have each child list their top ten and settle overlaps by lot. Whatever you use, write it down. The page on [dividing personal property and family heirlooms](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms) lays out several methods, and [leaving jewelry and sentimental items](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-jewelry-heirlooms-and-sentimental-items) is useful if the will left instructions that someone now disputes.

Anything with real market value, such as art, a coin collection, antiques or a good watch, deserves an appraisal before it is sold or divided. Splitting a ring by eye is a good way to shortchange a sibling.

## When should you hire a liquidator or a cleanout service?

Hire help when the volume, distance or time pressure is the problem. A few distinctions help.

| Option | Best for | How you pay | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family sorts and donates | Modest house, nearby family | Dumpster and truck rental | Burnout, quarrels over who did the work |
| Estate sale company | Furniture, collectibles, a full house | Commission on sales, often a third to a half | Pricing, what happens to unsold items |
| Estate liquidator | Buying the contents outright | Flat offer for everything | Lowball offers, no itemized list |
| Cleanout or junk crew | Hoarded or very full homes | Flat fee or by the load | Items thrown out unsorted |
| Donation pickup | Clean, usable furniture and clothing | Free, with a receipt | Charities that refuse bulky items |

Get any arrangement in writing. Ask who has keys and when, how the company handles items you flag as keepers, whether insurance covers damage and what you receive after the sale. An estate sale is a sale of the estate's property, so the money goes to the estate, not to the family member who arranged it.

## What if the house is a hoarder house?

A heavily packed home changes the job. Start by looking at safety: blocked exits, damaged floors, mold, pests and spoiled food. A mask, gloves and sturdy shoes are the minimum. If the structure is questionable, a contractor should look before a crew goes in.

Then slow down on the sorting, not the hauling. Crews that sort as they work will pull out papers, jewelry, photographs and cash that a bulk haul would bury. One approach is to have the crew bag items in three groups, keep, sell or donate, and trash, with a family member present for the first day. Photograph the house before you begin, which also helps if a neighbor or city official has complained about conditions.

Take care of your own feelings, too. Cleaning out a home of a person you thought you knew can reveal a side of their life you did not. Many adult children say the work is easier in two or three shorter sessions, with a friend, than in one grim weekend.

## What if my parent rented?

A rental changes the clock more than the work. The lease and the landlord decide how long you have, and some landlords will agree to a short extension if you ask in writing. Tell the landlord about the death, ask about the security deposit and ask whether rent will be prorated. Keep paying rent from estate funds if you have access to them, or at least talk to the landlord before skipping a month. Many states restrict how a landlord can dispose of a tenant's belongings, so a quick call before anything is thrown out is worthwhile.

## How state law changes this

State law matters more for who has the authority than for the cleanout itself. Some states let a small estate be handled by affidavit, which gives a relative authority over the personal property with no court appointment at all. Others require letters from the court for almost anything. Rules on landlord handling of belongings, firearm transfers and when a vacant house loses coverage also vary. Look at your state's page, such as the [Florida guide](/estate-planning/florida) or [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas), or ask an attorney in the county where the house sits.
