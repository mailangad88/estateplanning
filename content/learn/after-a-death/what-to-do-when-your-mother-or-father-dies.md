Priya was at her office in Edison when the nursing home called to say her father had died in his sleep. Her mother had died years earlier. She has one brother in Chicago and a sister in the next town. Within an hour the funeral home was asking about arrangements, her sister was asking about the house, and her brother wanted to know whether anyone had checked Dad's will.

This page is for the adult child who gets that call. It sets out what is urgent, what only feels urgent, and how to share the work with brothers and sisters. For a full step-by-step list that covers any death, use our [what-to-do checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist). This one is about parents, and about the way a family changes when the parent was the center of it. The wider [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) covers the legal side, and a related overview sits on our [death of a parent](/life-events/death-of-a-parent) page.

## What has to happen in the first 48 hours?

Not much, and most of it is practical.

1. **Confirm the death is recorded.** If the parent died in a hospital, hospice or nursing home, the staff will pronounce and document it. If the parent died at home and was under hospice care, call the hospice number first. If there was no hospice and the death was unexpected, call 911; do not move anything.
2. **Choose a funeral home** and give them the parent's written wishes, if any exist. The person who decides is usually named in a health care directive or by statute. If siblings disagree, see [who decides funeral arrangements when family disagrees](/learn/after-a-death/who-decides-funeral-arrangements-when-family-disagrees).
3. **Secure the home.** Lock up, take in the mail, and check on pets and plants. If the home will be empty, tell the insurer. Our guide to [pets after an owner dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-takes-care-of-a-pet-after-its-owner-dies) covers animals.
4. **Tell the surviving parent in person,** if there is one, before the news spreads by text.
5. **Take medications and valuables** into safekeeping, with a list of what you took.

That is the whole first two days. The rest of the list can wait until after the funeral.

## What should you do in the first week?

The week is for papers, calls and a family meeting.

1. **Order certified death certificates.** Banks, insurers, pension offices and the motor vehicle agency each want a certified copy. Most families need ten or more. Our article on [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need) gives a count by task.
2. **Find the will without moving it.** Look in the home safe, a file cabinet, or with the parent's lawyer. If you find an original, do not staple, mark or unfold it more than needed. Take it to the county court or Surrogate and ask how to file it, or give it to the executor named in it. A will in a drawer is not a will anyone can act on.
3. **List the people who need to hear.** Employer or former employer, pension payer, Social Security, insurers, landlord or mortgage servicer. [Who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) gives the full list.
4. **Hold a family call.** Agree on roles as below.
5. **Look after the surviving parent.** If your mother or father lives, check that bills are paid, food is in the house, and someone is staying with them. A death often ends one parent's income, such as a pension or a Social Security check, and starts another.

## How do siblings split the work?

Fights among siblings rarely start over the estate. They start over who made the decisions in the first month. A one-page list sent by email solves most of it.

| Job | Who | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Funeral home contact | One sibling | Others give input by text, not by calling the funeral home |
| Paperwork keeper | One sibling | Holds the will, death certificates, copies of every letter |
| House and mail | The sibling nearest | Photographs the rooms before moving anything |
| Money log | One sibling, usually not the executor | Records every payment and who paid |
| Lawyer contact | The executor named in the will | Others route questions through them |
| Updates | One sibling | Weekly email, even if there is nothing new |

If your parent named one child as executor, that child has the legal job after the court appoints them. The others are not helpless. They can ask for the will, the inventory and updates. Our article on [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) shows what a good executor does, which also tells everyone else what to expect.

## What should you not do yet?

These are the mistakes that cause the most harm in the first month.

- **Do not use or empty the parent's bank accounts.** An account in your parent's name alone belongs to the estate. A child on the signature card is not the same as an executor, and taking money out can be treated as taking estate property. See [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account).
- **Do not sell the house or the car.** A sale usually needs the court's letters, and the family may not know yet whether there is a mortgage, a reverse mortgage or a lien.
- **Do not hand out belongings.** Items that "Mom promised me" are best listed and decided together. See [clearing out a parent's house](/learn/after-a-death/clearing-out-a-parents-house-after-death).
- **Do not pay your parent's debts from your own money.** Debts belong to the estate. Pay the funeral bill if you must, keep the receipt and expect reimbursement from the estate later. Our page on [who pays the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral) explains the order.
- **Do not sign anything from a creditor, collector or buyer** until the executor has looked at it.
- **Do not make large decisions about your own life in the first month.** Grief makes some choices look urgent that are not.

## What if there is no will?

If your parent did not leave a will, state law decides who inherits and the court appoints an administrator, who does the executor's job. In both states the surviving spouse and children are first in line, but the shares differ. For Illinois see [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois). For New Jersey see [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey).

An example. Priya's father had no will. His assets were a checking account of about $22,000 and a house worth $410,000, both in his name alone. As the three children are his only heirs, each takes an equal one-third by default. No child can sell the house until the court names an administrator. Priya, as the child who offered, petitions for the role after her siblings agree. The court's process, not any one child's decision, gives her authority.

## What about the first month?

The month is for choosing a lawyer, opening the estate and learning what exists. A short sequence helps.

1. **Meet with an estate attorney** with the will, the death certificates and a list of accounts. You do not need every number.
2. **Decide whether probate is needed.** Many small estates and many with beneficiary forms can use a simpler route, and some need none. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) shows which assets go through court and which pass by form.
3. **Collect statements** for the last three months for every account and loan you know about.
4. **Let the lawyer file** the will and the petition. The executor can then get a tax ID, open an estate account and begin to pay bills.
5. **Plan a gathering for sorting belongings** once the family is ready.

## How does state law change this?

The first-week steps are the same everywhere. The legal pieces differ by state. Illinois requires anyone holding the original will to file it with the circuit court clerk right away, and New Jersey sends the original to the county Surrogate. The small estate shortcuts, the time to file, the creditor claim deadlines and the inheritance or estate taxes are all state matters. Our guides to [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) summarize each.
