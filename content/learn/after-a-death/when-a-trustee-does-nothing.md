Priya's father died in the spring. His trust leaves everything to Priya and her two sisters, and his friend Gordon agreed to serve as trustee. It is now the following winter. No one has seen an account statement, the house is still in the trust's name with the utilities running, and Gordon stops answering texts. Priya wonders whether she is allowed to push, and how.

If you are in that position, you are not being impatient. This article explains why trustees stall, what the law expects of them, how to escalate step by step, and what a court can do.

## The short version

- A trustee must manage the trust prudently and move it along without unreasonable delay.
- Silence and delay are warning signs, but many have innocent causes.
- Start in writing, keep copies, and escalate one step at a time.
- Courts can compel action, order an accounting, make a trustee repay losses and remove a trustee.
- Claims have time limits that vary by state, so keep the timeline moving.

## Why would a trustee do nothing?

Understanding the cause helps you pick the right tone. The usual reasons are:

- **Overwhelm.** Many family trustees have never done this. Inventories, tax returns, property sales and beneficiary questions pile up, and avoidance sets in.
- **Grief.** The trustee may have lost a parent, spouse or friend, and cannot face the paperwork.
- **Family conflict.** The trustee may be waiting on a sibling, fear a fight over the house, or be in the middle of one.
- **Confusion about the document.** An unclear or outdated trust can freeze a careful trustee.
- **Self-dealing.** Occasionally a trustee delays because they are using trust property, living rent-free in the house, or hiding a shortfall.

You may not know which one applies. That is fine. The steps below work either way, and they produce the information that shows what is happening.

## What does a trustee have to do?

A trustee is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary-duty). In most states that follow the Uniform Trust Code approach, that means a duty to administer the trust in good faith, in line with its terms and in the beneficiaries' interests, and to act as a prudent person would. In practice, that includes:

- Locating and securing trust assets, and keeping them insured and maintained.
- Investing prudently rather than leaving large sums idle without reason.
- Paying valid debts, expenses and taxes.
- Making distributions as the trust requires.
- Keeping beneficiaries informed and providing accountings, which our article on the [trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) covers in detail.

Our [trust administration article](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) lays out the full list of early and later tasks, so you can compare them to what your trustee has done.

## What are the signs of a problem?

These patterns suggest a real issue rather than normal slowness:

1. No notice or copy of the trust months after the death.
2. No answer to polite written requests.
3. Assets sitting uninsured, unmaintained or uninvested.
4. Bills, property taxes or tax filings missed.
5. A trustee or relative living in or using trust property without a clear arrangement.
6. Vague explanations about where money went, or refusal to show statements.
7. Fees or reimbursements the trustee pays themselves without a record. See [trustee compensation](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation) for what is normal.

One missed call is not a pattern. A few of these together deserve action.

## What are the steps to push a trustee to act?

Move in order. Each step builds a record that helps if the matter reaches court.

1. **Send a written request.** Be specific and polite. Ask for a copy of the trust, a list of assets, a timeline for distributions, and the next steps the trustee plans. Use email plus a letter, and keep copies.
2. **Make a written demand for an accounting.** Name a reasonable deadline, such as 30 days. An accounting shows assets, income, expenses, fees and distributions, and often resolves the mystery.
3. **Send a letter from an attorney.** A short letter from a lawyer often prompts a response that months of texts did not.
4. **Offer mediation.** A neutral mediator can help when the trustee is stuck, outmatched or in conflict with the family, and it costs far less than court.
5. **File a petition to compel.** If the trustee will not act, a beneficiary can ask the court to order an accounting, require specific actions, or set a timetable.
6. **Ask for removal or a surcharge.** For serious or repeated breaches, you can ask the court to remove the trustee and appoint a successor, and to surcharge, which means to make the trustee repay the trust for losses their conduct caused.

Many matters end at step 2, 3 or 4. The trustee realizes the beneficiaries are serious, asks for help, or resigns and a successor steps in.

## What can a court order?

Depending on the facts and state law, a court can:

- Order the trustee to provide an accounting or documents.
- Order specific actions, such as securing property, filing tax returns or making a distribution.
- Reduce or deny the trustee's fees.
- Surcharge the trustee for losses, lost income or penalties caused by the breach.
- Suspend or remove the trustee and appoint a successor.
- Freeze or restrict trust accounts when assets are at risk.
- Shift attorney fees to the trustee in appropriate cases.

Courts generally prefer to fix the problem rather than punish, so a trustee who corrects course often avoids the harshest outcomes. For the executor equivalent, see [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed), which describes a similar process for a will-based estate.

## Is there a deadline to act?

Yes, but it varies. States set time limits for claims against trustees, and the clock can start on different events, such as receiving a report that discloses the problem, the trustee's resignation, or the end of the trust. Some limits are short once a report has disclosed the issue, and others run for years, so the deadline that applies depends on your state and the facts. Practical rule: do not let months of silence pass without sending a written request, and talk to an attorney if you have been waiting a long time.

## Who pays the costs?

Court costs and attorney fees can add up, so weigh them against what is at stake. Often each side starts by paying its own. A trust may allow the trustee to pay defense costs from trust assets, which effectively means the beneficiaries fund both sides, so courts review that closely. If a trustee acted improperly, a court can require repayment of fees or order the trustee to cover the beneficiaries' costs. If a beneficiary files a groundless claim, the court can shift costs the other way. A written demand and a mediation offer also show the court you tried to resolve it sensibly.

## How state law changes this

States differ on the notice a trustee must give, how fast it is due, the standard for removal, deadlines for claims, and fee-shifting.
