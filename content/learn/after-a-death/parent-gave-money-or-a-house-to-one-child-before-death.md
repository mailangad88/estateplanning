Their mother, Nadine, died in March. At her funeral lunch Brian mentioned that she had signed over the family house in Cherry Hill to his sister Alicia two years earlier, and that she had also paid off Alicia's car loan. Nadine's will, read a week later, splits what is left equally among her three children. The estate is mostly a checking account. Brian wants to know whether the house still counts.

This page explains what happens when a parent gave away money or property before death, when siblings can challenge the gift, how a deed or a joint account changes the picture, and how to approach a family discussion. It is part of our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death). The planning side is in [the will says split equally but the assets are not equal](/learn/what-if/will-says-split-equally-but-the-assets-are-not-equal).

## Can the estate take back a gift a parent made before death?

Not simply because it was unequal. Property a parent gave away while alive is no longer theirs at death, so it is not part of the estate the executor controls. The executor distributes what is left under the will or intestacy law, and has no authority to adjust for earlier gifts.

A parent with capacity is entitled to be generous to one child. The law presumes that a signed deed, a check or a transfer was meant. To undo it, someone has to prove a specific defect.

## When can a lifetime gift be reversed?

| Defect | What has to be shown | Who brings the claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lack of capacity | The parent could not understand the gift when it was made, often from medical records and witnesses | The executor or another heir |
| Undue influence | The child pressured or controlled the parent, usually with a close or fiduciary relationship, secrecy, and an unnatural result | The executor |
| Power of attorney misuse | An agent gave property to themselves without authority in the document | The executor or the court |
| Forgery or fraud | The signature, notary stamp or witness is not genuine | The executor |
| Loan, not a gift | A note, an email, or a pattern of repayment showed the parent expected the money back | The executor, as a creditor claim |

The common thread is proof. An executor in Illinois or New Jersey can sue to recover a defective transfer, and such lawsuits have their own deadlines, so talk to an attorney soon. For how a power of attorney agent fits in, see [can a power of attorney make gifts](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-a-power-of-attorney-make-gifts) and [power of attorney abuse](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-abuse).

## What if the parent just meant it as an advance?

An "advancement" is a gift treated as an early part of the child's inheritance. It counts against the share only if the parent said so in writing, or the child acknowledged it, or the will includes the rule. Many states require a written statement, and a parent who simply told the other children "this is her share" has not created one.

Say Nadine's estate is $90,000 and she had told Alicia verbally that the house, worth $400,000, was her whole inheritance. The will says equal thirds. Without a writing, each child receives $30,000 from the estate and Alicia keeps the house. If Nadine had signed a letter saying the house was an advance, the estate might have been divided only between Brian and his other sibling. That is why a letter drafted with the gift matters.

## What about a house deeded to one child?

A recorded deed is the simplest case. It is a completed transfer, and the title company will treat the child as the owner. Look up the deed at the county recorder or clerk's office to check the date, what the deed says about a life estate, and who signed. A deed signed years before death by a sound parent is hard to challenge. A deed signed in the last few months by a parent with dementia, with the child present and no other family told, is where attorneys look closely. Our page on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) explains the risks, and [giving your house to your child](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs) covers the tax cost.

On taxes, a house gifted during life keeps the parent's old cost basis. Say Nadine bought it for $60,000 and it is worth $400,000. Alicia's basis is $60,000, and if she sells she reports a gain of about $340,000, subject to the home sale exclusion if she lives there. Had she inherited the house, her basis would generally have been $400,000 at death. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). The unexpected cost often shows up years later, when Alicia sells.

## What about joint accounts and added names?

Adding a child to a bank account is the most common source of fights. Ask what the account was for. If the parent wanted the child to pay bills, many courts treat the child as a convenience signer, and the money still belongs to the estate. If the parent meant the child to get the money, the account passes to the child outside the will. Evidence includes the bank's signature card, who deposited, who withdrew, and what the parent said to the bank. Our page on [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) explains the sorting by account type.

## Do lifetime gifts have tax or Medicaid effects?

Yes, in two places. A gift above $19,000 per recipient in 2026 requires a gift tax return from the giver, and no tax is due until gifts exceed the $15,000,000 federal exemption. And if the parent applied for Medicaid for nursing home care within five years of a gift, the transfer can create a penalty period under the look-back rule. Most states use 60 months; see [the Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period). If the estate faces Medicaid recovery, that adds another claim against what is left.

## What should a sibling do if the gift looks wrong?

1. **Do not accuse anyone.** Ask for information first.
2. **Gather the facts.** The deed, bank statements for the last five years, power of attorney documents, medical notes from the months around the gift, and the lawyer's file if there was one.
3. **Ask the executor to review the transfers** and report in writing. An executor has a duty to marshal assets and to investigate claims.
4. **Look for a written explanation.** A letter, a note or a will provision that explains the gift answers many disputes.
5. **See an attorney before the deadlines run.** The time limits to challenge a will, bring a fraud claim or seek an accounting vary and they run out.
6. **Consider an equalizing agreement.** In many families the child who got the house agrees to pay a sum to the others. A written agreement, with each sibling advised, ends the fight. Our page on [buying out a sibling](/learn/property-and-assets/buying-out-a-sibling-or-co-owner-of-an-inherited-house-or-forcing-a-partition) shows how.

Siblings who stay out of court usually spend less and speak to each other afterward. Our article on [siblings who fight over a parent's estate](/learn/what-if/siblings-fight-over-a-parents-estate) describes what a contested case costs.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how advancements are proved, how long fraud and undue influence claims can be brought, how a joint account is presumed to be owned, and whether an agent under a power of attorney may make gifts without express authority. Illinois and New Jersey each have statutes on powers of attorney that limit gifts by an agent. Check your state guide, [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) or [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey), and ask an attorney about the deadlines that apply.
