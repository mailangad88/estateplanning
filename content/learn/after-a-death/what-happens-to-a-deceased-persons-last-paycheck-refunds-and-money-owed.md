A few weeks after the funeral, a check arrives in the mail. It is a final paycheck, or a utility refund, or a small insurance credit, and it is made out to your father. You are not sure whether you can cash it, whether you must hand it over to someone, or whether it belongs to the family at all.

The short rule is that money someone was owed at death belongs to the estate, not to whoever opens the envelope. This page explains who is entitled to the common kinds of money owed, how to collect it, and where the process slows down. This is one step in the larger [after-a-death process](/learn/after-a-death). For the broader task of finding what a person owned, see [how to find a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets).

## What counts as money owed to a deceased person?

It is any payment the person had earned or paid for but not yet received. People tend to think only of the last paycheck. The list is longer, and the small items add up.

| Source | Typical examples | Usually claimed by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employer | Final wages, unused vacation, expense reimbursement, a last bonus | Spouse or estate, depending on state law and policy |
| Tax authorities | Federal and state income tax refund for the year of death | Executor, or a spouse on a joint return |
| Landlord or utility | Security deposit, overpaid account balance | Executor |
| Insurers | Refund of unused auto or homeowner's premium | Executor |
| Health providers | Overpayment on a medical bill, a reimbursement from a plan | Executor |
| Retirement and pension plans | A final monthly payment, a death benefit with no named beneficiary | Named beneficiary, otherwise the estate |
| Courts and lawyers | A pending settlement, a class action payment | Executor, substituted in the case |
| The state | Dormant accounts held as unclaimed property | Executor or heirs, with proof |

An item with a named beneficiary, such as a life insurance policy or a payable-on-death account, does not follow this table. It goes to the person named on the form and does not pass through the estate.

## Who is entitled to the last paycheck?

The employer pays final wages to the estate or, in many states, to a surviving spouse or other close relative, often without any court order. Wage law is state law, so the rules differ. Some states let an employer pay a spouse or a dependent a limited amount of unpaid wages on a simple written request. Others require the payment to go to a court-appointed representative.

Tricia worked as a dental hygienist and died on a Tuesday. Her husband Marcus called her office manager, who said payroll could release the final check and accrued vacation to him once he sent a death certificate and a signed statement that he was her spouse. In another office, the same request might have required letters from the court. Marcus did not need to guess. He asked for the employer's requirements in writing and followed them.

Ask the employer for four things:

1. The final pay statement, showing hours worked through the date of death.
2. Whether accrued vacation or paid time off is payable and how much.
3. Any benefits that ended or continue, such as group life insurance or a 401(k) match.
4. The name of the person who will receive the payment, and the proof they need.

Group life insurance through work is a separate claim with its own beneficiary form. Our page on [claiming life insurance, pension and VA survivor benefits](/learn/after-a-death/claiming-life-insurance-pension-and-va-survivor-benefits) walks through it.

## How is a tax refund collected?

A refund for the year of death is claimed when the final return is filed. The final return covers January 1 through the date of death, and the refund is an estate asset. If a surviving spouse files a joint return, the spouse can claim it directly. If a court-appointed executor files, a copy of the court certificate goes with the return. Anyone else who is entitled to the money files Form 1310, titled Statement of Person Claiming Refund Due a Deceased Taxpayer.

State refunds follow their own forms. Our guide to [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) covers who files, which returns are needed and the usual timeline, so this page does not repeat it. One practical point: if the person had stopped filing, the refund for earlier years can still be claimed, but only within the IRS time limit for a refund. Do not wait.

## What do you do with a check made out to the dead person?

Do not deposit it, and do not sign the person's name. A bank will normally refuse it, and signing for a dead person can cause a real problem.

1. Photograph the check and the envelope.
2. Write to the sender with a copy of the death certificate. Say who you are and your relationship.
3. Ask them to void the check and reissue it to "the Estate of" the person, or to you as the executor.
4. Deposit it into the estate's bank account, not your own.

If no executor has been appointed, the estate has no account yet. A small amount may be reachable through a [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit), a sworn statement that lets certain payers release money without a full court case. Each state sets the dollar limit and waiting period. Illinois and New Jersey each have their own version, explained in our pages on the [Illinois small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit) and the [New Jersey small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit).

## What if you do not know who owes the estate money?

Look for patterns in the paper trail, then check the state. Bank statements show direct deposits from employers, pension plans and insurers. A tax return shows interest and dividends, which point to accounts. Mail that looks like a junk offer sometimes turns out to be a refund notice.

After that, search state unclaimed property records. States collect dormant accounts, uncashed checks, utility deposits and forgotten insurance money, and they hold them without a deadline for the owner or heirs. Searching is free, and it is the step most families skip. Our pages on finding unclaimed property for an [Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/finding-unclaimed-property-for-an-illinois-estate) and for a [New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/finding-unclaimed-property-for-a-new-jersey-estate) explain what proof the state will ask for. Be wary of anyone who calls offering to find money for a percentage. The state's own search is free.

## What if the person owed money and also owed debts?

The two sit on the same ledger. Money collected for the estate pays funeral costs, taxes and valid debts first, and heirs receive what remains. A refund does not go straight to a child just because the child found it. The executor needs to know what the estate owes before sharing anything out. Our page on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) shows the order of payment.

If the estate is small and the debts are large, collecting small refunds quickly can be the difference between covering the funeral bill and leaving it unpaid. If it is the other way around, the money simply joins the pool. Either way, deposit it, record it and keep the paperwork for the inventory. The executor will need that list later for the court inventory and for the final accounting, and a refund that arrives six months in is easy to forget unless it was written down the day it came.

Keep a simple running log with the date, the payer, the amount, and where it was deposited. A notebook works. So does a spreadsheet. If an heir questions the numbers a year from now, that log answers most of the questions before they become arguments.
