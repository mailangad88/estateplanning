You have just read the will, or been told what it says, and your name is not in it. Or it is, with a figure so small it feels like a message. Maybe a sibling or a home aide who has been around for two years walks away with the house.

The shock is real, and so is the question underneath it: can anything be done? This page sorts the situations where the answer is no from those where it is yes, and lays out what to do in the first month while the evidence still exists. It sits within our broader guide to [what happens after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## Does a child have a legal right to inherit?

Almost never. In nearly every state, a parent may leave property to anyone, including strangers and charities, and may leave nothing to a child. Louisiana is the best-known exception, with forced-heirship rules that protect some young or disabled children. Even there, the protection is limited and does not usually help a healthy adult child.

If a person dies with no will, the picture is different. Intestacy laws list who inherits, and children are typically near the top. If you were left out because the will is invalid and an earlier one has been replaced, intestacy may even apply. Our guide on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) shows the usual order.

Wills sometimes say, in a sentence or two, that a child was left out on purpose. That sentence matters. It prevents the argument that the parent forgot, and it is one reason attorneys write it. Our article on [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) covers how a person can do this carefully.

## Who does have protected rights?

A surviving spouse, in most states, cannot be disinherited entirely. The spouse can claim an "elective share," which is a fixed portion of the estate, or in community property states a share of what the couple earned during the marriage. See [spouse elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) for how this works.

Children born or adopted after the will was signed may be protected too. Many states have "pretermitted heir" statutes that give such a child a share, unless the will shows an intention to exclude them. These laws are aimed at accidents of timing, not at adult children who knew the will existed.

| Your position | Usual legal protection |
|---|---|
| Spouse | Elective share or community property rights in most states |
| Child born after the will | Often a statutory share if not provided for |
| Adult child named earlier, then removed | None unless the new will is invalid |
| Adult child never mentioned | Usually treated as a choice |
| Stepchild or unmarried partner | Generally none without a will or beneficiary form |

## When can a will actually be challenged?

When the way the will was made was flawed, not when the result is unfair. The grounds are narrow:

- **Lack of capacity.** The person did not understand who their family was, what they owned or what the document did when they signed. Poor memory alone is not enough. See [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity).
- **Undue influence.** Someone replaced the person's judgment with their own. See [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence).
- **Fraud or forgery.** The person was tricked about what they were signing, or the signature is not theirs.
- **Improper signing.** Missing witnesses, wrong number of signatures, or an unsigned page. Our guide on [will signing defects](/learn/wills/will-signing-defects-witnesses-dates-and-missing-pages) lists the common ones.
- **Revocation.** A later will or valid revocation replaced the one being offered.

Our deeper guide on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) covers evidence and costs. The shorter point for now is that the question is never "was it fair?" It is "was this truly the person's own decision?"

## What if the change came right before death?

A last-minute will is legal. A will signed on the final Tuesday of someone's life is not suspicious simply because of the timing. People often make changes when they know time is short. The question is who was involved and how.

Facts that raise concern look like this. The person had a dementia diagnosis or was heavily medicated. One beneficiary found the lawyer, drove them there and sat in the meeting. The new will cut out people the person had always supported. The family was kept away in the weeks before. No one fact is enough. Together they can be.

Facts that calm the picture include a long-standing lawyer who met the person alone, notes from that meeting, a doctor's note on the same day, and an explanation in the person's own words for why the plan changed. Many wills that surprise children turn out to have an ordinary reason, such as an estrangement the siblings did not know about.

## What if a caregiver, friend or new partner got everything?

These cases get the most attention from courts because the trusted-person problem is real. A paid or unpaid caregiver sees the person daily, controls who visits, and may handle money. In many states, a gift to someone in that position, combined with their involvement in preparing the will, creates a legal presumption that they must rebut. Some states also bar certain caregivers or professionals from taking gifts above a set amount unless the document is independently reviewed.

Keep in mind that the opposite also happens. An aide who gave years of kind attention may have been rewarded by a person who wanted to. The court is not asking whether the arrangement seems deserved. It is asking whether the person acted freely. Our guide to [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) lists patterns that suggest the person was being taken advantage of.

## What should you do in the first 30 days?

1. **Get a copy of the will.** If probate has been opened, the court file is public in most states. If not, ask the named executor. Our guide on [seeing the will after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-see-the-will-after-someone-dies) explains your options.
2. **Find the deadline.** Time limits to object can be as short as a few weeks to a few months after notice in some states. Look at the notice you received, and call a lawyer before the date passes.
3. **Ask for earlier wills.** The previous version is the one that would apply if the new one fails. Without it, you may gain nothing by winning.
4. **Write down what you know.** Dates, names, conversations, who visited, who handled money. Do it now, because memory fades.
5. **Preserve records.** Save texts, emails, voicemails and photographs. Do not take originals from the house.
6. **Do not contact the executor with accusations.** A calm request for information gets further than a threat, and anything you write can end up as an exhibit.
7. **Check for assets outside the will.** Retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death accounts and jointly held property pass by their own rules. A parent who left you out of the will may have named you on an account.

## Is it worth fighting?

Sometimes. A contest takes months and often more than a year, it freezes distributions, and legal fees can reach tens of thousands of dollars in a hard-fought case. A [no-contest clause](/glossary/no-contest-clause) can cost you a smaller gift if you file and lose, although many states enforce these clauses narrowly or not at all when the challenger had good cause.

The cases most worth considering involve strong evidence of incapacity or pressure, a large gap between the old and new plans, and enough money at stake to justify the cost. Settlements are common, often through mediation, and a negotiated share is a respectable outcome. Our overview of [how to object to probate](/learn/probate/how-to-object-to-probate) explains the procedure step by step.

### An example

Denise, 52, learned that her father's will, signed five weeks before his death, gave everything to his longtime housekeeper. A prior will from 2018 divided the estate between Denise and her brother. The lawyer who drafted the new will had never met her father. The housekeeper had booked the appointment. A doctor's chart from the same month noted "confusion, disoriented to date." Denise's own lawyer sent a preservation letter, obtained the medical records and the lawyer's file, and the matter settled at mediation with Denise and her brother receiving more than half of the 2018 shares. Nothing she did depended on the will feeling unfair. It depended on records.
