You have inherited a house, a lot or a farm, and you are thinking about selling it. Maybe it is too far away, maybe the carrying costs are piling up, or maybe the family wants to split the proceeds. The questions come in a predictable order: who is allowed to sell it, when, and how much tax will come out of the sale.

The good news is that inherited property is taxed more gently than most people fear. The harder questions are usually about authority and family agreement, not tax. This article covers both.

## Who has the right to sell inherited property?

The person with legal authority over the property has the right to sell it, and that is often not the person who will receive the money. Which person depends on how the property was owned:

- **Property in the dead person's name alone.** The executor named in the will signs, after the court issues letters. If there was no will, a court-appointed administrator signs. Our guide to [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) explains how authority is obtained.
- **Property in a living trust.** The successor trustee signs, using a certification of trust and the death certificate. See [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).
- **Property with a transfer-on-death deed.** The named beneficiary takes title after recording the deed and a death certificate, and then signs as owner in states that allow these deeds. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).
- **Property owned with a survivor.** A surviving joint tenant owns the house automatically and can sell it after recording the death certificate and any required affidavit.
- **Property owned by several heirs.** All co-owners generally must sign. See [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property).

Some states let an executor sell without court approval if the will grants the power. Others require a court order for sales of real estate, or notice to heirs. Title companies will not close until they see the documents that prove authority.

## When can you sell?

You can sell as soon as the person with authority is in place and the title company is satisfied, which can be before probate closes. Executors often sell during probate, using their letters. A sale before letters issue is not possible unless the property already belongs to someone else, such as a trust or a TOD beneficiary.

Practical points that affect timing:

1. **The mortgage.** The loan survives the owner, and payments continue. Lenders usually cannot call the loan due because of a transfer to an heir, though they can require a plan to pay. See [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/blog/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-when-the-owner-dies).
2. **Carrying costs.** Insurance, taxes, utilities and upkeep continue. A vacant home often needs a special insurance policy, so check coverage right away.
3. **The creditor period.** If the house might need to be sold to pay debts, the executor should know the debt picture before distributing proceeds. See [what happens to debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).
4. **Personal belongings.** The house must be emptied. Decide on keepsakes first, and do not give away or discard items of value until the executor approves.

## How is an inherited house taxed?

There is no federal tax on receiving an inheritance. Tax arises only if and when you sell, and it is measured from your **basis**, which for inherited property is generally its fair market value on the date of death. The IRS calls this a stepped-up basis. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) for the rule and its limits.

Here is an example. Hector's mother bought her house in 1988 for $85,000. It was worth $410,000 when she died. Hector's basis is $410,000, not $85,000. If he sells for $420,000 and pays $25,000 in commissions and closing costs, he has no taxable gain: the net sale price of $395,000 is below his basis, and the difference is a loss that is generally not deductible on a home he did not use for business. If the house were worth $480,000 at sale, the net gain over basis would be about $55,000.

Three details matter:

- **The gain is long-term.** Inherited property is treated as held more than one year, no matter how quickly you sell.
- **Get an appraisal.** A professional appraisal as of the date of death is the best proof of basis. If an estate files a federal estate tax return, the executor also reports values to heirs, and the basis you use should be consistent with it.
- **Report the sale.** Sales of real estate are reported on Form 8949 and Schedule D of your tax return, and the settlement statement shows the sale price and costs.

Losses on inherited property you did not live in or rent out are generally not deductible, though rules differ when the home was held as an investment or rental. Ask a CPA about your situation.

## Can you use the home sale exclusion on an inherited home?

Usually not, because the exclusion requires that you owned and lived in the home. The federal exclusion lets a seller exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for a married couple filing together, if the home was their main residence for two of the five years before the sale. An heir who never lived in the house does not meet the test. An heir who moved in and lived there for two years might, after the ownership requirement is met.

A surviving spouse gets a special rule. If the spouse sells within two years of the other spouse's death, and the conditions that would have applied to a married couple were met just before the death, the spouse may exclude up to $500,000. For most surviving spouses who inherit a house in which they lived, the stepped-up basis already makes the gain small, but the extra exclusion can matter for a long-held house in a high-priced area.

## Are there state taxes on selling or inheriting property?

Possibly. Most states do not tax an inheritance, but a handful have an estate tax or an inheritance tax that may apply to the transfer, and some have thresholds well below the federal level. The seller may also owe a transfer tax or recording fee on the sale of real estate, usually shared with or paid by the buyer. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for which states tax and how.

## What are the steps to sell inherited property?

1. **Confirm authority.** Obtain letters, a trustee certification or a recorded deed. Ask the title company what it needs.
2. **Get a date-of-death appraisal.** Keep the report with the estate records.
3. **Secure and insure the property.** Lock it, forward the mail, call the insurer about vacancy.
4. **Learn the payoff on the mortgage and any liens.** Order a payoff statement.
5. **Decide among the heirs.** Agree in writing on selling, listing price, repairs and how proceeds are divided.
6. **Handle the contents.** Distribute keepsakes and clear the rest.
7. **List and sell.** Work with an agent who has handled estate sales, and ask the title company to confirm the closing documents early.
8. **Report the sale.** Keep the settlement statement for the estate's or your own tax return.
9. **Distribute the proceeds.** The executor or trustee pays out only after debts and expenses are accounted for.

## How do different situations compare?

| Situation | Who signs | Likely taxable gain | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sold within months of death | Executor or trustee | Often small or none | Keep the appraisal and settlement statement |
| Sold years later | Executor, trustee or heirs | Gain above stepped-up basis | Track improvements and costs for basis |
| Heir lived in the home | Heir as owner | May qualify for the exclusion | Two-year ownership and use rules |
| Trust property | Successor trustee | Same step-up rule | Certification of trust for the title company |
| Co-owned by siblings | All owners | Split by share | One holdout can stall the sale |
| TOD deed property | Beneficiary after recording | Same step-up rule | Record the deed before listing |

## What if the heirs disagree?

When siblings disagree, the cheapest solution is almost always a written agreement. Common arrangements are a buyout by one heir at appraised value, a sale with an agreed split, or a period of shared use with agreed costs. A mediator can help when emotions run high. If nothing works, any co-owner may ask a court to order a sale through a partition action. Many states have adopted a uniform law that gives family co-owners a right to buy out the seller before a forced sale. Our article on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) explains it.

If the house is in another state, the executor may need a second proceeding there.
