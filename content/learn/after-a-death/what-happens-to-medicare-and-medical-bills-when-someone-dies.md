Within a day of a parent's death, the mail starts to include things like a Medicare Summary Notice, a hospital statement and a letter from a drug plan. Most people have two questions at once. Do we have to cancel something? And who pays for all this?

This page answers both. It covers what happens to Medicare, Medigap and drug plans, how to deal with medical bills that arrive after the funeral, what a surviving spouse should do about their own coverage, and where Medicaid is the exception. For the full to-do list of the first weeks, see our [checklist for what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

## Does Medicare end when someone dies?

Yes. Medicare belongs to the person and ends at death. It cannot be passed to a spouse or child. You do not need to cancel Original Medicare yourself, but the people who run the paperwork need to know.

Here is who to contact and what each one does.

| Coverage | What happens | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original Medicare (Parts A and B) | Coverage ends at death | Social Security is told of the death; confirm by calling |
| Part B premium | Usually taken from the Social Security payment | Ask Social Security to refund any amount taken after death |
| Medicare Advantage plan | Enrollment ends | Call the plan, give the date of death, ask about prorated premium |
| Part D drug plan | Enrollment ends | Call the plan; cancel mail-order refills |
| Medigap policy | Policy ends | Ask the insurer for any unearned premium back |
| Retiree or employer plan | Depends on the plan | Ask HR or the plan administrator for surviving-spouse options |

Our page on [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) explains why the last payment often must be returned and how the survivor's benefit works, which affects the Medicare premium picture too.

## Who pays the medical bills?

The estate does. Valid medical bills, including those from the final illness, are debts. The executor or administrator pays them from the person's assets, in the order state law sets, before heirs receive their shares. In New Jersey, last-illness bills have their own place in the priority list, described in our page on [creditor claims in a New Jersey estate](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate). Illinois sets its own order and deadlines, covered in [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate).

If there is not enough money, the creditors are paid in order and the remaining bills go unpaid. Heirs are not asked to make up the shortfall. See [what happens when an estate owes more than it has](/learn/probate/what-happens-when-an-estate-owes-more-than-it-has).

## When do family members owe the bill personally?

Rarely, but the exceptions are worth knowing before you sign anything.

- **You co-signed or promised to pay the bill.** A signature on a financial responsibility form makes you liable, no matter who received the care.
- **You were a joint account holder.** If the debt was in both names, the survivor owes it.
- **You signed a nursing home contract as the "responsible party" and promised to pay from your own funds.** Federal rules bar a facility that takes Medicare or Medicaid from requiring that promise as a condition of admission. Read what you signed and ask whether it was optional.
- **Community property or similar rules in other states.** These do not apply in Illinois or New Jersey.

A relative who simply helped with care, managed the paperwork or paid a few bills out of kindness does not take on the debt.

## What should you do with the bills that arrive?

Do not pay the first bill that comes. Hospitals and doctors sometimes send a balance before Medicare or a secondary insurer has paid, and the amount can drop substantially once claims process.

1. **Collect everything in one folder.** Keep the Medicare Summary Notice, explanation-of-benefits letters and every provider statement.
2. **Ask for an itemized bill** and compare it with the explanation of benefits.
3. **Wait for insurance to finish.** Medicare and secondary coverage need time to process claims for care given before death.
4. **Tell the provider the person has died** in writing and give the executor's name and contact information. If the executor has not yet been appointed, say so.
5. **Pay from the estate account** once the executor has authority. Paying from your own funds creates a reimbursement problem; see our page on [who pays the executor's expenses](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-the-executors-expenses).
6. **Keep a log** of every bill, its date, the amount, and what you did.

Collection agencies sometimes call a child as if the debt were theirs. You can say, in writing, that the person has died, give the executor's contact information, and ask them to stop contacting you. You do not need to confirm that you will pay. For more on handling the mail, see [how to handle a deceased person's mail and bills](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-handle-a-deceased-persons-mail-and-bills).

## What about a surviving spouse's coverage?

This is where real problems arise. A spouse who has their own Medicare is not affected. A spouse who was covered as a dependent on the other's employer plan, or who is under 65 and not yet on Medicare, may lose coverage.

- **COBRA.** If the employer has 20 or more workers, a covered spouse can generally keep the plan through COBRA after the employee's death, for up to 36 months, but pays the full premium. The election window is short, so ask HR in the first week.
- **Marketplace.** Losing coverage because of a death generally opens a special enrollment period. Check the deadline with the marketplace right away.
- **Retiree plans.** Some give a surviving spouse continued coverage. Ask for it in writing.
- **Turning 65 soon.** If the spouse is close to 65, ask whether it is better to sign up for Medicare now. A late-enrollment penalty can apply in some cases.

Do this before worrying about the estate. A gap in coverage is expensive in a way that no estate bill is.

## How does Medicaid differ from Medicare?

Medicare is health insurance for people 65 and older and some younger people with disabilities. Medicaid is a needs-based program that pays for things like nursing home care. They are not the same, and only Medicaid has an estate repayment rule.

Federal law requires states to try to recover certain Medicaid costs from the estate of a person who was 55 or older when they received long-term care benefits, with some limits. Illinois and New Jersey both have programs. They are covered in our pages on [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery) and [New Jersey estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery). Recovery is generally paused while a surviving spouse is alive or while a child under 21, or a blind or disabled child, survives. If a parent was on Medicaid, tell the estate's attorney before selling the house.

## How much time do you have?

Not as much as you might hope on the paperwork, but more than collectors suggest. Notify Social Security and the plans in the first week or two. Hold off paying medical bills until the claims settle, which can take a month or two. A formal creditor claim period runs in the probate case: in Illinois at least six months after publication, in New Jersey nine months from death. The deadlines are different for each estate, so ask the attorney handling it. The same holds for the final tax return, discussed in [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).
