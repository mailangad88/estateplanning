Almost every family finds out, a few weeks in, that the person had more accounts than anyone knew about. An old savings account at a bank across the state. A life insurance policy bought in 1994. A paycheck from the last job that never reached the mailbox. Sometimes the problem is the opposite, and you cannot find any records at all.

This page is a search plan, one piece of the larger [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death). It starts with the cheapest clues in the house, then moves to free searches that cover life insurance, unclaimed property and pensions, and ends with the safe deposit box. If you are the executor, this is part of building the inventory described in our [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

## Where should you look first?

Look at paper and screens before you phone anyone. The person left a trail even if they never meant to. Make a stack of everything that arrives for the next several months, because the mail is a map.

Gather these and read them with a pen in hand:

1. **The last two or three years of tax returns.** Look for schedules of interest and dividends. They name each bank and brokerage that paid income.
2. **Tax forms that arrive in January.** A 1099-INT or 1099-DIV points to an account. A 1099-R points to a pension or IRA. A 1098 points to a mortgage. A 1099-B points to sales of stock.
3. **Bank statements, check registers and canceled checks.** A monthly payment to an insurance company is a clue to a policy.
4. **Email.** Search the inbox for words like "statement," "policy," "account" and "premium." Our guide to [online accounts after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist) covers how to approach the email itself.
5. **Wallet, desk and glove box.** Old checkbooks, business cards of financial advisors, a key tagged with a bank name.
6. **The financial advisor, CPA and attorney.** They hold copies and may know of accounts you do not.

Then ask the post office to forward the mail, as covered in our page on [who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies).

## How do you use a credit report?

A credit report finds debts, not bank accounts. It will list every credit card, auto loan, mortgage and student loan that reports to the bureaus, along with the lender and the balance. That matters, because the estate must deal with creditors, and some are never mentioned in the person's papers.

The report also shows which address the person used and when. Old addresses are leads for unclaimed property searches. Our page on [who to notify](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) explains how to request the report with a death certificate and proof of authority.

## How do you find a lost life insurance policy?

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners runs a free life insurance policy locator. A spouse, child or the executor submits a request with the person's name, birth date and death details. Participating insurers search their records and, if there is a policy, contact the beneficiary.

It is not a single database, and a non-reply does not prove no policy exists. Support it with other steps.

- **Ask the employer.** Group life insurance and accidental death coverage are common, and the employer's HR office will know. Retirees sometimes keep a small policy.
- **Ask the union, alumni association or credit card issuer.** Some offer small group policies.
- **Look at the bank statements for premiums.** A quarterly debit to an insurer is the giveaway.
- **Check the state unclaimed property office.** Policies whose beneficiary was never found sometimes end up there.

Once you find a policy, our guide to [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and the one on [claiming an account as a beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/how-to-claim-an-account-as-a-beneficiary) show how the claim works. Insurers usually pay a named beneficiary directly, outside probate.

## How do you search for unclaimed property?

Every state keeps a list of money it holds for owners it could not find. Typical items include uncashed checks, final paychecks, utility deposits, insurance proceeds, dormant bank accounts and shares of stock. State treasurers and comptrollers also run a joint national search site.

Search under each state where the person lived, worked or owned property, and try name variations and maiden names. When you find a match, the claim form asks for the death certificate, your ID and proof you are the heir or executor.

Be careful about "asset recovery" companies that contact families. Claiming unclaimed property from the state is free. A finder who offers to do it for a percentage is charging you for something you can do yourself in twenty minutes.

## How do you get into a safe deposit box?

The bank will not open a sealed box for a person with no legal authority. The usual route is a death certificate and letters from the court naming the executor. Many banks then open the box in front of an officer and may inventory what is inside.

Many states have a shortcut for the first look. If no one has been appointed, a family member can sometimes open the box just to find a will, a deed to a burial plot or burial instructions, and nothing else may be removed. Some states also let the bank release the original will to the court. The rule varies, so ask the bank which procedure it follows in your state.

What if you cannot find the key? Look for a rental fee on the statements and call the branch. A bank can drill a box if the executor pays for it. If you do not know which bank holds the box, search the check register and old statements for an annual rental fee.

Florence, 66, was named executor for her brother Walter. His only paper trail was a stack of 1099 forms in a shoebox. One was from a brokerage she had never heard of. The firm confirmed the account only after she sent her court letters. A second form came from a small credit union, which held a savings account and a safe deposit box. The will was in the box. None of it was visible until she read the January forms.

## What about tax refunds, final pay and pensions?

Small amounts of money tend to be owed in several places.

**Tax refunds.** If the person overpaid, the refund goes on the final return. When a refund is owed to someone who is not a surviving spouse or a court-appointed representative, Form 1310 is attached to the return. Our guide to [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) covers the return itself.

**Final pay.** Contact payroll. Unused vacation pay and the last check often go to a spouse or family member under a state rule, sometimes by affidavit.

**Pensions and survivor benefits.** Ask the plan about a survivor benefit or a refund of contributions. For a plan from a company that has been bought or closed, the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation has a lost-pension search. Veterans' survivors can ask the Department of Veterans Affairs about survivor benefits.

**Social Security.** The funeral home normally reports the death. See our page on [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) for the one-time payment and the returned-benefit rule.

## Which assets need the court, and which do not?

Finding assets is step one. Step two is sorting them. Accounts with a named beneficiary, joint owners or a payable-on-death designation usually skip probate. Accounts in the person's sole name usually do not. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) is a quick way to sort what you found, and our page on [probate vs non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the difference.

Record each asset with its date-of-death value in an inventory. If a court is involved, the [probate inventory and accounting](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting) guide shows what the judge expects.
