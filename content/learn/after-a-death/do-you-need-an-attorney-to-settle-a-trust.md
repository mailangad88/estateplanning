You were named successor trustee, you have the trust in a folder, and a friend says you can do it all yourself. Another says one mistake will cost you your house. Both are partly right. The honest answer is that it depends on what the trust owns and who the beneficiaries are.

This page sets out when a family member can reasonably manage without a lawyer, and when hiring one is the cheaper choice. It belongs to our wider guide on [what families do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## The short version

- No law forces a trustee to hire a lawyer to settle a trust.
- A trustee who skips legal help takes on all the risk of a mistake.
- Simple cash-only trusts with agreeing beneficiaries are the best candidates for going without.
- Real estate, a business, tax questions and conflict are the signs to hire.
- One consultation at the start is often enough to set the course.

## When the answer is yes, you can do it yourself

A trust with a few bank and brokerage accounts, a short list of beneficiaries who get along, and no debts to worry about can be handled by an organized person. Carla served as trustee of her aunt's trust, which held two accounts and a car, and left everything equally to two nephews. She read the trust, got an EIN, opened an account, retitled the investments, paid the funeral bill and the final taxes, and split the rest. She hired a CPA for the tax returns and nothing else. It took ten months.

Her safety net was ordinary. She wrote down what she did, kept statements, sent the nephews a short report and got signed receipts. That discipline is the real requirement, with or without a lawyer. Our guide on [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) lists the steps.

## When the answer is no, and a lawyer pays for itself

| Situation | Why legal help matters |
|---|---|
| The trust owns a house or other real estate | Deeds, title companies, sale authority and property in other states |
| A business or professional practice is involved | Valuation, contracts, buy-sell terms and continuity |
| The estate may owe federal or state estate tax | Return deadlines, elections and valuation |
| Beneficiaries are in conflict, or one is difficult | Risk of surcharge or removal petitions |
| A beneficiary is a minor, disabled or receiving benefits | Continuing trusts and benefit rules |
| The trust language is unclear | Interpretation is a legal judgment |
| Retirement accounts are payable to the trust | Distribution rules and tax timing |
| There is a risk of creditor claims | Notice rules vary by state |

If two or more of these apply, the question is no longer whether to hire, but whom.

## What a lawyer actually does in a trust administration

Most of the work is not in court. An attorney reads the trust and tells you what it requires, prepares the certification of trust that banks accept, drafts the notices to beneficiaries and heirs, and checks which state's rules apply. They coordinate the CPA and appraiser, review the proposed distributions, draft the receipts and release, and help with a final accounting. If a court filing is needed, they handle it.

Compare the cost of that help with the cost of an error. A trustee who distributes before a creditor claim is known may owe the shortfall personally. A trustee who sells a house to a relative at a price below market invites a challenge. These problems are slow, expensive and usually avoidable.

## How to hire help without paying for a full engagement

1. **Start with one consultation.** Bring the trust, a list of assets and your questions. Ask what the biggest risks are and what you can do yourself.
2. **Ask for limited-scope help.** Many firms will review documents, draft specific notices or check a final accounting for a fixed fee, while you do the legwork.
3. **Ask how fees are set.** Some firms charge hourly, some flat. Our article on [how to choose an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) lists the questions to ask.
4. **Plan for the paperwork trail.** Keep each invoice with a one-line note on how it served the trust.
5. **Come back at the end.** A short review of the final accounting before distribution is cheap protection.

## A case where skipping the lawyer went wrong

Hector was trustee of his mother's trust, which owned her house and a brokerage account for him and his two brothers. He moved fast. Within four months he sold the house, paid out the proceeds in thirds and closed the accounts. Then two things surfaced. A hospital sent a bill for his mother's final stay that had gone to the wrong address, and the IRS sent a notice about her last income tax return, which an old accountant had never filed. Nothing was left in the trust.

Under the law of most states, a trustee who pays beneficiaries before settling known and reasonably knowable claims can be responsible for the shortfall. Hector's brothers were willing to return some money, but one of them had spent his share on a down payment. A one-hour consultation at the start would have told Hector to keep a reserve, search for creditors and confirm the tax filings before paying anyone. That is the pattern: the lawyer's value is often in the three things you did not know to ask.

## Questions to ask yourself before you decide

- Does the trust own anything that needs a deed, a title, a license or a contract signed?
- Can I name every beneficiary and say what each one gets, without guessing?
- Do all the beneficiaries trust me, and each other?
- Am I confident about the dates for notices, tax returns and creditor periods in this state?
- Can I afford to repay the trust from my own pocket if I get something wrong?

If you answer no to any of these, get a consultation. If you answer yes to all, you may still want an hour to have a lawyer look over your plan.

## Who pays the legal bill?

Reasonable legal fees for administering the trust are generally paid from trust assets, which means the beneficiaries bear the cost indirectly. That is one reason a trustee should avoid unnecessary work. If a trustee also takes a fee, the combined total should make sense. Our page on [trustee pay](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation) covers what is reasonable.

Fees for the trustee's personal defense are different. If beneficiaries claim the trustee acted wrongly and a court agrees, the trustee may end up paying their own lawyer and sometimes the beneficiaries' lawyer.

## When there is also probate

A trust does not always avoid court entirely. An asset left outside the trust may need probate, usually under a pour-over will. Many states have a [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) for modest amounts. Our guide on [whether you need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer) explains the line between handling it yourself and hiring.

## How state law changes this

Procedures differ in how much a trustee must file, whether trust accountings get court approval, how creditors are notified and how long claims can linger. Some states have simple statutory procedures for nonprofessionals, and others are more technical. If the trust is governed by one state and the property sits in another, local counsel in the property state may be worth a short call. The [Illinois guide](/estate-planning/illinois) and [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas) show how two states approach it.
