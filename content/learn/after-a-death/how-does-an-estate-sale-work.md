Three weeks after her mother died, Denise stood in a Joliet living room with her brother, staring at forty years of furniture, china and a basement full of tools. Neither of them wanted most of it. A neighbor suggested an estate sale. Neither of them knew how one worked or whether they were allowed to hold one.

This page explains what an estate sale is, who may authorize one, what to do before the company arrives, how the money is handled and when a different approach is better. If you are clearing a whole home, our guide to [clearing out a parent's house after death](/learn/after-a-death/clearing-out-a-parents-house-after-death) covers the larger job.

## What is an estate sale?

It is the sale of the contents of a home, by the people settling the owner's affairs, over a few days. A professional company usually runs it. They sort and price the items, photograph and advertise them, stand in the house while buyers browse, collect payment and clean up afterward. A sale often runs Thursday to Saturday, with prices dropping on the final day.

It is not the sale of the house. The house itself follows a separate path, discussed in [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property).

## Who has the right to hold one?

Someone with legal authority over the property. That depends on how the items are owned.

| Situation | Who can sign the contract |
| --- | --- |
| Will, probate opened | The executor, after the court issues letters testamentary |
| No will, probate opened | The administrator the court appoints |
| Items in a living trust | The successor trustee |
| Small estate, affidavit used | The person who signs the affidavit, within its limits |
| Nothing opened yet | No one. Wait, or open the estate first |

Family members often start before this is settled, because the house is a burden. Resist that. A sale held before an executor is appointed, or one that sells an item the will gave to someone, leads to disputes that last far longer than the sale. If probate is not yet open, the first task is to find out who will be the executor and get them appointed. Our page on [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) explains the role.

## What should happen before the company comes?

More than most people expect. Work through these steps in order.

1. **Read the will.** Look for gifts of specific items: a ring, a piano, the dining table. Those are not for sale. See [dividing personal property and family heirlooms](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms).
2. **Let family take what they want.** Set a short window, tape names on items or use a written list, and settle disputes before the sale rather than in the driveway.
3. **Pull out the sensitive things.** Remove documents, tax records, checkbooks, passports, medications, firearms and electronics before anyone else enters. Firearms have their own rules; see [firearms in estate planning](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning).
4. **Get valuables appraised.** Jewelry, art, coins, watches, collectibles and anything signed or old should be looked at by an appraiser, not priced by the sale company alone. Our page on [leaving jewelry and heirlooms](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-jewelry-heirlooms-and-sentimental-items) shows what sentimental and market value mean here.
5. **Photograph the house as it is.** The pictures document what existed on the date of death.
6. **Check insurance.** A vacant house hosting strangers needs the right coverage. Call the insurer and tell them about the sale.
7. **Choose the company.** Interview more than one, ask for references and check for a written contract.

## What does a company charge?

Two models are common. A commission is a percentage of what sells, and a flat fee is a set price. Many companies combine them or add charges for advertising, cleanup and insurance. The percentages and the rules vary by company and region, so do not rely on a number you read online.

What you want in the contract:

- The commission rate or fee, and every other charge, spelled out.
- Whether the company buys leftovers or removes them.
- Who is responsible for theft or damage during the sale.
- The date the money is paid, and a sales report that lists each item.
- Who has the right to withdraw an item before the sale.

An illustration with invented numbers: Denise's sale brings in $9,400. The company keeps 35 percent, $3,290, and charges $250 for advertising. The estate receives $5,860 and a list of what sold. That sum is part of the estate and counts as an asset on the inventory.

## What happens to the money?

It goes to the estate or the trust. The executor deposits the check in the estate bank account, opened with the estate's tax ID, and not in a personal account. The money is then used to pay expenses or is divided with everything else under the will. If the executor spends estate funds on the cleanout first and wants to be repaid, the proper path is described in our page on [who pays the executor's expenses](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-the-executors-expenses).

Keep a copy of the sale company's report, the check and the deposit slip. They are part of the accounting an executor eventually prepares. In Illinois a final account may be needed; in New Jersey, family releases or a formal accounting close the file. [How an executor values the estate](/learn/after-a-death/how-an-executor-values-the-estate) explains why a record of the day-of-death value helps.

## Are the proceeds taxed?

Rarely in a meaningful way. Most property you inherit takes a basis equal to its value at death (see [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis)), so selling a couch for less than that produces no gain. Items worth more, such as art, jewelry or a coin collection, should be appraised so the estate can show the starting value. If the estate is large, the executor includes the items in the inventory regardless of whether they are sold. This is general information, and an accountant should look at an estate with significant collectibles.

## What do you do with what does not sell?

Plan for it before the sale. Companies handle leftovers in different ways.

- **Donation.** Charities pick up furniture and clothing, and may give a receipt for the estate's records.
- **Buyout.** Some companies make a lump sum offer for the remaining contents.
- **Consignment or online sale.** Good for a few valuable pieces that did not move.
- **Junk removal.** Last, and a cost to the estate.
- **A local municipal rule.** Some towns require a permit or limit signs and parking for sales at a home. Call the municipal clerk in Illinois or New Jersey before advertising.

## Is an estate sale always the right choice?

No. A professional sale makes sense when a house is full and the contents have real resale value. It does not make sense when there is little of value, when family wants most of the items, or when the sale would draw crowds to a home that is easy to rob. Some alternatives:

| Option | Best when | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Estate sale | Full house, decent contents | Commission, theft, timing |
| Online auction or listing | A few good items | Shipping, effort |
| Donation and haul-away | Little of value | No cash back |
| Family division only | Items are mostly sentimental | Disputes; keep a written list |
| Sale with the house | Buyer wants furnished | Price and condition issues |
