Marcus's aunt left him a brokerage account with a few thousand shares of one company and a note: "Don't do anything dumb." He wants to sell, because he does not need a pile of one company's stock. He does not know whether it is his yet. He does not know if a big tax bill is coming, or whom to call first.

The answers depend on how the account was titled and on one tax rule that works in the heir's favor. This page covers when you can sell, what the tax looks like, and how to move shares into your own name. For the wider tax picture, see [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs), and for the steps after a death more generally, start at our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## Whose shares are they, and when can you sell?

The answer turns on how the account was set up. Look at the statement or ask the brokerage.

| How the account was titled | Who controls it after death | When can the heir sell? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transfer-on-death (TOD) or beneficiary named | The named beneficiary | After the firm retitles it, often a few weeks after receiving a death certificate |
| Joint with right of survivorship | The surviving owner | Right away, once the firm updates the account |
| Held in a living trust | The successor trustee | After the trustee shows trust papers and a death certificate |
| Individual account, no beneficiary | The executor, under court authority | After letters are issued, and usually after distribution |
| Retirement account (IRA, 401(k)) | The named beneficiary | Rules are different; see the retirement guide below |

If you are an heir of an individual account, you cannot sell anything yet. You need the executor to receive [letters testamentary](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration), collect the account and either sell the shares or transfer them to you. The executor might sell to raise money for debts, funeral costs or taxes. Heirs often want the shares themselves, and that is a request to make in writing.

For a TOD account, our page on [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) shows how that route skips probate. Retirement accounts are taxed differently, and [tax on inherited IRAs, 401(k)s and annuities](/learn/after-a-death/tax-on-inherited-ira-401k-annuity-and-savings-bonds) covers them.

## How does the step-up in basis work for stock?

Your cost basis is the starting number for working out gain. For stock you bought, it is what you paid. For stock you inherit, it is generally the market price on the date the owner died. That reset is the [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), and it wipes out the gain the owner built up during life.

Here is a worked example. Marcus's aunt bought her shares years ago for $8,000. On the date she died they were worth $60,000. His basis is $60,000. If he sells for $61,500 two months later, his taxable gain is $1,500. If the price falls and he sells for $58,000, he has a $2,000 loss. Neither the $52,000 of growth in the aunt's lifetime nor the aunt's original price figures into it.

Two features help. First, inherited property is treated as a **long-term** holding no matter how briefly you owned it, so gains get the lower long-term rates. Second, a loss on a sale is generally deductible as a capital loss. For the underlying rules and the exceptions, see [how step-up in basis works](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

Joint accounts need a closer look. A married couple's joint stock may get a step-up on only half in most states, and on all of it in community property states. The details are in [step-up in basis for married couples and joint property](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis-for-married-couples-and-joint-property).

## Should you sell inherited stock right away?

There is no deadline to sell, but there is a trade-off. Because the basis is reset, selling soon costs little tax. Waiting means you carry market risk on a position you did not choose.

Questions worth asking before you decide:

1. **Would you have bought this?** If not, selling and buying what fits your plan is usually reasonable.
2. **How much of your net worth is it?** One stock that dominates your holdings is a risk. Index funds or a broad mix are less so.
3. **Do you need the cash soon?** A down payment or tuition bill argues for selling.
4. **Is there an emotional reason?** Some families keep a company's shares because a grandparent worked there. That is a fair reason, if it is a conscious one.

Dividends and interest are another point. Dividends paid after the date of death belong to whoever owns the account when paid, which can be the estate before distribution and you after. They are taxable to that owner. Mutual funds can also pay out capital gains distributions that the fund realized during its year, and those are taxable even if your own gain is nil.

Keep an eye on the estate's needs. If the executor is still paying debts or may owe estate tax, an heir who sells shares that the estate needs to sell can cause trouble. Coordinate with the executor, who may hold back funds until [the creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) ends.

## How do you get the shares into your name?

The steps are mostly paperwork. Expect each brokerage to be slightly different.

1. **Call the firm and say the owner has died.** Ask for the estate or inheritance department, and for its checklist.
2. **Send certified copies of the death certificate.** Ask first whether they return the originals.
3. **Add the authority papers.** A TOD beneficiary usually sends a claim form and ID. An executor sends letters testamentary. A trustee sends a certification of trust.
4. **Open a new account in your name.** Many firms transfer shares "in kind," so you can hold them without selling. Others will cash them out on request.
5. **Ask for a date-of-death basis report.** Check that the firm used the right date. If the basis shows "unknown" or is the old purchase price, correct it before you sell.
6. **Keep the paperwork.** Save the date-of-death statement with your tax records. When you sell years later, it proves your basis.

Paper certificates go through the company's transfer agent, which has its own requirements and can take weeks. Foreign shares add a layer, since a foreign broker may require a local probate step.

## What goes on your tax return?

You do not report the inheritance itself on your income tax return. You report the **sale**, on Form 8949 and Schedule D. The brokerage's statement lists the sale price, and the basis is stepped up to the date-of-death value. Many firms report a basis for inherited shares but it is sometimes incomplete, and the IRS gets the number you file. Check it.

If the estate was large enough to file a federal estate tax return, the value on that return fixes your basis, and the executor should give you the figure. Most estates are far below the federal threshold, which is $15 million per person in 2026. State inheritance or estate taxes are separate; see [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) if the person lived in or owned property in one of the few states that charge them.

Stocks that were in the estate and sold by the executor produce a gain or loss on the estate's tax return, not yours, so ask which applies. Our guide on [how an executor values the estate](/learn/after-a-death/how-an-executor-values-the-estate) shows where the date-of-death numbers come from.
