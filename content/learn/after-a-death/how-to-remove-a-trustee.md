Removing a trustee is the last resort in most families, and a decision that has been building for months. Karen's brother has been trustee of their father's trust since the spring. He has not sent a single statement, and Karen recently learned from the county website that the family's rental property was sold. She wants him gone, and she wants to know if she is allowed.

This article explains the three routes to replace a trustee, what a court needs to see, how to prepare a petition and what happens afterward. It builds on [what to do when a trustee does nothing](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-trustee-does-nothing), which covers earlier steps like demand letters. It is part of our broader guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## What are the ways to remove a trustee?

There are three, and the first two avoid court.

1. **The trust's own method.** Some trusts say who may remove a trustee, such as a trust protector, a majority of adult beneficiaries or a named person. Follow what the document says and give the notice it requires.
2. **Resignation or agreement.** A trustee can usually resign by giving written notice to the beneficiaries and a successor. Many states also let all beneficiaries and the trustee agree to a change.
3. **A court order.** If the first two fail, an interested person asks the court to remove the trustee and appoint a successor.

Start by reading the trust. Many people head straight to court when a short clause would have solved the problem.

## What grounds do courts accept?

Judges are reluctant to remove a trustee the grantor chose. The grantor trusted this person, and courts give that trust weight. So you need real reasons. In states that follow the Uniform Trust Code, the list generally looks like this:

| Ground | What it looks like in practice |
|---|---|
| Serious breach of trust | Self-dealing, taking trust money, selling property at a bargain to a friend. |
| Persistent unfitness or failure to administer | No accounting for a year, missed tax returns, ignored requests. |
| Lack of cooperation among trustees | Co-trustees who cannot agree, which stalls the trust. |
| Substantial change in circumstances | The trustee develops dementia or moves away, and removal best serves everyone. |
| Hostility that harms administration | Not mere friction, but a breakdown that stops the trustee from doing the job. |
| Unfitness through conflict of interest | The trustee's personal interests clash with the beneficiaries'. |

What does not usually count: you dislike the trustee, the trustee made a single honest error, the trustee is a family member you prefer not to deal with, or you think another sibling would do better. A court will not choose between two acceptable trustees for you.

For a sense of how fiduciary failures are judged, our article on [trustee liability](/learn/after-a-death/can-a-trustee-be-personally-liable) lists the mistakes that most often bring trouble.

## Who can ask a court to remove a trustee?

An "interested person" can petition. That generally includes current beneficiaries, remainder beneficiaries, a co-trustee and, in many states, the grantor if still living. A distant relative with no stake normally cannot.

Karen is a beneficiary, so she has standing. A neighbor who dislikes her brother does not. If a beneficiary is a minor, a parent or guardian files, and the court may appoint a guardian ad litem to protect the child's interests.

## What steps lead to a successful petition?

Preparation decides these cases. A petition that says "he is a bad trustee" loses. A petition with dates and documents has a chance.

1. **Gather the trust and amendments.** Get every version, plus the death certificate and any court papers.
2. **Write down a timeline.** Dates of requests, answers, silences and any known transactions.
3. **Send a written demand for an accounting,** with a reasonable deadline, and keep proof of delivery. Our guide on the [trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) explains what you can ask for.
4. **Collect outside evidence.** County records for a sale, bank statements you are entitled to, insurance cancellation notices, or tax notices.
5. **Offer mediation.** Courts like to see you tried. It also gives the trustee an exit.
6. **Talk with an attorney.** Ask what your state requires, such as venue, notice to other beneficiaries and the likely burden of proof.
7. **File the petition.** It asks the court to remove the trustee, name a successor, order an accounting and, if appropriate, surcharge the trustee for losses and award fees.
8. **Attend the hearing.** The judge may hear testimony, review records and rule from the bench or later in writing.

## Can the court do something short of removal?

Often it does, and that is one reason petitions get filed with several requests at once. A judge can order an accounting, require the trustee to post a bond, limit the trustee's powers, restrict a bank account, appoint a co-trustee or supervisor, reduce the trustee's fees, or order repayment of a loss. These steps fix the problem without ending the relationship, which suits trusts where the grantor picked the trustee for a reason.

The same toolbox appears for executors, as described in our article on [whether an executor can be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed). The reasoning is nearly identical, though a trust usually has less court oversight to begin with.

## Who takes over after a trustee is removed?

The trust usually names a successor, and if the removed trustee was the first choice, the next person on the list steps in. If no one is named or the named person cannot serve, the beneficiaries may agree on a replacement, or the court appoints one. Courts sometimes appoint a professional fiduciary when the family is deeply divided, and that person's fees come out of the trust.

The old trustee must turn over the assets, account numbers, tax records, keys and the full file. A new trustee should send a letter to every bank and broker with the court order, change signers, secure real estate and request an accounting for the old trustee's entire period of service. Then the work in [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) picks up again, often with a longer to-do list than usual.

## What does it cost, and who pays?

A trustee who resigns after a firm letter costs very little. A contested removal can mean depositions, a hearing and months of delay, and the legal fees can be significant. Who pays varies. Some trusts let the trustee defend with trust money, so the beneficiaries fund both sides, and courts look at that skeptically. If the trustee is found to have breached, many courts reduce fees, order repayment and shift legal costs. If the petition was filed without good cause, costs may land on the petitioner.

Weigh the dollar amount at stake. If a trust holds a few thousand dollars, mediation or a negotiated resignation often makes more sense than a trial.

## What can you do now to avoid this problem in your own plan?

Name a trustee with the time and temperament to do the job, and name a backup. Include a clear removal and replacement clause, and consider a trust protector for larger or longer trusts. Our article on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) discusses the tradeoffs among a family member, a friend and a professional. If a trust will last for years, because it pays a child over time or supports someone with a disability, the stakes of that choice are higher.
