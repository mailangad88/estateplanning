Someone you love has died and the funeral director asks how many copies of the death certificate you want. It feels like a small question in the middle of a very large day. It turns out to be one of the few decisions in the first week that you will notice again later, when a fourth bank asks for an original and you only have two left.

This page covers how many certified copies to order, who needs them, what they cost, how long they take, and what to do when something goes wrong. For the larger picture, start with our guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## What is a certified death certificate?

A certified death certificate is an official copy issued by the state or county vital records office. It carries a raised or embossed seal, a registrar's signature or special security paper. That mark is what banks and agencies rely on to know the document is real.

A photocopy has none of that, which is why it is rarely accepted. Two other documents get confused with it. A **death notice** is the obituary. A **proof of death** letter from a hospital or hospice is not the same as the filed record. For nearly every legal task, you need the certified certificate.

The certificate also contains sensitive details: full name, birth date, Social Security number, parents' names and the cause of death. Treat spare copies like a passport. Do not leave a stack on a kitchen counter, and do not mail one without tracking.

## How many copies do you really need?

Count institutions, not documents. Each organization that has to confirm the death will want its own certified copy, and some keep it. Here is a realistic list for a typical estate.

| Who asks for one | Why |
|---|---|
| Each bank or credit union | To retitle, freeze or close accounts, or pay out a payable-on-death balance |
| Life insurance companies | To process each claim, one per policy |
| Pension, annuity and IRA custodians | To pay survivor or beneficiary benefits |
| The probate court | To open the estate and get letters from the court |
| Brokerage firms | To transfer or liquidate securities |
| County recorder or title company | To clear a deed or sell real estate |
| DMV | To transfer or cancel a vehicle title |
| Employers and unions | For final pay and benefits |
| Your tax preparer or the IRS, if asked | To file the final return |

Maria, 54, lost her father in the spring. He had two bank accounts, a brokerage account, an old 401(k), a small life insurance policy through work and a house in his name alone. She ordered five copies and was out of them by June. The second round took three weeks to arrive, and the closing on the house waited.

Ten to twelve is a sensible starting point for an estate like that one. If the estate is smaller, order fewer. The cost of an extra copy is trivial next to the cost of a delayed claim.

## What do death certificates cost?

The state or county sets the fee, so any specific number depends on where the death occurred. In general, the first copy costs a set amount and each additional copy in the same order costs the same or somewhat less. Funeral homes often add a small service charge for ordering on your behalf, which is usually still worth it for convenience.

Three practical points:

- **Order from the right place.** The record is held where the person died, not where they lived or where you live now.
- **Pay attention to who is selling.** Several commercial websites charge a markup on top of the government fee. The state or county vital records website is usually the cheapest direct route.
- **Fee waivers exist in limited cases.** Some states provide free certified copies to claim certain veterans' or survivor benefits. Ask the agency you are claiming from.

## How long does it take?

Two steps happen before you can hold the certificate. A medical professional completes the medical section, and the funeral director completes the personal information and files the record with the state, usually electronically. Once the state registers it, copies can be issued.

When the cause is clear and the physician acts quickly, that often takes under two weeks. Delays come from a few predictable places:

- A **medical examiner or coroner case**, such as an unexpected or accidental death, which requires investigation.
- A pending **autopsy or toxicology report**, which can take weeks or months.
- A **doctor who is slow to sign**, which happens more often than anyone admits.
- Paper rather than electronic filing in some counties.

If the cause is still under investigation, the state can usually issue a certificate showing the cause as "pending." Many banks and insurers accept that for ordinary tasks. A life insurer may hold a claim until the final cause is listed, especially for a recent policy.

## How do you order extra copies?

1. **Ask the funeral director to order your whole batch at once.** They have the process down and can often get the paperwork moving the same day.
2. **Decide the number** using the list above, then add two or three spares.
3. **Confirm the details on the draft.** Check spelling of the name, dates, marital status and Social Security number before the record is filed. Errors are easier to catch before registration.
4. **Order more later from the vital records office** if you run short. Most offices take orders online, by mail or in person. Expect to show your own ID and proof of your relationship.
5. **Keep a log.** Write down which institution has which copy, the date you sent it and whether it was returned.

This is exactly the kind of record the executor will want when settling accounts. ## How can you stretch the copies you have?

Not every institution needs to keep a certified copy. A few habits go a long way.

Ask whether you can **mail or upload a scan first**. Some banks open a case from a scan and ask for the original only at the end. Ask whether the **original will be returned**. Many insurance companies and some banks will photocopy it and send it back in the prepaid envelope you include. Where you are dealing with several accounts at one firm, such as three accounts at one brokerage, one certificate often covers all of them if you list the accounts in the cover letter.

Finally, the probate court needs its own. Once you have court-issued [letters testamentary or letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration), you will often use those in place of the death certificate for some tasks, because they prove you have authority, not merely that the person died.

## What if the certificate is wrong?

A typo in a name or a wrong birth year can freeze an insurance claim or a title transfer, because the paperwork no longer matches. Start with the funeral home, which filed it, then with the vital records office. Correcting a record usually takes a short application with a supporting document, such as a birth certificate or passport, and in some states a notarized affidavit.

A different problem is the cause of death listed on the certificate. If a family disagrees with the medical finding, the correction process is far slower and usually involves the certifying physician or medical examiner. Talk to an attorney before spending money on that fight, especially if an insurance payout turns on it.
