Within a few weeks of taking over, an executor is asked for numbers. The probate court wants an inventory. The bank wants to know what is in the account. A sibling asks what the house is worth, and the accountant wants figures for the final tax return. All of them are asking for the same thing: the value of each asset on the day the person died.

That date is the anchor for nearly everything that follows. This page explains how to value each common kind of asset, when to pay for an appraisal, and where the numbers end up. For the wider picture, start at the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death). For the court filing itself, see our [probate inventory and accounting guide](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting), and for the broader role, the [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

## The short version

- Use the date of death, not today's value and not what the person paid.
- Statements and brokerage letters cover most financial assets.
- Order appraisals for real estate and for anything unusual or valuable.
- Write the source of every number next to it.
- Do not guess on the house. It is usually the largest asset and the one siblings argue about.

## Why does the date of death matter?

Three things depend on it. The first is the probate inventory, which in most states must list the value of what the estate owns at death. The second is tax. Under the [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) rule, most inherited property takes a new tax basis equal to its value at death, so an heir who later sells owes capital gains tax only on growth after that date. The third is the federal estate tax. As of 2026 the basic exclusion is $15 million per person, so most estates owe nothing, but an executor still needs the numbers to know that.

Consider Elena, executor for her father. Her father bought the house in 1991 for $95,000. If it is valued at $410,000 on the day he died and the heirs sell it for $420,000 a year later, they report a $10,000 gain, not a $325,000 gain over thirty years. A careless date-of-death figure of $300,000 would have put an invented $120,000 of profit on their tax return.

## How do I value each type of asset?

| Asset | Usual method | What to collect |
|---|---|---|
| Checking and savings | Balance on the date of death plus interest earned to that date | Statement or bank letter |
| Stocks, bonds, ETFs | Average of the highest and lowest quoted prices that day | Brokerage date-of-death valuation |
| Mutual funds | Net asset value (closing price) on that day | Fund company statement |
| House or land | Appraised fair market value | Licensed appraiser's report |
| Cars, boats, RVs | Fair market value from a pricing guide, or a dealer quote | Printout with mileage and condition |
| Household goods | Reasonable estimate; appraisal for items of real value | Photos, list, appraisals |
| Jewelry, art, collections | Appraisal | Written appraisal |
| Closely held business | Business valuation | Valuation report, financial statements |
| Life insurance | Face amount paid at death | Insurer's claim statement |
| IRAs and 401(k)s | Balance on date of death | Custodian statement |

The stock formula is the part people miss. The value is not the closing price. For a listed security it is generally the mean of the day's high and low trading prices. Most brokerages will produce a letter showing that figure.

## What about the house?

Order a date-of-death appraisal from a licensed appraiser. Tell them the date, since the appraiser works backward to what a willing buyer would have paid on that day, not on the day they visit. The report usually costs a few hundred dollars and takes a couple of weeks, longer in a busy market.

If an heir plans to buy out siblings, an appraisal is also the neutral number everyone can accept. Our article on [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) covers the options, and [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) explains why the same report protects the seller on the tax side.

A broker's price opinion is cheaper and can serve for a rough estimate in a small estate where no tax return is expected and the family agrees. It is not what you want when heirs disagree or the house is the main asset.

## Which assets do not get a new basis?

Retirement accounts, annuities, savings bonds and similar income-in-respect-of-a-decedent items are valued at death, but the heir pays income tax on withdrawals. They do not get the step-up. Our article on [tax on inherited IRAs, 401(k)s, annuities and savings bonds](/learn/after-a-death/tax-on-inherited-ira-401k-annuity-and-savings-bonds) walks through it. Assets given away during life also do not step up, because the recipient takes the donor's basis.

In community property states, both halves of a married couple's community property generally step up when the first spouse dies. In other states, only the half the deceased spouse owned does. A surviving spouse in a community property state should ask a tax professional how this applies, because it can be worth a great deal on a long-held house.

## What is the alternate valuation date?

For a large estate that must file a federal estate tax return, the executor may choose to value everything six months after death instead, but only if that lowers both the total value and the tax owed. For nearly all estates this never comes up. If yours is near the $15 million level, our guide on [how assets are valued for estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/how-assets-are-valued-for-estate-tax) goes through it, and an accountant should run the numbers.

## What steps should the executor follow?

1. **Get the death certificate** and your letters from the court, which the banks and brokers will want. See [letters testamentary and administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration).
2. **List every asset,** including ones with beneficiaries. Our [how to find accounts and assets guide](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) helps with the hunt.
3. **Request date-of-death statements** from every bank, broker, fund company and retirement custodian.
4. **Order appraisals** for the house, any other land, valuables and any business interest.
5. **Record each value and its source** in a single spreadsheet or binder.
6. **Report probate assets on the inventory** and share the numbers with beneficiaries as your state requires.
7. **Hand the file to the tax preparer,** who will need it for the final return, any estate income tax return and the heirs' basis letters.

## What if heirs disagree with a value?

Appraisals are opinions. If a sibling thinks the house was undervalued, offer to share the report, and if the doubt persists, a second appraisal paid by the objecting heir is a fair way to settle it. A neutral report beats an argument, and an executor who is open about the numbers rarely gets sued over them.

## How state law changes this

Some states require a formal appraiser appointed by the court, or ask for appraisals of real estate within a set number of days after appointment. Others let the executor value things with no outside help for a small estate. A handful of states have their own estate or inheritance tax with lower thresholds than the federal one, which makes accurate numbers more important. Our [state death tax checker](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) is a good place to see whether your state applies a tax, and your state's page, such as the [New Jersey guide](/estate-planning/new-jersey), has the local details.
