Waiting for an estate to settle is hard enough without feeling that the person in charge is stalling, hiding things or taking more than their share. If you are a beneficiary and are worried about how an executor or trustee is behaving, you have more options than you may think, and many of them do not involve a lawsuit.

This article explains your rights, what counts as grounds for removal, and the steps to take. It is part of our guide on [what families face after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## What rights do beneficiaries have?

Beneficiaries generally have the right to be informed and the right to have the estate or trust managed honestly and carefully. The executor or trustee is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), which means the law holds them to a high standard.

In most states you can expect:

- **Notice** that the estate or trust exists, and often notice of the court filing.
- **A copy of the will,** which becomes a public record once filed, or the parts of a trust that affect you.
- **Information about the assets,** such as an inventory and statements.
- **An accounting** of what came in, what went out and what is left, either periodically or at the end.
- **Fair treatment,** meaning the fiduciary cannot favor themselves or one beneficiary unless the document allows it.
- **Reasonable speed.** The work must be done without unnecessary delay.

A fiduciary does not have to discuss every decision or consult you before every payment. They also do not have to give you the money early. Many disputes come from impatience on one side and silence on the other.

## When can a court remove an executor or trustee?

A court can remove a fiduciary when they have breached their duties or cannot perform them, and when removal is in the interest of the estate or trust. Judges tend to prefer keeping the person the deceased chose, so you need evidence.

Typical grounds include:

- **Theft or misuse of funds,** including paying personal expenses from estate money.
- **Self-dealing,** such as buying estate property at a low price or lending estate money to themselves.
- **Neglect,** such as letting a house go uninsured or failing to file tax returns.
- **Failure to account** after being asked or ordered to.
- **Serious conflict of interest** that harms beneficiaries.
- **Incapacity,** illness or dementia that makes serving impossible.
- **Failing to act** on a will or trust for a long period without reason.
- **Refusing to follow the document,** or following only some of the instructions.

The following normally do not justify removal on their own: a difficult personality, taking a normal amount of time, hiring a lawyer or accountant, or making a reasonable investment choice that lost value in a falling market.

## What should you try before going to court?

Court is the last step, not the first. A staged approach is often faster and cheaper, and it builds a record if you do need to file.

1. **Ask for information in writing.** Send a polite email or letter listing what you want: a copy of the will or trust, the inventory, recent statements, and an accounting. Keep a copy.
2. **Allow reasonable time.** Thirty days is a common expectation for a response, but the real answer depends on state law and the complexity of the estate.
3. **Talk, or arrange mediation.** Many disputes are about communication. A neutral mediator can help siblings reach an agreement on timing, distributions or selling property.
4. **Consult an attorney.** An attorney can review the documents, tell you what your state's standard is and whether your evidence is enough.
5. **File a petition** with the probate or trust court if concerns remain.

For example, Hannah is one of three beneficiaries of her father's trust. Her brother, the trustee, has not sent a single update in eight months. Hannah writes asking for a copy of the trust and an accounting. He sends the trust but not the accounting. Her attorney sends a formal demand. The brother produces the records, which show that expenses are normal but that the house has been vacant and uninsured. The siblings agree on a plan to sell it, and the matter ends without a hearing.

## How does the removal process work?

You file a petition in the court that supervises the estate or trust, explain what the fiduciary did wrong and ask for a remedy. The fiduciary is notified and may respond. The court may hold a hearing and listen to evidence.

Courts have several tools short of full removal. A judge may order an accounting, require the fiduciary to post a bond, limit their powers, freeze accounts, appoint a co-fiduciary or order repayment of losses, which is often called a surcharge. If the person is removed, the court names a successor and the removed person hands over the assets and records.

Time limits matter. Many states set a deadline to challenge a trustee's accounting or to bring claims once notice has been given, so do not wait for years. If you believe you are in the dispute-heavy territory, contact an attorney early.

## What if the problem is the will, not the executor?

Removing a fiduciary and contesting a will are different. A removal petition asks the court to replace the person administering, while a will contest asks the court to say the will itself is invalid because of problems such as lack of capacity or [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence). Read [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) if that is your concern.

## What if you are the executor or trustee, and someone is complaining?

Answer promptly and keep records. Most complaints can be settled by sharing information. Give updates every few months, send an accounting, keep estate money separate and ask an attorney about any decision that might look like a conflict. You can also resign if the burden is too great, though a court may need to approve it. Our guides to [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) and [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) describe what good practice looks like.

If you are planning your own estate, you can reduce future conflict by choosing carefully, naming alternates and asking for regular reporting. See [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).

## What does it cost, and is it worth it?

A contested removal case can cost thousands of dollars in legal fees and take months, and those costs may come out of the estate, which means they can reduce every beneficiary's share. Courts sometimes order a fiduciary who acted improperly to pay the other side's fees, but you should not count on that. Before filing, ask your attorney to estimate costs against what is at stake. If the problem is mainly slow communication, a firm letter and mediation usually cost far less and often work. If money is actively disappearing, acting quickly matters more than saving on fees.

A trustee who will not share information can be asked for an accounting first; see [the trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account).
