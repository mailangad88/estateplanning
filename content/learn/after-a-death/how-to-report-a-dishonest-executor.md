Nadia's mother died in June. Her brother Omar was named executor, and for four months Nadia heard almost nothing except that "it's handled." Then a neighbor mentioned that their mother's car had been sold, and the bank said the estate account had never been opened. Nadia wanted to know what she was allowed to do and whom she could tell.

This page gives you the order of operations. You will see which signs matter, which records to ask for, where each kind of report goes in Illinois and New Jersey, and what to leave alone. If you are weighing removal, our article on [when an executor can be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) is the companion piece, and the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) places the whole process in context.

## The short version

- Most executor problems are slowness, not theft. Start by asking in writing for the facts.
- If you see real signs of taking or hiding money, go to the probate court, not the funeral home or the bank.
- Theft and forgery are crimes. You can report them to the police or the county prosecutor.
- A lawyer who is helping the executor cheat can be reported to the state's attorney discipline office.
- Do not accuse anyone in a group text and do not sign a release.

## What are the signs of a bad executor?

An executor is a fiduciary, which means the law holds them to a high standard of loyalty and honesty toward everyone who inherits. Some behavior is a warning, some is simply a sign of an overwhelmed person. This table separates the two.

| What you see | Could be innocent | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| No contact for months | Executor is overwhelmed or grieving | Date letters were issued and a status update |
| No estate bank account | Executor has not started | Name of the bank and the estate's tax ID |
| Property sold with no notice | Quick sale to stop carrying costs | Sale contract, who bought it, the price and how it was set |
| Executor lives in the house rent-free | Caretaking, if the will allows it | Whether it was agreed and for how long |
| Large fees with no records | Fee is allowed by statute or will | An itemized list and the legal basis |
| Executor pays own bills from the estate account | Mixing accounts by mistake | Statements showing each payment |
| Pressure to sign a "receipt" quickly | Wants to close the file | The accounting the receipt refers to |
| Missing jewelry or accounts | Items were given away early | An inventory with a date and valuation |

A single row is a reason to ask. Several rows together, or an executor who refuses to answer, are reasons to see a lawyer. Our page on [executor conduct and what is allowed](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate) explains which payments an executor can legitimately make to themselves.

## What should you gather before you report?

Courts act on documents. A few hours of collection can turn a hunch into a petition a judge takes seriously.

1. **The will and the letters.** Look up the probate case at the county courthouse or its online docket. The file shows who was appointed, when, and what has been filed. Our article on [how to look up a probate case](/learn/probate/how-to-look-up-a-probate-case) explains the steps.
2. **The inventory.** Many courts require one. Compare it to what you know the person owned.
3. **Bank and brokerage records.** The estate account statements, if you can get them, and the date-of-death statements for the person's own accounts.
4. **Real estate records.** The county recorder's office shows deeds, and a deed signed after the death is a prompt to ask who signed it.
5. **Vehicle records.** The state motor vehicle office can show retitling.
6. **A short timeline.** Dates, who said what, and where it is written down. Keep texts and emails.
7. **A note of what you do not know.** The court expects questions, not conclusions.

Do not go into the person's house, take records or change passwords to get this. Let the court, or an attorney with a subpoena, ask for them.

## Where do you report a dishonest executor?

There are four places. They do different jobs and you can use more than one.

### 1. The probate court

This is the main route because the court appointed the executor and supervises the work. A beneficiary or other interested person can file a petition asking for an accounting, an inventory, an order freezing assets, the executor's removal, or repayment of what was taken. The court has the power to remove an executor and to make the executor repay losses from their own money.

In Illinois, the circuit court in the county where the estate is open handles it, and the removal statute (755 ILCS 5/23-2) lists grounds including waste or mismanagement and failure to file an ordered inventory or accounting. Read [what heirs can do about a slow Illinois executor](/learn/illinois/what-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-illinois-executor). In New Jersey, the county Surrogate and the Superior Court's Probate Part handle it, and N.J.S.A. 3B:14-21 lists grounds such as misusing estate property and disobeying a court order. Read [what New Jersey heirs can do about a slow or silent executor](/learn/new-jersey/what-new-jersey-heirs-can-do-about-a-slow-or-silent-executor).

### 2. The police or county prosecutor

If you can point to a theft, a forged signature or a fake document, report it to the local police, the state's attorney in an Illinois county or the county prosecutor in New Jersey. A criminal case can punish the wrongdoing, but the prosecutor works for the public. The money still needs a civil claim in probate court to come back to the estate. Often the best order is the court first, because a petition can stop the loss while a criminal case takes months.

### 3. The lawyer's disciplinary body

If the estate's lawyer is part of the problem, such as signing off on a false accounting, a complaint can go to the Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission in Illinois or the Office of Attorney Ethics in New Jersey. This does not recover money, and it does not replace the court case.

### 4. The financial institution

A bank's fraud department can freeze an account when there is evidence of misuse. Banks move more cautiously when two people dispute control of an estate account, so a court order carries more weight than a phone call.

## What happens after you report?

A realistic path looks like this. Nadia sends Omar a dated letter by email and mail asking for the date letters issued, a list of assets, and a date for a status call. Omar does not reply. She then sees the docket and finds no inventory has been filed.

Her attorney files a petition asking the court to order an inventory and accounting by a set date, with a hearing if Omar refuses. At the hearing the judge orders Omar to produce the records within 30 days. When the accounting shows a $14,000 payment to Omar with no explanation, Nadia's attorney asks the court to surcharge him, which means to make him repay the estate, and to remove him if he does not cooperate. A successor administrator takes over if the judge removes him. The family's costs come first from Nadia, and the court can shift part of them to Omar if his conduct was wrongful.

This takes months, not days. The court acts on notice and a hearing, and a judge generally wants evidence before removing someone the dead person chose.

## What should you not do?

- **Do not sign a receipt and release.** A signed release can end your right to complain about what the executor did. Read [receipt, release and closing the estate](/learn/after-a-death/final-distribution-receipt-and-release-and-closing-the-estate) before you sign anything.
- **Do not take items yourself.** Even something you believe was promised to you becomes a problem if it was not given through the estate.
- **Do not post accusations online.** A false statement about a named person can bring its own claim.
- **Do not wait too long.** Time limits can apply to claims against a fiduciary, and evidence gets harder to get. Talk to an attorney in the first weeks, not the first year.

## How does state law change this?

Both states let an interested person ask the court to supervise an executor, but the paths differ. Illinois estates are often independently administered, which lets the executor act without court approval at each step, and a petition can end that status. New Jersey estates often close with informal releases instead of a court accounting, and the usual request for information goes to the Surrogate or to the executor directly before any action. Time limits and who pays the cost of an accounting vary, so check the current rules with a local attorney. See the guides for [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey).

If the problem is a trustee rather than an executor, the pattern is similar but the court and rules differ. See [how to remove a trustee](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-remove-a-trustee).
