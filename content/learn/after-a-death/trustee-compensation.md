Maria's mother named her as successor trustee of the family trust. Eight months in, Maria has spent most weekends on paperwork, calls to the bank and trips to the house, and her brother Daniel asks whether she plans to "take a cut." If you are in a similar spot, either as the trustee or the person watching one, the question is fair and it has a straightforward framework.

This article explains who sets trustee pay, how fees are usually calculated, what the tax effect is, and what beneficiaries can do if the numbers look wrong. For the full job description, see our article on [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).

## The short version

- A trustee is generally allowed to be paid, but the trust document and state law decide how much.
- If the document says nothing, the usual standard is "reasonable compensation."
- Pay and expense reimbursement are different things, and both should be recorded.
- A family trustee can waive pay in writing, and many do.
- Beneficiaries have a right to see what the trustee has charged and can challenge unreasonable fees.

## When the answer is yes: where the right to pay comes from

A trustee's pay comes from one of three places, in this order of priority.

1. **The trust document.** The person who made the trust can set a flat amount, a percentage, an hourly rate, a cap, or no pay at all. A clear clause is usually followed.
2. **A written agreement.** Beneficiaries and the trustee sometimes agree on a fee after the trust becomes irrevocable. Get it in writing and signed.
3. **State law.** If the document is silent, most states allow reasonable compensation. Many follow the Uniform Trust Code approach, which uses that standard. Some states instead provide a formula or schedule by statute, and courts in those states may still adjust it for unusual work.

Reasonableness is not a precise measure. Courts typically weigh the time and skill involved, the size and makeup of the trust, the risk and responsibility the trustee took on, the results achieved, and what comparable trustees charge in the area.

## How are trustee fees usually calculated?

Different trusts call for different methods. Here are the main ones.

| Method | How it works | Where it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Percentage of assets | A yearly fee based on the trust's value, often billed quarterly | Large, long-running trusts with investments |
| Hourly | The trustee tracks time and bills at a set rate | Short administrations with uneven workloads |
| Flat fee | A set amount for the whole administration or for stages | Simple trusts, such as splitting a house and one account |
| Statutory schedule | A formula in state law, sometimes with a court adjustment | States that regulate fees by statute |
| Hybrid | Percentage for investments plus hourly for extra work, such as selling real estate | Trusts with mixed assets |

We are not giving percentages here on purpose. Rates differ by institution and region, and a number that sounds typical in one place may be unreasonable in another. Ask for a written fee schedule from any corporate trustee before you sign.

### Corporate trustees versus family trustees

A bank or trust company usually works from a published fee schedule, may have a minimum annual fee, and often charges extra for special services such as managing real estate or filing returns. Its fees are generally accepted as reasonable if they match the schedule disclosed to the beneficiaries.

A family trustee has no schedule, so the standard is the work actually done. A family trustee who keeps a simple time log and records what each task involved is in a much stronger position than one who sets a number at the end. Our article on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) compares the two options for people still planning.

## When the answer is no, or "not much"

A trustee should not be paid, or should be paid little, in these situations:

- **The document waives compensation.** Some trusts say the trustee serves without pay. Taking a fee anyway is a breach.
- **The trustee has agreed to serve free.** An agreement not to charge usually holds.
- **The work was not trustee work.** Time spent on the trustee's own affairs, or on tasks that were never needed, does not count.
- **The trustee failed in the job.** Courts can reduce or deny fees when the trustee delayed, made costly errors or ignored duties.

Waiving fees is common for a parent's trust when the trustee is also a beneficiary. Elena, the sole trustee of her father's trust and one of three children, waived pay because she did not want a fee to turn into friction with her sisters. She did keep the receipts for every cost she advanced, which brings up the next distinction.

## Is reimbursement the same as compensation?

No. Compensation is payment for the trustee's time and responsibility. Reimbursement repays money the trustee spent from their own pocket for the trust, such as a locksmith, a property tax bill, a filing fee or an appraisal. Reimbursement needs receipts, should be recorded on the accounting, and is generally not income to the trustee because it simply returns what they advanced. Better still, pay trust expenses directly from the trust account so reimbursement is rarely needed.

Professional help is another category. Fees for the trust's attorney, accountant or investment adviser are usually trust expenses, not trustee pay. A trustee who does paid work that a professional would normally do, such as legal work, should be careful about being paid twice for the same task.

## Are trustee fees taxable?

Yes. Trustee compensation is generally taxable income to the trustee and should be reported on the trustee's own return. It may also be subject to self-employment tax in some situations, depending on how much the person acts as a trustee and in what capacity. A beneficiary's inheritance, by contrast, is generally not income tax for the beneficiary. That gap is why a family member who is also a beneficiary sometimes waives fees and simply takes a larger share.

The trust may be able to deduct the fees on its own return. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) for how estate and trust returns work, and ask a tax preparer about the specifics.

## What can beneficiaries do if fees look excessive?

Take it in steps, starting with the least costly.

1. **Read the trust.** Confirm what it says about compensation before assuming a problem.
2. **Ask for the numbers in writing.** Request the fee amount, how it was calculated, and the dates and hours behind it. The trustee's duty to report is covered in our article on the [trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account).
3. **Compare to the method.** A percentage fee should match the stated schedule; an hourly fee should match a log.
4. **Raise the concern directly.** A short, polite letter often resolves a misunderstanding.
5. **Try mediation.** A neutral mediator can help when the issue is the amount, not honesty.
6. **Ask the court.** A beneficiary can petition for review of the fees, and a court can reduce them, order a refund, or in serious cases remove the trustee. See [what beneficiaries can do when a trustee does nothing](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-trustee-does-nothing) for the broader escalation path.

Many states set a limited time to object after receiving a report that discloses the fees, so do not wait for months.

## How state law changes this

States differ in whether they use the reasonable-compensation standard or a schedule, whether court approval is needed, whether a corporate trustee's disclosed schedule is presumed reasonable, and how long beneficiaries have to object. Check the guide for your state, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), and have an attorney confirm the current local rule.
