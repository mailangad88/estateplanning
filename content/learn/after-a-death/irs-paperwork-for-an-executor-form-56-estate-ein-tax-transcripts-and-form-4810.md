Most advice about taxes after a death starts with the final return. Before that return can be prepared, though, an executor has a quieter set of tasks with the IRS. You have to tell the agency you exist, get a tax number for the estate, find out what the person actually filed, and decide whether to ask for a faster close-out.

These steps are small forms with big consequences, and they are the part families most often skip or do in the wrong order. This page explains each one in plain terms. The returns themselves are covered in our guide to [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns), and the whole job is outlined in [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties). If you are earlier in the process, start with the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## In what order does an executor deal with the IRS?

The order matters because each step needs the one before it.

1. **Get your court authority.** The IRS wants proof that you are the fiduciary, usually a copy of the court's letters. See [letters testamentary and letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) if you do not have them yet.
2. **File Form 56.** It puts the IRS on notice and routes mail to you.
3. **Apply for the estate EIN.** You need it for the estate bank account and for any estate return.
4. **Request transcripts.** You learn what the person filed and what the IRS has on record.
5. **File the missing or final returns.** Use the transcripts to catch gaps.
6. **Consider Form 4810 and Form 5495.** These shorten the period of exposure after the returns are in.
7. **Distribute, only after tax questions are settled.**

If you have not been appointed yet, steps two to four can often wait a few weeks without harm. What cannot wait is a notice with a response date. Open IRS mail the day it arrives.

## What does Form 56 do?

Form 56, the Notice Concerning Fiduciary Relationship, tells the IRS that you are acting for the person who died. Without it, the IRS keeps sending letters to the last known address, and the person who needs to read them is somewhere else.

You fill in the person's name and Social Security number, the date of death, your name and address, and the type of authority you hold. You attach the court letters or the relevant pages of the will if the estate is not going through court. The form also gets filed again to end the relationship, once you are discharged or a trustee passes the job to a successor.

Janelle was named executor for her uncle and lived out of state. His last return was in the mail to the uncle's apartment, which the landlord had already cleared out. A letter about an unfiled year sat in a mail room for two months. When she filed Form 56, the next letter came to her and she had time to answer it.

Form 56 is a notice, not an approval. Filing it does not give you powers you do not already have.

## How does the estate get its own EIN?

The estate is its own taxpayer, and an EIN is the number it uses. You apply on the IRS website for free, or on Form SS-4. The online application takes a few minutes, and you receive the number at the end. Be careful with look-alike websites that charge a fee.

You need the number when:

- The estate opens a bank account, which it should, since mixing estate money with your own is a common executor error.
- The estate earns more than a small amount of income, such as interest, rent or dividends, and a Form 1041 is due.
- An asset such as a brokerage account is retitled into the estate.

The estate chooses a tax year at the beginning. A calendar year is simplest, but a fiscal year can postpone tax on income and is worth a conversation with a tax professional. Living trusts that become irrevocable at death need their own EIN too, a different process explained in our page on the [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election).

## How do you get the dead person's tax transcripts?

A tax transcript is the IRS's own summary of what is on record for a person. It is not a copy of the return, and for fixing problems it is often more useful. Request it with Form 4506-T, attach a copy of the death certificate and your court letters, and mail it as the form instructs. A surviving spouse who filed jointly can usually get the same records.

There are three kinds worth asking for:

| Transcript | What it shows | Why an executor wants it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Return transcript | Most line items from the filed Form 1040 | Confirms what was reported and the filing status |
| Account transcript | Payments, penalties, balance due, refunds | Shows whether tax is owed or a refund is waiting |
| Wage and income transcript | Forms W-2, 1099 and similar sent to the IRS | Finds income and accounts nobody told you about |

The wage and income transcript is the sleeper. If the person had a brokerage account, an old annuity or a small pension, the form that reported it appears there. Pair it with your other searches in [how to find a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets).

Ask for the last three to six years. If the person had not filed in some years, the transcript will say so, and you can address the gap on your own timetable instead of the IRS's.

## What is Form 4810, and should an executor file it?

Form 4810, Request for Prompt Assessment, asks the IRS to look at the returns now. The normal period in which the IRS can assess more tax is three years from filing in most cases. Once the executor files this request, the period for the decedent's returns and the estate's return is shortened to 18 months from the date the IRS receives it.

It does not make the IRS audit anyone, and it does not change what is owed. It narrows the window in which a surprise can arrive. That helps an executor who wants to close the estate on a firm date.

You file it only after the returns are in, and the request is made in writing to the IRS office where the returns were filed. It is not for estates still gathering assets, and it is not a fix for a return you know is wrong. Most estates with simple finances do not need it. Larger or messier ones, such as an estate with a business, rental property or several years of late returns, often do.

The related Form 5495, Request for Discharge From Personal Liability, goes a step further. When approved, it relieves the executor from personal liability for any tax later found due on those returns. Your attorney or tax professional can say which of the two suits your situation.

## Why do executors worry about personal liability?

Because the IRS is paid ahead of heirs. Under federal law, an executor who pays other creditors or distributes assets while knowing about an unpaid federal tax can be personally responsible for the amount. This is the reason experienced advisers say not to hand out the last of the money until the returns are in and the IRS has said what it thinks.

Nothing about this is meant to scare you. It is the reason for the sequence above. If you are doing the job unpaid and are uneasy about the exposure, our page on [hiring help and staying protected as an executor](/learn/after-a-death/executor-hiring-help-and-staying-protected) shows what a fiduciary can reasonably delegate, and the [Form 706 page](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) explains the separate return for estates large enough to owe federal estate tax.
