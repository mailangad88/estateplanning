For months, the executor has been collecting, paying and waiting. Then the house sells, the last bill is paid, and a beneficiary calls to ask a reasonable question: when do I get my share?

The answer is "after a few more steps," and those steps protect both the executor and the people waiting. This page walks through the end of an estate: what has to be true before the last checks go out, in what order gifts are paid, what a receipt and release really does, and how the estate is closed. For the full list of an executor's tasks from the start, see our guide to [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) and the wider [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## When can an executor make the final distribution?

Only after four things are settled. If any one is open, wait.

1. **The creditor claim period has ended.** Probate sets a window for creditors to file claims, and its length is set by state law. Until it closes, a surprise bill can still arrive. Our page on the [probate creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) explains how it works.
2. **Debts, funeral costs and expenses are paid.** That includes court fees, attorney and appraiser charges, and the executor's own approved fee.
3. **Tax returns are filed and the taxes paid.** The final personal income tax return, the estate's own return and, for large estates, the federal estate tax return. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns). An executor who distributes everything and then finds a tax bill may be personally responsible for it.
4. **Any dispute is resolved.** A will contest, a challenge to a creditor claim or a disagreement about a sale means money should stay put.

Executors often keep a reserve for the last expenses, for example the final tax preparation fee, the cost of closing out accounts, and a cushion for something unexpected. How large it should be depends on the estate. A small, simple one needs little. An estate with a business, a rental property or a pending estate tax audit needs more.

## In what order are gifts paid?

The will's structure sets the order, and most wills follow the same pattern:

| Type of gift | Example | Paid |
|---|---|---|
| Specific gift | "My piano to Carlos" | First |
| General gift | "$10,000 to my niece Elena" | Second |
| Residuary gift | "Everything else to my three children equally" | Last |

Our explanation of [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) shows how each is written. The reason order matters is shortage. If debts and expenses use up more than expected, the residuary gift shrinks first, then general gifts, and specific gifts last. Many states call this abatement. A will can change the order, and some states have special rules for the family home.

If a person died without a will, state law sets the shares, usually to a spouse and children in a stated proportion.

### What about gifts of property, not cash?

Executors often distribute "in kind." The house, a car, shares of stock or a collection of paintings can go to a beneficiary without being sold. The executor signs a deed or transfer form, and the beneficiary takes the asset at its value on the date of death for tax purposes. That value comes from the inventory. See [how an executor values the estate](/learn/after-a-death/how-an-executor-values-the-estate).

If the will gives the residue to several people and there is a single house, the executor and the beneficiaries may agree on who takes it and who is paid the difference. Put that agreement in writing before the deed is signed, and have everyone sign it.

## What is a receipt and release?

It is a one-page statement a beneficiary signs after receiving their share. It says what they received, that it matches what the will gave them, and that they release the executor from further claims about that share. It is the single best protection an executor has once the money leaves the account.

A useful form has these parts:

- The name and date of death of the person who died.
- A list of what was paid or transferred, with amounts or descriptions.
- A statement that the beneficiary has received an accounting or had the chance to ask for one.
- A release of the executor from claims related to that distribution.
- A signature and date, with a notary if your state expects one.

A release is only as strong as the information behind it. A beneficiary who was given a clear accounting and signed freely is bound. A beneficiary who was given a number with no explanation can later argue they did not understand. So send the numbers first, answer questions, and only then ask for a signature.

## What is a release and refunding agreement?

It handles a different problem: paying part of the estate early. Suppose the estate has $400,000 in cash and a claim might still be coming. Rather than make everyone wait, the executor pays each beneficiary part of what they will receive. Each signs an agreement that they will return their share of any unexpected cost.

Here is a simple example. Leah's mother left her estate equally to Leah and her brother Tomas. The house was sold, and about $300,000 sat in the estate account. The creditor deadline would pass in two months. The executor paid each of them $100,000 now, kept $100,000 in reserve, and had both sign a refunding agreement. When the deadline passed with no new claims, the reserve was split in half.

Use these agreements sparingly. They require trust, and collecting money back from a beneficiary who has spent it is hard. A reserve held by the executor is safer than a promise.

## What does the executor have to give beneficiaries?

More than many executors expect. The details vary by state, but the following is common:

- **Notice** that the estate is open, and a copy of the will.
- **The inventory** of estate assets, on request, and in some states automatically.
- **An accounting** of money received and paid, including executor and attorney fees. Some courts require it at the end. Others allow beneficiaries to waive it in writing.
- **Information about timing**, such as when the claim period ends and when the first payment is expected.
- **A Schedule K-1**, if the estate earned income and passed it out.

Beneficiaries who feel kept in the dark are the ones who file objections. A short email every month or two, with no news if there is none, heads off most of that. The same ideas apply to a successor trustee, covered in our guide on a [trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account).

## How is the estate actually closed?

Formal closing depends on the state and on whether the court supervised the case.

1. **Account for everything.** The executor lists receipts, payments and what remains.
2. **Make the distributions** and retitle assets.
3. **Collect receipts and releases**, or file the account with the court and give notice to all beneficiaries.
4. **File a petition or closing statement.** Some states let the executor sign a sworn statement that all duties are complete. Others require a court order approving the account.
5. **Receive the discharge.** The court releases the executor from further duties, or the time to object runs out.
6. **Close the estate bank account** and the estate's tax ID account, and keep the records for several years.

A cautious executor also waits for the IRS to process the estate's final return, and if the estate asked for a closing letter on an estate tax return, waits for it. Our page on the [federal estate tax return](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) explains why that can take longer than the rest of the estate.

## How long does all of this take?

Often a year or more. A simple, small estate with cash only might close in six months. An estate with real estate, an out-of-state property or a tax return can take well beyond a year, and a contested one can take several. Our guide on [how long it takes to receive an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-long-does-it-take-to-receive-an-inheritance) gives realistic ranges. Most states expect an executor to move steadily, and a beneficiary can ask the court to require progress if nothing happens.

If an executor will not account or distribute, the options are in our article on [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).
