When a trustee dies, two very different things may have happened, and the first steps depend on which one it was. Either the person who created the trust and managed it has died, or someone who was serving as trustee partway through an administration has died. Families often mix these up, so this page covers both.

This page sits inside our wider guide to [what families do after a death](/learn/after-a-death). The short version is that the trust keeps going. Someone has to be found to run it, and the paperwork has to prove that person has authority before a bank or title company will deal with them.

## The short version

- Find the trust document and every amendment. Do not rely on a copy someone remembers.
- Order certified death certificates. Banks, brokerages and the county recorder each keep one.
- Identify the successor trustee, and have that person accept in writing before acting.
- Lock, insure and inspect the house. Gather valuables and mail.
- Hold off on selling, distributing or paying anyone, including the new trustee, until there is a plan.
- Talk to an attorney early if the trust holds real estate, a business or more than one beneficiary.

## When the person who made the trust has died

This is the common case. Most revocable trusts name the creator as trustee while healthy. When that person dies, the trust becomes irrevocable and the successor named in the document takes charge. Our article on [what a successor trustee must do](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) covers the whole administration. These are the opening moves.

Marcus's mother, Eleanor, created a living trust and served as its trustee until she died in March. The trust names Marcus first and his sister Dana second. Marcus does not need a judge. He needs the document, a death certificate and a signed acceptance, which many trusts include as a form near the back.

Check the exact wording of the successor clause. Some trusts name co-trustees who must act together. Others say the first named successor serves only if willing and able, which means a person who is overseas, ill or simply unwilling can pass the job to the next name. A decline should be written down and dated.

## When a successor trustee dies in the middle of the job

If a trustee who was already administering the trust dies, the next person named in the document steps in, and the trust's assets stay put. Three practical points matter here.

First, the dead trustee's own estate is responsible for turning over trust records and any trust money that ended up in the wrong place. Trustees sometimes kept trust funds in an account that was also used for personal bills. A family executor will need to separate them.

Second, the new trustee should ask for an accounting of what the earlier trustee did. A short statement of receipts, payments and what remains protects the new trustee from being blamed for old mistakes. Beneficiaries are entitled to the same information. Our page on [notices and accountings](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) explains what it should contain.

Third, a filing deadline does not stop because a trustee died. If a fiduciary income tax return or a state filing is coming up, the new trustee inherits the task.

## What if nobody named can serve?

When no named trustee is willing or able, the trust document's replacement method controls. Many trusts let a majority of adult beneficiaries pick someone, or let the last serving trustee name a successor in advance. If the document has no method, most states give the court power to appoint, and judges usually go with a person the beneficiaries propose who is not in obvious conflict.

A corporate trustee, such as a bank trust department, is a common fallback. It charges a fee, often a percentage of assets, and many have minimum sizes. A family friend or a professional fiduciary who does this for a living is another route. If you are choosing between them, our guide to [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) lists the questions to ask.

## Steps for the new trustee

1. **Get the documents.** The original trust, amendments, any pour-over will, a list of assets and the most recent statements. If the original is missing, ask the attorney who drafted it.
2. **Order certified death certificates.** Start with ten. Banks typically want an original or certified copy.
3. **Accept the job in writing.** Date and sign the acceptance. Make a certification of trust, a short summary that shows the trust exists and who has authority without revealing its private terms.
4. **Protect the property.** Change the locks if keys are scattered, confirm homeowner's insurance covers a vacant house (many policies limit coverage after a set period of vacancy), and stop automatic deliveries.
5. **Get a tax ID and open an account.** The trust is now its own taxpayer. See [how to get a trust EIN](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election).
6. **List assets and date-of-death values.** Real estate will need an appraisal. Values on the date of death matter for the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).
7. **Send the notices.** Beneficiaries first, and heirs where your state requires it.
8. **Pause on distributions.** Debts, final taxes and expenses come first.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how quickly notice must go out, whether beneficiaries can appoint a replacement without a judge, and whether the trustee must file anything with a court. Some states have a trust code based on the Uniform Trust Code, which gives clear rules. Others rely on older case law and local custom. If the trust or the dead trustee lived in another state from the property, [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) may enter the picture, since real estate is governed by the law of the state where it sits. Our [Illinois guide](/estate-planning/illinois) shows how one state lays out these rules.

Anything the trust failed to hold may still need probate, and a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) is what moves those leftovers into the trust.

## A worked example of a mid-job death

Ravi was the successor trustee for his late father's trust, which held a rental duplex and two brokerage accounts for three siblings. Eight months in, Ravi died suddenly. His sister Priya was the next name in the trust. She found that rent checks were still going to an account in Ravi's name alone, that the duplex insurance renewal had lapsed two weeks earlier, and that no accounting existed.

Priya's order of work was reasonable. She accepted the trusteeship in writing and sent Ravi's widow a polite request for the records. She called the insurance agent the same day and reinstated coverage. She asked the bank to retitle the rent account to the trust under its tax ID. Only then did she write to her siblings with a short summary and a promise of a full report within sixty days. Nothing in that sequence needed a lawsuit, but it did need a lawyer's letter template and a patient tone with a grieving sister-in-law.

## Mistakes to avoid in the first month

Do not let a relative "just handle" the accounts because the bank has not noticed the death. Using the dead trustee's debit card or online login is a quick way to turn a family into a defendant. Do not give a beneficiary an advance on their share while debts are unknown. Keep funeral and personal expenses of the trustee separate from trust money, because the trust pays only its own bills. And do not sign a listing agreement for the house before the trustee's authority is confirmed in writing, since a buyer's title company will ask for it.
