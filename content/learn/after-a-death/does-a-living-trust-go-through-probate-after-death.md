Rosa, 52, became successor trustee of her mother's living trust in Elizabeth, New Jersey. The trust held the house and two bank accounts, and Rosa expected to close it all without a court. Three weeks in, she opened an old envelope and found a brokerage statement for $27,000 in her mother's name alone. The trust did not own it. Rosa asked the question this page answers: does she now have to open probate?

The short answer is that the trust avoids probate for what is inside it, and the outside piece needs its own treatment. This page explains how to sort the two, which assets usually need a court, what a pour-over will does, and what the trustee can do in the meantime. For the full trustee roadmap, read our [trust administration guide](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration), and for the bigger picture see the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## When does a living trust avoid probate?

A trust avoids probate for property whose title is in the name of the trustee. Probate is the court process for passing on property that the dead person owned in their own name. Property already owned by the trust is not owned by the person in that sense, so the court has nothing to supervise. The successor trustee named in the document steps in, shows the death certificate and the trust pages to the bank or the county recorder, and follows the instructions.

Three details matter.

- **Funding decides everything.** A trust holds only what was retitled or made payable to it. Our article on [what happens if a trust was never funded](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) shows the worst case.
- **The trust becomes irrevocable at death.** The trustee now has a fixed job and a duty to inform beneficiaries. See [when a living trust becomes irrevocable](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-living-trust-becomes-irrevocable).
- **No probate does not mean no deadlines.** Creditors, taxes and notice duties still run. The difference is that they run without a judge watching.

## Which assets still need probate?

The test is simple: does the asset have a title, a joint owner or a beneficiary that sends it somewhere without a court? If not, it is a probate asset, even if the person had a trust.

| Asset | In the trust? | Probate needed? |
|---|---|---|
| House deeded to the trustee | Yes | No |
| House still in the person's name | No | Usually yes, or a small-estate route if allowed |
| Bank account titled to the trust | Yes | No |
| Bank account with a payable-on-death beneficiary | No, but it passes by form | No |
| Brokerage account in the person's own name | No | Yes, unless a small-estate route applies |
| 401(k) or IRA with a named beneficiary | No, passes by form | No |
| Life insurance with a named beneficiary | No, passes by form | No |
| Car titled to the person | No | Often handled with a state transfer process |
| Final paycheck, tax refund, lawsuit claim | No | Often a probate asset |

The sorting is the heart of the job. Our [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) article has the longer list, and the [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) walks you through it.

## What does the pour-over will do?

Most people with a living trust also sign a pour-over will. The will names an executor and says that anything the person owned at death, and had not put into the trust, goes into the trust after probate. That way the trust's terms control everything in the end.

Two points trip families up. First, the pour-over will is a court document. It must be filed and admitted, and the executor must be appointed, before the stray asset can move to the trust. Second, the will does not skip probate; it simply tells the court where the assets go afterward. Read [what a pour-over will is](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) if you have not seen one.

An executor can be the same person as the successor trustee, and often is. That does not merge the jobs. The executor deals with the probate estate under the court's rules, and the trustee deals with the trust under the trust document.

## A worked example in two states

Return to Rosa. The $27,000 brokerage account is in her mother's name alone. She is a New Jersey resident, and the sole beneficiaries are her and her brother, who are next of kin.

- **New Jersey.** The Surrogate's small estate affidavit route allows $20,000 for next of kin and $50,000 for a surviving spouse or partner. The $27,000 is above the next-of-kin limit, and there is no spouse, so Rosa likely needs a formal or informal probate case for the account. The pour-over will would then send the proceeds into the trust. See [New Jersey's small estate affidavit](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-small-estate-affidavit) for the rules and forms.
- **Illinois.** The same facts in Illinois would likely fit the small estate affidavit, which as of 2026 covers up to $150,000 of personal property and does not reach real estate. Rosa could collect the account without a case, deposit the money in the trust account and distribute as the trust says. See [Illinois's small estate affidavit](/learn/illinois/illinois-small-estate-affidavit).

Same trust, same account, different path. The difference is state law, which is why a trustee should check the local limits before choosing.

## What can the trustee do while probate is pending?

A lot. The trust does not wait for the court.

1. **Take control of the trust assets.** Get a tax ID for the now-irrevocable trust and open a trust account. See [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election).
2. **Send the beneficiary notices** the law and the trust require.
3. **Inventory both sides.** List what is in the trust and what is outside it, with date-of-death values.
4. **Pay the bills the trust can pay,** keeping receipts.
5. **Start the outside asset's route** the same week: filing the will, a small estate affidavit, or a probate petition.
6. **Hold a reserve.** Keep enough in the trust to cover expenses, taxes and claims until the outside asset arrives.
7. **Distribute the rest in stages** if the trust permits, with a written note to beneficiaries about what is still pending.

Our article on [how long a trustee has to distribute assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-long-does-a-trustee-have-to-distribute-assets) covers timing in more detail.

## Is a trust still private if some assets go to probate?

Partly. The trust itself stays private. Our article on [whether trust administration is public](/learn/after-a-death/is-trust-administration-public) explains that a recorded deed or a probate filing can bring details into public view. The pour-over will is filed with the court and becomes a public record, and the inventory of the probate estate may be too. The trust terms generally do not need to be filed, but the will names the trust and its date.

## How does state law change this?

States differ in four places that matter here: the small estate limits, whether an executor can use informal probate, what a trustee must tell beneficiaries, and the taxes on death.

In Illinois, a living trust avoids probate on what it owns. Read about [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) and the state guide for [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois). In New Jersey, a trust keeps assets out of probate, but the state's inheritance tax can still apply to what passes to certain beneficiaries, with a return due eight months after death. See [New Jersey living trusts](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) and the state guide for [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey).

If you are still planning, see [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) for the other tools that keep assets out of court.
