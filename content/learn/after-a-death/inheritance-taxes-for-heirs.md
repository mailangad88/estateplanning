If you have just inherited money or property, you may be worried a large tax bill is about to arrive. For most people, it will not. But a few specific situations can create a tax, and learning which one applies to you early helps you avoid unpleasant surprises.

This article explains the four kinds of tax that touch an inheritance: estate tax, inheritance tax, income tax and capital gains tax. It belongs to our guide on [what families face after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## Is an inheritance taxable income?

For federal purposes, no. Receiving cash, a home, stocks or personal belongings from an estate is not income and does not go on your Form 1040. You also do not need to pay anything just to receive it.

There are important exceptions, because some assets carry income tax that the person who died never paid:

- **Traditional IRAs, 401(k)s and similar plans.** Withdrawals are ordinary income to you.
- **Annuities.** The growth portion is taxable when paid out.
- **Savings bonds** with unpaid interest.
- **Income earned after death,** such as interest, dividends or rent from an inherited asset.

Money from a Roth IRA is generally tax-free if the account met the five-year rule, though non-spouse heirs still have to empty it on a schedule.

## Who pays federal estate tax?

The estate pays it, not the heir, and almost no one owes it. In 2026 the federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, so an estate must exceed that amount before any federal estate tax applies. The top rate is 40 percent on the amount above the exclusion. Spouses can pass everything to each other without tax through the [unlimited marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction), and a surviving spouse can use the unused part of the first spouse's exclusion through [portability](/glossary/portability), which allows a married couple to shelter up to $30 million.

If the estate owes tax, the executor pays it from estate assets before heirs are paid, so you receive the net amount. Read more in our guide to the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

## What is a state inheritance tax?

An [inheritance tax](/glossary/inheritance-tax) is charged to the person who receives property, and only a few states have one. The tax rate usually depends on your relationship to the person who died. Spouses are generally exempt. Children and grandchildren often pay little or nothing. Siblings, nieces and nephews often pay a lower to moderate rate, and friends or distant relatives usually pay the highest rate.

Some states also have a separate state estate tax, with an exemption far lower than the federal one. A state can apply its tax based on where the person lived, and some apply it to real estate in that state even when the owner lived elsewhere. Our guide to [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) describes how they differ. State rules change, so ask an attorney or the state's revenue department for current thresholds and forms.

Many of these states give heirs a discount if the tax is paid early. Ask the executor who is responsible for filing and paying, because it may come out of your share.

## How are inherited retirement accounts taxed?

Retirement accounts are the place where heirs most often owe tax. You pay income tax on each withdrawal from a traditional account, and the timing is set by federal rules.

Under the SECURE Act, most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA or 401(k) within 10 years of the owner's death. Certain eligible designated beneficiaries have other options: a surviving spouse, a minor child of the owner until they reach majority, a disabled or chronically ill person, and someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner. Depending on whether the owner had already begun required distributions, annual withdrawals may also be required during the 10 years.

For an example, Priya inherits her father's $400,000 traditional IRA. She does not have to take it all at once. She can spread withdrawals over up to ten years to avoid pushing herself into a higher bracket in one year. A spouse who inherits can often roll the account into her own IRA. See [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) before you move or withdraw anything, because some mistakes cannot be undone.

## What about capital gains on inherited property?

Inherited property generally receives a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) to its fair market value on the date of death. This means that if your mother bought a house for $80,000 and it was worth $400,000 when she died, your starting basis is $400,000. If you sell for $410,000, you owe tax only on the $10,000 gain.

Assets sold at a loss may produce a deductible loss, subject to normal limits. Inherited property is treated as long-term for holding-period purposes regardless of how soon you sell. Assets in an irrevocable trust set up long ago may not get a step-up, which is a reason to ask the trustee for the basis information. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) for detail.

Keep the appraisal or other date-of-death valuation. You will need it when you sell. Our article on [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) covers property-specific issues.

## What are your next steps?

1. Ask the executor or trustee whether the estate owes federal or state estate tax, and who pays it.
2. Check whether your state charges inheritance tax and which rate applies to your relationship.
3. If you inherited a retirement account, pause before withdrawing and confirm your options and deadlines.
4. Get date-of-death valuations for any property you may sell.
5. Consider a short consultation with a tax professional or attorney if the estate is large or complicated.

You may also be curious about the estate's own filings. Those are described in [final tax returns for someone who died](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## What if the estate owes money to creditors first?

Debts and taxes come out of the estate before heirs are paid, so the amount you receive is the net figure. An estate with a large mortgage or credit card balance may leave less than expected. Because you generally do not owe the person's debts yourself, a shortfall usually reduces what heirs receive rather than creating a bill for you. Our overview of [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains the order of payment.

You can also decline an inheritance. A formal [disclaimer](/glossary/disclaimer), made in writing within a short window after the death and before you accept any benefit from the asset, treats you as though you died first, and the property passes to the next person in line. This can make sense when a relative needs it more or when taxes are a concern, but the rules are strict, so speak with an attorney before you accept or sign anything.

## Do gifts received before death work differently?

Yes. A gift made during life does not receive a step-up in basis, so the person who received it generally keeps the giver's original basis. This is one reason families sometimes choose to leave an appreciated asset through the estate rather than give it away early. The gift tax annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient in 2026.
