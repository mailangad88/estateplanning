While Helen was alive, her revocable trust was easy to change. She added a granddaughter, took out a rental property and once moved the whole thing to a different bank. After Helen's death last month, her son Marcus has the trust binder and a sinking feeling. Does anything about the trust change now? Does he have to call everyone? Can he fix the wording that always bothered Helen?

This article walks through what happens at the moment a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) hardens into an irrevocable one, who gets notice, and what the trustee does next. It belongs to our broader guide on [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## What does "becomes irrevocable" mean?

It means the trust is now fixed. During life, the person who created it (the [grantor](/glossary/grantor)) could amend it, revoke it or take property out at will. Death ends that power. Nobody holds it anymore, so the terms in place on the date of death are the terms everyone follows.

Three practical things follow.

- **The successor trustee takes over.** The person named to step in becomes the active [trustee](/glossary/trustee) with a legal duty to the beneficiaries. See [successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) if that term is new to you.
- **The trust becomes its own taxpayer.** It needs a new tax ID, which our guide to the [trust EIN and 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election) explains.
- **The trustee's job shifts from managing someone's money to closing out their affairs.** Collecting assets, paying bills and taxes, and distributing property replace the old work of investing and spending for the grantor.

Do not confuse this with a trust that was irrevocable from the start, such as the kind used for [Medicaid planning or life insurance](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts). Those never had the flexibility. A revocable trust gains its fixed status at death.

## What happens, in order, in the first weeks?

Every trust differs, but the early sequence is usually the same.

1. **Locate the documents.** Find the signed trust, every amendment, and any related will. Check for a certification of trust, a short summary that banks accept in place of the whole document.
2. **Confirm who the trustee is.** Check the named successor and whether they are willing and able. If the first choice declines, the document names the next.
3. **Order death certificates.** Banks, brokerages and the county recorder will each want a certified copy.
4. **Get a trust tax ID** and open a trust account.
5. **Send the required notices** to beneficiaries and heirs.
6. **Secure and inventory the assets**, and get date-of-death values.
7. **Pay debts, expenses and taxes**, then distribute as the trust directs.

Our [trust administration guide](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) covers steps 6 and 7 in depth. The rest of this page focuses on the notice step, because it is the one people skip and the one that can matter most later.

## Who must be told?

The rule is set by state law, but the pattern is similar across the Uniform Trust Code states. The trustee generally must tell the following people once the trust becomes irrevocable.

| Person | Why they get notice |
|---|---|
| Current beneficiaries | They are entitled to payments or property now. |
| Remainder beneficiaries | They will inherit later, for example after a spouse dies. |
| Heirs of the grantor | Some states notify those who would inherit if there were no trust, so they can challenge it. |
| A surviving spouse | Often has rights of their own, even when not named. |
| Beneficiaries who are minors or incapacitated | Notice goes to a parent, guardian or other representative. |

Not every state is the same. Some require notice only to beneficiaries. Some also require it to heirs. Others leave it to the trustee's judgment but make later claims easier for the people who were left out. When in doubt, notify more people rather than fewer, because a notice you did not need to send is cheaper than a lawsuit you did not see coming.

## What does the notice say, and when is it due?

A typical notice covers four things: that the grantor has died, that the trust is now irrevocable, the trustee's name, address and phone number, and the right to request a copy of the trust document. Many states also require a statement of the time limit for contesting the trust.

On timing, states set different periods. Some, California among them, give the trustee a short fixed window after death, such as 60 days. Others use phrases like "within a reasonable time" or give 30 to 90 days after the trustee accepts the job. Treat the earliest plausible deadline as your own, and send notice by a method you can prove, such as certified mail, with a copy for the file.

Marcus, in our example, checks his state's rule, sends letters to his two sisters and to his father's surviving sibling (an heir under state law), and keeps the green return cards. Two weeks later, his cousin, who was not on the list, writes asking for the trust. Marcus sends it with an email explaining the timeline. That small courtesy turns out to matter later.

## Why does the notice date matter so much?

In many states, a person who wants to challenge a trust has a limited time to file. The clock often starts when the trustee sends the statutory notice, and it can be short, in some places only a few months. If no notice goes out, the clock may not start, which leaves the trust open to challenge for far longer.

That cuts both ways. Trustees should send notice partly to protect themselves and the trust. Beneficiaries who receive a notice and are unhappy about a trust should speak to an attorney promptly instead of waiting. Our article on [whether a living trust can be contested](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested) covers the grounds and the deadlines that follow.

## Can the trustee fix a problem in the trust now?

Not alone. Because the document is fixed, the trustee cannot rewrite it to match what Helen "would have wanted." There are limited routes:

- **Powers in the trust itself.** Some trusts give a trustee or a trust protector the right to correct errors or adjust for tax law changes.
- **Agreement among the beneficiaries.** Many states allow a non-judicial settlement agreement signed by everyone affected, so long as it does not violate a material purpose of the trust.
- **A court petition.** A judge can reform a trust to fix a drafting error, if clear evidence shows what the grantor intended, or modify it when circumstances have changed in a way the grantor did not foresee.

Otherwise, the trustee follows the words on the page, even awkward ones.

## What about the joint trust of a married couple?

A joint trust is the exception that confuses people. At the first spouse's death, the trust may divide. The survivor's share often stays revocable and the survivor can still change it. The deceased spouse's share may become irrevocable, and sometimes it moves into a separate sub-trust meant to use the estate tax exemption or protect children from a first marriage. Some joint trusts do not split at all, and the whole trust stays flexible until the second death.

The wording decides. If you are the surviving spouse, ask the attorney who drafted it, or another estate attorney, to read the split provisions before you retitle anything. For couples with children from earlier marriages, our guide on [estate planning for second marriages](/learn/blended-families) shows why the split matters so much.

## What should a trustee avoid doing right away?

- Do not sell the house or move money before you have letters or a certification of trust.
- Do not pay beneficiaries early on a hunch that everything will be fine.
- Do not keep using the grantor's Social Security number for trust accounts.
- Do not discuss disputed matters by text. Put them in letters or email.

Careless early steps are one way trustees end up personally exposed, which we cover in [whether a trustee can be personally liable](/learn/after-a-death/can-a-trustee-be-personally-liable).
