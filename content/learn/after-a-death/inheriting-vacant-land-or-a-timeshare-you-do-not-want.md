Some inheritances arrive as a gift, and some arrive as a bill. A parcel of ground in a county you have never visited, or a week at a resort that your father stopped using ten years ago, can land in that second group. It has no income, you cannot easily use it, and the paperwork says you now own it.

You have more choices than the family usually thinks. This page explains what you owe while you decide, the ways out, and the order in which to try them. If you are early in the process, the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) shows where this fits.

## What does inherited land or a timeshare cost you each year?

It costs something every year whether or not anyone uses it, and the amount is set by the property, not by your plans.

| Item | Vacant land | Timeshare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Property tax | Billed by the county to the owner of record | Often bundled into the maintenance fee |
| Annual fees | Rarely, unless in an association | Maintenance fees plus special assessments |
| Insurance | Liability coverage worth having if people use it | Included in fees at most resorts |
| Upkeep | Mowing, brush removal, fences, drainage | Handled by the resort |
| Liability | Injuries or dumping on the land | Limited |
| If unpaid | Tax lien and eventual tax sale | Collection, credit damage, possible foreclosure |

The numbers differ widely. A small rural lot may cost a few hundred dollars a year. A timeshare may carry fees that rise every year while its resale value is close to zero. The sums are small compared with a house, which is exactly why families ignore them. Unpaid property tax is the quiet danger: it grows with penalties and can end in a sale of the land at a county tax auction.

## Can you refuse an inherited property?

Yes, by disclaiming it. A disclaimer is a signed, written refusal that treats you as if you had died before the owner, so the property passes to the next person in line under the will or by state law. The rules are strict: it must be in writing, it must be delivered to the executor in the right form, and under federal tax law it must be made within nine months of the death for the tax treatment to hold. You also must not have accepted the property by using it, collecting income from it or taking a benefit from it.

That has two consequences. First, you cannot choose who receives the property by disclaiming. It goes where the document or the law sends it, which may be a sibling, or your own children if you have them. Second, a disclaimer is final. If you disclaim the land and it later turns out to hold valuable timber or a pipeline lease, there is no taking it back. Read [how to disclaim an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) before you sign, and see the [Illinois disclaimer rules](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois) if the property is there.

Dolores inherited 11 acres of scrub land from an uncle. She had never been there, and the county sent a tax bill of a few hundred dollars. She asked a local real estate agent for an opinion and learned the land was landlocked, with no road access. She disclaimed it within three months, and it passed to her uncle's alternate beneficiary, a nephew who wanted it for hunting. Both of them were better off than if she had held it for a decade.

## How do you sell inherited land?

Start with a title check, then price it, then choose a buyer. The sequence is:

1. **Confirm who owns it.** The deed may still be in the dead person's name, which is usual. The executor, with court letters, can transfer or sell it. If there are title gaps, see [fixing title problems on inherited property](/learn/property-and-assets/fixing-title-problems-on-inherited-property).
2. **Get the facts.** Order the survey, check zoning, road access, easements, flood zone, and any back taxes. Our page on [hidden property problems](/learn/property-and-assets/hidden-property-problems-deed-restrictions-flood-and-contamination) lists what to check.
3. **Get a local opinion of value.** A land agent in that county knows the buyers. An out-of-town agent usually does not.
4. **Check the mineral and timber rights.** If they were severed earlier, you may sell the surface only. See [mineral rights and royalties](/learn/property-and-assets/mineral-rights-and-royalties-after-the-owner-dies).
5. **Consider the neighbors.** An adjoining owner is often the most motivated buyer, and may not need an agent at all.
6. **Close through a title company or attorney.**

If the land is in another state, the estate may need a separate court filing there. Our guide to [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) and the page on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) describe when this applies.

## What does the tax bill look like when you sell?

Usually small. Inherited property generally receives a step-up in basis, which resets its tax value to its fair market value on the date of death. If you sell for about that amount soon after, there is little gain to report. Get a written appraisal or a broker's opinion dated near the death, because that number becomes your cost basis. The [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) page explains how it works, and the executor's job of [valuing the estate](/learn/after-a-death/how-an-executor-values-the-estate) shows where the figure comes from. Land that has risen in value since the death is taxed on the gain from that date forward.

Land that cannot be sold at a reasonable price is a different problem. A buyer-less parcel may be worth more as a gift to a land trust or a local government than as a decade of tax bills.

## What can you do about an unwanted timeshare?

Read the contract first, then ask the resort. A timeshare is a contract with rules about who can own it and how it can end. Many are deeded as real property, and some are only a right to use. Heirs commonly inherit the duty to pay fees along with the right to use the week.

Your options, in rough order of preference:

1. **Disclaim before accepting**, if the timeshare passes under a will and you have not used it.
2. **Ask the resort for a surrender or deed-back program.** Many resorts now allow owners, especially heirs, to hand back a deeded week when fees are current. Ask in writing and get the terms in writing.
3. **Sell it** on a resale site, understanding that the price may be zero or even negative.
4. **Give it to a family member or friend who wants it,** by deed, with the resort's approval.
5. **Hire a professional only after checking them.** Look up the state attorney general's consumer office and complaint records. Be very cautious of any company that charges a large upfront fee and promises a sure exit.

The most expensive mistake is silence. Letting fees go unpaid hurts credit and, for a deeded timeshare, can lead to foreclosure. A phone call to the resort's owner services line with a death certificate and a copy of the will is better than hoping the account disappears. If the fees are overdue and the estate has little money, the claim is generally a debt of the estate, not of you personally, provided you did not sign the contract. Our page on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) shows how creditor claims are paid.

## What if you want to keep the land?

Then treat it as an asset worth looking after. A walk of the boundaries, a posted sign, a call to the neighbor and a visit to the county every year will do. If siblings co-own, agree in writing on who pays tax and who can use it, because a shared parcel with unclear rules is a standard source of conflict. Our page on [keeping family land in the family](/learn/property-and-assets/keeping-family-land-in-the-family) covers co-ownership and how families arrange it.
