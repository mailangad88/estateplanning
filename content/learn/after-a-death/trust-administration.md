If you are the successor trustee of a loved one's living trust, you have taken on a legal role at the same moment you are grieving. The good news is that trust administration is usually quieter and faster than probate, because it does not require a judge to approve each step. The bad news is that there is no judge guiding you, so you must be careful on your own.

This article explains what happens to a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) when its creator dies and what the trustee does next. It is part of our broader guide to [what families need to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## What happens to a living trust when the creator dies?

A revocable living trust becomes irrevocable at the death of the person who created it, and the successor trustee named in the document takes over. From that moment, the terms can no longer be changed, and the trustee's job is to carry them out.

The [grantor](/glossary/grantor) usually served as trustee while alive. When they die, either the trust names a successor trustee, such as an adult child or a bank, or it gives a method for choosing one. If no one is able to serve, a court can appoint a trustee. Joint trusts for married couples often continue after the first spouse dies, with only part of the trust becoming irrevocable. Read the trust carefully to see which rule applies.

## What are the first steps for a successor trustee?

Begin with documents and protection, not distributions. A reasonable order looks like this:

1. **Read the whole trust and any amendments.** Note who the beneficiaries are, what each gets, when they get it, who your co-trustees or successors are and any special instructions.
2. **Get certified death certificates.** You will use several.
3. **Confirm your own authority in writing.** Sign an acceptance of the trusteeship if the trust requires it, and prepare a certification of trust.
4. **Secure the property.** Lock and insure the home, collect valuables, and forward mail.
5. **Meet an attorney.** A short consultation can tell you whether the trust must be handled with special state notices and whether anything needs to go to probate court.

## Who must be notified?

Most states require the trustee to give written notice to beneficiaries, and often to heirs, within a set number of days after the trust becomes irrevocable. Typical deadlines are in the range of 30 to 60 days or so, but this varies, so check with a local attorney. The notice usually includes the trust's name, the date of death, the trustee's contact information and the beneficiaries' right to request a copy of the trust.

In some states, giving this notice also starts a limited period in which someone can challenge the trust. That is why correct timing and delivery matter.

## Do you need a tax ID and a trust bank account?

Yes. Once the trust is irrevocable it is a separate taxpayer, so you need to apply for an Employer Identification Number for it, which is free through the IRS. Take the EIN, a certification of trust and a death certificate to a bank and open a trust account. Use this account for all trust income and expenses.

Existing trust accounts at banks and brokerages may need to be retitled in the name of the trust or the successor trustee. Keep investments in place while you assess them. A trustee who has to manage investments is usually expected to act prudently, which includes diversifying rather than leaving a large sum in one stock or one property without a reason.

## How do you identify and value trust assets?

List every asset in the trust and its value on the date of death. Real estate usually needs a date-of-death appraisal, which also sets the new tax basis for the heirs. Most assets in the trust receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which can greatly reduce capital gains if they are sold. You will also review whether the person funded the trust fully. Our article on [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) explains what that means, and an unfunded asset may have to pass through probate instead, often under a pour-over will.

Retirement accounts and life insurance usually pass by beneficiary designation, not by the trust, unless the trust itself is the named beneficiary. These are handled separately. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

## What about debts and taxes?

The trustee must pay the person's valid debts, funeral costs, final medical bills and taxes from trust assets before final distribution to beneficiaries, if probate assets are insufficient. Many states allow a trustee to publish or send a notice to creditors that shortens the time creditors have to make claims. Without one, claims can sometimes be made later, so ask an attorney whether a notice is wise.

Tax duties include the person's last Form 1040, the trust's own Form 1041 for income earned after death if it meets the filing threshold, and potentially an estate tax return for very large estates. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns). Hold back enough in the trust to cover these before making a final distribution.

## How and when does the trustee distribute assets?

Follow the trust exactly. Some trusts say, "divide everything equally among my three children." Others hold shares in continuing trusts for young beneficiaries, pay out in stages at ages like 25 and 30, or give a lifetime income to a surviving spouse. See our overview of [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) for how those work.

Before the final distribution:

- Confirm all debts, taxes and expenses are paid.
- Prepare an accounting showing everything received, spent and distributed.
- Get each beneficiary's signed receipt, and a release if your attorney recommends one.
- Keep a reserve until tax returns are filed and accepted.

For an example, Daniel is successor trustee for his mother's $900,000 trust, which leaves everything equally to him and his two sisters. He opens a trust account, appraises the house, pays final bills, sells the house, files the trust's first tax return and then divides what is left in thirds. A year later all three siblings sign receipts, and the trust is closed.

## What are the trustee's duties and risks?

A trustee must follow the trust terms, act in the beneficiaries' interest, avoid conflicts of interest, keep clear records and treat beneficiaries impartially. Trustees can be personally liable for improper payments or losses. This is why you should keep trust money separate from your own and never borrow from the trust.

If a beneficiary believes you are not performing your duties, they may ask a court to compel an accounting or to remove you. Our article on [removing an executor or trustee](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) explains this process. If you are considering whether to serve in the first place, read [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).

## When should a trustee call an attorney?

Call early if the trust holds real estate in more than one state, a business, retirement accounts, or if beneficiaries disagree. A one-hour consultation in the first month often prevents expensive errors later.

Beneficiaries are entitled to notice and reports, and [does a trustee have to notify beneficiaries and give an accounting?](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) explains the first notice and what an accounting includes.
