If someone you love has just died, you are probably tired, numb and being asked to make decisions you never expected to make. This page is meant to lower the pressure. It tells you what has to happen quickly, what can wait, and in what order the rest tends to go.

You do not have to do all of this this week. The urgent tasks come first, and the rest is spread across the coming weeks and months. For the bigger picture, see our [guide to what to do when a loved one dies](/learn/after-a-death).

## What has to happen in the first 24 hours?

Four things cannot wait: a legal pronouncement of death, notifying the people closest to the person, care for anyone or anything that depended on them, and arrangements for the body.

1. **Get the death pronounced.** If the person was under hospice care, call the hospice nurse. They will come, pronounce the death and tell you whom to call next. If the person died in a hospital or care facility, staff handle this. If the death was unexpected and happened at home, call 911.
2. **Tell close family and friends.** Ask one or two people to help spread the news so you do not have to make every call.
3. **Make sure dependents are safe.** Children, a dependent adult, or pets who were relying on the person need a caregiver today. If a minor child has no other parent, tell a trusted relative right away. The person's will may name a [guardian](/glossary/guardian), and our guide on [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) explains the court process if not.
4. **Contact a funeral home or cremation provider.** You can choose one today or tomorrow. They will transport the person, help you write the obituary and file the death certificate. You are not required to decide on the full service right away.

If the person lived alone, lock up the home, take in the mail, and secure medications, jewelry, cash, firearms and important papers.

## What can wait the first few days?

Almost everything financial can wait a few days, and nothing is lost by pausing. This includes calling banks, canceling subscriptions, selling a car, cleaning out the house and posting on social media about accounts. If anyone presses you for a quick signature or payment, slow down.

A few early items are worth doing, but they are not emergencies:

- Find out whether the person pre-arranged funeral or burial plans or owned cemetery plots. Look in their files for a contract.
- Look for a will, trust and any funeral instructions. A fireproof box, a desk drawer, a home safe, a safe deposit box and the office of their attorney are the usual places. See our article on [where people keep a will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
- Ask the funeral director to report the death to Social Security, and give them the person's Social Security number.

## What should you do in the first week?

By the end of the first week, the goal is to have the funeral planned, certified death certificates ordered and the key documents found.

### Order certified death certificates

The funeral home normally orders them for you. Ask for 10 to 15 certified copies. Copies are used for life insurance claims, retirement accounts, bank accounts, transferring vehicle and real estate titles, pension plans and sometimes utilities. Photocopies are rarely accepted.

### Locate the legal documents

Gather the original will, any trust documents, powers of attorney, deeds, vehicle titles, insurance policies, retirement account statements, tax returns and a recent bank statement. If you cannot find a will, check with the county court, the person's attorney and the person's financial advisor. A [power of attorney](/glossary/power-of-attorney) ends at death, so a person who served as agent has no authority after that moment, and the executor takes over.

### Protect the property

Tell the home insurer about the death and, if the house will sit empty, ask what coverage applies. Many policies limit coverage when a house is vacant for 30 to 60 days. Keep utilities on, especially heat in cold climates.

### Do not distribute belongings yet

Family members often start dividing keepsakes right away. Please pause, especially if anyone disagrees about who gets what. Photograph rooms before anything is moved.

## What should you do in the first month?

In the first month, you identify who has legal authority, meet with an attorney, and notify the major agencies and institutions. This is a practical sequence:

| When | Task | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1 to 2 | Meet with an estate planning or probate attorney | Confirms whether probate, a [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) or neither is needed |
| Weeks 1 to 2 | File the will with the court if your state requires it | Many states set a deadline for filing an original will after death |
| Weeks 2 to 4 | Notify Social Security, pension plans and the VA if applicable | Stops overpayments and begins survivor benefits |
| Weeks 2 to 4 | Notify the three credit bureaus | Reduces the risk of identity theft on a deceased person's file |
| Weeks 2 to 4 | Claim life insurance and retirement account benefits | Beneficiary claims do not require probate |
| Weeks 2 to 6 | Forward mail through the post office and cancel subscriptions | Keeps bills and sensitive mail from piling up |
| Weeks 3 to 6 | Apply for the executor's authority, called [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary) in many states | Banks and title offices will want this document before talking with you |

### Notify Social Security and other agencies

Social Security should know about the death. A surviving spouse or a child may be eligible for a one-time death payment of $255 if they meet requirements, and a surviving spouse may qualify for monthly survivor benefits. Call 800-772-1213 or visit a local office, and verify details at [ssa.gov](https://www.ssa.gov). Other agencies to notify include the VA if the person was a veteran, a pension administrator, the DMV and any government benefit programs the person used.

### Notify the credit bureaus

Send a copy of the death certificate to Equifax, Experian and TransUnion and ask them to add a deceased alert to the file. Ask for a copy of the report so you can find open accounts you did not know about. This is one way to uncover forgotten debts and to prevent fraud.

### Claim insurance and account benefits

Life insurance, retirement accounts and accounts with a named beneficiary or payable-on-death designation generally pay directly to the beneficiary without going through probate. Each company has its own claim form and wants a certified death certificate. Our overview of [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains which assets follow which route.

## When does an executor or trustee take over?

An executor named in a will has authority only after a court appoints them, and a successor trustee has authority as soon as they accept the role and can show the trust document. Until then, no one should be moving money out of accounts.

If there is a will, the named executor petitions the probate court. After the judge issues the formal authority, the executor opens an estate bank account, gathers the assets, pays valid debts and eventually distributes what remains. Our step-by-step [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) covers each stage. If the person had a living trust, the successor trustee follows the trust's own instructions, which our [trust administration guide](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) explains.

If you are the one who agreed to take on this role, do not be alarmed by the size of the task. Most of it is paperwork done over months.

## What about debts, bills and taxes?

Debts do not pass automatically to family members. In general, a person's debts are paid from the estate before heirs receive anything, and relatives are not responsible for them unless they co-signed or share the account. Spouses in community property states can have different obligations, so ask an attorney about your state.

Do not pay old debts from your own pocket or agree to pay a collector by phone. Refer them to the executor. Our article on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) lays out the order in which debts are paid.

Taxes come later. The final income tax return is due in the spring after the year of death, and some estates must also file an estate return. See [final tax returns for someone who died](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) for what is required and when.

## What can wait several months?

Closing accounts, transferring the house, selling vehicles, filing the estate's tax returns and making final distributions are best done slowly. Most probate cases take several months to a year or more, and hurrying a distribution is the most common executor mistake. Grief affects memory and judgment, so avoid permanent decisions, such as selling the family home, in the first few weeks unless you must.
