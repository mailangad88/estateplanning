Social Security looks like one of the simpler institutions to deal with after a death, and it mostly is. But a few of its rules catch families out: the last check that has to be returned, the $255 payment that nobody tells you about, and the survivor benefits that go unclaimed because no one applies.

This page walks through what to do, in the order it comes up. It sits alongside our general guide on [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death), and the numbers reflect 2026. Check [ssa.gov](https://www.ssa.gov) for anything that has changed.

## Who tells Social Security about the death?

In most cases the funeral home does. When you give the funeral director the person's Social Security number, they report the death as part of the paperwork. Social Security then stops benefits, and the Treasury Department stops direct deposits.

Still, confirm it. Call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213 within a few days, or visit a local office, and ask whether the death has been recorded. If a payment arrives after the death, a missed report is usually the reason.

If the person was receiving other government benefits, such as a pension from a former employer, veterans' benefits or railroad retirement, those are separate offices with separate notices. The funeral home does not report to them. Our [checklist of what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) lists the others.

## Do you have to give back the last payment?

Yes. Social Security pays benefits a month behind. The check you receive in May is the benefit for April. That means a person who dies in April is not entitled to the payment for April, and the deposit in May has to be returned. The same is true for any deposit after that.

What usually happens:

1. The bank learns of the death, often through Social Security's own report, and reverses the deposit.
2. Social Security sends a letter if money was already withdrawn and asks for repayment.
3. You can respond by returning the money or, if you think there is an error, by explaining why.

A payment received before the date of death for the month before is yours to keep. Dan's mother died on June 12, for example. The payment deposited in June was her May benefit, and it stays. The payment deposited in July was for June, the month she died, and goes back.

Notice that there is no refund of the partial month. Families sometimes expect to receive a prorated benefit up to the day of death. Social Security does not do this.

Tell the bank directly. If you are managing the account, our guide to [what happens to a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) explains how it will be frozen and who may touch it. Do not withdraw the money in the hope that nobody notices. Social Security will find it, and the repayment demand is easier to deal with when the funds are still there.

## Who gets the $255 death payment?

It is a one-time lump sum of $255. It is not much and it is not meant to cover a funeral, but it is real money that families forget to ask for.

Three people can receive it, in this order.

- A **surviving spouse** who was living with the worker at the time of death.
- A spouse who was not living with the worker but was receiving or eligible for benefits on the worker's record.
- A **child** who is eligible for benefits on the worker's record, if there is no such spouse.

It cannot go to the estate, to the funeral home or to an executor in that role. You apply, usually on the same call as a survivor benefit claim. There is a time limit for the application, so do not let it sit for long. The worker also has to have earned enough credits during working life, which most people have.

## What survivor benefits might your family qualify for?

Monthly survivor benefits are more valuable than the lump sum, and they are not automatic. Someone has to apply.

| Who | Basic rule |
|---|---|
| Surviving spouse | Can claim as early as age 60 at a reduced rate, or 50 if disabled. The full amount comes at the survivor's full retirement age. |
| Spouse of any age with a young child | May receive a benefit while caring for the worker's child under 16 |
| Children | Unmarried children under 18, or up to 19 if still in high school, and disabled adult children whose disability began early |
| Surviving divorced spouse | Marriage lasted at least ten years, claimant is 60 or older (50 if disabled), and generally has not remarried before 60 |
| Dependent parents | Parents who were financially dependent on the worker, 62 or older |

Two ideas help here. First, the survivor's own work record and the deceased's can interact. A widow who is also entitled to her own retirement benefit generally receives the larger of the two, not both added together, and she can sometimes switch from one to the other at different ages. Second, the age you claim changes the amount permanently, so ask Social Security for the figures at several ages before filing.

Remarriage matters in a narrow way. A widow or widower who remarries before 60 generally loses survivor eligibility, while remarrying at 60 or older does not end it.

## How do you apply for survivor benefits?

Survivor benefits generally cannot be claimed entirely online. Call Social Security or make an appointment at a local office. Have these ready:

1. The deceased person's Social Security number and a certified copy of the [death certificate](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need).
2. Your own Social Security number and proof of age.
3. Your marriage certificate, and divorce papers if relevant.
4. Birth certificates for any children claiming.
5. Bank details for direct deposit.
6. Recent W-2 or self-employment tax return of the deceased, if available.

Ask for all the benefits you might qualify for in a single appointment. People often learn about the lump sum, the child's benefit or the divorced-spouse option only when someone mentions it. It is fine to ask the representative, "What am I eligible for on this record?"

## What mistakes cost families the most?

The most common is not applying at all. A widow in her sixties assumes Social Security has already moved her to a survivor payment. It has not, and she waits months for nothing.

The second is spending a deposit that has to be returned, then facing a repayment demand when the account is empty. If the account was joint with the person who died, check the balance carefully before writing checks.

The third is claiming too early without comparing numbers. Taking a reduced survivor benefit at 60 may be the right choice when a family needs the income. It may be the wrong one if there is no urgency. A widow can often take the survivor benefit first and let her own retirement benefit grow, or the reverse. This is a calculation worth doing with the agency before committing.

The fourth is forgetting that **other benefits have their own rules**. A pension, a union annuity or a veteran's survivor benefit will have its own claim form. If the person was a veteran, ask the Department of Veterans Affairs what survivor support exists. ## What should an executor do about Social Security?

Executors tend to hear the same advice and miss the details. Confirm the death was reported. Make sure no deposit is spent. Notify the bank. Keep the notice from Social Security that lists any repayment owed, because it counts as a debt of the estate if it cannot be taken from the account. And remember that the final tax return may need to report the benefits received through the date of death. Our guide on [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) covers that.

Social Security benefits paid to the person before death are not part of a probate dispute in the usual sense, but money left in the account is part of the estate or passes by account title.
