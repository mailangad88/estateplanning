When a person dies at home, the first thing a visiting relative may notice is a dog by the door or a cat watching from the stairs. Nobody has fed the animal since the morning of the death. The family is in shock, the house is full of people, and no one knows who is responsible.

The immediate question is practical, and the legal question comes second. This page answers both: who looks after the animal in the first days, who owns it in the eyes of the law, how a will can direct it and what to do when no one in the family can take it. If you are working through the larger list of tasks, our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) places this in context.

## Who should look after the pet right away?

Whoever can do it today. No court order or letters from the court are needed to feed an animal or take it to the vet. The person who finds the pet, a neighbor with a key, or the relative nearest the house can step in.

In the first 48 hours, take these steps.

1. **Do not leave the animal alone in a house that is empty overnight.** Take it with you, or arrange for someone to stay. A frightened animal in an empty house can hurt itself or damage the house.
2. **Collect what it needs.** Food, medication, the leash, the carrier, the bed and the vet's name.
3. **Find the medication list.** An older cat on thyroid pills or a dog on insulin cannot skip doses. The vet's office will tell you the schedule.
4. **Call the vet.** Explain that the owner died and ask who is listed on the file and whether the clinic will treat the animal on the family's word.
5. **Keep receipts.** Whoever pays can usually be reimbursed by the estate later.

Birds, reptiles, horses and farm animals need the same urgency and often an experienced hand. If the animals are on a property, a neighbor or a stable can help while you decide.

## Who legally owns a pet after the owner dies?

The estate does. Law treats an animal as personal property, in the same category as furniture, a car or jewelry. When the owner dies, the pet becomes an asset of the estate, and the executor is in charge of it until it is given to someone. For a general view of how personal property passes, see our page on [what a will covers](/learn/property-and-assets/what-your-will-covers-timeshares-storage-units-safe-deposit-boxes).

That sounds cold, and families rarely experience it that way. In practice, the executor asks the person who has been caring for the animal whether they would like to keep it. The legal label matters in only a few situations: when two heirs want the same animal, when the will says something specific, or when an animal has real value, such as a show dog or a horse.

A pet may also be owned jointly or belong to someone else on paper. An animal the dead person adopted in a spouse's name belongs to the spouse. An animal bought from a breeder, or adopted from a rescue, can come with a contract that limits resale or transfer. Check the adoption papers and registration before you assume.

## What can a will or trust say about a pet?

A will can name a person to receive the animal, and a good one names a backup too. The executor then gives the animal to that person. The gift stands on its own, and the will has to say separately if money is meant to go with the animal.

Compare the common approaches:

| Approach | What it does | Weak spot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gift of the pet to a named person | The executor hands over the animal | No money for care unless added |
| Gift of the pet plus a sum of money | Funds food and vet bills | The person can spend the money on other things |
| Pet trust | A trustee pays for the animal's care for its life | Costs more to set up and needs a trustee |
| Written wishes in an informal letter | Guides the family | Not binding unless the will says so |

Tomas, 71, left his terrier to his neighbor Alicia in his will and set aside a sum for her. Alicia took the dog the day after his death and the executor sent the money once the estate had cash. Without the will, his two grown children, living in different states, would have had to work it out themselves while the dog sat in a kennel.

A pet trust is a legal arrangement for the animal's care, with money held by a trustee. Illinois and New Jersey both recognize them, and our pages on [pet trusts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/pet-trusts-in-illinois) and [pet trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/pet-trusts-in-new-jersey) describe how they work. The general idea is covered in [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts).

## What if the will says nothing about the pet?

Then the heirs decide, and the executor has the last word if they cannot. The family should look for the answer in the animal's real life. Who walked the dog, who took the cat to the vet and who does the animal already trust? That person is usually the right choice, even if they are not the heir who would inherit the pet on paper.

If two people want the animal, try these in order:

- Ask whether either would let the other have the pet with visiting rights.
- Offset the animal's value against other items in the estate, such as furniture or jewelry.
- Ask the executor to decide, and write it down.

Rarely does a pet go to a court. The cost of a dispute over a family cat would outrun anything it could win. Our page on [dividing belongings and heirlooms](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms) discusses similar disagreements, and the same calm approach applies.

## What if no one can take the animal?

Call a rescue before the family reaches a crisis. Breed rescues, senior pet programs and no-kill shelters are more open to an animal whose owner died than to a stray. They want information: age, health, medication, temperament and how the animal gets along with other pets or children. Write it down in a one-page summary, and attach the vet record.

Use these steps:

1. Ask the vet whether the clinic knows of families or rescue groups.
2. Contact two or three rescues and ask about their waiting time.
3. Offer a donation toward the animal's care, which the estate can pay from its funds as a reasonable expense.
4. Keep the animal safely boarded in the meantime, with the executor's approval.

An animal control shelter should be the last stop. Some are excellent, but many have limited holding times and cannot promise a placement.

## What paperwork changes when the pet changes hands?

Three records need your attention: the microchip registration, the vet file and any license. A chip is only as useful as the contact details tied to it, and these will still read as the dead person's phone number. Call the chip company or the registry, give them the new owner's name and send proof of the transfer. Do the same with the town's dog license, and pet insurance if there is a policy. Ask the insurer whether the policy can be moved to a new owner or must be cancelled.

If the house is going to sit empty while the animal lives elsewhere, read about [insurance and upkeep of a vacant inherited house](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters), and add pet-related damage to your walk-through list.
