Inheriting a house can feel like both a gift and a burden. There may be memories in every room, a mortgage you did not expect, siblings with different ideas and a lawn that still needs mowing. The decisions you do not have to make quickly are more numerous than the ones you do.

This guide explains what to do first, how the house legally becomes yours, the choices in front of you and the tax rules that matter. It is part of our wider guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## What should you do first with an inherited house?

Protect it, and do not make big decisions yet. The steps below keep the property safe while the legal ownership is sorted out:

1. **Secure the house.** Change or rekey the locks if several people have keys, and remove valuables and important papers.
2. **Call the insurer.** Explain that the owner has died and ask what coverage applies. Many policies restrict coverage if the house sits vacant for 30 to 60 days, and you may need a vacant-property policy.
3. **Keep the bills current.** Pay property taxes, insurance, utilities and any mortgage from estate funds if possible.
4. **Find the deed and the mortgage statement.** The deed shows how title is held and the statement shows the loan balance and the servicer.
5. **Photograph the house** and keep a list of the contents before anything is moved.
6. **Talk with the executor or trustee,** since they control the property until it is formally transferred.

## How does the house legally pass to you?

The path depends on how the owner held title. Each path has different documents and timing, as the table summarizes.

| How the owner held title | How it passes | What you typically need |
|---|---|---|
| Sole name with a will | Through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) to the named heir | Court-issued authority, then an executor's deed |
| Sole name, no will | Through probate to heirs set by state law | Court appointment of an administrator |
| Living trust | The successor trustee transfers it per the trust | Certification of trust, trustee's deed |
| Joint tenancy with survivorship | Directly to the surviving owner | Death certificate and affidavit filed with the county |
| Transfer-on-death deed | Directly to the named beneficiary | Death certificate and recorded affidavit |

If a house is held with right of survivorship, the survivor becomes the owner without probate. A [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) works in much the same way in states that allow them. Joint ownership has risks, which we cover in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

If the house is in another state from where the owner lived, a second court proceeding may be needed. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

## What happens to the mortgage?

The mortgage stays in place, and the debt is paid from the estate or by whoever keeps the home. A federal law, the Garn-St Germain Act, generally stops lenders from demanding full repayment merely because a relative inherits the home as a residence. Contact the servicer, send a death certificate and proof of your authority, and ask about assuming the loan or modifying it in your name.

If the house is worth less than the loan, you are generally not personally responsible for the shortfall unless you co-signed. You can also let the lender foreclose or sell the house in a short sale. If there is a reverse mortgage, the rules differ: the loan usually comes due after the borrower dies, and heirs have a limited time to repay it, sell or sign over the home. Contact the servicer promptly and ask for the deadlines in writing.

## What are your options for the house?

You can keep it, sell it, rent it or, with co-heirs, buy the others out. Think of the choice in terms of cost and use.

### Keep it as your home

You take on the taxes, insurance, repairs and mortgage. Some states reassess property taxes when ownership changes, and exemptions the owner enjoyed, such as a senior or homestead discount, may not carry over to you. Ask your county assessor about filing deadlines.

### Sell it

Selling is the simplest choice if no one wants to live there. Get a date-of-death appraisal first, since it sets your tax basis. List the house only after you have legal authority. A surviving spouse who sells within a limited period after the death may be able to use the larger capital gains home sale exclusion for married couples, so ask a tax professional.

### Rent it

Rental income is taxable, and you take on landlord duties. Estimate repairs, vacancy and the rules in your area before deciding.

### Buy out co-heirs

If siblings inherit equally, one can buy the others' shares at an agreed or appraised value, using savings or a new mortgage. Put the terms in writing and have an attorney prepare the deed. If heirs cannot agree, any co-owner can generally ask a court to order a sale, which is slower and costlier than agreeing.

For example, Marcus and his sister Elena inherit their mother's house, appraised at $360,000, with no mortgage. Elena wants to keep it. She refinances, pays Marcus his half, $180,000, and he signs a deed. Both avoid a sale and agent commissions.

## What are the tax consequences?

Most heirs receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), so your cost basis is the home's fair market value at the date of death, not what the owner paid. If you sell soon after at about that value, you owe little or no capital gains tax. Growth after the date of death is taxable when you sell.

The home sale exclusion generally requires that you lived in the house as your main home for two of the last five years, so heirs who never lived there usually cannot use it, and the step-up does the work instead. Inherited property is generally treated as long-term for tax purposes regardless of how long you hold it. For a wider view, read [taxes on an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs). A few states charge an inheritance tax on real estate, which is covered in the same article.

If the home was in a revocable trust, the trust typically becomes irrevocable at death and the trustee handles the sale or transfer. See [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).

## What if the house is in poor shape or has liens?

Check the title and the condition before committing. Order a title search to reveal unpaid property taxes, contractor liens, judgments or a second mortgage, because these stay with the property. An inspection before you move in or list the house will show whether repairs are needed and help you set a realistic price. If the cost of repairs plus liens exceeds the value, selling as is to an investor, or even declining the inheritance through a written disclaimer, can be reasonable. Ask an attorney how your state handles liens against a decedent's property.

If you decide to sell, [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) explains who can sign, how the stepped-up basis works and what to do when siblings disagree.
