If you are a beneficiary, you may have heard that a parent's trust now holds the family's assets, and no one has told you what is in it. If you are a trustee, you may be wondering how much you have to tell people, and in what form. Both questions have the same answer: trustees owe beneficiaries information, and the details depend on the trust and the state.

This article explains the duty in plain terms, who is entitled to what, what a first notice and an accounting look like, and what to do if one side is not cooperating. It is written for both trustees and beneficiaries.

## What is the duty to inform and account?

A trustee is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), someone legally required to act for the benefit of others. Part of that role is transparency. The duty has four pieces:

- **Inform.** Tell beneficiaries the trust exists, who the trustee is and how to reach them, and that they have a right to request information.
- **Respond.** Answer reasonable requests for information promptly.
- **Keep records.** Maintain statements, receipts and a clear record of every transaction.
- **Account.** Report assets, income, expenses and distributions, at least on request and often on a schedule.

While the person who made the trust is alive and the trust is revocable, the duties run to that person, not the children. The beneficiaries' rights usually begin when the trust becomes irrevocable, typically at death. Our article on [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) puts these duties in the context of everything else a successor trustee must do.

## Who is entitled to information?

Rights vary by type of beneficiary. In many states that follow the Uniform Trust Code, the main group is "qualified beneficiaries": people who are currently eligible to receive distributions, plus those who would receive if the current beneficiaries' interests ended. A common example:

Maria's father left a trust that pays income to her stepmother for life, and then the remainder to Maria and her brother. The stepmother is a current beneficiary. Maria and her brother are remainder beneficiaries, and they usually have a right to notice and reports too, because the trustee's choices about the principal affect what they will eventually get.

Some limits apply. A trust may restrict what contingent beneficiaries see, and a settlor can sometimes delay or limit disclosure. A beneficiary who is a minor or lacks capacity is generally represented by a parent, guardian or court-appointed representative. The exact scope of who gets what is a state law question, so ask a local attorney before assuming that the trust document controls everything.

## What is the first notice, and when is it due?

The first notice is a written message from the trustee to the beneficiaries after the trust becomes irrevocable or the trustee accepts the job. Many states require it within a set period. Sixty days is the common figure in states that follow the Uniform Trust Code, but other states use different periods or require notice only on request.

A good first notice usually includes:

1. The name of the trust and the date it was signed, and a statement that the person who made it has died.
2. The trustee's name, address and phone number.
3. A statement that the beneficiary has a right to request a copy of the trust document, or the relevant parts of it.
4. How the trustee will report, and when.
5. A statement of the beneficiary's right to ask for an accounting.

Some states require specific notice language or add a time limit after which the beneficiary cannot contest the trust. Do not guess; a short consultation with an attorney is worth it here. Our [trust administration article](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) describes the other early tasks, such as getting an EIN and securing assets.

## What does an accounting look like?

An accounting is a written report showing what the trustee has done with the trust's money over a period. A standard format has these parts:

- **Opening balance:** every asset and its value at the start of the period. For the first accounting after a death, this is the date-of-death value.
- **Receipts:** all income and money coming in, such as interest, dividends, rent and sale proceeds.
- **Disbursements:** all payments out, including taxes, repairs, insurance, legal and accounting fees and the trustee's own compensation.
- **Distributions:** what was paid to each beneficiary, with dates.
- **Closing balance:** every asset and its value at the end of the period.

Statements from banks and brokerages should back up every number, and receipts support expenses. The trustee should also share the trust's tax reporting. A trust that earns income files Form 1041 and issues each beneficiary a Schedule K-1 for the income passed to them. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) for how trust and estate returns work.

## How often must a trustee report?

How often depends on the trust and the state, and it falls into three typical patterns.

| Event | Notice or report | To whom | Typical timing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trust becomes irrevocable | Notice of trust, trustee's name, right to a copy | Qualified beneficiaries | Often within about 60 days |
| Ongoing administration | Annual report of assets, receipts, disbursements | Current beneficiaries | Annually, or on request |
| Material change | Notice of change in trustee or compensation | Qualified beneficiaries | Before or soon after the change |
| Request | Copy of the trust, accounting or relevant information | Any beneficiary entitled | Reasonable time after request |
| Termination | Final accounting and receipts | All beneficiaries | At the end of administration |

Some trusts require annual court-reviewed accountings. Most do not, and an informal accounting that the beneficiaries approve and sign is common. A formal court accounting is more expensive but gives the trustee a court order that protects against later claims.

## What should trustees do to comply?

Trustees can avoid most conflict by being early, thorough and consistent:

1. Send the first notice on time, in writing, and keep proof of delivery.
2. Keep a separate trust account and never mix trust money with your own.
3. Record every transaction, and keep statements and receipts for each one.
4. Send regular reports, even if the trust does not strictly require them.
5. Answer questions promptly, including the uncomfortable ones, such as the trustee's fees.
6. Get a signed receipt or release when making a final distribution.

Silence is the most common cause of disputes. A beneficiary who cannot see what is happening assumes the worst, even when the trustee has done nothing wrong. See [what a trustee actually does each year](/blog/what-does-a-trustee-actually-do-each-year) for a practical annual routine, and [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) if you are still deciding who should serve.

## What can beneficiaries do to get information?

Start with a written request, keep it polite and be specific. Ask for a copy of the trust, a list of assets, the most recent statements, and an accounting for a stated period. Send it by a method that proves delivery. Many requests get a prompt answer.

If the trustee does not respond, the next steps usually are:

1. A second written request that sets a reasonable deadline and states that you will ask the court if there is none.
2. A letter from an attorney, which often changes the response.
3. Mediation, which is usually faster and cheaper than court.
4. A petition to the court to compel an accounting or disclosure, and in serious cases to remove the trustee. See [can an executor or trustee be removed?](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

Deadlines matter. Many states set a limited time after receiving a report or an accounting in which a beneficiary can object. Ask a lawyer about your state's period before it passes. If your concern is the validity of the trust itself, see [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will), which also describes the challenges that apply to trusts.

## What if the trust says no accounting is required?

A trust can often loosen reporting duties, but it usually cannot remove them entirely. Some states let a settlor waive formal accountings. Many states, however, keep a minimum duty to inform that no document can override, and courts can still intervene if there is evidence of mismanagement or self-dealing. The wording of the waiver matters, so have a lawyer read it.
