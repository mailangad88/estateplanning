When someone you love dies, grief arrives together with a long list of tasks. People call, forms appear, and you may be expected to know what comes next. This guide is meant to slow things down. It separates what truly cannot wait from what can, explains who has legal authority, and leads you to deeper articles when you need them.

You do not need to read everything today. If you are in the first few days, jump to the first section and then stop. Come back when you are ready for the next stage.

## What is urgent, and what can wait?

Very little is urgent. A few hours or days matter for the following, and the rest can be done at your own pace.

**This week:**
- A medical professional or hospice nurse pronounces the death.
- Close family is told.
- Dependents and pets are cared for.
- A funeral home or cremation provider is chosen.
- The home is locked and valuables are secured.
- Certified death certificates are ordered.

**Within a few weeks:**
- The original will and trust are found, and an attorney is consulted.
- The will is filed with the court if your state requires it.
- Social Security, pension plans, the VA and the credit bureaus are notified.
- Life insurance and retirement account claims are started.

**Over the following months:**
- Probate or trust administration is completed.
- Debts are paid and the final tax returns are filed.
- Property is transferred or sold.
- Assets are distributed to heirs.

Our [week-by-week checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) lays this out in detail, including what to say on calls and what not to sign.

## Who has legal authority to act?

Legal authority comes from a document or a court, not from being the closest relative. Knowing who the right person is saves arguments and mistakes.

| Situation | Who is in charge | How they get authority |
|---|---|---|
| A will names an executor | The executor | The court issues [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary) |
| No will | An administrator, often a spouse or adult child | The court appoints them, and state law ranks who may apply |
| A living trust exists | The successor trustee | The trust document, proven by a certification of trust |
| Assets with named beneficiaries | The beneficiary | A claim form and death certificate |
| Jointly owned property with survivorship | The surviving owner | A death certificate and sometimes an affidavit |

Powers of attorney and healthcare directives end at death. A person who was the agent no longer has authority and should hand matters to the executor. If you are the one who has been named, read our [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) for the steps in order, or the [trust administration guide](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) if you are a successor trustee.

## What is probate, and is it always needed?

Probate is the court process that confirms a will, appoints an executor and oversees payment of debts and distribution of assets. It is not always needed. Property that passes by beneficiary designation, joint ownership or a living trust generally skips it, and very small estates can often use a simplified affidavit.

A quick way to see where a particular asset goes:

| Asset | Usually goes through probate? |
|---|---|
| Bank account in the person's sole name, no beneficiary | Yes |
| Retirement account or life insurance with a named beneficiary | No |
| House held in a living trust | No |
| House in the person's sole name | Yes |
| Joint account with right of survivorship | No |
| Car titled in the person's sole name | Often yes, unless a simple transfer process applies |

Probate rules differ from state to state. Our [probate guide](/learn/probate) covers how it works, what it costs and how long it takes, and our article on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) helps you sort the person's property. If the person left a will, the first stage is described in [wills: what they do and don't do](/learn/wills).

## What does an executor actually do?

An executor gathers the assets, pays the debts and taxes, and distributes what is left, under the court's supervision. The role takes many months, and it carries personal responsibility, so organized records and caution matter.

The core duties are to file the will and get court authority, notify heirs and creditors, open an estate bank account, value and protect the assets, pay valid debts, file the tax returns, and make distributions only when it is safe. The full sequence is explained in [executor duties: a step-by-step guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties). Executors can hire attorneys and accountants and pay them from the estate, and they can generally receive reasonable compensation.

If you named an executor in your own will, or are asked to serve, see [how to choose an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).

## What happens when there is a living trust?

If the person set up a living trust and moved assets into it, the successor trustee takes over without going through probate for those assets. The trust becomes irrevocable at the death, and the trustee follows its terms. That includes notifying beneficiaries, getting a tax ID, managing the assets, paying debts and taxes and distributing the property.

Trust administration is usually quieter than probate, but it is not automatic, and trustees can be held personally responsible for errors. Our guide to [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) walks through it. For background on how these trusts work, read [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) and our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts).

## Are debts passed on to the family?

Generally no. A person's debts are paid from the estate, in an order set by state law, and heirs receive whatever remains. If the estate cannot pay everything, many unsecured debts, such as credit cards, simply go unpaid. Family members are responsible only in limited cases, such as a co-signed loan, a joint credit card account, or a spouse's responsibility under a state's community property rules.

Do not pay old bills from your own money, and do not let a collector pressure you into agreeing to pay. Pass the collector's contact information to the executor and the attorney. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) for the order of payment.

## What taxes should families expect?

Several taxes can touch an estate, but most families pay little. In summary:

- **Final income tax return.** The person's last Form 1040 is filed by the executor, and the estate and trust may have returns of their own. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).
- **Federal estate tax.** Paid by the estate, only if it exceeds $15,000,000 per person in 2026. Married couples can combine exclusions with portability. Read the [estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).
- **State estate or inheritance tax.** A small number of states have one, with lower thresholds than the federal tax.
- **Income tax on retirement accounts.** Heirs pay ordinary income tax when they withdraw from a traditional IRA or 401(k), and most non-spouse heirs must empty the account within 10 years.
- **Capital gains on inherited property.** Heirs usually receive a step-up in basis, so only growth after the death is taxed.

For a closer look, read [do you pay taxes on an inheritance?](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## What about the house?

An inherited house can pass through probate, a trust, a transfer-on-death deed or joint ownership, and the path decides who signs what. Meanwhile it needs insurance, upkeep, tax payments and a plan for the mortgage. Heirs can keep it, sell it, rent it or buy out co-owners.

Rushing to sell or move in is rarely necessary. Our guide to [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) covers insurance for vacant homes, mortgages, siblings who disagree and taxes. If the home is in another state, read about [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property). When the family decides to sell, see [selling inherited property: taxes, timing and who can sign](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property).

## What if you think the executor or trustee is doing it wrong?

Beneficiaries have the right to information and honest management. If you are worried, start by asking for an accounting in writing, and consider mediation before a petition. A court can compel information, limit powers or remove a fiduciary who has stolen, neglected the estate or acted in their own interest. Our article on [removing an executor or trustee](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) explains the options and the evidence courts look for. Trustees also have a duty to notify and report, which is covered in [does a trustee have to notify beneficiaries and give an accounting?](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) If a trustee simply stops acting, [what beneficiaries can do when a trustee does nothing](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-trustee-does-nothing) lays out the escalation steps, from a written request to a court petition. Questions about what the trustee is charging for the work are answered in [can a trustee be paid?](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation)

## How do digital accounts fit in?

A phone, email and social media accounts can hold financial records, photos and cryptocurrency, and companies have their own rules for access after death. Do not guess passwords or sign into the person's accounts without checking the rules, since that can violate terms of service and, in some cases, the law. Our [digital estate guide](/learn/digital-assets) and the article on [social media and email after death](/learn/digital-assets/social-media-after-death) explain what is possible.

## A short document checklist

Gather these, as far as you can find them, for the attorney visit:

1. Certified death certificates
2. The original will and any trust, codicils or amendments
3. Names and contact details of family, beneficiaries and the executor
4. Recent statements for bank, brokerage and retirement accounts
5. Deeds, mortgage statements and vehicle titles
6. Life insurance policies and pension information
7. The last three years of tax returns
8. A list of debts, credit cards and monthly bills
9. Passwords or access instructions, if known

If anything is missing, that is normal. An attorney can help you find it.

## How do you take care of yourself during all this?

Settling an estate often falls on one person during the same weeks that person is grieving. That combination is hard, and it is reasonable to protect your own energy. A few habits help:

- **Share the work.** Ask one sibling to handle the funeral home, another the mail and the house, and one to keep a notebook of calls. Dividing tasks reduces both effort and resentment.
- **Keep one notebook or folder.** Record each call's date, the person you spoke with and any reference number. You will be glad of it months later.
- **Put off permanent decisions.** Selling the family home, giving away belongings and making large purchases can usually wait until the fog lifts.
- **Say no to pressure.** Funeral providers, salespeople, relatives and collectors may each press for a fast answer. A short pause almost never costs anything.
- **Ask for professional help early.** A brief consultation with an attorney or CPA costs far less than fixing a mistake.

Many families also find that a written update every month or two, sent to everyone who has an interest in the estate, prevents most arguments. People tend to worry when they hear nothing, and a few lines saying what has been done and what comes next keeps trust intact.

## What if the family disagrees?

Disagreements are common, and they are usually about communication, timing or sentimental items, more than money. Start with the document: the will or trust says who gets what, and the executor or trustee must follow it. For keepsakes the document does not cover, some families take turns choosing, or ask a neutral person to help. If a conflict grows, mediation is often faster and kinder than court. If you believe someone has acted improperly, see our article on [removing an executor or trustee](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed). If you believe the will itself is not valid, see [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## When should you call an attorney?

Call one if the person owned real estate, a business or substantial accounts, if there is no will, if family members disagree, or if you are unsure what you are signing. Many first meetings are a single conversation that tells you whether probate is needed and what to do this month. If the estate is small and simple, you may need very little help, but a quick check can still save you from a mistake that is expensive to undo.
