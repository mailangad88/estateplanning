Sometimes an inheritance is not a welcome one. It may be a house with a mortgage and a broken roof, shares in a business you do not want, or money that will push a disabled child over a benefit limit. Or you may simply have enough, and your own children could use it more.

You can say no. The legal word is **disclaimer**, and it is a precise tool with a short fuse. This page explains how it works, the deadline, what happens to the property afterward and the cases where a disclaimer is a mistake. It is one of the decisions covered in our guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## What is a disclaimer?

A disclaimer is a signed, written refusal of an inheritance. The law treats you as though you had died before the person who left you the property. You are out of the picture, and the asset passes to whoever would have received it in your absence.

Three consequences follow, and they trip people up.

1. **It is permanent.** You cannot change your mind.
2. **You do not get to choose the recipient.** If you could, it would be a gift from you, not a refusal.
3. **You get nothing from it.** No tax deduction, no payment and no credit from the family.

The same word covers a few settings: a gift in a will, a trust share, a beneficiary-designation account such as an IRA or life insurance policy, and property that passes by survivorship, such as a joint account. Our [glossary entry on disclaimers](/glossary/disclaimer) gives the short definition.

## When can you disclaim, and what is the deadline?

Federal tax law sets a rule that most attorneys treat as the standard, because breaking it can turn a refusal into a taxable gift. A **qualified disclaimer** has to meet these requirements:

- It is **in writing** and signed.
- It is **delivered within nine months** of the date of death, or, for a minor, within nine months after turning 21.
- You have **not accepted** the property or any of its benefits.
- It is **irrevocable and unqualified**, meaning you cannot attach conditions or direct where it goes.
- The property passes **without your direction** to someone other than you. A surviving spouse who disclaims may still end up as a recipient of the disclaimed share, which is the one exception.

State law adds its own details: who must receive the notice, whether it is filed in court and how fast. Some states have shorter periods, or require recording for real estate. Because the federal and state rules can differ, count the strictest deadline and finish well before it.

The nine months run from the death, not from when the will is read or the estate opens. A probate that drags on does not extend the time.

## What counts as accepting the inheritance?

Acceptance is the quiet trap. If you take any benefit from the property, you have likely lost the right to disclaim it. Examples:

- Living in an inherited house, collecting rent from it, or driving the inherited car.
- Cashing a check or taking a distribution from an inherited account.
- Voting inherited stock or directing how it is sold.
- Signing a document that says you accept.

Merely being named does not count. Caring for a vacant house, such as paying an insurance bill or keeping the heat on to prevent damage, is generally not acceptance, though keep the receipts and be careful about how you describe it. If you think you may want to disclaim, pause before you touch the asset and talk with an attorney.

## Where does the property go after a disclaimer?

It depends on the document and state law, and this is the step that deserves the most thought.

| Situation | Where the disclaimed property usually goes |
|---|---|
| The will names a backup beneficiary | To that backup |
| A beneficiary form names a contingent beneficiary | To the contingent beneficiary |
| The will says descendants of a disclaiming heir take their place | To your children, in equal shares if there are several |
| The will says nothing | Under many state statutes, as though you died first, so to your descendants or to the other residuary beneficiaries |
| No will and you are an heir | To the next heirs under state intestacy rules, often your own children |

Diane, 61, inherits her father's investment account. She has no need for it, and her two adult children are in the early years of a career and a mortgage. She disclaims within five months. Her father's will says that a disclaimed share passes to the disclaiming person's descendants per stirpes, so the account goes to her children. That is the result she wanted. If the will had said that disclaimed shares go to her brother, the same signature would have sent it to him. Always check before signing, and see our article on [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) for how these designations work.

## When does disclaiming make sense?

The usual reasons are practical, not dramatic.

- **Letting it pass to your children** to avoid piling it onto your own taxable estate. Most people are well under the federal exemption of $15 million per person in 2026, so this reason matters mostly to wealthy families.
- **An unwanted burden**, such as an underwater property, a contaminated lot or a business with liabilities.
- **A surviving spouse** who wants to place part of an estate into a trust or send it to children, using the disclaimer as planned flexibility.
- **Family fairness**, when a parent mistakenly left everything to one child.
- **Protecting a benefit**, where a person with disabilities would otherwise lose eligibility, though this requires special care. See the next section.

## When is disclaiming a bad idea?

Three situations come up often enough to name.

**You receive Medicaid or SSI.** Refusing an inheritance you are entitled to can be treated as giving away an asset, which may create a penalty period for long-term care coverage under Medicaid's rules. Our article on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains why. If your benefit is the concern, a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) is often a better answer than refusing.

**You owe debts.** Creditors cannot be dodged by disclaiming. A federal tax lien in particular can prevent an effective disclaimer, and in bankruptcy a trustee can often challenge a disclaimer made to keep assets away from creditors. A state court may do the same. For more on that subject, see [can creditors take what a beneficiary inherits](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits).

**The next person in line is not who you imagine.** An estranged sibling, an ex-spouse who remained a contingent beneficiary, or a stepchild may be next. A disclaimer cannot be undone.

Disclaiming is also not the right tool if you want to share the inheritance after taking it. If you accept it and later give some to a sibling or child, that is a gift. Over the annual exclusion of $19,000 per recipient in 2026, it requires a gift tax return, even though no tax is likely to be due. A disclaimer made correctly avoids that.

## What are the steps to disclaim?

1. **Stop and do not accept anything.** Leave the account or property untouched.
2. **Get the documents.** Obtain the will, trust, beneficiary forms and a recent statement. Our article on [seeing the will](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-see-the-will-after-someone-dies) shows how if the executor is slow.
3. **Identify who gets the property next** by reading the backup provisions.
4. **Have an attorney prepare the disclaimer,** matching your state's form requirements.
5. **Sign and deliver it before the nine-month deadline** to the executor, trustee, plan administrator or whoever holds the asset. Keep proof of delivery.
6. **File or record it** where your state requires, which usually means the probate court, and the county recorder for land.
7. **Tell your tax preparer.** A disclaimed inheritance does not appear on your return, but the estate's accounting should reflect it.

For a house, the dates matter even more because a recorded deed may be needed. See [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) for how title moves.

## Do you owe tax when you disclaim?

If the disclaimer qualifies under the federal rules, you are not treated as having made a gift. The property is treated as passing directly from the person who died to the next recipient. If it does not qualify, you may be treated as having received the property and then given it away, and that can mean a gift tax return and a use of your lifetime exemption. Our article on [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs) covers what heirs owe when they do accept.
