Ruth's father farmed 160 acres in central Illinois for fifty years. He died in October, after harvest but before the rent for next year was set. Ruth lives three states away. Her brother Al farms next door. A neighbor rents the land on a handshake. Nobody has the deed, and the property tax bill arrives in a few months. Ruth is the executor and does not know what to do first.

If you have inherited a farm, you have inherited land, a business and sometimes a home all at once. This page covers what to do when you inherit a farm, in the order that protects the most value. It sits in our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death). If you are planning ahead rather than reacting, see [farm succession planning](/learn/business-owners/farm-succession-planning).

## What should I do first after inheriting a farm?

Work through three questions in the first two weeks: who is using the land, who is paying the bills, and who has authority to act. A short list helps.

1. **Find out who is on the land.** A tenant, a family member, a crop-share partner, a hunting lessee or a pipeline or wind company with an easement. Get the lease or write down what the arrangement is.
2. **Secure the insurance.** Farm property and liability policies often lapse or change at the owner's death. Call the agent and ask what remains in force.
3. **Locate the deed and the estate paperwork.** Until the court appoints an executor, nobody has authority to sign a lease or sell grain. Our page on [how to prove you are an heir](/learn/probate/how-to-prove-you-are-an-heir) and [letters testamentary](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) explain the documents.
4. **Protect the crop and stored grain.** Stored grain and growing crops belong to the estate or the tenant, depending on the lease. Do not sell or move anything until you know which.
5. **Pay the property tax and loans.** Missing either creates liens and penalties that follow the land.
6. **List the equipment, livestock and grain bins.** Value them as of the date of death. Appraisals matter for taxes and for dividing the estate.

## What happens to the farm lease and the rent?

A valid lease generally survives the owner's death and binds the heirs, so the tenant keeps farming and the rent goes to the estate. A handshake lease is still a lease in many places, and a tenant who is already farming may have rights to harvest the current crop. Do not send a termination notice before reading the terms and the state rules.

Notice is the trap. In farming, the period to end a lease before the next crop year can fall months before the year ends. Ask an attorney about the notice dates in your lease, and calendar them. If the lease is oral, put the terms in a short written lease with the tenant. A written cash-rent lease with a clear acreage, a rent amount, payment dates, who pays property tax and who carries insurance protects both sides.

If a family member farms the land rent-free, decide now whether that continues. Siblings often disagree when one farms and the others do not. Our page on [keeping family land in the family](/learn/property-and-assets/keeping-family-land-in-the-family) covers fair rent and who gets what.

## How is inherited farmland taxed?

The inheritance itself is not income, and the land generally takes a new basis equal to its value at death. That "step-up" matters more for farmland than for most assets, because land bought decades ago may have a tiny original cost. Our guide to [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains the rule, and it is also covered for the family business in [taxes when a family business passes to heirs](/learn/business-owners/taxes-when-a-family-business-passes-to-heirs).

Four tax points apply to most inherited farms.

- **Estate tax.** The federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most farms do not owe it. A very large operation might. Farm valuation elections exist for estates that do, as described in [farm valuation and estate tax elections](/learn/estate-tax/estate-tax-elections-alternate-valuation-deferral-and-farm-valuation).
- **Illinois.** Illinois assesses farmland by its soil productivity, not its market value, and the property tax bill follows. Keep the farmland classification by keeping the land in agricultural use. The state has its own estate tax, covered in [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).
- **New Jersey.** The state has an inheritance tax that depends on who inherits, not on the size of the estate alone. See [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax). New Jersey also offers a farmland assessment that lowers the property tax if the land stays in qualifying agricultural use. Selling the land or changing its use can trigger back taxes, so confirm the rules before any change.
- **Selling grain, livestock or equipment.** Income from the farm after death is taxable to the estate or the heir who receives it. Our guide on [the estate income tax return](/learn/estate-tax/does-an-estate-pay-income-tax-form-1041-interest-and-dividends) explains who files.

## What if several heirs inherit the farm together?

This is where family farms are won or lost. When several people inherit one parcel as co-owners, any one of them may be able to ask a court to divide the land or order it sold. A single unhappy heir can end the farm. Our page on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) explains the risk.

The usual options, from simplest to most involved:

| Option | How it works | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| One heir buys out the others | A price is set by appraisal and paid over time or with a loan | One child farms and wants to own |
| Lease to the farming heir, others keep title | All heirs receive rent shares, the farming heir pays a market rate | Land stays in the family, one operator |
| Put the land in an LLC or trust | Shares can be held and passed down; a manager makes decisions | Several heirs want to stay involved |
| Sell all or part | The proceeds are divided | No one wants to farm or hold the land |

Our page on [buying out a sibling or co-owner](/learn/property-and-assets/buying-out-a-sibling-or-co-owner-of-an-inherited-house-or-forcing-a-partition) covers pricing and the court route. A family meeting with the numbers on the table, led by someone neutral, saves more land than any document.

Here is an example with invented numbers. Ruth, Al and a third sibling inherit 120 acres equally. The land is worth $1,200,000, so each share is $400,000. Al wants to keep farming. The siblings agree that Al leases the land at a market rent and has an option to buy each sister's share at appraised value within ten years. Nobody forces a sale and Al keeps his operation.

## What paperwork changes after a farm changes hands?

Several agencies track farm land by owner, and each needs the new name. Update these after the estate has authority:

- The county recorder, with a deed or a court order.
- The property tax assessor, including any farmland classification.
- The local USDA farm service office, which keeps farm records for program payments and conservation contracts.
- The crop insurance agent, for policies and prevented-planting claims.
- Any lender with a mortgage or operating loan, as described in our page on [what happens to a mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).
- Mineral and easement holders. Royalties and leases may have separate rights, covered in [mineral rights and royalties after the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mineral-rights-and-royalties-after-the-owner-dies).

## What mistakes should I avoid?

- Selling the equipment or grain before the court appoints someone to act.
- Ending a lease without checking the notice date.
- Letting insurance lapse because "nobody lives there."
- Leaving the deed in the dead owner's name for years. It makes a sale or loan harder. Our page on [fixing title problems](/learn/property-and-assets/fixing-title-problems-on-inherited-property) shows how to clean it up.
- Dividing the land without a written agreement among heirs.
