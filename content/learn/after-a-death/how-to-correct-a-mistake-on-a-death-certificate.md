The certificate arrives from the funeral home and a name is spelled wrong. Or the birth date is off by a year, the marital status is incorrect, or the cause of death says something the family does not recognize. You will be showing this document to every bank and insurer for months, so a mistake on it can feel like a threat to the whole process.

Most mistakes are fixable, and the fix is a routine filing. The first question is whether the error is one that will cause trouble. This page is part of our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) and walks through who corrects what, how the process works, and what to do while you wait. If you have not ordered copies yet, read [how many death certificates you need](/learn/after-a-death/how-many-death-certificates-do-i-need) first, because you will want to hold back the order until the record is right.

## Which errors actually matter?

An error matters when it makes the certificate fail to match another legal document. Banks and insurers compare the name, date of birth and Social Security number on the certificate with their own records.

| Error | Likely effect | Fix needed? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Misspelled last name | Bank cannot match the account | Yes |
| Wrong Social Security number | Problems with Social Security and the IRS | Yes |
| Wrong date of birth | Insurers and pension plans may refuse | Yes |
| Wrong middle initial or a missing suffix such as Jr. | Sometimes accepted with an explanation | Maybe |
| Wrong marital status or spouse name | Can slow survivor benefits | Yes |
| Wrong parent names or birthplace | Rarely affects money | Usually optional |
| Cause or manner of death | Can affect insurance and benefits | Depends |
| Wrong address or occupation | Almost never matters | Rarely |

If a document with the error is going to be used once, such as a utility account that is closing, you may not bother. If it will be shown to a bank, the court and an insurer, correct it.

## Who supplies what on a death certificate?

There are two parts, and different people are responsible for each. The personal and demographic section is filled in by the funeral director, using what a family member tells them. That is the name, birth date, parents' names, education, marital status and Social Security number. The medical section, with the cause and manner of death, is completed by the doctor, coroner or medical examiner.

This is why the fix depends on the error. A family mistake or a funeral home typo is corrected through the vital records process. A medical error, or a cause of death the family disputes, goes back to the person who signed it.

Rosa's father died in a hospital and the funeral director took the details from her over the phone. The certificate showed her father's surname with one letter missing, a spelling she had not noticed on the draft. She caught it three weeks later when the bank rejected the certificate. Because the director had the file, he filed the correction in a week. If she had waited a year, she would have dealt with the state office directly.

## How do you correct a personal detail?

Start with the funeral director, then go to the state if needed. The steps in order:

1. **Compare the certificate with the person's driver's license, passport, birth certificate or Social Security card.** Mark each difference.
2. **Call the funeral home.** Ask them to review the record they filed. While the record is recent they can often fix it in the electronic system.
3. **If the funeral home cannot, ask the vital records office** (the state health department or county clerk where the death occurred) for the amendment or affidavit-of-correction form.
4. **Complete the form** with the correct fact, and attach proof such as a birth certificate, a passport or an old tax record. Many offices require a notarized signature.
5. **Pay the fee** and ask whether it covers new certified copies.
6. **Wait for the amended record.** The office issues certified copies that show the correction.
7. **Order replacements.** Copies you already hold stay wrong, so send replacements to anyone who has the old version.

Keep a copy of every document you send. If the person died in a different state or country from where they lived, the record is held where the death occurred, so apply there. Our page on [deaths in hospitals, hospice and out-of-state](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-in-a-hospital-hospice-nursing-home-or-out-of-state) explains which office to contact.

## How do you challenge the cause of death?

Only the certifier can change the medical section, so you begin with a conversation. Call the physician, hospice, medical examiner or coroner and ask what the finding was based on. Many disagreements come from words rather than facts. "Cardiac arrest" may be written when the family was told "stroke," or a terminal diagnosis may be listed as the cause when a fall was the trigger.

If the certifier agrees to change it, they file a supplemental or amended medical certification, and the registrar updates the record. If they decline and you believe the finding is wrong, you can ask for a review or involve a lawyer. In some states a court can order a change, though this is uncommon and slow.

Think about why you want the change. The cause can matter for life insurance, accidental death benefits and some veterans' claims, and these are worth a call to the plan before spending time on an amendment. Our page on [claiming life insurance, pension and VA survivor benefits](/learn/after-a-death/claiming-life-insurance-pension-and-va-survivor-benefits) shows which claims ask for the certificate and what else may help. When emotion drives the request, such as a family that wants a more accurate description, the certifier may be willing to talk through the wording even if it cannot change it.

## Can an executor fix it, and does it have to be you?

Anyone close to the person can start the process, but the executor has the clearest standing. Vital records offices ask for proof that you are the spouse, a child, a parent or the legal representative. If a court has appointed an executor, a copy of the letters usually settles the question. If not, a birth certificate showing the relationship works for a child or parent. A friend or a distant relative may be turned away.

When several relatives disagree about a fact, such as a legal name or a place of birth, the office will go by documents, not memory. Gather the oldest official record you can find. A certified birth record outranks a driver's license, and a passport outranks a utility bill. Tell the family what you filed so that nobody files a competing request. The [executor's role](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) is partly about keeping this kind of record straight.

## What do you do while you wait?

Do the work that does not depend on the certificate. Notifications that need only a phone call can go ahead. Our checklist for [who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) separates the calls that need a certificate from the ones that do not. Social Security is normally told by the funeral home, and the agency takes the Social Security number from there, so a wrong number needs prompt attention; see [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death).

If a probate filing cannot wait, tell the court clerk. Courts see corrected certificates often, and some will accept the original with a short affidavit explaining the error and attaching proof of the right fact. A lawyer drafting [the probate petition](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) can give you the practical rule for your county.

If you have already sent the certificate to several places, keep a list of where. When the amended record arrives, send each recipient the new copy with a short note.
