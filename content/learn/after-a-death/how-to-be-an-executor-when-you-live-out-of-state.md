Priya lives in Seattle. Her mother died in Joliet, Illinois, and named Priya executor in a will signed six years ago. The house is still full of furniture. The mail is piling up. A neighbor has the key. Priya has a full-time job, two children and no plan for how to run an Illinois estate from the other side of the country.

An executor can live out of state, and many do. This page explains how to be an executor when you live out of state: what the courts require, which jobs you can do from home, which you cannot, and how to keep trips and costs down. For the job in general, see [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties), and for the rest of the topic start at our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## Can an executor live in a different state?

Yes. Neither Illinois nor New Jersey requires the executor to live in the state. What changes is the paperwork.

| | Illinois | New Jersey |
|---|---|---|
| May a nonresident serve? | Yes, if a U.S. resident and otherwise qualified | Yes |
| Bond | The court may require one for a nonresident, even if the will says none is needed | Required for a nonresident unless the will says no security is needed |
| Legal papers | Ask the clerk what the court needs for service on a nonresident | A nonresident files a power of attorney naming the Surrogate to receive legal papers |
| State pages | [Who can be an executor in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-can-be-an-executor-in-illinois) | [Who can be an executor in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-can-be-an-executor-in-new-jersey) |

A bond is an insurance policy that protects the estate if the executor misuses money. The premium comes out of the estate. Our page on the [Illinois surety bond](/learn/illinois/illinois-letters-of-administration-and-the-surety-bond) shows how the amount is set.

## What can I handle from home?

Most of the paper. Almost every institution now accepts a certified letter of authority by mail, email or upload. The table sorts the work.

| From home, by mail, phone or online | Needs a person on site |
|---|---|
| Order death certificates by mail | Securing the house and changing locks |
| Notify Social Security, banks, insurers and credit bureaus | Walking through the house with a lawyer or appraiser |
| Hire the probate attorney | Sorting and removing belongings |
| Apply for the estate EIN and open the estate account | Meeting buyers, contractors and a realtor |
| Pay bills and file the tax returns | Funeral arrangements and the first week |
| Cancel subscriptions, memberships and utilities | Collecting mail not forwarded |

The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) helps you see which items need the court.

## Who should I hire where the estate is opened?

A local probate attorney, licensed in the state where the case is filed. Your own lawyer at home cannot appear in that court unless they are admitted there. A local attorney also knows how the clerk or Surrogate handles nonresident executors, which forms they want, and whether a short affidavit will do instead of a hearing. Our page on [hiring help and staying protected](/learn/after-a-death/executor-hiring-help-and-staying-protected) explains what to ask.

Other local people to line up in the first month:

- **A realtor or house watcher.** Someone who visits weekly and reports what they see.
- **A cleaning or hauling service** when you are ready to clear the house.
- **An accountant** if the estate will file income or estate returns.
- **A neighbor or relative** with a key, who is paid for specific tasks, not left on goodwill.

If you are a co-executor with a family member who lives nearby, split the jobs in writing: the local person handles the house, you handle money and the court. The court still holds both of you responsible.

## What should I do about the house?

The house is the biggest risk for an executor who lives far away. An empty home with unpaid utilities can freeze a pipe, grow mold or draw trespassers within weeks. Do these things in the first days.

1. **Call the insurance agent** and say the owner has died. Many policies have limits for vacant homes, so ask what is covered and what must change.
2. **Keep the utilities on.** Set heat or cooling to a safe level. Move billing to you as executor.
3. **Forward the mail** to your address with the post office, using your authority as executor.
4. **Change or add locks** after listing who has keys.
5. **Take photographs** of every room before anyone removes anything.
6. **Schedule weekly checks** and ask for a short written note each time.

Our guide on [vacant inherited houses](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters) goes further on insurance, repairs and squatters. When you are ready to clear it, see [clearing out a parent's house](/learn/after-a-death/clearing-out-a-parents-house-after-death).

## How do I plan the trips?

Aim for two, and make each one count. Book the first trip once the court has appointed you, so you have authority to act when you arrive.

**Trip one: a few days, early.**

1. Meet the attorney and sign what is needed.
2. Walk the house with a camera and a list.
3. Find the original will, the deed, bank statements, insurance and tax papers.
4. Set up the weekly check and the mail forwarding.
5. Take only items of real value or sentiment that the will allows, and record what you took.

**Trip two: later, to clear and sell.**

1. Hold the family walkthrough so each heir picks items before anything leaves.
2. Schedule the hauler, the estate sale or the donation pickup.
3. Meet the realtor and sign the listing.
4. Attend the closing, or sign the deed before a notary near home.

Between trips, hold a call every few weeks with the attorney and the heirs so no one feels left out.

## What about the money for travel and help?

Reasonable, documented costs of settling the estate are generally paid by the estate. That can include the attorney, the house sitter, the hauler and your flights for estate business. Keep a log with date, amount and purpose, and keep receipts. Our page on [who pays the executor's expenses](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-the-executors-expenses) explains the line between estate costs and personal ones. Executors may also be paid a fee, which is taxable income to you.

## What if the dead person owned property in another state?

Then you may need a second case there. If your mother's house is in Illinois but she also held a lake cabin in Wisconsin, the cabin may need its own proceeding. See [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) for how that works. It is one more reason to hire local counsel early.

## What mistakes do distant executors make?

- Waiting months to visit while the house sits unchecked.
- Letting a helpful relative sign or sell without authority.
- Paying the helpers in cash with no record.
- Skipping the bond question until the day of the hearing.
- Doing the estate in the wrong county.
