Taxes do not stop when a person dies. Someone has to file the last personal return, and sometimes the estate and a trust have returns of their own. This is one of the executor's less welcome duties, but the filing schedule is manageable and most estates need only one or two returns.

This article lists each return, who files it and when. It belongs with our guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death). It is general information, and a CPA or attorney should confirm the details for a specific estate.

## Which tax returns might be needed?

There can be up to four returns, and most families file only the first two.

| Return | Who it covers | Who must file | Typical due date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form 1040 (final) | The person, January 1 to date of death | Executor, or surviving spouse | April of the following year |
| Form 1040 for earlier years | Any prior year not yet filed | Executor | As soon as possible |
| Form 1041 | The estate or a trust, for income after death | Executor or trustee | Fiscal or calendar year end plus 3.5 months |
| Form 706 | Federal estate tax | Executor, if estate is above the exclusion or electing portability | 9 months after death, with extensions |

State returns may also be required, including state income tax returns and, in some states, estate or inheritance tax returns.

## What is the final Form 1040?

The final Form 1040 reports all income from the first of the year through the date of death. Income received after death belongs to the estate, not the final return. Use the person's regular filing status, and note "deceased" with the date of death at the top.

A surviving spouse can generally file a joint return for the year of death, which usually gives the best tax result. If the spouse later remarries in the same year, the joint return generally cannot be used, and a surviving spouse with a dependent child may be able to use special filing status for the following two years.

Include income from wages, retirement distributions, interest, dividends, Social Security, the sale of assets and final paychecks. Deductions, such as unreimbursed medical expenses paid within one year of death, can sometimes be claimed on the final return. A tax professional can help decide which year is better for a medical deduction.

Refunds belong to the estate. If you are a court-appointed executor, attach a copy of the court certificate. If you are a surviving spouse filing jointly, you can claim the refund without extra forms. Anyone else claiming a refund usually files Form 1310.

## When does the estate file Form 1041?

The estate files Form 1041 whenever it has gross income of $600 or more in a tax year. Income includes interest on estate bank accounts, dividends, capital gains from sale of assets, rental income and, in some cases, retirement distributions paid to the estate.

The estate is its own taxpayer. You need to apply for an EIN, covered in our [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties), and you may choose a calendar year or a fiscal year ending on the last day of any month within 12 months of death. A fiscal year can defer the tax on estate income and simplify the work. Once the estate pays income to beneficiaries, it usually reports the amount on Schedule K-1 forms, so that the beneficiaries pay the tax at their own rates instead. This is why a beneficiary may receive a K-1 months after the death.

Form 56 notifies the IRS that you are acting as fiduciary. Filing it ensures notices go to you.

## Does a living trust file its own return?

Yes, once the trust becomes irrevocable at death, it generally needs its own EIN and files Form 1041 if it has income above the threshold. A trust can often elect to be treated as part of the estate for a period, which allows a shared fiscal year and a single filing. Trustees should read our guide to [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) and ask a CPA whether this election helps.

## When is a federal estate tax return required?

Form 706 is required only if the gross estate plus certain lifetime gifts exceeds the basic exclusion amount, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. It is due nine months after death, and a six-month extension is available. Any estate tax is due at the original deadline even if the return is extended.

Many smaller estates still file Form 706 for another reason. When one spouse dies, the surviving spouse can claim the deceased spouse's unused exclusion by electing [portability](/glossary/portability) on a timely return. For a couple with $6 million of assets, this may seem pointless today, but rising values and future law changes can make it valuable. The IRS currently offers a simplified late election for certain estates that were not required to file, with a deadline of several years after death. Ask a professional for the current rule. Our article on [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples) explains the idea, and the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) article covers the thresholds.

## What if the person did not file taxes in prior years?

The executor is responsible for filing past-due returns, so look through the person's records for the last several years. Missing returns, unpaid balances and IRS notices all become the executor's concern. A professional can advise how many years to file and whether to request transcripts from the IRS. Unpaid taxes are debts of the estate, covered in [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate), and they are paid before heirs receive anything.

## A sample timeline

Say Robert died on March 10, 2026. His executor, Jane, would likely:

1. Apply for an EIN and file Form 56 within the first couple of months.
2. Choose a fiscal year for the estate that ends the last day of a convenient month.
3. Collect Robert's 2026 tax documents by early 2027 and file his final Form 1040 by April 15, 2027, with his surviving spouse, if any, filing jointly.
4. File the estate's Form 1041 by the due date for its tax year, and send K-1s to beneficiaries.
5. File a Form 706 within nine months only if required or if electing portability.
6. Request an IRS closing letter or confirmation before final distributions, where appropriate.

Heirs, in turn, may have their own tax questions, which we cover in [taxes on an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## Who pays the tax, and from what?

Taxes owed on the final return, the estate return and any estate tax are paid from estate assets, not from the pocket of the executor or heirs. This is one reason an executor should wait to make final distributions until the returns are filed and any balance is paid. If the executor distributes everything first and a tax bill later arrives, the executor can be personally responsible for the shortfall. Many executors keep a reserve until the IRS accepts the final estate return, and ask for written confirmation where it is available.
