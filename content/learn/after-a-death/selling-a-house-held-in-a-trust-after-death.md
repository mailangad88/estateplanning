Nadia's mother put her house into a living trust ten years ago and named Nadia as successor trustee. Her mother has now died. Nadia and her brother Oleg agree that neither can afford to keep the house, and a neighbor has already asked if it is for sale. Nadia has one question: can she just list it?

Often she can, and faster than a house that has to go through probate. But there is a sequence to follow, and skipping steps is how a simple sale turns into a closing delay or a complaint from a sibling. This article is part of our guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death) and covers the process from the trustee's side.

## Who has the power to sell a house in a trust?

The trustee. Once the owner dies, the successor trustee holds legal title. A trust usually lists a power to sell real estate among the trustee's powers, and state law fills in the gap if it does not. The beneficiaries have a right to the proceeds under the trust, not a right to sign the deed.

This is why a trust-held house can be sold more quickly than one that sits in [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate). No court has to appoint anyone, and no judge has to approve the price in the usual case. The trade-off is that nobody is looking over the trustee's shoulder, so the trustee's own paperwork has to do that job.

First, confirm the house really is in the trust. Look at the recorded deed. A house that the owner meant to put in the trust but never retitled may still be in their personal name, and then it needs probate or another process. Our article on [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) shows what a correct deed looks like.

## What are the steps to sell it?

Here is the usual order. Local practice varies, but the sequence rarely does.

1. **Confirm your authority.** Read the trust for the trustee's powers and any limits, such as a requirement to offer the house to a particular child.
2. **Get certified death certificates and a certification of trust.** The certification is a short document, signed by the trustee, that confirms the trust exists, who the trustee is and what powers they hold. It lets you avoid handing the whole trust to strangers.
3. **Secure and insure the house.** Tell the insurer about the death and ask what a vacant home requires.
4. **Order a date-of-death appraisal.** A licensed appraiser sets the value as of the date the owner died. This number becomes the tax basis and is your best evidence of fair value.
5. **Decide what to do with the contents.** Photograph rooms, keep family items aside and document what is sold or donated.
6. **List with an agent, or sell by owner.** Interview agents who have handled trust sales. Choose on local knowledge and fee terms.
7. **Accept an offer and open escrow.** Give the title company the certification of trust and a death certificate.
8. **Sign the trustee's deed at closing.** You sign as trustee, not as an individual.
9. **Deposit the proceeds in the trust account.** Never into a personal account.
10. **Keep the closing statement and appraisal** in the trust file.

The mistake we see most often is skipping step 4 because "we all know what it's worth." That works until one beneficiary suspects the house went cheap.

## What paperwork does the title company want?

Title companies insure the buyer against a bad deed, so they check carefully that the seller has authority. Expect requests for:

| Document | What it proves |
|---|---|
| Certification of trust | The trust exists, its date and the trustee's powers. |
| Certified death certificate | The prior trustee or grantor is dead. |
| Trustee's identity and acceptance | The successor named is the person signing. |
| Recorded deed into the trust | The trust really holds title. |
| Any amendments | Nothing changed who may sell. |
| Trust EIN or tax information | The seller's tax reporting identity. |

If the trust has two co-trustees, both usually sign. If it names someone who has not accepted, a short written acceptance may be needed. A title officer who asks for something strange is usually looking for the same proof of authority, just in a different form.

The trust's tax ID matters at closing, because the sale proceeds belong to the trust. Our guide to the [trust EIN and 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election) shows how to get it.

## What are the tax consequences of the sale?

Assets in a revocable trust generally receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) to their fair market value at death. If the trustee sells for roughly the appraised value, the taxable gain is small or nil. If the market climbs during a long delay, any increase above the date-of-death value is a gain that the trust or the beneficiaries report. That is the reason to sell within a reasonable time, and to hold the appraisal.

Two cautions. The step-up usually applies to a revocable trust's property because it is included in the grantor's estate. It does not apply to an irrevocable trust set up and funded during life that is outside the estate. Check which kind you have. And income tax on the gain lands where the trust's income lands: on the trust's return if the trust keeps the money, or on the beneficiaries' returns if it is passed out. Our article on [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) covers the tax side in more detail, including the home-sale exclusion rules for heirs, and [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) explains the filings.

## Can the house go to a beneficiary instead of being sold?

Yes, and it is common. The trust may say the house goes to a named child. Or the siblings agree that one will keep it and buy out the others. In either case the trustee signs a deed out of the trust.

A buyout works best with an appraisal, a written agreement and a clear plan for how the buying sibling pays. If Oleg wants the house and Nadia wants cash, Oleg might refinance in his own name and pay Nadia her share, or the trustee might distribute the house to Oleg and a cash equivalent to Nadia from other assets. Equalizing the shares is a trustee's job, not a favor.

For more on a deed out of the trust, see [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house). Many counties also require a form for transfer tax or property reassessment, and some states give a break for transfers between parents and children. Ask the county recorder or an attorney before recording.

## What mistakes should a trustee avoid?

- **Selling before confirming the trust's terms.** A trust might require the house to be offered to a specific person first.
- **Pricing by guesswork.** A below-market sale invites claims. Use an appraisal and a listing agent.
- **Buying it yourself without approval.** That is a conflict of interest.
- **Letting it sit.** A vacant house loses value, can lose coverage and can cost thousands in carrying costs. See our guide on [what a trustee owes beneficiaries when it does nothing](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-trustee-does-nothing).
- **Distributing sale proceeds immediately.** Hold a reserve for bills and taxes, as explained in [how long a trustee has to distribute assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-long-does-a-trustee-have-to-distribute-assets).
- **Ignoring out-of-state rules.** A house in another state may involve that state's recording and tax rules. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

## What if a beneficiary lives in the house?

It happens often. A sibling stays in the house after the parent dies, sometimes for years of caregiving. The trustee needs to decide whether that person pays rent or expenses, how long they may stay and what happens if they refuse to leave. Put any arrangement in writing. If a resident beneficiary will not cooperate with a sale, the trustee may need a court order or an eviction process, and delay can become a breach of the duty to treat beneficiaries fairly. The details depend on state law and on what the trust says.
