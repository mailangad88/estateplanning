Three words keep showing up when someone dies: executor, administrator and personal representative. A bank teller says one, a court form says another, and a cousin says a third. People reasonably assume they are three different jobs, and then worry they have the wrong person or the wrong paperwork.

They are not three different jobs. They are three labels for one role, and the label depends on how the person ended up with it. This article, part of our guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death), sorts out which word applies when, what changes in practice, and what does not change at all.

## What is the difference in one sentence each?

An **[executor](/glossary/executor)** is the person a will names to carry out the will. An **[administrator](/glossary/administrator)** is a person a court appoints to settle an estate when no one qualified is named in a will. A **[personal representative](/glossary/personal-representative)** is the legal catch-all that includes both.

Think of "vehicle" for a moment. A pickup and a sedan are both vehicles, and which one you drive depends on what you bought. In the same way, a will-named executor and a court-appointed administrator are both personal representatives, and which you are depends on whether a valid will named you.

| Title | How the person gets the job | Usual situation |
|---|---|---|
| Executor | Named in the will, then confirmed by the court | There is a valid will with a willing executor |
| Administrator | Appointed by the court, ranked by state law | No will, or the named executor cannot serve |
| Administrator with the will annexed | Appointed by the court to carry out an existing will | There is a will, but the executor died, declined or was disqualified |
| Special administrator | Appointed for a narrow, temporary purpose | A dispute or delay leaves assets unprotected |
| Personal representative | The umbrella label used on many states' court forms | Any of the above |

Some states use "executor" and "administrator" on their forms. Others, including many that follow the Uniform Probate Code, call everyone a personal representative and drop the older words. A few older terms survive in places, such as "executrix" for a woman executor. The legal effect is the same.

## Does the title change what the person can do?

Mostly no. All of them have to find and protect assets, give notice to heirs and creditors, pay valid debts and taxes, and distribute what is left. Our guide to [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) walks through that work in order, and it applies to administrators too.

The differences are small but real.

- **The will's instructions.** An executor can follow special instructions in the will, such as permission to sell real estate without a court hearing. An administrator with no will has only what state law grants.
- **Bond.** Wills often waive the requirement that the executor post a bond, which is an insurance policy protecting the estate. An administrator frequently has to buy one, and the premium comes out of the estate.
- **Supervision.** Some courts watch an administrator more closely, requiring approval for steps that a trusted executor could take alone. That varies by state and by the size of the estate.
- **Who decides who serves.** The testator chose the executor. For an administrator, a judge chooses, using the statute's priority list.

## Who becomes administrator when there is no will?

State law ranks the candidates. The usual order, with variations, is the surviving spouse, then adult children, then grandchildren, parents, siblings and more distant relatives. If nobody in line wants the job or is suitable, a creditor or a public administrator can be appointed.

Several people can be at the same rank. Three adult children, for example, are equally entitled, and they must agree on who applies or ask the court to choose. This is one of the places where an estate gets stuck for months. If your family is in that spot, our article on [what happens if there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) covers how the court breaks a tie and what happens while nobody is appointed.

An administrator also must give notice to others in the same priority group, so they have a chance to object. If you are one of three siblings and the oldest files alone, you will probably get a notice, and you can agree, object or step aside.

## What are letters, and why does everyone ask for them?

Whatever your title, the document that proves your authority is issued by the court. Executors typically receive [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary). Administrators typically receive letters of administration. In some states both receive "letters of authority" or a certificate of appointment.

A will by itself proves nothing to a bank. Marcus can be named executor in his mother's will and still be turned away at the credit union until the court has issued letters. The same applies to an administrator: a court order of appointment is the key. Banks, brokerages, title companies and motor vehicle offices all ask for a recent certified copy, often dated within 60 or 90 days, so order several.

## A worked example

Elena dies with a will naming her friend Tomas as executor. Tomas turned down the role last year after a health scare, and the will names no backup. Her two daughters, Rosa and Inés, want to handle the estate together.

Because there is a will but no executor able to serve, the court will appoint an administrator with the will annexed. The will still decides who gets what. The daughters, as the closest relatives, have priority to serve. They can ask to be appointed together, or one can serve and the other can sign a consent. The court may require a bond unless both consent to waive it, and the judge issues letters naming them.

Contrast that with Dennis, who dies with no will at all. Rosa's cousin, a creditor and a neighbor all have an interest, but the statute puts Dennis's surviving wife first. She petitions, the court appoints her as administrator, and the estate passes to heirs under state law. It is the same work with a different path to the job. ## What if you were named executor and do not want the job?

You can decline. Naming someone in a will does not bind them. Most states let the named person file a short declination with the court, or simply never petition. Once you have started acting, however, you generally need court permission to resign, and you remain responsible for your actions until a successor takes over.

If you decline, the court looks to the next person named in the will, and if there is none, it appoints an administrator with the will annexed. That is a common, ordinary result, not a failure. People often decline because they live far away, because they have a conflict with the family, or because the estate is complicated. If you are planning your own estate and are choosing, our article on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) shows how to pick well and name a backup.

## Common mistakes with these titles

- **Signing as executor before being appointed.** Until the court issues letters, you generally have no authority to sign for the estate. You can secure property and pay urgent bills from your own pocket for reimbursement later, but do not sell or transfer anything.
- **Assuming an out-of-state appointment works everywhere.** A personal representative appointed in one state usually has no power over real estate in another. That often requires a second proceeding, which our article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) describes.
- **Assuming a trustee is a personal representative.** A trustee runs a trust, not a probate estate. Many families have both jobs, sometimes filled by one person, sometimes by two. The trustee's work is described in [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).
- **Using the wrong title on tax forms.** The IRS asks for the fiduciary's capacity, and the estate needs its own tax ID. Wrong labels create mismatched records. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## Is the title different if a trust avoids probate?

Yes, and this surprises people. If the person put everything in a [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), there may be no probate estate to administer. The successor trustee, not an executor, does the work, and nobody needs a court to appoint them. A will may still exist, usually a pour-over will, naming an executor for any stray assets. For what happens inside the trust, see [what happens to a living trust when you die](/learn/trusts/what-happens-to-a-living-trust-when-you-die).
