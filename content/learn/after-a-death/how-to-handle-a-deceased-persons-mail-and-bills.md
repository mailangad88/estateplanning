Two weeks after Dolores died in Evanston, her daughter Janet had a shoebox of mail: eleven envelopes from credit card companies, a hospital statement, a property tax bill, a magazine renewal, three charity appeals and a letter from a law office she did not recognize. Janet did not know which to open, which to pay and which to throw out.

This page, part of our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death), shows how to sort a deceased person's mail, how to redirect it, who pays which bills, and in what order. It covers the practical middle weeks, after the funeral and before the estate is settled. If you are still in the first days, start with our [what to do when someone dies checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

## What should I do with a deceased person's mail first?

Gather it in one place and keep it, all of it, for the first month. The mail tells you what the person owned and owed. A statement from a brokerage you never heard of is an asset. A bill from a medical lab is a debt. A tax notice is a deadline. Our page on [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) uses the mail as its first source for this reason.

Here is how Janet's shoebox sorts out.

| Type of mail | What it usually means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credit card statements | Debt, owed by the estate | Log the balance, do not pay yet |
| Bank, brokerage, retirement statements | Assets | File, and note account numbers for the executor |
| Medical statements and explanations of benefits | Debt, possibly insured | Wait for the insurer's explanation before paying |
| Property tax and utility bills for a house | Costs of keeping the asset | Pay if the house is occupied or being sold |
| Government mail: IRS, state tax, Social Security | Deadlines | Read the same week and call the sender |
| Mortgage statement | Secured debt | Keep paying, see [mortgage when the owner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) |
| Charities, catalogs, offers | Junk | Recycle or opt out |
| Insurance renewals | May lapse | Check coverage on the house and cars |

Do not toss anything that looks like a bill until you know what it is. Collectors send official-looking mailings, and so do legitimate creditors.

## Is it legal to open the mail?

The person who will serve as executor can generally open it to administer the estate, and families do this every day before any court appointment. Keep the originals together and unaltered, and open only what is addressed to the person who died. If someone else shares the address, their mail is theirs. Avoid opening mail on a joint account with a surviving spouse without asking.

## How do I forward a deceased person's mail?

A post office will usually ask for proof that you are the one to act, which means the court's letters naming the executor or administrator (see [letters testamentary and letters of administration](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration)), a death certificate and your own identification. Ask your local post office what it requires, because practice varies by location. Until the court acts, many families ask the postal carrier to hold the mail or have it delivered to the house while a relative picks it up.

If the person lived alone and the house is vacant, forwarding or collecting matters more. A mailbox that fills becomes a signal that nobody is home, and it can attract thieves. Our page on [vacant inherited houses](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters) covers other risks.

## How do I stop the junk and protect against identity theft?

Take four steps in the first month:

1. Ask the three credit bureaus to place a deceased flag on the file and send each a copy of the death certificate. This stops new credit from being opened in the person's name.
2. Register the person on the Association of National Advertisers' deceased do-not-contact list for national mailers.
3. Mark junk mail "deceased, return to sender" and call recurring charities.
4. Cancel catalogs and subscriptions with a note. See [ending a deceased person's lease, utilities and insurance](/learn/after-a-death/ending-a-deceased-persons-lease-utilities-phone-and-insurance) and [who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies).

Watch for scams that arrive by mail or phone. A letter demanding immediate payment, a caller claiming the person owed a debt to a lottery or a "prize", or a bill from a funeral vendor you did not hire should be checked before anything is paid.

## Who pays the bills, and from what?

The person who signed a contract owes it. A child who did not co-sign the card does not owe the card. The bills of someone who has died are paid from the estate, not from relatives, and if the estate runs short, some creditors go unpaid. A collector may speak to the executor, but may not tell a child they must pay. Our page on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains the order of payment, and [credit card debt after death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/credit-card-debt-after-death-in-illinois) covers the Illinois details.

Practically, this means:

- **Before the executor has authority:** do not pay from your own funds except the funeral, which the estate generally repays. Keep receipts. See [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral).
- **After letters are issued:** open an estate account with the estate's tax ID and pay everything from there. Mixing estate money with your own is the mistake that creates liability. The [IRS paperwork page](/learn/after-a-death/irs-paperwork-for-an-executor-form-56-estate-ein-tax-transcripts-and-form-4810) explains the account and tax ID.

## In what order should bills be paid?

Each state ranks claims by statute, so treat this as a general order and check the exact ranking before paying from a small estate:

1. **Funeral and burial, then estate administration costs.** Both rank at or near the top.
2. **Secured debts the family wants to keep paying:** the mortgage, a car loan.
3. **Taxes owed to the federal and state government.**
4. **Last-illness medical bills and the final bills for care.**
5. **Everything else:** credit cards, personal loans, store accounts.

The key rule is not to pay lower categories while higher ones are unpaid and the estate might be short. Illinois ranks claims in classes (755 ILCS 5/18-10), and New Jersey has its own order. See [how creditor claims work in an Illinois estate](/learn/illinois/how-creditor-claims-work-in-an-illinois-estate) and [creditor claims in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/creditor-claims-and-paying-debts-of-a-new-jersey-estate).

## What about autopay and recurring charges?

Turn off autopay on things the estate does not need: streaming services, gym memberships, subscriptions, a phone line nobody uses. Keep paying homeowner's insurance, property tax, heating and water on a house the estate still owns, because a lapsed policy can leave a vacant house uninsured. Check the checking account for automatic deductions before you freeze it. A missing check can cause a late fee that is hard to reverse.

If a bank account shows deposits after the death, such as pension or Social Security payments, they may need to be returned. Our page on [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) shows which ones.

## What records should the executor keep?

A simple ledger works: date received, who sent it, amount, whether paid, from which account, and check number. Keep each statement and each reply to a collector. When the estate closes, the ledger becomes part of the accounting. A tidy ledger also answers a sibling's question six months later without an argument. The [executor workload tool](/tools/executor-workload) can help estimate how much of this work you are facing.

## How state law changes this

Creditor notice periods, the order of payment, and what an executor may do before court appointment differ by state. Illinois executors publish a notice to creditors and set a claims deadline, and New Jersey has its own process and time limits. Your state guide, [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) or [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey), has the local steps.
