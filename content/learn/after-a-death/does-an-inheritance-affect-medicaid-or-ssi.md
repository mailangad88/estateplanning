Imagine you get a call that an aunt left you $40,000. You are on SSI, or your Medicaid covers a home health aide, and your first reaction is not joy but a knot in the stomach. Will the money end the only coverage you have?

It can. It does not have to. This page explains how SSI, Medicaid, Medicare and Social Security count an inheritance, what happens in the month it arrives, and the steps people take to keep their benefits. If you are mostly here for the practical steps after a death, the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) covers the rest of the process.

## Which benefits does an inheritance affect?

Only programs that look at your income and assets, called means-tested programs. The rest do not care.

| Program | Does an inheritance affect it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| SSI | Yes | Income and resources are tested |
| Medicaid (aged, blind, disabled) | Often | Asset limit applies, usually low |
| Medicaid for adults under expansion rules | Often not | Income based, and rules vary by state |
| SNAP and housing help | Often | Resource or income tests, varying by program and state |
| Social Security retirement | No | Based on work record |
| SSDI | No | Based on work record |
| Medicare | No | Not means tested, though higher income can raise some premiums |

State programs differ, and some states apply their own rules to Medicaid. Treat the table as a map, not a ruling on your case.

## How does SSI count an inheritance?

SSI has two clocks. An inheritance counts as **income** in the month you receive it, which can reduce or wipe out that month's check. If you still have any of it the following month, it becomes a **resource**. In 2026 a single person can hold $2,000 in countable resources, and a couple $3,000. Your home and one car usually do not count.

So a $40,000 inheritance sitting in a checking account makes you ineligible from the next month on. You stay off SSI until your resources drop below the limit, and then you reapply or ask for reinstatement. For people on SSI, Medicaid follows SSI in many states, so losing one can mean losing the other.

You must report the inheritance, usually within ten days after the end of the month in which it arrived. Do it in writing and keep a copy. A missed report becomes an overpayment, and the agency collects it from future benefits. If you suspect an overpayment, ask for help before the letter arrives.

## How does Medicaid count an inheritance?

It depends on the type of Medicaid, and it depends on your state. Long-term care Medicaid and Medicaid for people who are aged, blind or disabled have asset limits, often in the low thousands of dollars for one person. A new inheritance can push you over. Medicaid for adults under income-based rules counts income rather than assets, and an inheritance is often not counted as income, although a state may treat the money differently in later months.

Medicaid also looks back. If you give away or sell an asset for less than it is worth, you can face a penalty period for long-term care coverage. Most states look back 60 months. Our page on the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) explains why gifts, and disclaimers, can cause trouble.

Do not confuse these rules with Medicaid **estate recovery**. Recovery is the state billing the estate of someone who received long-term care. It is not a rule about your own benefits. Our guide to [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) covers that side.

## What can you do when an inheritance is coming?

Act before the money lands in your account. Once it is in your name, your options shrink. Rank the steps by speed.

1. **Tell the executor or trustee about your benefits.** Ask them to hold the distribution until you have a plan. A short delay is common and usually harmless.
2. **Call a benefits counselor or an elder law attorney.** The rules differ for SSI, state Medicaid and housing programs, and a mistake is expensive.
3. **Consider a special needs trust.** If you are younger than 65 and disabled, a first-party trust, sometimes called a (d)(4) trust, can receive the money, though it must repay Medicaid for care at your death. A trust that someone else created in a will is a third-party trust and has no payback.
4. **Consider an ABLE account.** You can put money into an ABLE account up to a yearly limit tied to the annual gift tax exclusion, which is $19,000 in 2026. The age-of-onset rule has been widening, so check whether you qualify. Our guide to [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts) has the details.
5. **Spend on yourself, for fair value.** Paying off a mortgage, fixing the roof, replacing a car, buying hearing aids or prepaying funeral costs are examples. Keep receipts.
6. **Do not give the money away or refuse it without advice.** Gifts and disclaimers can be treated as transfers that trigger a penalty.

Our [special needs trust and SSI rules](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust-ssi-and-medicaid-rules) guide shows how a trust works with the benefits.

## Should you disclaim the inheritance?

Almost never. A disclaimer is a legal refusal, and it passes the property to the next person in line as if you had died first. For most people, that is a clean solution. For a person on SSI or Medicaid, agencies often treat the refusal as giving away an asset you were entitled to. Our article on [how to disclaim an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) explains the rules, and why the benefit recipient is the main exception to the standard advice.

Here is a real-feeling example. Denise, 44, has a disability and gets SSI and Medicaid. Her father died and left her a third of his estate, about $60,000. The executor held the distribution for six weeks while an attorney set up a first-party trust for her. The money went into the trust instead of Denise's checking account, and her benefits never paused. If it had been deposited first, she would have lost both for months.

## How can a family plan ahead so this never happens?

The best fix is in the will or trust of the person who is leaving the money. If you have a child, sibling or parent on SSI or Medicaid, do not leave them an outright gift. Leave their share to a third-party special needs trust, with a trustee who can pay for things the benefits do not cover.

Families also forget the beneficiary forms. An IRA or life insurance policy that names a person on benefits directly will go straight to them, whatever the will says. Our page on [a disabled heir who inherits directly](/learn/what-if/disabled-heir-inherits-directly) shows how this goes wrong, and the [beneficiary audit](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps you check your own forms.

## What if the money has already arrived?

Do not panic and do not spend it all at once. Report it to the agencies. Ask whether the funds can be moved into a trust or ABLE account, and whether a spend-down for fair value will bring you back under the limit. The first month matters most, because income counts only in the month received.

If you are already over the limit, there are often ways to restore eligibility, but each has rules and time limits. A benefits advisor or an attorney can sort that out in a single meeting.
