One of the main reasons people set up a living trust is privacy. Probate is a court case, and the file can be read by anyone who walks into the clerk's office or searches online. People want to know whether a trust really keeps the family's business out of view, or whether that claim is oversold.

The honest answer is that a trust is much more private than probate, but not invisible. This article shows what stays private, what leaks out and how to keep it that way. It is part of our guide to [what families do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## The short version

- A trust's terms are not filed with a court or a government agency in the ordinary case.
- A will is different. Once it goes through probate, it is open to the public.
- A deed to a trust property, a certification of trust and some notices may appear in public records.
- Court fights bring documents into the open.
- Privacy from the public is different from secrecy from beneficiaries. Beneficiaries are entitled to information.

## Why probate is public and a trust is not

Probate is a judge-supervised process. The will is filed with the clerk, the inventory may be filed, and notices and orders sit in the case file. The public has access because courts are public institutions. See [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) for the full process.

A trust works through a private contract. The trustee holds property under written terms and carries them out without asking a judge. There is no case and therefore no docket to search. That is the privacy advantage, and it is real. For a family with a vacation home, a business or a child who needs support, there is value in not publishing the details.

## What can become public

| Step | What is exposed | How much |
|---|---|---|
| Deed recorded in the county | Names of the trustee and the trust, the parcel, sometimes the price | Moderate, but not the terms |
| Certification of trust given to a bank or title company | Trust name, date, trustee, powers | Limited, and shared only with the institution |
| Notice to creditors, where used | Name of the person who died and the trustee's contact | Small |
| Pour-over will filed for probate | The whole will, and possibly an inventory | Significant |
| Court petition about the trust | The terms or parts of them | Depends on what is attached |
| Sale of real estate | Sale price in public land records in many places | Moderate |
| State trust registration, where it exists | The existence of the trust, not its terms | A few states only |

## The pour-over will problem

Most trust-based plans include a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) as a backstop. If everything is in the trust, the will is never filed. If one asset was left out, it may need probate, and then the will is filed with the court. The will is short and usually says little beyond "everything goes to my trust," but it becomes public, and the trust's name appears with it.

That is why funding the trust matters for privacy as well as for avoiding court. Read our article on [what happens if you never funded your living trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) to see how an asset left out can pull a family back into court. Small leftover assets may qualify for a [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit), which avoids a full probate file in many states.

## How to keep a trust administration private

1. **Share the trust only where necessary.** Give banks a certification of trust, and give beneficiaries the parts of the document that affect them.
2. **Ask about recording.** A trustee's deed or a certification of trust may be recorded, but the full trust should not be.
3. **Avoid unnecessary court filings.** Many trustees never go to court. Petitions for instructions or approval of accounts are optional in many states.
4. **Keep disputes out of court when possible.** A mediation is private, and a lawsuit is not.
5. **Mind the paper trail inside the family.** A trust that is emailed to ten relatives is not private for long. Send the document to people who are entitled to it.

## Private from the public, open to beneficiaries

Privacy from strangers should not be confused with secrecy from the people the trust is for. A trustee generally must tell beneficiaries the trust exists, give notice after a death and provide information that lets them protect their interests. Beneficiaries can usually ask for a copy of the terms that affect them. Some trusts do restrict what heirs who are not beneficiaries receive. The balance is covered in our guide on [a trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account).

A trustee who answers every question with "the trust is private" is not protecting the family. That response often leads to a petition for an accounting, and a petition is public.

## What the IRS and other agencies see

Privacy from the public does not mean privacy from the government. The trustee obtains an EIN and files a fiduciary income tax return, so the IRS knows the trust exists and what it earned. The return is confidential, not published. Banks and brokerages see the account and the certification of trust. A title company reads whatever it needs to insure a sale. If the estate is large enough to require a federal estate tax return, that return lists assets and values, again confidentially. None of this shows up in a Google search, but a trust is not a way of hiding anything from a lawful inquiry, and no trustee should treat it as one.

## Does a trust keep the amounts private from relatives?

Not from beneficiaries. A sibling who gets a share is entitled to know the structure and, in most states, to receive reports. A relative who gets nothing generally cannot demand the document, though in some states heirs who would have inherited without the trust receive a notice and a short window to ask questions or object. That notice is where many contests start, so it is worth thinking about early. Our guide on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains the kinds of claims people raise, and the same grounds often apply to a trust.

## Example: two sisters, one house

After their father died, Alicia and her sister Beth, as co-trustees, sold his house. The deed to the buyer was recorded, which showed the sale price in public records. The trust itself, which left some money to a grandson with a special-needs trust, was never filed anywhere. A cousin who looked up the house found only the buyer's name and the sale details. Had the sisters used probate, the will would have carried the grandson's name into a public file.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether a trustee may or must publish creditor notices, what a recorded document must contain, and whether any trust registration exists. Some states have a short list of required filings, and some let a trustee seek court approval of accounts, which makes the accounts public. Property-state rules also matter for [real estate held in other states](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate). Ask a local attorney which records are likely to be created in your situation.
