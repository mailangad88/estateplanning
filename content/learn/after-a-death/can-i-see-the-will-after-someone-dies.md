When a parent dies, one of the first questions in the family is whether there was a will. Hours later it becomes who has it, and why nobody will let them read it. Silence from the person in charge makes people suspicious, and sometimes the suspicion is fair and sometimes the executor is simply overwhelmed.

This article explains your rights, how to find out whether a will exists, how to get a copy, and what to do when someone is stalling. It belongs to our broader guide on [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## Who is entitled to see a will?

Once a will is filed with the probate court, almost anyone is. A will that goes through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) becomes a public court record in nearly every state. A stranger can ask the clerk for a copy, and heirs and beneficiaries certainly can. We discuss the privacy side in [is probate public](/learn/probate/is-probate-public).

Before the will is filed, the picture is less tidy. While the person was alive, the will was private, and nobody besides the maker had a right to see it. After death, the situation changes:

- **The person holding the original** generally must deliver it to the court, or to the named executor, soon after learning of the death. State law sets the time, and it is often short, such as a few weeks.
- **The executor** has to open probate if probate is needed, and doing so puts the will in the court file.
- **Heirs and beneficiaries** are usually entitled to notice, and to see the will once it is filed.

Hiding a will is not allowed. In many states, withholding or destroying one is a crime and a ground for removing an executor.

## What if I am not sure there is a will?

Look in the places people actually keep them. Carl, whose father died in another state, found his father's will through the third item on this list:

1. **Ask the family and friends.** Someone usually knows an attorney's name.
2. **Search the home.** Look in a filing cabinet, desk, fireproof box and safe. Wills often sit with the deed, insurance policies and tax returns.
3. **Call the attorney who drafted it.** Many firms hold originals for clients. The clerk can tell you if the lawyer was a family one.
4. **Check the county probate court.** Search the index under the person's name in the county where they lived. Some courts have online search, and others need a call or a visit.
5. **Ask the bank about a safe deposit box.** Our article on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) explains why that is risky, and what the box rules usually are after a death.
6. **Look for a state will registry or deposit.** A few states let people file wills with the court or a registry during life. Ask the court clerk if yours does.
7. **Check mail and email.** Bar association notices, storage bills and attorney letters can point to the place.

If you find only a copy, not the original, read our guide to a [lost original will](/learn/wills/lost-original-will). A copy can often be admitted, but it takes extra proof.

## How do I get a copy of a filed will?

The steps are not complicated.

1. Find out which county and court handled the estate. It is normally the county where the person lived at death.
2. Search the probate index by name, or call the clerk with the date of death.
3. Ask for a copy of the will and the petition. Some courts offer them online.
4. Pay the copy fee. It is often modest.
5. Ask for a certified copy if you need to prove the document is genuine, for a bank or title company.

A court file may also show who petitioned, who was named executor, and whether anyone has objected. Read the petition and the list of heirs. Your name should appear if the law treats you as an heir.

## What notice should I expect from the executor?

Most states require the executor or administrator to send notice once appointed. The names vary, but notices commonly go to heirs and named beneficiaries within a set number of days, and they say that probate has started, who is in charge, and where to see the will. Creditors get a separate notice or a published one.

Some states also require the executor to send a copy of the will itself. Others only require the notice and make the file public. Either way, a beneficiary who asks in writing for a copy and for the executor's contact information should get it. If you are an executor, the quickest way to avoid fights is to send a copy to each beneficiary early, along with a cover letter that explains the next steps. Our guide to [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) lists the notices on the to-do list, and [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate) shows where the will goes first.

A trust works differently. Trusts are private documents and do not become public records through probate. Still, beneficiaries have a legal right to request the trust terms that affect them. See [the trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) and the [trust administration public or private](/learn/after-a-death/is-trust-administration-public) article for how this works.

## What if the executor will not show me the will?

Escalate in steps, in writing.

1. **Send a polite written request** to the executor. Ask for a copy of the will and the court case number.
2. **Check the court file yourself.** If probate has been opened, you can see it.
3. **Send a second letter,** firmer, with a deadline such as 14 days.
4. **Contact an attorney.** A short letter on firm stationery often ends the standoff.
5. **Ask the court.** Courts can order a person holding a will to deposit it. If probate has not been opened, an interested person can usually petition to open it, or to have a will located and filed.
6. **Raise removal if the delay is willful.** Deliberately concealing a will is a serious breach. Our article on [whether an executor can be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) describes the process.

Do not take, copy or alter anything from the house in an effort to find the will unless you have authority. Let the executor or a court handle that. If you are worried that the documents will disappear, tell the court and ask for an order to preserve them.

## What if I find out I was left out?

It hurts, and it is not necessarily a legal wrong. Most people can choose who gets their property. A spouse often has special rights even if disinherited, such as an [elective share](/glossary/elective-share), and some states protect children in limited situations. Adult children generally have no right to inherit.

If you believe the will is not genuine, was signed under pressure, or was made when the person lacked capacity, an attorney can tell you whether you have standing and a case. Deadlines are short in many states, sometimes only a few months from the notice, so do not wait. Our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains the grounds, and the [no-contest clause](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) can change the risk of trying.
