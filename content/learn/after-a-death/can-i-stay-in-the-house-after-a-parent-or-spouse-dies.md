The funeral is over and the house is quiet. You may have lived there with your mother for years, or you may be the widow who has shared the same address with your husband since 1989. Now a relative has mentioned that the house "has to be sold," and you wonder whether you have to leave.

The answer is not the same for everyone. It turns on how title was held, who you are to the person who died, and what the will or trust says. This article sorts out the most common cases and what to do in each. It is part of our broader guide on [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## Who owns the house now?

Start there, because it decides who can tell you to stay or go. At death, the house usually lands in one of four places.

| How the owner held title | Who controls the house right after death |
|---|---|
| Jointly with survivorship (or as spouses by the entirety) | The surviving owner already owns it. |
| Sole name, with a will or no will | The executor or administrator, once the court appoints one. |
| Living trust | The successor trustee. |
| Transfer-on-death deed | The named beneficiary, after recording the proper form. |

If you were a co-owner with survivorship, nothing changes for you. You own the house, and you should take care of the paperwork described in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks). In all the other cases, a person has authority over the house even if they do not live there, and you need their consent or a legal right of your own.

## What are the rights of a surviving spouse?

They are usually the strongest. States protect a surviving spouse in several ways, and the details differ widely:

- **Homestead rights.** Many states let a spouse remain in the family home for a period or for life, and protect it from some creditors.
- **Spousal allowances and exempt property.** A court may set aside money or property for a spouse's immediate needs.
- **An elective or forced share.** If the will leaves the spouse little or nothing, many states let the spouse claim a fraction of the estate.
- **Community property.** In community property states, the spouse may already own half.

So a surviving spouse is rarely required to move out quickly. If the house belonged only to the late spouse and the will leaves it to someone else, the spouse should ask an attorney about occupancy and the elective share before packing a box. That is especially so in a second marriage, where the late spouse's children may inherit the house, as our guide on [estate planning for blended families](/learn/blended-families) explains.

## What if I am an adult child living in my parent's house?

Your position is weaker, and that is a hard thing to hear after years of caring for a parent. Unless you own a share, you generally have no legal right to stay. If you were paying rent, you may be a tenant, and tenants usually keep their lease rights until it ends or a landlord follows the proper notice process. If you lived there free, most states would treat you as a guest or tenant at will, who can be asked to leave after reasonable written notice.

Being named in the will changes things. If the house passes to you under the will, you will own it once the estate is settled, and the executor should let you stay in the meantime. If the house passes to you and your siblings, you are co-owners, and nobody can force the others out, though a sibling can ask a court to order a sale. The article on [what happens when siblings inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) goes through that fight.

## Can the executor make me leave?

An executor or trustee has a duty to preserve estate property for the heirs. If you are living in a house that will be sold, the executor may reasonably say you must leave by a date so it can be listed. But even an executor cannot simply lock you out. They have to follow your state's process, which generally means written notice and, if you refuse, a court filing.

Practically, most families do not get that far. A simple written agreement works better. It should say how long you will stay, who pays utilities and insurance, whether you pay rent, who handles repairs and what happens if the house sells sooner. Keep a copy. The executor will have to explain the arrangement to the other heirs, and a clear paper protects both of you. Our article on [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) covers how a sale proceeds when someone is still inside, and [can heirs sell the house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) covers the timing.

## What does staying cost?

Even free housing has costs. The house still carries property tax, insurance and maybe a mortgage. A vacant house often loses insurance coverage, which is one reason an executor may prefer an occupied house, but an occupied one still needs a policy that fits.

If you stay in a house that belongs to the estate or to several heirs, expect questions about fairness. Siblings may argue that a resident heir should pay rent, or that the resident's share should be reduced to account for free use. Courts in some states can order a co-owner who has exclusive possession to account for the rental value. Pay the carrying costs, document improvements, and keep receipts.

## What are the steps to protect yourself?

1. **Find out who has authority.** Ask for the will, the trust, or the deed. Our guide on [seeing the will](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-see-the-will-after-someone-dies) shows how.
2. **Do not move out in a panic.** A voluntary departure can weaken a later claim.
3. **Talk to the executor or trustee,** and propose a written arrangement.
4. **Keep paying what you owe,** such as rent under an existing lease, and keep proof.
5. **Keep receipts for repairs and carrying costs.**
6. **Do not change locks or remove property** that does not belong to you.
7. **Get legal advice early** if you were left out of the will, if there is a dispute or if you have a long-term caregiving arrangement.

## What if I took care of my parent and was promised the house?

A verbal promise to leave you the house is hard to enforce. Most states require real estate agreements to be in writing. Some allow a claim for the value of services in unusual cases, and a court may take caregiving into account. Others may give you nothing beyond what the will says. If you gave up a job or paid for care, talk to an attorney before the estate is distributed, and gather proof such as bank records, medical logs and texts.
