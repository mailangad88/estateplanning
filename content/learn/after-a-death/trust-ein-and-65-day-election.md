If you are the successor trustee, one of the first things a bank will ask for is a taxpayer ID for the trust. That is a nine-digit Employer Identification Number, or EIN, and it is the key to opening the trust's account and filing its tax return. This page shows how to get one.

The second half covers a tax rule trustees tend to hear about from an accountant in March, the 65-day election. It is a small timing choice that can save real money in the right year. Both topics sit inside our guide on [what families do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## The short version

- Get a new EIN for the trust soon after the death. It is free.
- Apply online at irs.gov, or send Form SS-4 by fax or mail.
- Use the EIN for the trust account, investments and tax return.
- A trust with $600 or more of gross income generally files Form 1041.
- The 65-day election is optional, and it depends on when distributions are made.

## Why an irrevocable trust needs its own EIN

While the creator was alive, a revocable trust was treated for income tax as a part of the creator. It usually reported under the creator's Social Security number, and nothing separate was filed. At death the trust becomes irrevocable, which means it can no longer be changed, and the law treats it as its own taxpayer. See [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) for how that shift happens.

Joint trusts of married couples add a twist. When the first spouse dies, the trust may split into a survivor's trust that stays revocable and a bypass or family trust that becomes irrevocable. Only the irrevocable part needs a new EIN. The attorney who drafted the trust, or a new one, can tell you how yours divides.

## How do I get a trust EIN?

1. **Gather the facts.** The trust's exact name from the document (for example, the "Eleanor J. Shaw Revocable Trust dated June 4, 2018"), the date of death, the trustee's name, address and Social Security number, and the type of entity: a trust.
2. **Apply with the IRS.** The online application at irs.gov for an EIN is free, and the number is issued as soon as you finish. The paper Form SS-4 can be faxed or mailed. Do not use a website that charges a fee, because the IRS does not.
3. **Name the responsible party.** This is the individual who controls the trust, normally the successor trustee.
4. **Save the confirmation letter.** Print the notice (CP 575 or the online confirmation). Banks ask for it.
5. **Open the trust account.** Bring the EIN, a certification of trust, a death certificate and your photo ID.
6. **Update the payers.** Tell banks and brokerages to report future interest and dividends under the trust's EIN, not the dead person's number.

Once the account is open, income and bills run through it. Our guide to [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) covers the rest of the steps, and [what to do when a trustee dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-a-trustee-dies) explains the first week in more detail.

## Does the trust have to file a tax return?

If the trust has gross income of $600 or more in a year, or any taxable income, it generally files Form 1041, the fiduciary income tax return. The trustee gives each beneficiary a Schedule K-1 showing their share of income that was passed through to them. The beneficiaries report that on their own returns.

Assets that get a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) at death are valued as of the date of death, so selling them soon after often produces little taxable gain. Interest, dividends, rent and any gain on a later increase in value are what the trust may owe on. For how these returns fit with the decedent's final personal return, see [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

## Why trusts pay high tax: the compressed brackets

Trust income tax rates reach the top bracket at a small fraction of the income that it takes for an individual. A trust that keeps the income it earns can hit the highest federal rate on only a modest amount. The same income, passed out to an adult child in a lower bracket, may be taxed at a much lower rate.

That gap is why trustees think about when to distribute. The general rule: if the trust distributes income, the trust deducts it and the beneficiary reports it. If the trust keeps the income, the trust pays tax on it. Distribution of principal usually does not carry income out, so what counts is the trust's "distributable net income" for the year.

## What is the 65-day election?

The 65-day election lets a trustee treat a distribution made in the first 65 days of a new tax year as if it were made on the last day of the prior year. For a calendar-year trust, the window runs from January 1 to early March, which is March 6 in most years and March 5 in a leap year.

Here is an example. A trust earned $20,000 of dividends in 2026. By December, the trustee had distributed only $5,000 to the beneficiary, Monique, a retired teacher in a lower bracket. The trustee discovers the problem in January and sends Monique $15,000 on February 10. With the election, the $15,000 counts for 2026. The trust deducts it on the 2026 return, and Monique reports it on hers. Without the election, the trust pays tax on $15,000 at its compressed rates and the February payment counts for 2027.

Points to know:

- The election only applies to distributions that the trustee is already allowed to make, and the amount is capped by the trust's distributable net income.
- It is made on a timely filed Form 1041, including extensions.
- It applies only for that year, and it is generally irrevocable once made.
- It does not create a right to distributions that the trust does not give.

## Related choices a tax preparer may raise

- **Fiscal year for an estate.** An estate can often pick a year-end other than December, which gives different timing. A trust generally uses the calendar year.
- **The Section 645 election.** A qualified revocable trust and the executor of the estate can elect to treat the trust as part of the estate for tax purposes, using IRS Form 8855. That can allow a fiscal year and other flexibility.
- **Estimated payments.** Trusts and estates may owe quarterly estimated tax, with exceptions in the first two years for some estates.

These choices have deadlines. Talk to a CPA before the first return is due, not after. Our guide on [whether you need an attorney to settle a trust](/learn/after-a-death/do-you-need-an-attorney-to-settle-a-trust) helps you decide who to call first.

## How state law changes this

Most states that have an income tax require a fiduciary return in addition to the federal one, and the definition of which trusts owe tax can depend on where the trustee lives, where the beneficiaries live and which state's law governs the trust. Some states have no income tax at all. Check how your own state treats trusts, starting with our [state guides](/estate-planning/california) as an example. Do not assume the federal election carries over.
