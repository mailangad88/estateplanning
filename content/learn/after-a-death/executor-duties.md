You have been named executor, and you may be wondering what you agreed to. The role is a legal one, with a set of duties that follow a fairly predictable path. Most executors are ordinary family members with no legal training, and the work is manageable if you take it one step at a time.

This guide walks through those steps in order. It sits inside our wider guide on [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death), and it assumes the person left a will. If you are still in the first days after the death, start with the [week-by-week checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

## What does an executor do?

An executor carries out the instructions in a will under the supervision of a probate court. The job has five parts: getting authority, finding and protecting assets, handling debts and taxes, keeping records, and distributing what is left.

An executor is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), which means you owe the beneficiaries honesty, care and loyalty. In practice that means keeping the estate's money apart from yours, not buying estate property for yourself without court approval, treating beneficiaries evenly unless the will says otherwise, and being able to show where every dollar went. Not every state uses the word executor; "personal representative" and "administrator" (when there is no will) mean much the same thing.

## What are the steps, in order?

### 1. Locate the will and file it with the court

Find the original will. Many states require anyone holding the original to deliver it to the probate court within a short time after the death, and a copy is often not enough. Then meet with a probate attorney, who will prepare the petition to open the estate.

### 2. Get your authority from the court

The court reviews the will, confirms you are the named executor and issues [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary), a certified document proving you may act for the estate. Some courts require a bond, which is a type of insurance protecting the estate, unless the will waives it. Banks, brokerages and title offices will ask for these letters, usually a recently certified copy, so order several. Our guide to [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) explains the court process in more detail.

### 3. Notify the right people

Many states require the executor to give written notice to heirs and beneficiaries soon after appointment. You also publish or mail a notice to creditors, which begins a claims period. The length of that period varies by state and is often a few months.

### 4. Get a tax ID and open an estate account

Apply for an Employer Identification Number (EIN) for the estate on the IRS website, which is free. Take the EIN and your letters to a bank and open an estate checking account. Deposit all incoming money there and pay all expenses from it. Never run estate money through your personal account. For what you may and may not pay yourself from that account, see [can an executor take money out of the estate?](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate)

### 5. Inventory and value the assets

List everything the person owned and its date-of-death value. Typical items:

- Bank and brokerage accounts
- Real estate, which often needs an appraisal
- Vehicles, jewelry, art and collectibles
- Business interests
- Digital accounts and [cryptocurrency](/learn/digital-assets/cryptocurrency-inheritance)
- Refunds, final paychecks and money owed to the person

Not everything is part of the probate estate. Accounts with named beneficiaries, jointly owned property and trust assets usually pass outside of it, so check our overview of [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets). The executor still often needs to know about these assets, because they affect taxes and the overall picture.

### 6. Protect the assets

Keep the house insured, locked, heated and maintained. Keep investments where they are unless there is a reason to change. Cancel services the estate does not need, such as streaming and gym memberships, but keep insurance and utilities active until the property is sold or transferred. Secure the person's mail and, with an attorney's guidance, take care with the person's computer and phone, since they may hold financial records.

### 7. Handle debts and claims

Gather bills, credit card statements and loan notices. Valid debts are paid from the estate in an order set by state law, with funeral costs, administration expenses and taxes usually near the top. Do not pay a debt because a collector calls; verify that it is real and that the claim period has been followed. If the estate cannot pay everyone, the order matters, and paying a lower-priority creditor first can create personal liability for you. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

### 8. File the tax returns

The executor files the person's last income tax return, files an estate income tax return if the estate earns enough, and in rare cases files a federal estate tax return. These returns are explained in [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns). Hiring a CPA is a normal estate expense.

### 9. Distribute the assets

When the claims period has ended, debts and taxes are paid and the court allows it, you distribute to the beneficiaries according to the will. Specific gifts, such as "my watch to my nephew," come first, then the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate). Get a signed receipt from each beneficiary. Many executors keep a small reserve until the final tax returns are accepted.

### 10. Close the estate

File a final accounting with the court if your state requires it, ask the court to discharge you, and close the estate bank account. Keep your records for several years.

## How long does it take, and what does it cost?

A straightforward estate often takes six months to a year. Complicated ones, such as those with a business, out-of-state real estate or a contested will, can take longer. Costs can include court filing fees, attorney fees, appraisals, accounting fees, publication costs and the executor's own compensation. All are normally paid from the estate, not from your own money. Our article on [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) gives examples.

## What mistakes should an executor avoid?

The most common ones are paying heirs too early, mixing funds, missing a deadline and failing to communicate. A short table shows the pattern.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Distributing before debts are paid | Personal liability if the estate runs short | Wait for the claims period to end |
| Using the person's cards or accounts | You have no authority until appointed | Use the estate account |
| Skipping receipts | Beneficiaries may demand an accounting | Keep a ledger and every receipt |
| Selling property too cheaply | A beneficiary may claim you breached your duty | Get an appraisal and consider market listing |
| Ignoring family questions | Silence breeds suspicion and disputes | Send updates every few months |

## Do you need a lawyer as executor?

You are not always required to hire one, but most executors benefit from at least an initial consultation. A very small estate may qualify for a [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) and need little court involvement. If there is real estate, a business, a family disagreement or significant debt, legal help is usually worth the cost. An attorney can also tell you when beneficiaries have the right to ask the court to [remove an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed), so you know where the lines are.
