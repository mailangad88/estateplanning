Being named executor feels like an honor until the first form arrives. The court wants an inventory. The brokerage wants letters. The house needs an appraisal and the final tax return needs a preparer. Soon the natural question is whether you may bring in help, and who pays.

You may, and in most estates you should. The harder question is how to hire help and run the estate so that no beneficiary can later say you were careless with their inheritance. This page covers both. For the full list of what the job involves, see our [executor duties guide](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) and the wider [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## The short version

- Hiring professionals is a normal use of estate money, not a luxury.
- Keep estate money in an estate account, never your own.
- Put every fee agreement and every major decision in writing.
- Tell beneficiaries what you are doing before they ask.
- Do not distribute the last dollars until the taxes, claims and fees are settled and releases are signed.

## Which professionals can an executor hire?

Four kinds of help come up most often.

| Professional | What they do | When it is worth it |
|---|---|---|
| Estate attorney | Opens the case, advises on duties, handles notices, claims and the court | Almost always, when there is real estate, a dispute or a court case |
| CPA or tax preparer | Final personal return, estate income tax return, estate tax filing if needed | Whenever assets are more than a bank account or two |
| Appraiser | Date-of-death value of the house, valuables, a business | A house, collections, or anything heirs may dispute |
| Realtor, auctioneer, cleanout crew | Sells or clears property | When the executor lives far away or time matters |

Our guide on [whether you need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer) explains when you can manage without one. Even then, a one-hour consultation early on often pays for itself.

## Who pays for them?

The estate does. Reasonable professional fees are an administration expense, which ranks near the top of the order in which an estate pays bills. If the estate has no cash on hand yet because accounts are frozen, you can pay a small retainer yourself and be reimbursed once letters are issued. Do not make a habit of it: you are lending the estate money without any security, and a quarrel with the other heirs about reimbursement is an unhappy way to end a long job.

Attorney fees vary a great deal. Some charge hourly, some a flat fee for an uncontested estate, some a percentage of the estate where state law or local practice allows it. Our article on [probate lawyer fee agreements](/learn/probate/probate-lawyer-fee-agreements) shows how to read one. Ask for the agreement in writing, ask what is excluded, and ask how often you will receive an invoice.

## Who is the estate's lawyer working for?

The executor, in the executor's official role. That surprises people. The lawyer is not the family's lawyer and is not the beneficiaries' lawyer. They advise you on what the will requires and what the law says, but if two beneficiaries disagree with each other, the estate's attorney cannot advise either of them.

This matters for you personally in a second way. The lawyer represents the office of executor, not your own pocketbook. If your interests as a beneficiary conflict with your duties as executor, say you want to buy the house, or you think your fee should be higher, the lawyer will tell you what the rules are and will not take your side against the estate. Our article on an [executor who is also a beneficiary](/learn/probate/executor-who-is-also-a-beneficiary) goes further.

## How do I protect myself as executor?

An executor is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary), held to a high standard, and the law can make a careless one pay out of their own pocket. Good habits are the best protection.

1. **Open a separate estate account** after you get an EIN for the estate, and run every receipt and payment through it. Never pay estate bills from your own account or deposit estate checks into it.
2. **Get advice before big steps,** such as selling the house, paying a large claim or making a first distribution. Reliance in good faith on an attorney's or CPA's advice is strong evidence you acted prudently.
3. **Keep records of everything:** invoices, receipts, bank statements, emails with beneficiaries, the date and reason for each decision.
4. **Wait out the creditor period** your state sets before you distribute the bulk of the estate. Our guide to the [creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) explains why.
5. **File the tax returns,** including the final income tax return and any estate return, and keep proof. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).
6. **Insure the property.** An empty house with a lapsed policy puts you at risk if a pipe bursts.
7. **Update beneficiaries on a schedule,** monthly or quarterly. Most complaints about executors begin as unanswered messages.
8. **Get releases.** Before final distribution, ask each beneficiary to sign a receipt and release, or have the court approve your accounting.

A short example. Hannah, executor for her mother, had her mother's brokerage account valued, paid the funeral bill and sold the house. She sent her two brothers a one-page update every month. When one brother asked in the second year why the estate was not closed, she had the file and the answer ready. The conversation took ten minutes. Compare that with an executor who kept silent for a year and then received a petition to compel an accounting.

## What mistakes cause personal liability?

The ones that come up again and again:

- Paying beneficiaries before creditor claims and taxes are cleared, then finding the estate is short.
- Missing a tax return, or paying a penalty because a notice went unanswered.
- Selling property to a friend or relative below market value.
- Borrowing from the estate, even for a few days, with the best intentions.
- Failing to insure or maintain a house that was then damaged.
- Ignoring a beneficiary who is asking for information.

A beneficiary who thinks you failed can ask the court for an accounting, ask to surcharge you, which means to make you repay a loss, or in serious cases ask to remove you. See [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) for what that looks like. A will may say the executor is not liable except for willful misconduct, and that language helps, but courts read it narrowly.

## What about executor fees?

Most states allow a reasonable fee for the executor. Some set a schedule, some leave it to the court. The fee is taxable income to the executor, while an inheritance is not taxed as income. A son who is the only beneficiary may take no fee at all. The [probate costs guide](/learn/probate/probate-costs) puts typical numbers on this, and [what an executor can take from the estate](/learn/probate/can-an-executor-take-money-from-the-estate) covers the limits.

## How state law changes this

States differ on whether an executor needs court approval for fees, whether a bond is required, how long before distributions are safe, and what happens if the estate runs short. A few allow independent administration with very little court contact. Your state's guide, such as the [California guide](/estate-planning/california) or the [Illinois guide](/estate-planning/illinois), is a good starting point.
