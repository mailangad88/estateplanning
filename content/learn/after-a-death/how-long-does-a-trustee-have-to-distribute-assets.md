You have been told you are a beneficiary of a parent's trust, and months have gone by with no check. Or you are the trustee and a sibling keeps asking when the money will be ready. Both of you want the same thing, which is a clear answer to how long this should take.

There is no universal clock. But there are patterns, and knowing them lets you tell the difference between a trustee who is working and one who is stuck. This page is part of our guide to [what families do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## The short version

- The law usually says "a reasonable time," not a number of days.
- The trust itself can set dates, ages or events that control the timing.
- Many simple trusts are finished in nine to eighteen months, and longer when a house is sold or a return is contested.
- Interim distributions are normal when enough money stays behind for bills and taxes.
- Silence is the problem, more than slowness. A trustee should be able to explain the delay.

## When the answer is "a reasonable time"

Courts ask whether the delay has a good reason. For a trust with cash and a few brokerage accounts, a year is plenty. For a trust with a house to sell, a business to value or property in another state, two years is not unusual.

Take Naomi, trustee of her father's trust, which splits evenly among three children. The assets are a bank account, a brokerage account and a house. Naomi cannot distribute the house until it is sold. She cannot sell it at a fair price until she has an appraisal and has cleaned it out. She cannot close the trust until the decedent's final income tax return is filed in April of the next year. Her brother thinks everything should be paid in sixty days. He is thinking of the cash. The house sets the pace.

Compare that to Glenn, whose trust holds only two bank accounts. Glenn has done nothing for eleven months, will not return calls and has opened no trust account. That is not a reasonable time, and a court is likely to say so.

## What actually sets the pace

| Factor | Why it takes time | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Notice and creditor period | Many states give creditors a window after notice | Months, sometimes a formal cutoff |
| Final income tax return | Due the April after the year of death | Trustee holds a reserve until filed |
| Fiduciary income tax return | Covers income earned after death | Another spring, plus any amended filing |
| Estate tax return, if needed | Federal Form 706 is due nine months after death | Only for large estates above the [federal exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) |
| Real estate | Appraisal, repairs, listing, closing | Several months to a year |
| Business or other illiquid assets | Valuation, buyers, contracts | Often the longest item |
| Disputes | A contest or accounting demand pauses the process | Open ended |

Only a small share of families owe federal estate tax, so for most the tax holdup is income tax, not estate tax. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) for how those fit together.

## When the trust sets its own schedule

The trust can override the general picture. Some documents say the trustee distributes "as soon as practicable." Others pay out in stages, for instance a third at 25 and the rest at 30, or hold property for a surviving spouse for life. A trustee must follow those instructions, even if the beneficiaries would prefer a lump sum. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) for how staged payouts work.

If the trust lets the trustee delay at their discretion, the discretion still has limits. A trustee cannot use it to hold money for years without a reason.

## Interim and final distributions

An interim distribution is a partial payout before the trust closes. It makes sense when:

- the debts and expenses are known and a reserve covers them,
- the tax picture is clear enough to estimate what is owed,
- every beneficiary in the same class is treated the same way.

A final distribution happens after the reserve is released and the trustee has delivered an accounting and collected receipts. Some attorneys also ask for a release, so the beneficiary agrees not to sue over the trustee's reported actions. You are not required to sign a release just to get what the trust already says you are owed, and a lawyer should look at it first.

## How big should the reserve be?

There is no formula, and a trustee should be able to explain the number. A sensible reserve covers the known bills, the expected income tax on the final and fiduciary returns, the cost of the attorney and accountant, upkeep on any unsold house, and a cushion for surprises. Trustees who hold back too little risk personal liability. Trustees who hold back far too much invite a petition, because money sitting idle is money beneficiaries cannot use.

Gloria, trustee of a $600,000 trust for two children, paid each child $150,000 in month seven and kept the rest while the house sold. That left a cushion for taxes and legal fees, put real money in the children's hands, and was easy to explain. A written note to both children, setting out the amount held back and why, kept the peace.

## What to do if you are a beneficiary waiting

1. **Ask for a status report in writing.** Keep it short and polite: what has been done, what remains, and a target date.
2. **Ask for an accounting.** The trustee's [duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) usually supports this request.
3. **Offer help.** Beneficiaries sometimes can speed up a sale by agreeing on a price or removing belongings.
4. **Send a formal demand if replies stop.** A lawyer's letter often restarts a stalled trust.
5. **Petition the court as a last step.** A judge can order a report, set a deadline or remove the trustee. Our page on [what to do when a trustee does nothing](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-trustee-does-nothing) covers this in detail.

## What to do if you are the trustee

Make a simple plan with dates and share it. A one-page timeline that says "appraisal in December, listing in January, tax return in April, final distribution by August" prevents most arguments. Update it when something changes. Keep the reserve in the trust account, not your own, and keep receipts. If a single beneficiary is demanding speed, explain that you owe the same duty to every beneficiary and that one early payment can shortchange another.

## How state law changes this

States differ on notice periods, on how long creditors have to file a claim after notice, and on whether a trustee can get a formal court order approving the final accounting. Some states have a quick process that ends creditor claims within months, while others leave the window open longer unless the trustee acts. Property in another state may need its own steps. Check with a local attorney, or look at our [state guides](/estate-planning/texas) for how the rules read in one state.

For comparison, court-supervised estates have their own pace. Our article on [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) sets the two side by side.
