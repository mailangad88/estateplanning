The will leaves the house and savings to three children. Then reality complicates it. One child died last spring. Another is sixteen. The third is in the middle of a bankruptcy. The executor asks a reasonable question: who do I actually write the check to?

An inheritance is not always paid to the name on the list. This page looks at four situations where the heir cannot just receive their share, and what usually happens in each: the heir has died, is a minor, lacks capacity, or owes money. For the full process of settling an estate, see our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## What happens if an heir dies before the estate is settled?

There are two very different cases, and the dates matter.

If the heir died **before** the person whose estate it is, the gift may lapse. Many states have anti-lapse statutes. When a beneficiary who was a child or other close relative dies first, their share often passes to their own children. In an intestate estate, the share usually passes by representation, the idea behind [per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes). Otherwise the share drops into the residue or goes to the other heirs. Our page on [lapsed gifts and simultaneous death](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death) explains the rules and the short survival period many states require.

If the heir died **after** the person but before being paid, the heir already earned the share. It becomes part of the heir's own estate. The executor pays it to the heir's personal representative, and it passes under the heir's will or by the intestacy rules of the heir's state. This sometimes means a second probate and a second executor. A surviving spouse of the heir may end up with money the original owner never imagined.

| Timing of the heir's death | Usual result | What the executor needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before the person died, will leaves a gift to a relative | Often passes to the heir's descendants under anti-lapse | The will and the heir's death certificate |
| Before the person died, no anti-lapse rule applies | Gift fails and falls into the residue | A residuary clause or the intestacy list |
| After the person died, before payment | Share belongs to the heir's estate | Letters for the heir's estate |
| At the same time, order unclear | State survival rules decide | A lawyer's review |

Do not pay a relative of the dead heir "to keep it simple." Pay whoever holds legal authority over that heir's estate, or the court-approved heirs. A written receipt then protects you.

## What if an heir is a minor?

A child cannot give a valid receipt for a large sum, so the money has to go somewhere safe. The three usual answers:

- **A trust named in the will or trust document.** The trustee manages the money and pays for the child's needs, then distributes at the ages the document sets. This is the cleanest answer and costs the least to administer.
- **A custodian under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act (UTMA).** The executor or donor can often name a custodian, and the money is delivered to that adult. The child receives everything at 18 or 21, depending on the state. Our comparison of [UTMA accounts and trusts for minors](/learn/guardianship/utma-account-vs-trust-for-a-minor-child) shows why that age is a concern.
- **A court-supervised guardianship or conservatorship of the estate.** If nothing else exists, a judge appoints a guardian of the child's property. That brings bonds, annual reports and court approval for spending, and it ends at 18 with a lump sum.

Lena's father died without a trust, and her grandmother wanted to be named to hold the money for Lena's brother, aged 14. The probate court required a formal appointment, a bond and annual accountings. It worked, but it was slower and more expensive than a small trust would have been. Our page on [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) covers how to plan for this, and [a minor who inherits without a trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust) walks through what unfolds in court.

An executor with a minor heir should not distribute anything until the custodian, trustee or guardian is in place and signs.

## What if an heir is incapacitated?

An adult who cannot manage money has the same problem as a child, with fewer easy fixes. If the heir already has a financial power of attorney, the agent may be able to accept the funds on the heir's behalf, though some executors and banks refuse and ask for more. If not, a conservatorship is the court route, and it carries the same bonds and reports.

The better answer is a trust. A third-party trust holds the inheritance, and the trustee pays for needs. If the heir receives SSI or Medicaid, a direct inheritance can end eligibility. A special needs trust or an ABLE account can protect it, as explained in [what to do when an SSI recipient is left an inheritance](/learn/special-needs/ssi-recipient-inheritance-gift-or-settlement-what-to-do) and our overview of [what happens when a disabled heir inherits directly](/learn/what-if/disabled-heir-inherits-directly).

Executors have some room. In many states, if the will does not provide a trust, the family can ask the court to approve one, or can use a disclaimer so the share passes to someone else. Disclaiming a share can backfire when the heir is on means-tested benefits, so ask before signing. The [disclaimer guide](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) covers the traps.

## What if the heir has creditors?

The estate's own debts come first, but once the executor pays an heir, that money is theirs, and their creditors can go after it. The details differ by creditor.

1. **Judgment creditors.** With a court judgment, a creditor may garnish an account or record a lien on inherited real estate. Inherited cash sitting in a bank account is exposed.
2. **Bankruptcy.** An inheritance an heir becomes entitled to within roughly 180 days after filing generally becomes part of the bankruptcy estate. A filing in the middle of probate calls for a lawyer's advice.
3. **Child support and tax debts.** These collectors have strong tools, including intercepts and liens.
4. **Divorce.** The inheritance is usually the heir's separate property, unless it was mixed with marital money. Our page on [whether an inheritance is marital property](/learn/after-a-death/is-an-inheritance-marital-property) explains how to keep it separate.

A trust with a [spendthrift clause](/glossary/spendthrift-clause) is the standard protection. The trustee, not the heir, controls the money, so a creditor generally cannot demand it. That is a decision for the person writing the will, since an executor cannot create it afterward. For the planning side, see [beneficiary who is bankrupt, in prison, divorcing or struggling with addiction](/learn/what-if/beneficiary-bankrupt-in-prison-divorcing-or-struggling-with-addiction).

If you are the executor and you learn an heir has a judgment against them, do not pay them directly and do not make a deal on your own. Tell the estate's lawyer, and keep the receipts. A creditor with a valid garnishment order that you ignore can come after you.

## What should an executor do before distributing?

Go through this short list for every heir before the check goes out:

1. Confirm the heir is alive and has not been declared incapacitated.
2. Check ages and whether anyone on the list is a minor.
3. Ask whether the heir receives SSI, Medicaid or other benefits.
4. Look for a lien, garnishment notice or pending bankruptcy.
5. Get a signed receipt and release, which [closing the estate](/learn/after-a-death/final-distribution-receipt-and-release-and-closing-the-estate) discusses.

Most estates have none of these problems. But when one shows up, finding it before distribution is far cheaper than recovering money after.

If the share sits in a trust instead, [what happens to a trust when a beneficiary dies](/learn/trusts/what-happens-to-a-trust-when-a-beneficiary-dies) covers the trust rules.
