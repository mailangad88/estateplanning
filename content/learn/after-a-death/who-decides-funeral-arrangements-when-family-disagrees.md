A funeral should be one decision made by grieving people who love each other. Sometimes it becomes three decisions made by people who are not speaking. One sibling wants a church service and burial. Another says Dad wanted cremation and no fuss. A second wife has the phone number of the funeral home, and the grown children from the first marriage have a different one.

This page explains who actually has the legal right to decide, what proof of that right looks like, and what to do when the people at the same level cannot agree. For the money side, see [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral). For the full sequence of first steps, start with our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## Who has the legal right to arrange a funeral?

Your state decides, and the decision runs down a ranked list. The funeral director is bound by that list, because a wrong signature on a cremation form can lead to a lawsuit. The usual order looks like this:

| Rank | Who | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A person the deceased named in writing | A signed directive, a statutory form, or an appointment inside a healthcare power of attorney in some states |
| 2 | Surviving spouse | Often excluded if divorced or legally separated |
| 3 | Adult children | Usually as a group, with a majority sometimes allowed |
| 4 | Parents | Sometimes ahead of siblings, sometimes not |
| 5 | Siblings | Then grandchildren, grandparents and other next of kin |
| 6 | Someone who is not family | A close friend, guardian or public administrator, in some states |

The list is not the same everywhere. A few states put the executor named in the will near the top. Others treat a domestic partner as equal to a spouse. Illinois and New Jersey each have their own statute, and we cover them in [who controls funeral and burial decisions in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-controls-funeral-and-burial-decisions-in-new-jersey) and in [the Illinois Disposition of Remains Act](/learn/illinois/illinois-disposition-of-remains-act). If you are not sure which state's law applies, the funeral home usually knows the answer for the county where the person died.

## Do the deceased person's written wishes count?

Yes, and in many states they count more than anyone's opinion. A prepaid funeral contract is the strongest evidence, since the person chose and paid for it. A signed directive that says "cremation, ashes scattered at the lake" is next. A will can contain funeral wishes, though by the time anyone reads the will the service has often happened.

Notes in a drawer help less than people hope. A text message or an email can show intent, but a funeral home may hesitate to rely on one if a relative objects. If you are thinking about your own wishes, [planning your own funeral in advance](/learn/basics/plan-your-own-funeral-in-advance) shows how to put them in a form nobody can argue with.

Be aware of one gap. Wishes about what happens at the service are usually guidance. Wishes about disposition of the body, meaning burial or cremation, are the part states are most likely to enforce.

## What about unmarried partners, stepchildren and estranged relatives?

An unmarried partner is the hardest case. In states that rank only spouses and blood relatives, a partner of thirty years has no standing unless the person signed something. Daniel and Priya lived together for twenty-two years. He had no will and never named an agent. When he died, his adult son from an earlier marriage, whom Daniel had not spoken to in a decade, held the legal right to decide. He chose a short service in another city. Priya was not even told the date.

Stepchildren have the same problem. They are generally outside the list unless they were named or the others agree. An estranged child, on the other hand, usually keeps the right. A court can sometimes set aside an estranged relative who has abandoned the person, but that takes a filing and time.

Two fixes exist while someone is alive. They can sign a funeral directive or appointment of an agent on the form their state offers. They can also name a healthcare agent and read [what authority a healthcare agent has after death](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-agent-authority-after-death), because in some states that authority ends the moment the person dies and in others it extends to the body. For couples who never married, our guide on [the unmarried partner with no will](/learn/what-if/unmarried-partner-dies-without-a-will) covers the larger gap.

## What happens when relatives of equal rank disagree?

Most funeral homes will not proceed until the people at the top level agree or the law gives one of them the casting vote. Three children are three children. If two want burial and one wants cremation, some states let a majority decide and some require everyone. That is why the funeral home may say "we need all three signatures" even when you feel sure the answer is obvious.

Here is a calmer way to work through a disagreement:

1. **Gather the evidence.** Ask the funeral home and the family's lawyer or banker about prepaid plans. Look for a directive, a will, a veteran's file or a note in a safe deposit box.
2. **Name the real disagreement.** Is it burial versus cremation, the religious service, the date, or who is paying? Money and ritual are often tangled together.
3. **Ask a neutral person to help.** A clergy member, funeral director or family friend can often reach an agreement faster than the siblings.
4. **Propose a split.** Cremation now with a memorial later, or burial with a service in each family's town. Both sides can keep what matters.
5. **Go to court if a deadline is real.** A short petition to the probate or county court can name one person to decide. It is not expensive compared with a family feud, and it ends the standoff.

There is also a physical clock. Refrigeration and embalming can hold a body for a limited time, and funeral homes will not hold it forever while a family argues. That pressure is why many disputes resolve in days.

## Who signs for cremation?

The "authorizing agent" signs, and it is the person highest on your state's list. Cremation is permanent, so funeral homes and crematories are cautious. They may ask for proof of relationship, a waiting period, and a statement that no one of higher rank has objected. If an objection comes in, they usually stop.

If you are the person signing, read the form. It may say that you have the right to authorize and that you will accept responsibility for any claims. That clause is why some relatives step back and let others sign.

## Does having the right to decide mean you pay?

No, but it often turns out that way. The person who signs the funeral home contract is on the hook to the funeral home. The estate should reimburse that person as a priority expense. If the estate is thin or the executor is slow, the signer waits. If you are a sibling who disagrees with the plan, you are not responsible for the bill unless you signed. If you want to avoid a squabble over cost, ask the family to agree on the budget and the payer before signing.

Federal rules give you the right to an itemized price list before you sign anything, and [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral) explains how to use it.

## What if the funeral is already over?

If the service happened without you, there is usually little the law will do about it. A court can rarely undo a burial, and an exhumation requires strong reasons. The practical path is to talk, ask for a memorial or a scattering ceremony, and focus your energy on the part of the death that still has a legal timetable, such as the estate. Our [after-a-death checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) shows what comes next.

Family friction often lingers past the funeral. If the same people are now fighting over the will or the house, [what to do when siblings fight over a parent's estate](/learn/what-if/siblings-fight-over-a-parents-estate) explains how to keep it from taking over.
