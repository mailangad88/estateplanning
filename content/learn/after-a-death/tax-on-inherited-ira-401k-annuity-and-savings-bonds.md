Inheritance is not income. That is the general rule, and it is what most people have in mind when they ask whether they will owe tax. A house, a bank account, a car and a brokerage account all pass to heirs without income tax on receipt.

Retirement accounts, annuities and savings bonds are the exceptions. They hold money that was never taxed, so the tax follows the money to you. This page explains what each one costs, how the timing works and what choices you have. For the broader picture of what heirs owe, see the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) and our guide to [taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## Why are these assets taxed when a house or stock is not?

Because the tax was postponed, not skipped. The owner put pre-tax dollars into an IRA or 401(k), or let interest build inside an annuity or a savings bond. The government was waiting to tax that growth when the money came out. At death, the wait does not end. It passes to the beneficiary.

By contrast, most assets get a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) at death, which resets the cost basis to the date-of-death value and wipes out the owner's built-in gain. Retirement accounts, annuities and savings bonds are called income in respect of a decedent, and they do not get that reset.

| Inherited asset | Taxed when you receive it? | Taxed when you cash out? | Step-up in basis? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional IRA or 401(k) | No | Yes, ordinary income | No |
| Roth IRA or Roth 401(k) | No | Generally no | Not applicable |
| Non-qualified annuity | No | Gain is taxed | No |
| Series EE or I savings bond | No | Interest is taxed | No |
| Life insurance death benefit | Generally no | Not applicable | Not applicable |
| House, stock, mutual funds | No | Only gain after date of death | Yes |

## How is an inherited traditional IRA or 401(k) taxed?

Every dollar you withdraw is added to your income that year and taxed at your ordinary rate. Because the tax is progressive, the timing of withdrawals matters more than almost any other decision you make with the account.

Take Marcus, 52, who earns a good salary and inherits a $400,000 traditional IRA from his mother. If he takes all of it at once, most of the money will land in the top federal brackets in a single year. If he spreads it across ten years, each year's piece stacks on top of his salary, and the total tax can be much lower. State tax may also apply, depending on where he lives.

Now consider his younger sister Alana, 34, who has a lower income and might do the opposite. She may prefer to take larger withdrawals now, while she is in a lower bracket, and keep the balance small for later.

There is no one right answer. The goal is to avoid pushing yourself into a bracket you do not need to reach. A tax preparer can model the options in an hour.

## What is the 10-year rule?

Most beneficiaries who are not a spouse must empty an inherited IRA or 401(k) by the end of the tenth year after the owner's death. Eligible designated beneficiaries are the exception, including a surviving spouse, a minor child of the owner until majority, a disabled or chronically ill person and someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner. They have other choices, such as stretching withdrawals over a life expectancy.

Two parts catch people out.

1. **Yearly minimums may apply inside the 10 years.** If the owner had already started required minimum distributions, most non-spouse heirs must take a withdrawal each year and still empty the account by year ten. If the owner had not started, no yearly minimum applies, only the deadline.
2. **Missing a required withdrawal carries a penalty.** The IRS has granted relief in some past years, but you should not count on it.

Our page on [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) goes through who is in each group.

## Can you roll over an inherited IRA?

A spouse can. A surviving spouse can treat the account as their own, which restarts the clock on their own required minimum distributions, or can keep it as an inherited IRA, which can be better for someone under 59 and a half who needs the money. Our comparison of a [spousal rollover and an inherited IRA](/learn/beneficiary-designations/spousal-rollover-vs-inherited-ira) goes through the trade-offs.

Everyone else uses a **direct trustee-to-trustee transfer**. The money moves from the owner's custodian to a new inherited IRA titled something like "Pat Lee, deceased, IRA for the benefit of Sam Lee." Never take a check payable to yourself. Once the money is in your hands, it is a distribution, and the whole amount is taxable that year. A non-spouse cannot do a 60-day rollover.

## How are inherited Roth accounts taxed?

Usually not at all, if the account was open at least five tax years. Withdrawals of contributions and earnings are then tax-free. If the five-year period is not met, the earnings can be taxable, though contributions are not.

The 10-year emptying rule applies to most non-spouse heirs of Roth accounts too. Because withdrawals are tax-free, there is no reason to rush. Many people leave the money to grow for the full ten years.

## How is an inherited annuity taxed?

Only the earnings are taxed, as ordinary income. The original after-tax premium comes back to you tax-free. If the annuity sits inside an IRA or 401(k), the entire amount is treated like the account it is in.

A non-qualified annuity usually offers several ways to take the money: a lump sum, payments over five years, or payments over your life expectancy. A spouse may be able to continue the contract. The choice affects the tax. Ask the insurer for the contract's death benefit options in writing and the cost basis. The insurance company reports your gain on a Form 1099-R.

## How are inherited savings bonds taxed?

Series EE and I bonds earn interest that is generally not taxed until the bond is cashed or matures. When the owner dies, that deferred interest is still untaxed. It has to be reported by someone. Either the executor includes all of it on the owner's final return, or the person who redeems the bond reports it, subtracting any amount the final return already included.

Treasury will reissue the bond in your name or pay you out, and you will need the death certificate and, where the estate is open, proof of authority. The interest is exempt from state and local income tax but not federal. Our guide to [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) shows where those choices are made.

## What about life insurance?

A death benefit paid in a lump sum is generally not income to the beneficiary. Interest the insurer pays on a delayed claim is taxable. The estate tax can reach policies the person owned, but most estates are under the exemption, which is $15 million per person in 2026. Our page on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) goes through claims and tax in more depth.

## Is there a deduction for estate tax paid on these assets?

If the estate paid federal estate tax and part of it was due to the IRA or annuity, you can deduct that share on your own return as you withdraw the money. Few families face this because few estates owe federal tax, but it matters at the top. Our [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) page explains who owes.

## What should you do first?

1. Do not cash out or take a check. Ask the custodian for the beneficiary claim forms.
2. Find the owner's date of birth and whether required distributions had started.
3. Decide whether you are an eligible designated beneficiary.
4. Move the account into an inherited IRA by direct transfer.
5. Ask a tax preparer to estimate each year's bracket before you choose a withdrawal plan.
