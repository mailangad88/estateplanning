The funeral is over, the lawyer has been called, and you want to know when the money will arrive. Maybe you need it for a mortgage, a tuition bill or just breathing room. Maybe you do not need it at all and simply cannot understand why nothing has happened in four months.

The honest answer is a range. This page explains what sets the clock, why the range is so wide, whether anyone can safely pay you early and what you can do while you wait. For the whole process after a death, see our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## What decides how fast an inheritance arrives?

The route the asset takes matters more than the size. Four routes cover almost every case.

| How the asset passes | Typical timeline | What controls it |
|---|---|---|
| Named beneficiary (life insurance, IRA, 401(k), POD account) | Weeks | Claim forms, death certificate, insurer review |
| Joint ownership | Days to weeks | Retitling with a death certificate |
| Living trust | A few months to over a year | Trustee's inventory, taxes, debts, sale of assets |
| Probate (will or no will) | Six to eighteen months, longer if contested | Creditor claim period, court schedule, tax filings |

None of these are promises. A small, simple probate in a fast county can close in under six months, and a contested one can run for years. Our guide to [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) breaks the stages down by court.

## Why does probate take so long?

Because the law builds in waiting periods to protect creditors, and nothing can be paid out until they run. The usual path looks like this.

1. **Open the estate.** The executor files the will and a petition and receives [letters from the court](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration). This alone can take a few weeks.
2. **Notify creditors.** The executor publishes notice and mails it to known creditors. A claim window then runs, commonly a few months. See the [creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period).
3. **Collect and value assets.** Bank balances, a house appraisal, a stock statement. Selling a house adds weeks or months.
4. **Pay debts, expenses and taxes.** That includes the final income tax return and, where needed, an estate tax return.
5. **File an accounting and ask for approval.** Some courts require it and some do not.
6. **Distribute.** Only now do residuary beneficiaries typically receive the balance.

Heirs often feel the gap in step 2 and 3, because nothing seems to happen. The work is real, it just is not visible. Our [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) guide shows what the executor is doing in that time.

## Why do some trusts distribute faster than probate?

A trust skips the court and its waiting periods, so a well-run trust can pay out in a few months. A trust is not automatically fast. The trustee still owes taxes, must deal with creditors and may need to sell the house. Many trusts have a set final distribution after a year or more. Our guide to [how long a trustee has to distribute assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-long-does-a-trustee-have-to-distribute-assets) goes through the common deadlines.

If a trust sets the money to be held for you until a certain age, you will not receive it at death at all. That timing is the owner's choice, written into the document.

## Can you get an inheritance early?

Sometimes, and only with the executor's or trustee's judgment. An early payment is called a partial or interim distribution. Executors make them when the estate is plainly solvent. Imagine an estate of $900,000 with $60,000 of known debts and expenses. Paying a daughter $100,000 early is low risk. The same payment from an estate with unclear tax or a pending claim is a gamble.

Before paying early, a careful executor does three things.

- **Confirms the estate can cover debts, taxes and expenses** and holds back a reserve.
- **Gets a signed receipt and a refund agreement** from the heir, which says the heir will return money if it turns out the estate needs it.
- **Keeps a record**, so the final accounting shows each payment.

If you want one, ask in writing, explain what you need it for and offer the receipt. Do not ask an executor to skip the creditors.

## What happens if an executor pays out too early?

The executor can be held personally responsible. A creditor with a valid claim can ask the court to make the executor pay it, and so can the tax authorities. Heirs who received money may be asked to return it, depending on the state. An executor is a fiduciary, and getting this wrong is one of the better-known ways to end up in court. Our page on [debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains the order of payment.

Corinne, 47, was the executor for her father. Her brother pressed her to split the cash right away, and she paid each of them $50,000 in the second month. Four months later, a medical provider submitted a $38,000 claim. Corinne paid it from the estate account, but the account was nearly empty. She asked each sibling to return part of what they got. It was awkward and slow, and could have been avoided by waiting for the claim period or holding back a reserve.

## What should you do while you wait?

Waiting is easier when you have information. Ask for these in writing.

1. **A list of what the estate or trust holds**, at least in outline.
2. **The expected steps and dates**, such as when the claim period ends and when the house will be listed.
3. **A copy of the will or trust** if you are a beneficiary. You are generally entitled to see it. Our guide on [seeing the will after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-see-the-will-after-someone-dies) covers your rights.
4. **Periodic updates**, say every month or two.

If months pass with no reply, our page on [what to do when a trustee does nothing](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-trustee-does-nothing) and the one on [removing an executor](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed) explain the options. Start with a polite written request. Many delays end there.

## How do you find out what you inherited?

If you are named in a will, the executor should tell you after the will is filed. If you are a trust beneficiary, the trustee has a duty to inform you, which we explain in the guide to [the trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account). If you are a named beneficiary of an account, you may not hear anything. The institution waits for you to make a claim. That is why it pays to ask relatives whether anyone named you.

## Should you pay off debt with the money?

Not before you know what you have, and not before taxes are clear. Once the money has arrived, paying off a credit card at 24 percent is hard to argue with. Keep a cash cushion. An inherited traditional IRA brings a tax bill, covered in our guide to [taxes on inherited retirement accounts](/learn/after-a-death/tax-on-inherited-ira-401k-annuity-and-savings-bonds). If you receive Medicaid or SSI, talk to a benefits advisor before the money lands, as described in [does an inheritance affect Medicaid or SSI](/learn/after-a-death/does-an-inheritance-affect-medicaid-or-ssi).
