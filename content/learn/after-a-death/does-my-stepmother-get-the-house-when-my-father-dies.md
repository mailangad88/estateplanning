Frank died in March. He was 74 and had been married to Joan for twelve years. His two adult children, Dena and Marc, are from his first marriage. Frank owned the house on Alder Street, where Joan still lives. Dena had always assumed it would eventually come to the children. Now she is not sure, and nobody wants to ask Joan.

If you are asking whether your stepmother gets the house when your father dies, the answer turns on four facts you can check this week: how the deed reads, whether there is a will or a trust, how long the marriage has run, and whether a prenuptial agreement exists. This page walks through each case for Illinois and New Jersey. It belongs in our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death), and it pairs with the planning side in our [what-if page on a parent who remarries](/learn/what-if/parent-remarries-and-new-spouse-inherits-everything).

## Who gets the house depends on the deed

Start with the recorded deed from the county. Do not rely on what anyone remembers.

| How the deed reads | What happens at your father's death | Needs probate? |
|---|---|---|
| Frank and Joan, husband and wife, as joint tenants or tenants by the entirety | Joan owns the whole house at once | No |
| Frank alone | The house is a probate asset. It goes under his will, or by state law | Yes |
| Frank and a child as joint tenants | The child owns it by survivorship | No |
| Frank and Joan as tenants in common | His half passes under his will or by state law, and Joan keeps her half | Yes, for his half |
| A living trust holds the house | The trust says who gets it | Not for the house |
| A transfer-on-death instrument names someone | That person takes it | No |

If the house was joint with survivorship and the couple were married, Joan owns it, and Dena and Marc have no claim. Our page on [joint tenants, tenants in common and tenants by the entirety](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenants-vs-tenants-in-common-vs-entirety) explains how each form works, and we have state pages for [Illinois joint tenancy](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety) and [New Jersey joint property](/learn/new-jersey/joint-property-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety-in-new-jersey).

## What if the house was only in my father's name?

Then it belongs to the estate, and two things decide who gets it: the will and the spouse's rights.

**If there is a will.** The executor must follow it. A will that leaves the house to the children is generally valid, but Joan can still claim against it. In Illinois, a spouse may renounce the will within seven months after it is admitted and take one-third of the estate if there are descendants, as covered in [a surviving spouse's rights in Illinois](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death). In New Jersey, a surviving spouse can elect one-third of the augmented estate, explained in [the New Jersey elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse). In Illinois, a spouse can also claim a spouse's award for support during administration.

**If there is no will.** State law divides the estate. In Illinois, a spouse and descendants split the estate half and half (755 ILCS 5/2-1), even when the children are not the spouse's own. New Jersey's rule differs when some children are not the spouse's, and our page on [who inherits without a will in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) explains the formula. In neither state does the spouse get the house by default. She gets a share of the whole estate, and the house is usually the largest part of it. Our page on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) has the order.

Here is an example with invented numbers for Illinois. Frank left no will. The house is worth $320,000 and he had $60,000 in other assets, for an estate of $380,000. Joan takes one-half, which is $190,000. Dena and Marc share the other $190,000. Because the house is most of the estate, either Joan buys out the children's share, the family sells, or the court divides the assets. Joan can use her half toward the house, and the children can accept payment instead of a deed.

## Can my stepmother stay in the house while the estate is open?

Almost always, yes. A widow living in the home does not have to move out while the estate is administered, and the executor has no power to evict a spouse with a legal right to the property. Illinois also keeps a homestead exemption for a surviving spouse, described in our page on the [Illinois homestead exemption after a death](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death). It protects equity from creditors. It is not a right to own the house.

For the adult child, the practical meaning is that the house will sit while the estate runs. Taxes, insurance and upkeep continue. Our guide on [staying in the house after a parent or spouse dies](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-stay-in-the-house-after-a-parent-or-spouse-dies) goes through who pays and what to put in writing.

## What if my father meant for me to get the house?

Then his documents need to say it in a way that binds your stepmother. A will that says "everything to Joan" leaves the children nothing, whatever he told them in conversation. The options that protect both Joan and the children are a life estate, a trust that lets Joan live in the house for life and then passes it on, or a properly drafted marital agreement. See [the family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) for how each works. Those need to be in place before death. After death, the only change is a family agreement, which Joan may or may not sign.

## What should I do right now?

Take these steps, in this order.

1. **Get the recorded deed** from the county recorder or clerk's office. It costs little and answers the first question.
2. **Find the will, any trust and any marital agreement.** Ask Joan, check the lawyer's file, and look at the court records. See our page on [finding a will after a death in Illinois](/learn/illinois/finding-a-will-after-a-death-in-illinois) or [in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/how-to-find-a-will-after-a-death-in-new-jersey).
3. **Do not change locks, remove belongings or list the house.** An early move can look like a power grab and turn a quiet estate into a lawsuit.
4. **Talk to Joan before talking to an attorney.** Ask what she plans. Many widows want to keep the house for a few years and have no intent to take the children's share.
5. **Ask for a written agreement.** A short document that says who pays taxes and insurance, how long she may stay, and what happens at sale can prevent a fight.
6. **Mark the deadlines.** An Illinois spouse has seven months after the will is admitted, and a New Jersey spouse has six months after a personal representative is appointed. The children have their own contest deadlines.
7. **Get a lawyer's view before signing anything.** Release forms, buyout offers and disclaimers all change rights.

## What if my stepmother and I disagree?

Most disputes are about timing and money, not ownership. A buyout, a sale with the proceeds split by the statutory shares, or a life-use agreement resolves most of them. If it goes to court, the judge looks at the deed, the will and the law, not at who was kind to your father.
