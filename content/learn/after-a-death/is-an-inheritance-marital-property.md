An inheritance arrives, and one question follows close behind it, often from a lawyer, a friend or a worried parent: if you divorce, does your spouse get half? The honest answer is that the inheritance usually stays yours, but the way you handle it in the first year can decide whether that stays true.

This page explains how courts classify inherited money, how it gets "mixed" into marital property, and how it can still affect support payments. It sits within our guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death), and we explain the tax side in [inheritance taxes for heirs](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## Is an inheritance separate property?

Yes, in nearly every state. Courts divide property into two groups. **Marital property** is what spouses acquire together during the marriage, mostly from work. **Separate property** is what one spouse owned before the marriage, plus gifts and inheritances received by that spouse alone.

The two broad systems in the United States reach the same place on this point.

| System | How it works | Inheritance |
|---|---|---|
| Community property states | Property earned during marriage is owned half and half | Inheritance stays separate |
| Equitable distribution states | A judge divides marital property fairly, not always evenly | Inheritance is usually set aside as separate, though some judges may weigh it |

So the starting rule is simple. If your aunt dies and leaves you $90,000, that money belongs to you and not to the marriage. The same is true of a piece of land from your grandfather, or a share of your parents' stock portfolio.

The rule describes ownership during the marriage too, not only at divorce. Your spouse cannot spend your inheritance without your consent, and creditors of your spouse generally cannot reach it.

## How can an inheritance become marital property?

Separate property loses its status in two main ways. Lawyers call them commingling and transmutation, and both are common.

**Commingling** means mixing the inherited money with marital funds until it cannot be traced. Maria, 47, deposits a $60,000 inheritance into the joint checking account that pays the mortgage and the school fees. Over two years, the balance rises and falls with paychecks and bills. When she divorces, she cannot show which dollars were hers. A court may treat the whole balance as marital.

**Transmutation** means the property changes character because of what you did with it. Examples:

- Putting your spouse's name on the deed to a house you inherited.
- Using inherited money as the down payment on a jointly titled home.
- Retitling an account into joint names "for convenience."

In many states, a joint title creates a presumption that you intended a gift to the marriage. You can sometimes argue otherwise, but the burden is on you and the result is uncertain.

What helps:

1. **Keep the inheritance in an account in your sole name.** Do not add deposits from paychecks.
2. **Keep the paper trail,** including the estate distribution statement and the first bank deposit.
3. **Avoid paying joint debts from it** unless you accept that you are treating it as shared.
4. **Do not retitle** an inherited home, even to make refinancing easier, without advice.
5. **Write down your intent** if you decide to give your spouse a share. A deliberate gift is fine, as long as you mean it.

The same logic applies when a spouse inherits. Your own protection depends on what you do after the estate pays out, which is why the first deposit matters.

## Does growth or income from the inheritance stay separate?

This is where states differ most. The usual view is that **passive growth stays separate.** If your inherited stock rises in value because the market rose, that rise is still yours. **Active growth may not.** If you or your spouse worked to improve an inherited rental house or build an inherited business, a court may treat part of the added value as marital.

Income is a separate question. In some states, income from separate property, such as interest or rent, is itself separate. In others, and in some community property states, that income is shared. If the facts matter to you, ask a family law attorney in your state. Do not rely on a general answer.

## What if the inheritance arrives during a divorce?

Timing creates confusion, so it helps to split it into three cases.

1. **You inherit before filing.** It is separate unless commingled. You will likely list it on your financial disclosure and the court will exclude it from division.
2. **You inherit while the divorce is pending.** Most states still treat it as separate because it is a gift to you alone. A few look at the "cutoff date" for marital property, and an inheritance received after that date is clearly separate.
3. **The person has not yet died.** An expected inheritance is a hope, not property, and courts generally do not divide it. But a judge may consider it as part of your financial prospects when dividing property or setting support, especially in a long marriage.

If the person who dies is the spouse you are divorcing, the situation is different. A pending divorce does not always end a spouse's rights, and an estate plan that has not been updated may still pay the spouse. Our article on [an ex-spouse who is still a beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary) covers a related problem, and [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) explains what to change once it is final.

## How does an inheritance affect child support and alimony?

Here is the part that surprises people. Even where the inheritance is protected from division, it can still raise what you pay.

Child support formulas in most states start with the parents' income, and many define income broadly. Courts commonly count one or more of these:

- The **interest, dividends and rent** that the inheritance produces.
- A **large one-time inheritance,** sometimes spread over several years, sometimes ignored as non-recurring, depending on the state.
- A parent's **ability to pay** from assets, even if the assets are not producing income. Some judges can deviate from the formula on that basis.

Alimony follows similar logic. A judge looks at the need of one spouse and the ability of the other to pay. An inherited fund strengthens the payer's ability to pay and can reduce the recipient's need if the recipient is the heir.

A change after a support order is made can sometimes be raised by a petition to modify. If you inherit after a divorce and you are the payer, expect the other side to ask. If you are the recipient, the payer may ask to reduce support. In both cases, keep records of how the money is invested, since the income it generates, not the principal, often carries the weight.

## Can the person leaving the inheritance protect it?

Yes, and this is the most reliable approach. The gift-giver, not the heir, controls how it passes. A parent who wants to protect a child's share from a future divorce can leave it in a trust instead of outright. A well-drafted trust holds the assets in the trustee's name, gives the child limited access and can include a **spendthrift clause** that keeps creditors, and often an ex-spouse, from reaching the principal. See our article on [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) for how those work.

A few cautions follow. A trust the child controls fully and mixes with marital funds loses much of its protection. A trust distribution that the child deposits into a joint account is still a deposit into a joint account. And no trust fully insulates income that a court counts for support. Parents in a blended family also have to decide how their plan affects a second spouse, which our guide on the [spouse elective share](/learn/blended-families/spouse-elective-share-and-disinheriting-a-spouse) explains.

If you are the one planning, our [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) is a place to start thinking about whether an outright gift or a trust fits your family.

## What about an inheritance and a prenuptial agreement?

A prenuptial agreement can name inheritances, present and future, as separate property and describe how to keep them separate. It can also deal with income and growth, which the default rules do not. A prenup signed before the wedding, with full disclosure and time to read it, is the strongest tool a couple has for this purpose. See [prenuptial agreements and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).
