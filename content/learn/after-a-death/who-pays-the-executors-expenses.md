Marcus is the executor for his mother's estate in Bergen County. In the first six weeks he paid a $1,200 locksmith and lawn bill, a $400 home insurance premium and the first month of utilities on his own credit card. Now the estate bank account is open. He wants to know whether he can pay himself back, and who decides what counts.

The estate bears the cost of settling itself. This page explains which expenses qualify, how to get repaid, how an executor's fee differs, and the habits that keep an executor out of trouble. If you are still working out the job, start with our overview of [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) or the [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## What expenses does the estate pay?

Reasonable and necessary costs of administering the estate and protecting its property. In practice, the list looks like this.

| Category | Examples | Typical timing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Court and filing | Probate filing fee, certified copies, letters | Early |
| Notice | Newspaper publication in Illinois, mailings to creditors | First months |
| Bond | Surety premium if the court requires one | At appointment |
| Professionals | Attorney, accountant, appraiser, estate sale company | As needed |
| Property | Insurance, utilities, repairs, lawn, snow removal, locksmith | Monthly |
| Storage and moving | Storage unit, haul-away | During cleanout |
| Taxes | Return preparation, filing fees | At tax time |

Funeral costs are also paid by the estate, usually first; see [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral). For the Illinois items, our page on [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) lists them one by one. For New Jersey, the court costs are in [probate costs in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/probate-costs-in-new-jersey).

Not every expense qualifies. A trip to visit relatives, a new roof on a house you plan to keep, or a gift to a beneficiary is not an administration expense. If you are unsure, ask before spending.

## Who pays first when the executor has no cash yet?

This is the usual situation. Letters of appointment arrive, but the bank takes a week or two to open the estate account. Some bills cannot wait. Insurance lapses and utilities freeze pipes in winter.

An executor may advance the money and be repaid. Do it in a way that leaves a clean record.

1. Pay by check or card and keep the statement.
2. Save the receipt or invoice, with the date and what it covered.
3. Write a one-line note on why it was necessary, such as "homeowner's policy renewal; house vacant."
4. Open the estate account as soon as letters issue. Use the estate's tax ID, not your Social Security number.
5. Reimburse yourself by estate check with a memo that references the receipt. Do not simply withdraw cash.
6. Keep the paperwork in one folder or spreadsheet.

If the estate will not have cash for months, tell the beneficiaries what you have advanced. Asking them to approve repayment in writing is the simplest protection against questions later.

## Is reimbursement the same as the executor's fee?

No, and mixing them up causes arguments. Expenses repay money that was spent. Compensation pays for the executor's own time and skill.

- **New Jersey.** Commissions follow a schedule in the statute: 5% on the first $200,000 of the estate, 3.5% on the excess up to $1,000,000 and 2% above that. Income earns a separate 6% (N.J.S.A. 3B:18-14, 3B:18-13). The details are in [New Jersey executor commissions and the probate timeline](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-executor-commissions-and-probate-timeline).
- **Illinois.** There is no set percentage. An executor is entitled to reasonable compensation, and the court can review it. Many family executors waive it, particularly when they are also a beneficiary.

A family member who waives compensation can still be reimbursed for expenses. Each is a separate line in the final account. Some executors also decide to take no fee because executor pay is taxable income, whereas an inheritance is not.

## What mistakes get executors into trouble?

Most are about records, not about bad faith. Our attorneys see the same few patterns.

- **Using a personal account for estate money.** Mixing the two is called commingling. Even if every dollar is accounted for, it looks bad and takes hours to untangle.
- **Reimbursing without receipts.** If a beneficiary challenges an item with no paper behind it, the executor may have to repay it.
- **Paying family or friends for services at inflated rates.** Hiring a sibling to clean the house is possible, but the pay should be fair and disclosed.
- **Paying a lower-priority creditor first.** Administration expenses and funeral costs rank high, but credit card bills do not. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).
- **Distributing early.** Handing out money before the claim period and the expenses are known can leave nothing to pay the final bills.
- **Spending on property the executor wants to keep.** Upgrades to a house that a beneficiary will keep are a beneficiary cost, not an estate expense, unless the will says otherwise.

Executors who are worried about this can read about [hiring help and staying protected](/learn/after-a-death/executor-hiring-help-and-staying-protected). If a beneficiary thinks money has been misused, the possible remedies are covered in [can an executor be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

## How is reimbursement approved?

It depends on the state and on the estate.

In many Illinois estates, an executor who is acting under independent administration reimburses expenses without a court order, then reports on request. Where the court supervises, the account is reviewed before it is approved. In New Jersey, the executor usually prepares an accounting or obtains written releases from the beneficiaries, as described in [New Jersey court accounting or informal releases for an estate](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-court-accounting-or-informal-releases-for-an-estate). Either way, a clean file makes the closing quick, and it is covered in [probate inventory and estate accounting](/learn/probate/probate-inventory-and-estate-accounting).

An illustration with made-up figures: Marcus lists the $1,200 locksmith and lawn bill, $400 insurance premium and $350 first-month utilities, a total of $1,950. He sends the beneficiaries a one-page list with receipts attached and a note that he will reimburse himself on the first of next month. Nobody objects, and the estate pays him by check. That ten-minute step saved a later argument.

## What if the executor is also the main beneficiary?

Many are. The same rules apply. Because the executor is paying themselves from the estate, the record matters even more. Our page on [executor who is also a beneficiary](/learn/probate/executor-who-is-also-a-beneficiary) explains how to avoid a conflict. When all the beneficiaries are adults who agree in writing, the process is much simpler.
