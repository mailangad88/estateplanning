The phone rings at 4 a.m., or a nurse steps out of a room and closes the door behind her, or you walk into a bedroom and know. In every version, you are about to be asked a string of questions by people who do this every week while you are doing it for the first time.

Our [guide to what happens after a death](/learn/after-a-death) and the [checklist for the first days](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) covers the sequence that applies everywhere. This page covers what changes depending on where the person died, because the first calls, the paperwork and the delays are different in a hospital, a hospice bed, a nursing home, a kitchen floor and a hotel in another state.

## What happens when someone dies in a hospital?

The hospital does most of the early work. A physician or nurse pronounces the death and notes the time. A staff member, often a social worker or chaplain, calls the next of kin if they are not at the bedside. Someone will ask whether the person was an organ donor or whether the family wants to talk about donation. If the death was expected, you will usually be asked about an autopsy. A hospital autopsy needs written consent from the person with authority to give it, and you can say no.

You will then be asked which funeral home should collect the body. Choose one, or ask for a day to decide if you have no preference. Some hospitals will hold the body in the morgue for a short time, and some charge a fee after that. Do not sign a release that names a funeral home you have not spoken to, just because a form is in front of you.

Three practical points:

- **Belongings.** Ask for the inventory of what the person arrived with, including phone, hearing aids, wallet and wedding ring. Sign for what you take and keep the receipt.
- **Medical records and billing.** Request the final discharge summary later. The hospital bill goes to the estate, not to you, unless you signed as the "guarantor" on the admission paperwork. Read that line the next time you sign anything for a family member.
- **Organ and tissue donation.** If the person registered as a donor, the hospital works with the donation organization on timing. This can add a few hours before the funeral home can proceed. It is worth it for many families, and it is not mandatory.

## What happens when someone dies in a nursing home or assisted living?

The facility has a written procedure, and the staff know it well. A nurse pronounces or confirms the death, and the attending physician is called to certify. The facility calls the family and the medical director. It will ask which funeral home to call, and many require a pickup within a few hours.

What families often miss is the money side. A resident's room has to be cleared, usually within a few days, and the facility will tell you the date. Ask about any prepaid balance, since many facilities bill monthly in advance and owe a refund for the unused days. If the resident was on Medicaid, the state may later seek repayment from the estate; our guide on [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery) explains what can and cannot be claimed.

If the resident died with a roommate, the staff will handle privacy for the roommate's sake, and you may be asked to come at off hours. Ask for the person's personal items to be bagged and labelled, and take photographs of furniture or art you want to keep before anything is moved.

## What do you do when someone dies in hospice?

Call the hospice's 24-hour number, not 911. The hospice nurse will come, pronounce the death and call the physician. Many people forget this step in the moment. A 911 call at an expected death can bring paramedics who are required to try resuscitation unless they see a valid do-not-resuscitate order, and that is exactly what the family and the patient wanted to avoid. A signed [DNR or POLST](/glossary/polst) form on the refrigerator or by the bed is meant for this moment.

Hospice usually has a plan for the hours after death:

1. The nurse pronounces the death and records the time.
2. The nurse can dispose of leftover medications, especially opioids, so they do not remain in the house.
3. Hospice calls the funeral home you selected, or helps you choose one.
4. A social worker or chaplain checks in and explains the bereavement support the program offers, which often lasts a year or so.

There is no rush to move the body. Some families sit with the person for an hour or two, call relatives and then ask the funeral home to come. Our article on [hospice and comfort care orders](/learn/healthcare-directives/palliative-care-hospice-and-comfort-care-orders) explains what these programs do while the person is still alive.

## What if the death is sudden or unexpected?

Call 911. Police and paramedics will come, and if resuscitation is not appropriate they will say so. The scene can feel invasive. Officers may ask when you last saw the person, what medications they took and whether anything seemed out of the ordinary. These are routine questions, and they do not mean anyone suspects you.

Then the coroner or medical examiner steps in. Their job is to establish the cause and manner of death when no physician has been treating the person, or when the death is sudden, violent or unexplained. Whether your state uses a coroner, who is often elected, or a medical examiner, who is a physician, the process is similar. Family consent is not needed for an official autopsy, and in an official investigation your wishes about it carry little weight.

Expect some practical consequences:

- The body may be taken to a county facility, and release to the funeral home can take a day or several.
- The death certificate may show the cause as "pending" until reports come back. Funeral homes can still file the certificate, and an amended one follows. Ask for certified copies once the cause is entered, because insurers sometimes will not accept the "pending" version.
- The home may be sealed for a short time in cases that look suspicious. That is rare.

If you disagree with the findings, that is a slower path. Talk to a lawyer before you pay for an independent review, particularly if a life insurance payout depends on the cause.

## What if the person dies in another state or abroad?

Call a funeral home in the city where the death occurred. Ask them to coordinate with a funeral home at home, if you want the body returned. This usually costs more than a local service, and families who realize this early often choose cremation near the place of death and carry the ashes home. Cremated remains travel far more easily, though airlines and the Transportation Security Administration have their own container rules.

The death certificate is issued by the state where the person died. The state of residence, not the state of death, normally controls probate. If the person owned real estate in the state of death, a second court case, called [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate), may be needed there. Our guide to [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) shows how that works.

For a death in another country, the U.S. consulate helps with local procedures and issues a consular report of death abroad, which can serve as proof of death. Allow extra time and extra certified copies, and expect translation and authentication steps that vary by country.

### An example

Aaron, 61, lived in Ohio and died of a heart attack on a visit to Arizona. His daughter Priya flew out the next morning. A local funeral home told her shipping Aaron home would cost several times the price of cremation in Phoenix. She chose cremation, scheduled a memorial service in Ohio three weeks later, and ordered twelve certified copies from the Arizona vital records office. Because Aaron owned only his Ohio house, probate stayed in Ohio and no second case was needed.

## Which calls come first, wherever it happens?

| Setting | First call | Who pronounces | Typical delay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hospital | Staff call you | Physician or nurse | Hours, longer if donation or autopsy |
| Nursing home | Staff call you | Nurse, then physician certifies | Hours; room cleared within days |
| Hospice at home | Hospice nurse line | Hospice nurse | Often none; family sets the pace |
| Unexpected, at home | 911 | EMS, then coroner or examiner | A day or more if investigated |
| Another state | Local funeral home | Local physician or coroner | Days for transport permits |

Whatever the setting, the next step is the same: pick a funeral home, order death certificates and begin the notification list in our guide on [who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies). The [funeral cost guide](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral) explains who owes the bill.

## What should you not do in the first hours?

Do not sign a funeral contract on the spot because a facility pushed a form at you. Do not move or discard medications, papers or devices at the home until you have photographed them, particularly after an unexpected death. Do not promise a sibling a piece of jewelry on the drive home. Do not post the news on social media before close relatives have been called.

If you are not sure who has the legal right to make arrangements, ask. State law ranks the spouse, adult children, parents and siblings, and a named agent may also have authority. Family disagreements about the service are common and are better settled before the funeral home starts work.
