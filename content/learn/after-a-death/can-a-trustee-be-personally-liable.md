Naming a family member as trustee sounds like an honor. It is also a legal job with real exposure, and many people accept it without hearing that part. When Daniel agreed to be trustee of his late aunt's trust, he assumed that if he meant well, nothing could go wrong. Then a cousin asked why the house had been sold below the listing price, and Daniel realized he had no paper showing how he picked the buyer.

This article explains when a trustee must pay out of their own pocket, the mistakes that cause it, and what a careful trustee does to stay on the safe side. It is part of our wider guide to [what to do after a death](/learn/after-a-death).

## What does personal liability actually mean?

It means a court can order you, not the trust, to pay. A trustee who breaches a duty and causes a loss is generally responsible for putting the trust back where it would have been. That can include the lost amount, the interest or growth the money would have earned, and sometimes the other side's legal costs.

A trustee is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary-duty). The law holds fiduciaries to a higher standard than ordinary business dealings. In most states that follow the Uniform Trust Code, the main duties are loyalty, prudence, impartiality between beneficiaries, following the trust's terms, keeping trust property separate and clearly identified, and keeping beneficiaries informed. A breach of any of them can create liability.

What the law does not do is demand perfection. A trustee who acts in good faith, gathers information, asks for advice and makes a reasoned decision is on solid ground even if the decision turns out poorly. Hindsight is a weak argument in court. Process is a strong one.

## Which mistakes cause the most trouble?

Courts see the same errors repeatedly. Most are not villainy. They are shortcuts.

| Mistake | Why it creates liability | Safer approach |
|---|---|---|
| Putting trust money in a personal account | Mixing funds breaks the duty to keep property separate | Open a trust account under the trust's tax ID |
| Paying yourself without authority | Self-dealing, even at a fair price | Take only fees the trust or law allows, and record them |
| Distributing too soon | The money is gone when bills or taxes appear | Hold a reserve until debts and returns are done |
| Missing tax filings | Penalties and interest are a trustee's problem | Calendar every deadline and use a CPA |
| Favoring one beneficiary | Duty of impartiality | Treat each beneficiary according to the trust, and explain decisions |
| Buying trust property yourself | Conflict of interest | Avoid it, or get court or beneficiary approval in writing |
| Leaving a house empty and uninsured | Lost value from a fire or break-in | Insure and inspect from week one |
| Ignoring a written request | Breach of the duty to inform | Answer in writing, even if the answer is "not yet" |

Take distributing too soon. Our article on [how long a trustee has to distribute assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-long-does-a-trustee-have-to-distribute-assets) explains why a trustee who pays everyone out in month three can end up personally covering a tax bill in month ten.

## Are you liable for investment losses?

Only if the investing was imprudent. Under the prudent investor rule, which most states follow in some form, a trustee must look at the whole portfolio, consider the trust's purpose and the beneficiaries' needs, diversify unless there is a good reason not to, and keep costs reasonable.

Two examples show the line. Priya inherits the trustee job and holds a diversified account that falls with the market for a year. Priya did nothing unusual, and the loss is not a breach. Walt, in the same position, sells the diversified account and puts everything into a friend's startup. When the startup fails, Walt owes the trust the loss.

The trust can also change the standard. A trust may tell the trustee to hold a family business or a single stock, and then holding it is not a breach even if the value falls. If you are unsure, ask the lawyer to read the trust language before you sell anything or hold anything.

## How do you protect yourself as a trustee?

Most protection comes from habits you can start the week you accept the job.

1. **Read the trust and any amendments, start to finish.** Underline anything that restricts you. Duties come from the document first, then from state law.
2. **Open a separate trust account.** After you get a tax ID, as described in our guide to the [trust EIN and 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election), put every dollar through that account.
3. **Keep a ledger.** Record each receipt and each payment with a date, an amount and a reason. Save invoices and statements.
4. **Insure and secure property.** Call the insurer, tell them about the death, and ask what a vacant house requires.
5. **Hire the professionals the task calls for.** An appraiser for a house and a CPA for the returns are ordinary expenses, and most states say a trustee who relies in good faith on qualified advice is better protected. Our guide on [whether you need an attorney to settle a trust](/learn/after-a-death/do-you-need-an-attorney-to-settle-a-trust) covers when a lawyer is worth the cost.
6. **Communicate in writing.** Send beneficiaries the notices and reports your state requires, and answer their questions. See [the trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account).
7. **Pay out slowly and in stages.** Keep a reserve, then make final distributions only after the returns are filed and the bills are paid.
8. **Get releases when you distribute.** Ask each beneficiary to sign a receipt and release that matches what they got.

## Can the trust protect the trustee?

Sometimes, within limits. Many trusts contain exculpatory language, which reduces liability for ordinary mistakes. Courts read these clauses narrowly. They do not usually protect a trustee from bad faith, reckless indifference or self-dealing, and some states limit them when the trustee wrote the clause for themselves.

A formal accounting can help too. When beneficiaries receive a full report and sign off, or a court approves it, later claims about anything disclosed become much harder. That is a reason to account fully rather than to share as little as possible.

A trustee can also be reimbursed for legitimate costs out of trust property, including the cost of defending a claim that fails. If the trustee lost the case, the court can deny reimbursement, which is where personal liability comes in. For what trustees may charge for their own time, see [trustee compensation](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation).

## What if you inherit a trust that already has problems?

Successor trustees often find a mess: missing statements, an unpaid tax bill, or a house that has sat for a year. You are not automatically on the hook for what came before, but you do have a duty to take reasonable steps once you know. That usually means requesting the earlier trustee's records, arranging an accounting, securing assets and telling the beneficiaries.

Document what you found on day one, with photographs and copies. A dated note saying "the water was off and the roof leaked when I took over" can matter later. Our article on [what to do when a trustee dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-a-trustee-dies) covers the transition steps when the prior trustee is gone.

## When should a trustee talk to a lawyer?

Earlier than feels necessary. These are the moments where an hour of advice often saves a lot of money:

- A beneficiary threatens to sue, or hires a lawyer.
- The trust is unclear, or two provisions seem to conflict.
- The trust holds a business, rental property or real estate in more than one state.
- A beneficiary is disabled, a minor or on [Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery), where a direct payment could cost them benefits.
- You discover an error, such as an early or incorrect distribution.

If you realize you have made a mistake, say so early and fix it. Courts treat a trustee who corrects a problem quickly far better than one who hides it. Planning ahead also matters on the other side of the table: when you are [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) for your own plan, pick someone who will ask for help.
