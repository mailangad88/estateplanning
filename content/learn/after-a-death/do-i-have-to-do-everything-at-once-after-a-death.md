Everyone tells you what you must do after a death. Very few say what you do not have to do yet. The result is a family sitting at the kitchen table at midnight with a pile of mail and the feeling that every day of delay costs something.

Mostly it does not. This page separates the few things that cannot wait from the many that can, shows where the real deadlines fall, and gives some practical ways to carry the work while you are grieving. For the full step list, see our [guide to what happens after a death](/learn/after-a-death) and the [checklist for the days after a death](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

## What really cannot wait?

A short list, and most of it is common sense.

- **Arrangements for the body.** A funeral home is needed within a day or two, and a death certificate is issued through them.
- **Safety of the home.** Lock up, collect valuables, make sure the heat or air conditioning works, and arrange for pets, plants and perishable food.
- **Medications and sensitive papers.** Remove leftover prescriptions, especially opioids, from where visitors can reach them.
- **Vehicle and home insurance.** Tell the insurer. A vacant house can fall outside normal coverage after a period set by the policy.
- **Time-sensitive bills tied to a person who needs help.** If a surviving spouse or dependent relies on the same income, check that rent, utilities and a mortgage payment are covered for the next month.

Everything else is scheduling. If you only manage the items above in the first week, you have done what the situation demanded.

## When do the real deadlines fall?

Fewer and later than most people expect. State rules differ, so treat the right-hand column as a guide to what to ask, not a promise.

| When | What | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| First week | Funeral, certified death certificates, secure the home | Order extra copies in the first order |
| Within a few weeks | Notify Social Security, employers, insurers and the pension plan | See [who to notify](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies) |
| Soon after death | Locate the will and give it to the court if required | Some states set a short window to file; check yours |
| Month 1 to 3 | Open probate if needed, get the executor's authority | Letters from the court unlock accounts |
| Set by the state | Creditor claim period | Often several months after notice |
| Following April | Final income tax return for the year of death | Estate and trust returns have their own dates |
| Nine months | Federal estate tax return, if the estate is large enough | Six-month extension available when requested on time |

For 2026, the federal estate tax exclusion is $15 million per person, so most families have no estate tax return at all. Our guides to [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) and the [estate tax return](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) explain who has to file.

## What should you not do quickly?

The mistakes that cost money tend to come from speed, not delay.

- **Selling the house.** The market will be there in six months. A hurried sale can mean an underpriced home, a tax surprise, or a sale that the probate court has to approve. Our guide on [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) explains the order of steps.
- **Distributing belongings.** Give nothing away until the will is found and read, and until you know about debts. A sibling who takes the jewelry before probate opens can cause lasting resentment, and an executor has to account for it.
- **Closing accounts.** Closing a bank or card account ends the paper trail and can cause problems if bills are auto-paying. Leave them open until the executor decides.
- **Paying every bill that arrives.** Creditors send statements as though the debt is yours. It usually is not. Pay costs that protect the estate, like insurance and utilities, and hold the rest for the executor. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).
- **Trusting the first caller.** After a death, scammers phone about unpaid debts, sell "estate services," and offer to find lost assets for a percentage. Verify anyone who contacts you out of the blue.
- **Taking large financial steps.** Financial planners often suggest waiting several months before major investment or lifestyle decisions after a loss. That is sound advice, and it applies to the beneficiary of a life insurance payout as much as anyone.

## How do you carry the work while grieving?

Grief changes how the mind works. People forget appointments, reread letters three times, and feel guilty for being tired. These are ordinary effects, and a few habits help.

1. **One folder, one notebook.** Everything paper goes in the folder. Every call goes in the notebook with the date, name and what was promised.
2. **Two fixed work blocks a week.** Thirty to sixty minutes, same time. Between blocks, the estate is not allowed to interrupt.
3. **A second person on the important calls.** Someone calm who does not share your grief takes notes and asks the questions you will forget.
4. **A single message to the family.** One email a week, even a short one, answers the "what is happening?" questions and cuts the number of calls.
5. **Say "I will call you back."** You are allowed to end any call, from a bank or a funeral home or a relative, to think.
6. **Use the support that exists.** Hospice programs often offer bereavement counselling to families for a year or so after the death. Many employers have free counselling, and clergy and community groups do too.

If you cannot sleep, cannot eat, or find yourself unable to make small decisions for weeks, tell a doctor. That is not weakness, and no executor's job is worth losing your health over.

### An example

Monica, 47, was named executor for her father. Three days after the funeral she had a spreadsheet of 41 tasks and a sibling texting her daily about the house. She talked to an attorney, who sorted the list into four groups: do now, do this month, do after the court appointment, do later. Fourteen items were due in the first month, six were the attorney's job, and eleven were optional until the sale decision. Monica scheduled the two work blocks, sent her siblings one email each Sunday, and did not touch the house until the fourth month. She told us the relief of being allowed to wait was larger than the relief of getting things done.

## Can an executor get help?

Yes, and it is often the wisest decision. An executor can hire an attorney, an accountant, an appraiser and a house-clearing service and pay them from the estate, as long as the costs are reasonable. Our page on [executor help and staying protected](/learn/after-a-death/executor-hiring-help-and-staying-protected) explains what is allowed. If the job is too much, our article on an [executor who is overwhelmed or unable to serve](/learn/what-if/executor-overwhelmed-or-unable-to-serve) lays out the options, including stepping aside.

## What if someone is pressuring you to hurry?

Ask what the deadline is and who set it. A funeral director, a real estate agent, a sibling, a creditor and an investment salesperson all have reasons to want speed. A true legal deadline can be named, and it will have a date and a source. "The market is hot" or "we should settle this before the holidays" are not deadlines. Take a day. Take a week. The estate will still be there.
