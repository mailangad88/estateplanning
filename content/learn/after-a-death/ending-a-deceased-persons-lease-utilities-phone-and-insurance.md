The funeral is over and the mail keeps coming. There is an electric bill, a notice from the phone company, a renewal for an insurance policy, a landlord's letter about next month's rent. Each looks like a small thing, and together they pile up quickly.

Most people's first instinct is to cancel everything. That is usually a mistake, because a few of these bills protect something valuable. This page sorts the monthly obligations into three groups: keep, end and check. For the larger set of calls and notices, see [who to notify after someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/who-to-notify-after-someone-dies), and for the whole sequence, start with our [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death).

## Which bills should you keep paying for now?

Keep the ones that protect property until it is sold, transferred or distributed.

| Bill | Keep or end? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Homeowner's insurance on a house | Keep, and tell the insurer about the death | An empty house may be treated differently; ask about vacancy rules |
| Auto insurance on a car that still exists | Keep until the car is sold or retitled | A parked car can still be damaged or stolen |
| Electricity, gas and water at the house | Keep, in the estate's name if possible | Frozen pipes, mold and a dead sump pump cost far more than the bill |
| Property taxes and HOA dues | Keep | Unpaid taxes build liens and penalties |
| Mortgage payments | Keep, or make arrangements with the servicer | See [what happens to a mortgage after death](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) |
| Gym, magazines, streaming, meal kits | End | Pure cost; stop them at the card or bank |
| Phone line | Keep for a few months, then end | Used for security codes |

An empty house is where small savings turn into large losses. If the house will sit, read our page on [vacant inherited houses, insurance, repairs and squatters](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters) before you drop any coverage.

## What happens to a lease when the tenant dies?

In most states, death does not end a lease on its own. The lease is a contract, and the estate takes on the tenant's obligations. That means rent can keep accruing until the lease ends or is ended. In practice it works like this:

1. **Tell the landlord promptly.** Send a short letter with a copy of the death certificate. State who the contact is, usually the executor or the next of kin until one is appointed.
2. **Ask what the lease says about early termination.** Some leases, and some state laws, let the estate end the lease on 30 days' notice after a death.
3. **Ask the landlord to re-rent.** Many states require landlords to make reasonable efforts to find a new tenant, which reduces what the estate owes.
4. **Do the move-out.** Remove belongings by the date you agree on. Do not leave things for the landlord to dispose of, and do not toss anything of value, because it may belong to an heir.
5. **Get the deposit back.** The security deposit belongs to the estate. Ask for an itemized statement.

A roommate or spouse on the lease stays bound, and may be allowed to remain or move out. If the person lived in subsidized housing, ask the housing authority about its own rules, which can differ a lot. Some landlords have a right to hold the belongings for a short period, and the procedure for retrieving them varies by state. Our page on [clearing out a parent's house](/learn/after-a-death/clearing-out-a-parents-house-after-death) includes a short section for rental homes.

Nadia's father rented a one-bedroom apartment with eight months left. She wrote to the landlord within a week, offered to have the apartment empty in three weeks, and asked that the lease be ended on that date. The landlord agreed, kept part of the deposit for painting, and that was the end of it. She had not expected that a polite letter could cap what the estate owed.

## What happens to utilities, internet and mail?

Utility companies do not know someone has died until they are told. Call each one, give the date of death and the executor's contact details, and ask which of two courses they prefer:

- **Transfer the account into the estate's name.** Service continues, and the bill goes to the executor. This fits when the house is occupied, being sold or being cleared.
- **Close the account and have the heir open a new one.** This works when someone is moving in.

The estate pays the final bills as an administrative expense. Relatives are not personally liable for a utility account they did not sign. A utility that pressures a family member to pay a final bill out of pocket can be pointed to the executor.

Mail needs one decision. The executor can file a forwarding request with the post office, which usually asks for the death certificate and proof of authority. Go through the mail weekly. It is a list of what the person owned and owed, and the best clue to accounts you did not know existed, which our guide to [finding a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) uses as one of its first steps.

## What happens to the phone plan?

Do not close the line on day one. The phone number receives text codes for banks, email and other accounts. If it goes dead, you can lose the easiest route into those accounts. Read [what to do with a locked phone and the number](/learn/digital-assets/locked-phone-after-death-texts-two-factor-codes-and-the-phone-number) first.

Back up the photos and contacts, note which accounts text to this number, then ask the carrier to close or transfer the line. A family plan is simpler: the others stay as they are and the line comes off. If a device is still being paid off, the balance is a debt of the estate. Streaming and software subscriptions are covered in [subscriptions and purchased media after death](/learn/digital-assets/subscriptions-and-purchased-media-after-death).

## What happens to health insurance and Medicare?

Health coverage in the dead person's name ends at death. The consequences differ by situation.

- **An employer plan with covered dependents.** A spouse or child may be able to elect COBRA continuation coverage, generally for a period that can run to 36 months. The plan administrator has to send the notice, but call the employer instead of waiting.
- **A marketplace plan or a loss of coverage.** Losing coverage in this way usually opens a special enrollment period, commonly 60 days, to sign up for a new plan.
- **Medicare.** Social Security notifies Medicare, and premiums paid for time after death are refunded. A Medicare Advantage or drug plan should be told too. Our page on [Social Security after a death](/learn/after-a-death/social-security-after-a-death) shows what to expect.
- **Unpaid medical bills.** They are claims against the estate, paid in their place in line. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

Check the dates in your own plan documents, because the windows are short and vary by plan.

## Who is responsible for all of this?

The executor, once appointed, is the person with authority. Until letters are issued, the next of kin often handles the basics informally: calling the landlord, stopping a gym membership, forwarding mail. That is usually acceptable. Signing a new contract or paying a large bill from your own account is where you should stop and ask. Keep every receipt, because the estate reimburses the person who pays on its behalf.

A short list of the first calls, in order, saves a lot of confusion:

1. Insurance company for the house and car.
2. Landlord or mortgage servicer.
3. Utilities, to confirm service and move the account.
4. Phone carrier, to freeze rather than close.
5. Employer or health plan, about dependents' coverage.
6. Everything else, in the order the mail suggests.
