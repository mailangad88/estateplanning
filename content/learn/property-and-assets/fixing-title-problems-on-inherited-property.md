Dolores, 61, wants to sell the house her mother left her. The buyer's title company comes back with a short, polite letter: the deed names her mother and her mother's late husband, the legal description does not match the survey, and a 1996 mortgage was never marked paid. Closing is two weeks away.

Title problems are common on inherited property, because the paperwork was handled by a generation that kept things in a shoebox. This page explains how to find out what the record says and which repair fits which problem. It is part of our [real estate and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## First, find out who the record says owns the property

The county recorder's office, sometimes called the register of deeds or clerk, holds the deeds, mortgages and liens for every parcel. Most counties now post an index online, and the assessor's website shows the parcel number and the tax mailing address. You are looking for the last recorded deed into the owner, and anything recorded after it.

Do this before you pay anyone, because it tells you which of the problems below you actually have. If you hire a title company to run a search, ask for the full chain of title and a list of every item that would block a sale. That report is usually cheaper than a lawyer's hourly guesswork.

## What are the most common problems and their fixes?

| Problem | What it looks like | Usual fix |
|---|---|---|
| Typo or wrong legal description | Misspelled name, wrong lot number, boundary that does not match the survey | Corrective deed or scrivener's affidavit |
| Deed lost or missing | You never received a copy, or the original is gone | Certified copy from the recorder |
| Deed never recorded | A signed deed exists but nothing is in the county records | Record it, if the signer is still alive and valid; otherwise court help |
| Dead owner still on title | The record names someone who died years ago | Probate, heirship affidavit or small estate shortcut |
| Old lien or mortgage | A loan was paid but no release was recorded | Release from the lender, or a court order if the lender is gone |
| Unknown or missing heir | A relative may have a share | Court action, often quiet title |
| Boundary or access dispute | Neighbor claims land, or the lot has no road | Survey, agreement, or lawsuit |

## What if the deed has a typo or the wrong legal description?

A small error can often be fixed without a court. If the name is misspelled or a digit in the lot number is wrong, the original signer can sign a corrective deed or an affidavit that states what was meant, and it is recorded. The key is that the person who made the mistake, or their successors, must be available to sign. Lenders and title insurers each have their own forms, so ask them before drafting anything.

If the signer has died, the fix is harder. A court may be needed to reform the deed, and everyone with an interest generally has to be notified. For a wrong legal description, a new survey is worth buying first, so you know what the correct description is.

## What if the deed is missing or lost?

Losing the paper does not lose the ownership. The recorded deed is the legal record, and the recorder can issue a certified copy for a modest fee. Ask for the deed into your relative and any later deeds. If the deed was never recorded, the situation is more serious: until it is recorded, a purchaser or creditor without notice may have rights ahead of the family. Record it promptly if the signer is alive, and ask an attorney what to do if the signer has died.

## What if the deed names someone who has died?

This is the most common problem of all. If your father owned the house and has died, a sale cannot close in his name. Someone with legal authority has to sign. That means one of the following:

1. **Formal probate**, where a court appoints an executor or administrator who can sell or transfer the house. See [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate).
2. **A shortcut for small estates**, which some states allow for real estate after a waiting period. Check the rules in [small estate affidavits](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit), because real estate is excluded in many states.
3. **An affidavit of heirship**, accepted in some states for older estates and recorded to document who the heirs are.
4. **An out-of-state proceeding**, if the land sits in another state. The [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) article describes it.

If there was a co-owner, the answer may be shorter. When a spouse owned the house with survivorship, a death certificate and a short affidavit recorded with the county may be all you need. Our page on [a house still in a late spouse's name](/learn/what-if/house-still-in-late-spouses-name) goes through the common versions.

## What if the property is in a relative's name from decades ago?

You have to trace each death from the last recorded owner down to you. Suppose your grandfather bought land in 1958, died in 1989 without probate, and his wife died in 2004. Title may still be in his name. Each estate may need to be opened or resolved, and the heirs of each may need to sign or be accounted for.

This is how [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) problems start. Cousins who have never met may own fractional shares, and selling requires all of them or a court. Two practical things help. Start gathering death certificates and family trees right away, and avoid paying property taxes in a way that ignores co-heirs, because that can later become a dispute about who paid what. Many states now have laws that protect families in this position from forced sales at low prices.

## When do you need a quiet title action?

A quiet title lawsuit asks a court to declare who owns the land and wipe out competing claims. It is the tool when there is a real doubt that the other methods cannot resolve: an heir who cannot be located, a very old unrecorded claim, or a dispute over boundaries. You file in the county where the land sits, serve every known claimant, publish notice for unknown ones, and wait for a judgment. Expect months and, in contested cases, more.

It is also the expensive option, so check whether a corrective deed, an affidavit or a small probate would do the job first. Many title companies will tell you precisely what they need to insure the sale, which narrows the repair.

## What about liens and old mortgages?

An unreleased mortgage is a nuisance, not a lawsuit. If the loan was paid in 1996 and the lender never recorded a release, ask the successor lender for a satisfaction. If the lender is gone, many states have procedures to clear stale mortgages after a set number of years. Judgment liens, tax liens and a Medicaid estate recovery claim are different: those may be real debts that need payoff or negotiation. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) and [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery).

## What steps should you take now?

1. Pull the last deed and recorded documents from the recorder.
2. Order a title search or a lender-style report if a sale is planned.
3. Match the legal description to a survey if there is any boundary doubt.
4. List everyone who has died on the chain and who inherits from each.
5. Pick the lightest repair that satisfies the buyer or title insurer.
6. Gather signatures before the closing date is set, not after.

## How can you avoid leaving this behind?

Check your own deed. If the name is spelled wrong, or the property is in a deceased spouse's name, fix it while you can. A deed into a [living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) or a [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) means no court at all later. Keep the deed, survey and policy in one place and list them in a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction).
