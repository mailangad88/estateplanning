The word "homestead" shows up in three places at once, and people mix them up. It is on the property tax bill as an exemption. It appears in creditor law as a shield for your home. And in probate it is a spouse's right to stay in the family house. All three can matter after a death, and each is controlled by a different set of rules.

This page sorts out which is which, who ends up owning the house, and what to check if you are planning for a spouse or helping one now. It is part of our guide to [property and assets](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Who owns the house when one spouse dies?

Ownership is set by the title, not by the marriage certificate. Start by finding the deed.

| How the deed reads | What happens at the first death | Probate needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Joint tenants with right of survivorship | Survivor owns it all | No, once the death is recorded |
| Tenants by the entirety (where allowed) | Survivor owns it all | No |
| Community property with survivorship (where allowed) | Survivor owns it all | No |
| Community property, no survivorship | Half is the deceased spouse's, and passes by will or law | Usually yes |
| Deceased spouse's name only | Passes by will, or by intestacy | Yes, unless in a trust or TOD deed |
| Tenants in common | Deceased spouse's share passes by will or law | Yes, for that share |
| Revocable trust | Passes per the trust | No |

Our article on [joint tenants, tenants in common and tenants by the entirety](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenants-vs-tenants-in-common-vs-entirety) explains the differences. If the deed is not clear or still carries a dead spouse's name, see [fixing title problems](/learn/property-and-assets/fixing-title-problems-on-inherited-property).

A widow whose husband owned the house alone and left no will might think it is hers. In many states, with children from a prior relationship, intestacy gives the spouse only part of the estate and the children the rest. That is how a widow can end up sharing ownership with stepchildren, as described in our article on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## What are the three kinds of homestead?

**1. The property tax exemption.** A reduction in the taxable value of the home you live in, or a credit. It usually requires an application, and often a deadline each year or one time only. Sometimes seniors, veterans or surviving spouses get a larger benefit.

**2. Creditor protection.** Many states protect some or all of a home's value from unsecured creditors. Some states cap the amount, while a few protect the home by acreage limits instead. This protection usually does not stop a mortgage lender, a tax lien or a lien for work done on the house.

**3. The surviving spouse's right to stay.** Many states let a surviving spouse, and sometimes minor children, remain in the family home after the owner's death, either for a period during probate or for life. In a few states the homestead cannot be left away from a surviving spouse or minor child by will. Texas and Florida are examples of states with strong homestead rules, and the details differ sharply, so check your own state.

These work differently, and a house can qualify for one and not another. A house held in a trust may keep the tax exemption and still lose creditor protection if the trust's terms do not meet the state's test.

## Does a surviving spouse have the right to stay in the house?

Often yes, at least for a while. Many states give the surviving spouse the right to live in the home while the estate is open, and some extend it. Our guide on [staying in the house after a parent or spouse dies](/learn/after-a-death/can-i-stay-in-the-house-after-a-parent-or-spouse-dies) describes the usual rules and what happens if the home must be sold.

The right usually has limits:

- **It does not pay the mortgage.** The spouse still needs to keep the loan, taxes and insurance current. See [what happens to the mortgage](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).
- **It may not outlast the spouse's life.** A right to occupy typically ends at the spouse's death or remarriage if a will creates it, and the house then passes to the next person.
- **It does not always protect against a sale.** If the estate has debts and nothing else to pay them, a court may order a sale in some states.
- **It may not apply in a second marriage.** If children inherit the house and the spouse has no homestead right, the spouse may have to leave. Our article on [the family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) explains how to give the spouse a secure right.

Consider Elaine, 68, whose husband Frank died last month. The house was in Frank's name alone, and his will leaves it to his two children from a first marriage. Elaine can usually stay while the estate is open, but the children could ask a court to remove her afterward in a state with weak homestead protection. In a state that limits who Frank may leave the home to, the will might not work as written. Frank could have avoided the whole dispute with a life estate or a trust, either of which gives Elaine a clear right to stay. Many states also give a spouse an elective share to claim instead of the will.

## What happens to the property tax homestead exemption?

It generally does not carry over on its own. The county ties the exemption to an owner who lives in the property, so after a death someone must tell the assessor. A surviving spouse may keep the exemption or qualify for a different one. A child who inherits and moves in may qualify for a new one. A child who inherits and rents out the house will lose it.

Deadlines matter. Many counties only grant the exemption starting in the year you apply. If a parent's exemption is cancelled and you wait to reapply, the next tax bill can jump. A phone call to the assessor is the quickest way to learn the process.

Transfers also change things. When you deed a house to a child, to a trust, or to a new spouse, the exemption may have to be reapplied for, and some states reassess the property. Our guide to [moving a house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) goes through how most counties treat a revocable trust.

## How do you protect the homestead in your plan?

1. **Read your deed.** Know whether the house passes by survivorship or through your will.
2. **Check your state's homestead rules.** Especially if you have a second spouse or children from a prior marriage.
3. **Decide what you want the spouse to have.** Ownership, a life estate, or a right to live there for a set time, with the house going to your children afterward.
4. **Use a trust or life estate if ownership is split.** A trust can say your spouse lives there, pays the expenses and the children inherit later.
5. **Keep the tax exemption alive.** Reapply after any retitling.
6. **Do not assume.** Ask a local attorney how your state treats a will that tries to leave the home away from a spouse.
