Elena, 71, owns a home in Evanston worth about $520,000 and has about $200,000 in savings and an IRA. She has two daughters. One, Rosa, lives nearby and would love to keep the house. The other, Teresa, lives in Phoenix and has no wish to own a house two thousand miles away. Elena's first idea is simple: house to Rosa, cash to Teresa. Her second thought, a minute later, is that $520,000 and $200,000 are not the same number.

This page, part of our [property and assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets), shows how to leave one child the house and another money without leaving a mess. For the general ways to pass a house, see [leaving your house to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children).

## Can I leave the house to one child and money to another?

Yes. Nothing requires you to divide each asset equally, and a will or trust can name a different person for each. The legal question is easy. The practical question is whether the gifts will be worth the same when you die, and whether your children will see the result as fair.

Three facts decide that:

1. **What the house is worth then**, not now. Prices move, and ten years is a long time.
2. **What cash and other assets remain.** You may spend savings on care. Medical and long-term care costs are the usual reason the "cash" side shrinks.
3. **What the house costs to hold.** The owner pays property tax, insurance, repairs and, if needed, selling costs.

## What are the ways to even it out?

There are four common tools. Many families use two together.

| Method | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Equalizing cash | Leave the other child cash or investments equal to the house | The cash may be spent down before you die |
| Life insurance | Name the other child as beneficiary of a policy sized to match | Premiums, and the policy must stay in force |
| Retirement account | Name the other child on the IRA or 401(k) | Income tax on withdrawals and the 10-year rule for most non-spouse heirs |
| Buyout in the trust | The child who keeps the house must pay the sibling the difference, with a note if needed | The child must be able to pay or refinance |

The retirement account option needs care. A dollar of IRA is not worth a dollar of house, because the heir pays income tax as it comes out. A house, by contrast, gets a step-up in basis at death, which can erase the capital gain. Compare after-tax values, not face amounts. The [step-up in basis guide](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) covers the mechanics.

## A worked example with numbers

This is an invented example. Elena's assets today:

- House, $520,000
- Savings, $90,000
- IRA, $110,000

Her plan says house to Rosa, everything else to Teresa. On paper Rosa receives $520,000 and Teresa $200,000. Elena then thinks again.

She changes the plan: Rosa gets the house, and Teresa gets the savings, the IRA and a $150,000 life insurance policy. Now Teresa is at about $350,000 before taxes on the IRA. The gap is smaller, but Rosa keeps the house and the costs that come with it. Elena adds a sentence to her trust: if the house is worth more than the combined other gifts at her death, Rosa will pay Teresa half the difference, up to a stated amount, within five years at a stated interest rate.

That clause, an equalizing payment, is what keeps the plan fair when prices move. Without it, a housing boom helps Rosa and a downturn helps Teresa.

## Should the house go to a child outright or in a trust?

A trust is usually better, especially for a house. A trust can name a successor trustee to sell if the child cannot keep it up, can set the buyout terms, and avoids probate for the house. Without a trust, an outright gift in a will goes through probate, and the executor holds the house until the estate settles. See [how a house in a living trust avoids probate](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).

Think also about what happens if the child who receives the house dies first, divorces or is sued. In a will, the gift to a child who dies before you may lapse and pass to that child's own children, or to the residue. In a trust, you can write what you want. A [trust for the child's share](/learn/trusts/keeping-an-inheritance-safe-from-a-childs-divorce-addiction-or-bad-spouse) can protect the house from a divorce or a creditor.

## What if the child who gets the house sells it right away?

That is allowed unless your plan says otherwise, and it changes the fairness math. If Rosa inherits a $520,000 house and sells it a month later, she walks away with cash, less selling costs, and no further obligation. Teresa may feel she was treated worse for the wrong reason. If this worries you, write the intent down. A trust can give Rosa the right to occupy the house for as long as she wants, with a buyout price payable to Teresa only when Rosa sells or after a fixed period. Or it can say that if the house is sold within five years, the sale proceeds are shared in a stated way.

You do not need to control every outcome. You need to decide which outcomes would feel wrong to you and cover those.

## Does the house value need an appraisal?

If the plan depends on a formula, yes, at the time of your death. Use a licensed appraiser who is independent of both children. Do not let the child who wants the house set its value. Name the appraiser or method in the trust, and say what date controls: the date of death, or the date of distribution. Also say who pays for the appraisal. Arguments over value are a frequent way for a fair plan to turn into a family fight.

## What about taxes in Illinois and New Jersey?

Neither state's death tax is likely to be the deciding factor for most families, but check. Illinois has an estate tax with a $4 million threshold, which can matter if the house plus other assets add up. New Jersey has an inheritance tax, but children are in the exempt class, so a gift to a child from a parent does not trigger it. Our pages on the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) and the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax) give detail. The federal estate tax only applies to estates above $15 million per person in 2026.

## What mistakes should I avoid?

- **Leaving the house to one child "because she lives there" and saying nothing to the other.** The silence is what hurts.
- **Fixing dollar amounts in the will.** "$100,000 to Teresa" becomes wrong if the estate changes.
- **Forgetting the mortgage and the maintenance.** A house with a loan is not equal to a bank account of the same value.
- **Naming one child as both sole owner and the person in charge of paying the other.** Put a neutral trustee in between.
- **Relying on a verbal promise.** If you want your plan followed, put it in a signed document. See [unequal shares and fairness](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).
