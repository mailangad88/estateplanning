Plenty of people inherit a house they do not want to live in and cannot quite bring themselves to sell. Renting it looks like a gentle middle path: the rent covers the taxes, the house stays in the family, and the decision waits. That can be a good plan. It also changes the tax picture, because the house stops being something you merely own and becomes a business.

This page is general information, not tax advice. It explains how an inherited rental is taxed and what to ask a CPA. If you only want to know about selling, read [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) instead. This guide is part of our [property and special assets cluster](/learn/property-and-assets).

## What is my starting tax basis in an inherited house?

For most inherited houses, your basis is the fair market value on the date the owner died. Tax professionals call this the step-up in basis. We explain it in detail on our [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) page. The point for a landlord is that your basis is not what the owner paid decades ago. It is today's value at the date of death, which resets the gain to zero and also resets the depreciation.

Get a written appraisal for the date of death, even if it feels like an expense you can skip. It supports your basis, it separates the land value from the building value, and it protects you if the IRS ever asks. A county tax assessment is a weak substitute, since assessments often lag the market and do not split land from improvements the way depreciation requires.

An example. Denise inherits her uncle's duplex. He paid $90,000 for it in 1984. At his death it appraises at $400,000, with $90,000 allocated to the land and $310,000 to the building. Denise's basis is $400,000, and her depreciable base is the $310,000 building.

## What can I deduct once I rent it out?

Rental income goes on Schedule E of your federal return, and ordinary and necessary expenses reduce it. The usual deductions are these.

| Expense | Notes |
|---|---|
| Mortgage interest | Only if there is a loan on the property you are responsible for. |
| Property taxes | Deductible against rent, not subject to the itemized deduction cap. |
| Insurance | Landlord policy premiums. |
| Repairs and maintenance | Small fixes only; big upgrades are depreciated. |
| Property manager and legal fees | Management fees, advertising, eviction costs. |
| Utilities you pay | If the lease says so. |
| Travel to the property | Reasonable and documented. |
| Depreciation | A paper deduction, explained below. |

Repairs and improvements are treated differently, and mixing them up is the most common mistake. A repair keeps the property in its current condition and is deducted in full that year. An improvement that adds value or extends the life, such as a new roof, a kitchen remodel or an addition, is added to your basis and depreciated over time.

Keep a separate bank account for the rental, a lease for every tenant and receipts for everything. Rental activity is audited at a rate that makes this worth the effort.

## How does depreciation work on an inherited rental?

Depreciation lets you deduct a portion of the building's cost each year, even though the building may be rising in value. For residential rental property, the IRS spreads the building's basis over 27.5 years. Land is never depreciated.

Depreciation starts when the house is ready and available to rent, not the day you inherit it. If you spend four months cleaning it out and repairing it before listing, the clock begins when it is truly ready for a tenant.

With Denise's duplex, $310,000 divided by 27.5 is roughly $11,270 a year of depreciation. If the duplex brings in $30,000 in rent and her cash expenses are $14,000, her taxable rental profit before depreciation is $16,000. After depreciation it falls to about $4,700. The deduction costs her nothing in cash.

There is a catch. When you sell, the IRS taxes the portion of the gain that came from depreciation you took (or could have taken) at a rate capped at 25% federally. Depreciation is a deferral, not an escape. It is still worth taking, because the deduction helps in the years you hold the property, and because skipping it does not avoid the recapture.

## Are rental losses deductible?

Sometimes. Rental activity is generally passive under federal rules, so losses can usually offset only passive income. There is an allowance for landlords who actively participate that lets a limited loss offset other income. It phases out as income rises and disappears at higher incomes. Unused losses carry forward and can be used when you sell. Have your tax preparer check which category you fall in before you count on a loss.

## Can I do a 1031 exchange with inherited property?

A 1031 exchange lets an investor sell investment property and defer capital gains tax by buying other investment property. It applies to property held for investment or in a trade or business. An heir who inherits a rental and keeps it in service can generally exchange it. An heir who moved into the house, or who holds it as a vacation home, usually cannot.

Three practical points:

1. **The step-up changes the math.** Your basis starts at the date-of-death value. If you sell soon afterward at about that price, there is little gain, and little to defer. A 1031 matters when the house has appreciated a lot since the death or when you have been depreciating it for years.
2. **The deadlines are firm.** Under the rules, you have 45 days to identify replacement property and 180 days to close, and the money must go through a qualified intermediary rather than your own account.
3. **Co-heirs complicate it.** If three siblings inherit and only one wants to roll into a new property, the structure of ownership matters. Talk to a tax adviser before anyone signs a sale contract.

## Do I pay tax when a house goes into my name, or when the estate transfers it?

No federal income tax on receiving it. If the house passes through probate, the executor may have to deal with the estate's tax return for income earned between death and distribution, which includes rent. See [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns). If the house was in a revocable trust, the trust becomes irrevocable at death and may need its own tax ID. Our guide to the [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election) explains how the trustee handles that.

A handful of states levy an inheritance tax on certain heirs. See [taxes on an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/inheritance-taxes-for-heirs).

## What should I do first if I plan to rent the house?

Work through these in order.

1. **Get the date-of-death appraisal** with the land and building values stated separately.
2. **Confirm who owns the house.** If the estate is still open, the executor controls it. Do not sign a lease before you have authority.
3. **Insure the house for rental use.** Tell the insurer it will be tenant-occupied.
4. **Set up a rental bank account and bookkeeping** from the first expense.
5. **Check local rules.** Many cities require rental registration, inspections or lead-paint disclosures.
6. **Decide on ownership structure.** One owner, co-owners or an LLC each have tax and liability effects. Our page on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) covers the risks of co-ownership in particular.
7. **Meet a CPA before filing.** The first year sets the depreciation schedule you will follow for decades.

## When does renting stop making sense?

When the net rent is small and the property needs repairs; when co-heirs disagree about management; or when the house is far from where anyone lives. Keeping a house the family cannot manage can cost more than it earns. If the heirs are drifting apart on this, the guide on [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) sets out your options.
