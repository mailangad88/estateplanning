Dolores, 71, plans to leave her lake house to her two sons. She has owned it for thirty years and thinks of it as a gift. What she has not thought about is the 1994 covenant that bars year-round residence, the flood map revised two summers ago, or the buried heating-oil tank the previous owner mentioned once at closing. Her sons will inherit all three along with the porch swing.

A house comes with a paper history and a physical one. Most families look at neither until a buyer's lender or an inspector does it for them. This article shows what to check, in what order, and what a family can do when the answer is unpleasant. It belongs with the other pages in our [home, real estate and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## The short version

- Order a title search or read the recorded deed. Restrictions and liens show up there.
- Look up the flood zone and ask the insurer what claims the house has had.
- If the lot ever held a gas station, workshop, dry cleaner or fuel tank, get it looked at before anyone takes title.
- Problems change what the house is worth, so they change what a fair split looks like among heirs.
- An heir who is surprised has choices. A disclaimer, a sale and a negotiation are all on the table.

## What is a deed restriction?

A deed restriction is a recorded limit on what an owner may do with the land. It binds the current owner and every later owner, including someone who inherits the property.

The usual sources are a developer's covenants, a homeowners association declaration, a prior owner who sold part of a lot and wanted to protect what was left, and a conservation or agricultural easement. They range from boring (no sheds in the front yard) to expensive (no building above one story, no subdividing, no rentals under six months, owner must be fifty-five or older).

Restrictions matter to estate planning in a few concrete ways:

- **Your plan may be blocked.** If you intend a son to rent the house out for income, a rental ban ends that plan.
- **A sale may be narrower.** An age restriction or a rental ban shrinks the pool of buyers, and the price follows.
- **Dues and assessments travel with the house.** An association can place a lien for unpaid dues, and a new owner usually inherits the arrears.
- **Enforcement can be real.** Neighbors or an association can sue to enforce a covenant, and courts can order a violation removed.

Many covenants lapse after a stated term or can be amended by a vote of owners. Others are permanent. Do not guess which kind yours is.

## How do you run a title search?

You find the property's recorded history in the county where it sits, or you pay someone to do it. A title search reads the land records back through the chain of owners and lists everything attached to the property.

In most counties the recorder, register of deeds or clerk of the court keeps the records, and many now offer online indexes. A title company or an attorney can run the full search, often for a modest fee, and will read the documents rather than just the index.

1. **Gather the deed you have.** Find the legal description, the parcel number and the names on title.
2. **Pull the recorded documents.** Ask for the deed, any mortgage and satisfaction of mortgage, easements, covenants and recorded liens.
3. **Check the tax record.** Confirm that property taxes are current. Unpaid taxes can become a lien that outranks almost everything else.
4. **Look for judgments and liens.** A contractor's lien, a court judgment against a prior owner or an unpaid association assessment can follow the property.
5. **Read the plat or survey.** Confirm the boundaries, utility easements and any recorded right of way.
6. **Write down what you find.** A one-page list of each document and what it limits is the working summary your family will use.

If the search turns up a gap, such as a missing signature or a name that does not match, that is a title defect. Our page on [fixing title problems on inherited property](/learn/property-and-assets/fixing-title-problems-on-inherited-property) explains the quiet title action and other repairs.

## Is the house in a flood zone?

Start with the Federal Emergency Management Agency flood map for the address. FEMA publishes maps at msc.fema.gov, and your insurance agent or lender can also pull the designation. A high-risk zone is one with a meaningful yearly chance of flooding. A moderate or low-risk label does not mean a house has never flooded, only that the map does not currently show it.

Three practical points:

- **Maps change.** A house that was outside the high-risk area when you bought it may be inside it now. A revised map can add a flood insurance requirement for a mortgaged property.
- **Homeowner's policies do not pay for floods.** Water coming up from the ground or in from a river is excluded. Flood coverage is a separate policy.
- **Insurance history is evidence.** A seller or an estate can often get a claims history report, which shows prior water losses and whether they were repaired.

For heirs, the issue is timing. A vacant house is hard to insure, and a house with no flood policy that floods during probate is the estate's loss. Keep the policy in force, name the executor on it, and read the guidance on [insuring and securing a vacant inherited house](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters).

## What counts as an environmental problem?

The common ones are small and local: a buried heating-oil tank, lead paint in an older house, asbestos in insulation or floor tile, radon in a basement, mold after a leak, or a private well and septic system that fail inspection. Those are repair costs. They reduce what the house is worth, but they rarely stop a sale.

The serious ones are contamination of the soil or groundwater. Lots that once held a gas station, dry cleaner, auto repair shop, farm chemical storage or a leaking tank are the classic examples. Cleanup laws can place responsibility on the current owner of the land, with limited defenses for owners who acquired it without knowing and took reasonable steps to check. The rules are technical, and the numbers can exceed what the property is worth.

A cautious family will:

- Ask who owned and used the land before. A short interview with the longest-standing neighbor often tells more than a records search.
- Order a Phase I environmental site assessment if there is any industrial or fuel-storage history. It is a records review and site visit, not soil testing.
- Hold off on signing or accepting title until the report is back.

## What if you only discover the problem after a death?

You still have more choices than most people expect. Rushing into a deed or a quick sale on a bad day is the usual mistake.

| Situation | What the heir or executor can do |
|---|---|
| Restriction blocks a rental plan | Check for an amendment process, ask the association in writing, and value the house as an owner-occupied property |
| Lien or unpaid dues on title | Pay from estate funds at closing, and keep the payoff letter |
| House flood-prone, no policy | Get quotes now. Disclose the history at sale. Expect buyers to price it in |
| Suspected contamination | Pause. Order a Phase I report before accepting title or listing the house |
| Costs exceed the value | Consider a disclaimer if you have not accepted the property, or ask the estate attorney about abandoning it |
| Heirs disagree about the fix | Use the date-of-death appraisal as a neutral number, then negotiate a buy-out or a sale |

An executor is a fiduciary and has a duty to protect estate assets, so documenting what you found and what you did about it matters as much as the decision itself. If the house is going through [probate](/learn/probate), the court may also need to approve certain steps.

### Can an heir refuse the house?

In most states, yes. An heir can sign a written disclaimer so that the property passes as though that heir had died first. The heir cannot direct where it goes, and generally cannot have accepted rent, lived there or used the property first. Timing is set by state law, so act early. The page on [how to disclaim an inheritance](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-disclaim-an-inheritance) walks through it. Illinois and New Jersey each have their own disclaimer rules, and you can see how Illinois handles it in [disclaiming an inheritance in Illinois](/learn/illinois/disclaiming-an-inheritance-in-illinois).

### Does the tax basis change?

Usually. Most inherited property gets a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) to its fair market value at death, and a problem property is valued with the problem. A flood-prone house with a covenant that cuts the buyer pool should come in below a comparable clean house, and a lower basis follows the lower value. A formal appraisal as of the date of death is worth the cost. The appraiser should be told about the restriction and the flood history.

## How should you plan if you are the owner?

If you are the person leaving the house, you can solve most of this now, when everyone is calm and you can ask the neighbors.

1. **Pull your deed and any covenants.** Put copies in your estate planning folder with the insurance declarations.
2. **Tell your children what the restrictions say.** A paragraph in a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) beats a surprise.
3. **Keep flood and hazard coverage in the executor's name.** Make sure the policy does not lapse the day the owner dies.
4. **Document any tank, well or septic work.** Receipts and permits sell houses.
5. **Choose the right transfer method.** A will, a [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) and a trust all pass the problems along, but they differ in how fast the house can be sold.

Dolores did the first three after a conversation with her attorney. She found that the covenant would expire in four years, and she asked her sons to wait before deciding on rentals. The flood policy had been renewed under a prior agent and was nearly a year behind on its premium. That was a ten-minute fix, but it would have been a very bad discovery after a storm.

Out-of-state homes add another wrinkle, because records and rules sit in a different county. See [leaving real estate in another state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) for how that works.

## How state law changes this

Recording systems, seller disclosure duties and cleanup rules are mostly state and local. Many states require a seller to complete a property disclosure form, and some exempt or limit the duty for estates and executors. Illinois and New Jersey each have their own forms and recording offices, and our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides give the starting points. Your attorney should confirm the local rule before an executor signs anything about the condition of a house.
