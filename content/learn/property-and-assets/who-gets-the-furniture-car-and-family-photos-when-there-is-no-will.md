A parent dies without a will. Three weeks later the question in the family is no longer about the bank account. It is about the dining table, the 2014 Buick, the box of photographs in the hall closet and who has been wearing Mom's watch. Those objects carry feelings that money does not, and the law handles them in a surprisingly plain way.

This page explains who owns belongings when there is no will, what an oral promise is worth, how the car differs from the china, and a practical way to divide things. It is part of our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets), and it complements the broader piece on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Who owns the contents of the house after someone dies without a will?

The estate does, and then the heirs, in the shares the state sets. Nobody inherits "the kitchen" or "the jewelry." The household items are simply part of one pool, along with the accounts and the house, and the pool is divided by the heir list.

In Illinois, a surviving spouse and the descendants share the estate half and half, and a spouse with no descendants takes everything (755 ILCS 5/2-1). New Jersey's rules depend on whose children the descendants are and whether a parent survives, so a spouse does not always take all (N.J.S.A. 3B:5-3 and 3B:5-4). Our state pages, [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) and [in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey), lay out the order. Unmarried partners, stepchildren and friends inherit nothing, however long they lived in the house.

The result surprises people. A daughter who cared for her mother for ten years has no better claim to the sewing machine than a son who lived across the country, unless the other heirs agree. Fairness has to come from the family, because the statute does not weigh it.

## Does a promise count?

No. A promise that someone could have an item is not a will. Mary was told for years that she would get her grandmother's china, and her aunt insisted the same. With no will, the china is estate property, and the written wishes of a person who never signed a will have no legal force. They still have moral force, and heirs often honor them.

If you want a promise to be binding, the heirs can make it so. Everyone signs a short document agreeing that Mary takes the china, and the executor distributes it that way. It is only necessary that all of the heirs sign, since the property belongs to them in shares.

## What about the car?

The title decides. A vehicle with a surviving co-owner or a transfer-on-death beneficiary named on the title passes to that person with a death certificate. A vehicle in the dead person's name alone belongs to the estate, and a court-appointed administrator, or a small estate affidavit in states that allow one for vehicles, is usually how it is retitled. In Illinois, the small estate affidavit does not count registered vehicles toward its $150,000 limit (755 ILCS 5/25-1), and in New Jersey a family can use the affidavit route for $50,000 or less for a spouse or partner. Our page on [transferring a car, boat or RV after death](/learn/property-and-assets/transfer-a-car-boat-or-rv-after-death) walks through it.

Until the title is moved, keep the car insured and do not drive it casually. If an heir keeps driving it, the insurer should know about the death.

## Which belongings never pass through the heir list at all?

Quite a few, and sorting them first shrinks the argument.

| Item | How it usually passes |
|---|---|
| Car with a co-owner or TOD beneficiary | To that person, by the title |
| Joint bank account | To the surviving owner |
| Life insurance and retirement accounts | To the named beneficiary |
| Items owned by a living trust | To the trust's beneficiaries |
| Items belonging to someone else | Back to the owner, such as a borrowed ladder |
| Gifts already given before death | Stay with the recipient, if truly given |
| Everything else | To the heirs, through the estate |

The last two rows need care. A gift needs delivery and intent, and a dispute about whether Mom "gave" a ring is a classic family problem. Write down who remembers what, and ask each sibling to do the same. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) is a quick way to see which items are probate property and which are not.

## How can the heirs divide things fairly?

A method that has worked for many families is slow and deliberately boring. Here is one version.

1. **Freeze the house.** Nobody removes anything until the executor or administrator says so. Change the locks if several people have keys.
2. **Make a list.** Walk through every room with photos and a notepad. Include storage, the garage and any safe deposit box. Our page on [clearing out a parent's house](/learn/after-a-death/clearing-out-a-parents-house-after-death) offers a room-by-room approach.
3. **Get values for anything unusual.** A licensed appraiser can value jewelry, art or antiques. Everything else is worth what a used-furniture dealer would pay.
4. **Take turns choosing.** Draw numbers, then each heir picks one item at a time. Reverse the order every round. It sounds childish, and it works.
5. **Handle disputed items last.** If two people want the same thing, draw lots, trade for something else, or sell it and split the money.
6. **Put it in writing.** One signed page listing who took what is enough. If values differ, say so and describe any adjustment against each heir's share.
7. **Donate or sell the rest.** Share proceeds by the heir shares, with the executor keeping the receipts.

Our page on [dividing personal property and family heirlooms](/learn/life-stages/dividing-personal-property-and-family-heirlooms) goes further into the emotional side, and the piece on [jewelry and sentimental items](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-jewelry-heirlooms-and-sentimental-items) covers high-value pieces.

## What about photos, letters and digital copies?

Treat photos as shared. Scan them or ask each heir to photograph the ones they want, and let the heir who volunteers to keep the album act as custodian. Letters can be copied as well. Some items cannot be copied, like a wedding quilt, and those go through the choosing process.

## When should someone ask a court?

When heirs cannot agree, or when an item is valuable enough that a mistake would be costly. The administrator can ask the court for instructions, and in the worst case a judge can order items sold and the proceeds divided. That is slow and expensive for a sewing machine. It is also why mediation, even a single session with a neutral person, deserves a try first.

## How do you avoid this for your own family?

Make a will, and write a separate list of who gets which keepsakes. Attorneys draft the list so that it fits the will and your state's rules, because some states enforce a separate list and others do not. Then tell your children. It takes an evening, and it often prevents the argument entirely.
