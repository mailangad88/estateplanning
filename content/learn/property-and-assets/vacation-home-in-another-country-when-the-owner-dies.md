Hal and Renata, both 66, bought a small apartment on the coast of Portugal eleven years ago. They spend three months there each winter. Their U.S. wills leave everything to each other and then to their daughter. They assume that covers the apartment. Their daughter, who lives in Ohio, has never been inside the local registry office and does not speak the language.

This page is for the owner of a second home outside the United States and for the heirs who may one day inherit it. It explains whose law applies, how the U.S. tax system treats the property, and what to put in place now. The wider topic of out-of-town property is covered in our [home and real estate guide](/learn/property-and-assets), and we have a separate page on [property in another state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property), which follows different rules from those below.

## The short version

- Real estate follows the law of the place where it sits. Your U.S. will is a starting point and nothing more.
- Many countries reserve part of an estate for children, and a few for a spouse.
- The U.S. still counts the home for federal estate tax if you are a citizen or resident.
- Heirs often need a local lawyer, a notary, translated and certified documents, and local tax clearance.
- A local will, or a local ownership structure, often makes the job far simpler.

## Whose law decides who inherits?

For land and buildings, the country where the property is located usually decides. This is an old and widely accepted principle: each country controls title to the ground inside its borders.

For the family, it means that the will you signed in your attorney's conference room may be treated as one document among several. Some countries accept a foreign will after it is translated, authenticated and presented to a local court or notary. Others require their own form of will, or require a local procedure that gives heirs a certificate of inheritance. A few apply the law of the owner's nationality instead of the law of the property, particularly within the European Union, where a regulation lets an owner choose the law of their citizenship to govern the estate. Whether that works for a particular country and a particular owner is a question for a lawyer there.

The result differs from the U.S. mainland pattern. In the United States, a house in a second state needs an ancillary probate in that state. Overseas, the process may not be called probate at all. It may be handled by a notary, a land registry or a special court, and it may have different deadlines.

## What is forced heirship, and why does it matter?

Forced heirship, sometimes called reserved shares, is a rule that sets aside a fixed portion of an estate for particular relatives, usually children and sometimes a surviving spouse. The owner cannot disinherit them or reduce the share by will.

The portion is often a fraction of the estate, and a person's freedom to leave it to anyone they like applies only to what is left over. If Hal and Renata have one daughter and local law reserves a share for her, the arrangement may not matter. If Hal has two children, one from an earlier marriage, and a will that leaves the apartment entirely to Renata, local law might give the second child a claim to part of the property.

Some countries let a foreign owner opt out of the local default, and others do not. That is why Hal and Renata need a local lawyer's view, not a general one.

## How does the U.S. tax system treat a foreign home?

A U.S. citizen or resident is taxed on worldwide assets at death. The value of the apartment goes into the gross estate alongside the bank accounts, retirement plans and everything else. For a U.S. citizen, the federal exclusion is currently $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and married couples can combine exclusions through portability. Most families are well under that number, and if so, the foreign home creates no federal estate tax. See [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) for how the number works.

Three other points deserve a plain statement:

1. **Foreign death or inheritance taxes may apply.** Some countries tax the estate, some tax the heir, and some tax the transfer of real estate. Rates and exemptions vary widely, and relationship to the owner often changes the result.
2. **A tax credit or treaty may reduce double tax.** The United States has estate tax treaties with a limited list of countries. Whether one covers your situation is a question for a tax adviser who has seen it before.
3. **Basis generally steps up.** Most inherited property, including foreign real estate owned by a U.S. decedent, receives a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) to its fair market value at death. A later sale by the heir is measured against that number, though converting currency and local sale taxes make the arithmetic less tidy.

The IRS also asks U.S. persons to report certain foreign accounts and assets in some situations. Those reporting rules turn on specifics, such as account balances and ownership structure, so confirm them with a tax professional rather than assuming a rental or a bank account abroad is exempt.

## What can an owner do now?

Most of the solutions are not exotic. They are a short list of dull steps, done in order.

1. **Find a lawyer in the country where the home sits.** Ask what happens to the property if you die with only a U.S. will, and what a local will would change.
2. **Ask about a local will.** Many owners sign one limited to the foreign property, which leaves the rest of the estate to the U.S. will. The two documents must be written so that one does not accidentally revoke the other. Our page on [wills when you move or own property abroad](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) covers the drafting concern.
3. **Check how title is held.** Sole name, joint with a spouse, through a local company or through a trust each lead to a different result at death. Do not change title without local tax advice, because some transfers trigger fees.
4. **Name who can act.** A local power of attorney, signed before a local notary, lets a trusted person manage the property if you are ill or abroad. A U.S. power of attorney may not be accepted by a foreign registry.
5. **Make a binder.** Put the deed, the tax number, the utility accounts, the community or condo fees, the insurance policy and the local lawyer's contact details in one folder. Give your executor a copy.
6. **Decide what you want to happen.** Keep, sell or pass on. Tell your children, in writing.

## What will your heirs have to do?

Expect a longer timetable than a U.S. transfer. A typical path looks like this, though each country differs:

| Stage | What usually happens |
|---|---|
| Death certificate | A certified copy, often with an apostille, which is an international certification of a public document |
| Local lawyer or notary | Heirs hire a local professional and give a power of attorney |
| Proof of heirship | A local will is proved, or a certificate of inheritance is issued |
| Tax filings | Local inheritance or transfer taxes are calculated and paid, sometimes before title can change |
| Land registry | Title is changed into the heirs' names |
| Sale or retention | Heirs sell, retain or hold the property together |

Documents written in English usually need a certified translation, and the registry may accept only a translator it recognizes. An apostille from the issuing U.S. state's office can be needed for documents from the United States. These steps are routine to a local practitioner and baffling to a grieving daughter in Ohio.

If your heirs are several, remember the usual family problem: one wants to keep the home, one wants cash. Foreign co-ownership is harder to unwind than domestic co-ownership because you cannot easily force a sale. The same planning tools you would use at home, such as an agreed buy-out clause or a company that holds the property, may be worth discussing. Our page on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) explains how shared ownership goes wrong.

## What about currency, rent and upkeep?

An estate that includes a foreign home has ongoing costs: taxes, association dues, utilities and insurance, all in another currency. If the owner dies, those bills continue. Who pays them? A joint account at a local bank, funded and accessible to a trusted person, is a common answer, though local rules on accounts of a deceased owner vary. A local property manager can also handle repairs and, where allowed, short-term rentals. Rental income raises its own tax questions on both sides of the border.

If the property is rented out, the lease continues after the owner's death in most places, so be sure the heirs know where the tenant's deposit and contract are.

## How state law changes this

Your U.S. state still governs your U.S. will and the rest of your estate. Illinois and New Jersey follow the usual U.S. rules for valid wills, described in our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides, but neither can change what another country does with land inside its borders. The effect of the U.S. will on the foreign home is a question of foreign law.
