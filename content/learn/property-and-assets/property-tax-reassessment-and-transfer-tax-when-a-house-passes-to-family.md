Most people planning to leave a house to their children worry about the big taxes: estate tax, capital gains. The tax that actually arrives in the mailbox every year is the property tax, and a transfer can change the amount. Sometimes the change is large.

This page explains the local side of the picture: reassessment, family exclusions, exemptions you can lose, and deed transfer fees. For the federal income tax side, see [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house). The overall cluster is in our [property and assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Why can a transfer raise property tax?

Because in many states the tax bill is not based on today's market value. It is based on an assessed value that was set at a purchase and then allowed to rise slowly. Some states cap the yearly increase. A few set the value at the last sale and hold it there with small adjustments.

A change of ownership can reset that. The county treats the new owner as a new buyer and reassesses at current market value. If the house was bought in 1992 and is worth ten times as much now, the reassessed bill can jump sharply.

Not every state works this way. Some assess at market value every year or every few years regardless of who owns the house, so a transfer changes nothing. Others cap increases but reset on sale. Which system you live in is the first thing to learn.

Take Marguerite, 74, who bought her home decades ago. In her state the assessed value is capped and only resets on a change of ownership. If her son inherits, the bill could rise to a market-value level. If she instead puts the house in her own living trust and keeps it until death, nothing changes during her life, and the question moves to the day she dies.

## Does inheriting a house trigger reassessment?

In states with a reset rule, usually yes, unless an exclusion applies. In states without one, the house is simply assessed on the normal schedule.

| Situation | Typical treatment (varies by state) |
|---|---|
| Deed to your own revocable living trust | Usually not a change of ownership |
| Inherit from a spouse | Often excluded |
| Inherit from a parent or to a child | Excluded in some states, limited or conditional in others |
| Inherit from a sibling, friend or partner | Usually a change of ownership |
| Gift deed to a child during life | Often a change of ownership unless a family exclusion applies |
| Transfer to an irrevocable trust | Depends on who keeps the benefit |

The parent-and-child exclusion is the one most families ask about. California is the best-known case: it allows a family transfer without reassessment, and Proposition 19, effective in 2021, narrowed that exclusion so the child generally has to make the home a primary residence and the benefit is capped. Other states have their own versions, some stricter and some looser. Do not rely on what a neighbor in another county was told.

## How do you check what will happen to your own house?

You can learn this without paying anyone.

1. **Find your latest assessment notice or tax bill.** It shows assessed value and, often, the exemptions you now receive.
2. **Call or visit the county assessor's website.** Ask: "What happens to the assessment if the owner dies and a child inherits?" and "Is there a parent-child or spouse exclusion, and what are the conditions?"
3. **Ask about filing deadlines.** Some exclusions must be claimed on a form within a set number of months of the transfer, and late filing can cost you the benefit.
4. **Ask what happens to your exemptions.** See the homestead section below.
5. **Write down the answers with the name and date,** and give a copy to your estate planner.

Your attorney can then compare options. Leaving the house through a will, a transfer-on-death deed or a trust may produce the same property-tax result, or it may not, depending on how your state's rule is worded. See [leaving your home to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) for how those options compare in other respects.

## What about a deed to a child while you are alive?

A lifetime gift is the most common source of surprise. A deed to a child is a transfer of ownership immediately, so a reassessment can happen now, not at your death. It also loses the step-up in basis your child would get on inheritance, which affects income tax if the house is later sold. We cover that in [giving your house to your child: taxes and trade-offs](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs).

There is a federal gift tax filing question as well. A house is usually worth well more than the $19,000 annual exclusion in 2026, so a gift deed generally calls for a gift tax return. Filing the return does not mean tax is owed. With a federal exclusion of $15 million per person in 2026, most families owe nothing, but the return is still required.

## Do you lose your homestead or senior exemption?

Possibly, and this is the part people forget. Homestead exemptions, senior freezes and veteran or disability reductions are generally tied to the owner who lives in the house. When title changes, the new owner has to qualify and apply on their own.

If you put the house in a revocable trust, many states let you keep the exemption as long as you live there, but you may need to file a short form with the assessor. A few counties drop it silently, and the first sign is a higher bill. After a death, the heirs generally start from scratch: the dead owner's exemption ends and the new owner has to claim theirs. Our article on [homestead rights at death](/learn/property-and-assets/homestead-rights-at-death-surviving-spouse-and-the-family-home) covers the separate creditor and spouse protections that share the name.

## What is a real estate transfer tax, and does it apply to family?

It is a fee charged when a deed is recorded, usually based on the price or value. Some states and cities have one; others do not. It is separate from property tax.

Many places exempt:

- Gifts with no money changing hands
- Transfers into your own revocable trust
- Transfers between spouses
- Sometimes transfers between parents and children

The word "exempt" often means you claim it. The recorder may ask for a form stating the reason, and without it the clerk may calculate tax on assessed value. Read the deed instructions before you record, or have your attorney prepare the exemption statement. If you are transferring into a trust, our guide to [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) goes through the recording steps.

## What happens during probate and afterward?

The tax bill does not pause. If the owner dies in March, the first-half payment may come due in the spring. The estate, through the executor, pays it from estate funds. If the estate has no cash, an heir may advance the payment and be repaid later. Leave a note in the estate records so it is clear later. Falling behind can lead to penalties and, eventually, a tax sale, so do not let it slide.

When the house passes to the heirs, they should notify the assessor, file any exclusion claim, apply for their own exemptions, and update the mailing address so notices actually arrive.
