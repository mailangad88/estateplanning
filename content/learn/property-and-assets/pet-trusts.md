For many people, a dog, cat, horse or parrot is family. If you die or become unable to care for your animal, the law offers no automatic plan. A pet left behind may go to a shelter, and a pet that outlives you can outlive the arrangement you assumed would hold.

This page explains why pets need special planning, how a pet trust works, how to estimate the money to set aside, and what to do when a full trust is more than you need. It is part of our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Why can't you just leave your pet money in your will?

You cannot leave money to a pet because the law treats animals as property, and property cannot own property. A will that says "$20,000 to Biscuit" has no legal effect on its own. Courts in some states have saved such gifts by reading them as a gift to a caregiver, but you should not count on that.

You can leave the pet itself, as property, to a person. That works well if the person is willing and reliable. The weakness is that the gift is outright. If you leave a friend your dog and $10,000 and the friend takes the money and rehomes the dog, nothing in the will makes them do otherwise. Our page on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) lists other limits.

## What is a pet trust?

A pet trust is a [trust](/glossary/trust) created to pay for the care of one or more animals during their lives. It has the same basic parts as other trusts:

- **The trustee**, who holds and spends the money for the animal's care, keeps records and pays the caregiver.
- **The caregiver**, who lives with the animal and handles daily care. This can be the same person as the trustee, but separating the roles adds oversight.
- **The terms**, which say how the animal should be cared for.
- **The remainder beneficiary**, who receives whatever is left when the animals die.
- **Someone to enforce it.** Most states allow a person named in the document, or a court, to enforce the trust.

A majority of states have laws expressly allowing pet trusts, many based on the Uniform Trust Code. Other states have older "honorary trust" rules that allow similar arrangements but may limit them in time or make them harder to enforce. Because the details differ, have an attorney check your state. See our state guides, such as [Florida](/estate-planning/florida), [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) and [California](/estate-planning/california), for the state context.

## How does a pet trust work, step by step?

1. **You decide who will care for the pet,** and name one or two backups.
2. **You choose a trustee.** Think about someone organized and honest, who will not mind a modest oversight role. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).
3. **You write the care instructions.** Food, vet, exercise, medical limits, boarding, whether the pet can travel, and what to do at the end of life.
4. **You set the money.** The amount and how it is paid out, such as monthly to the caregiver and directly to the vet for larger bills.
5. **You say who gets the remainder.** Often the caregiver, a family member or a rescue organization.
6. **You sign the document and fund it.** The trust can be a section of your will or [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), or a separate stand-alone trust.
7. **You tell the people involved.** The caregiver, the trustee and the backup should all know their roles, and the vet should have their contact details.

### When does the trust begin?

A pet trust written into a will starts only after probate, which can take months. During that time, someone must care for the animal and pay the bills. A trust inside your living trust, or a stand-alone trust, can start immediately at your death or at your incapacity and does not wait for probate. If the pet is old or has medical needs, use a stand-alone or living trust.

## How much money should you put in?

Fund the trust from the pet's real costs and life expectancy. Overfunding is a common mistake, and in many states a court can reduce an amount that is substantially more than needed for the animal's care.

Use a simple worksheet:

| Item | Example, per year |
|---|---|
| Food and treats | $900 |
| Routine veterinary care and medicine | $800 |
| Grooming | $400 |
| Boarding or pet sitter when the caregiver is away | $500 |
| Pet insurance or reserve for emergencies | $600 |
| Trustee fee and records | $300 |
| **Total** | **$3,500** |

For a dog with eight expected years left, that is $28,000, plus a cushion for emergencies, perhaps $5,000 to $10,000. A cat that may live fifteen years more costs much more over time. Parrots, horses and tortoises can outlive their owners by decades and cost far more, and these animals deserve a longer-term plan with a professional trustee. Life expectancy varies by breed and health, so ask your veterinarian.

Remember to review the amount over time, because costs rise with inflation and the pet's age.

## What should the care instructions say?

Specific instructions work better than general ones. Cover these items:

- The animal's name, description, microchip number and vet.
- Food, medications and any special diet.
- Where the pet may live, whether outdoors, and how much exercise.
- Whether the caregiver can rehome the animal, and under what conditions.
- Medical limits: how much to spend on treatment, and when to stop, including euthanasia, which is often the hardest topic. Give the caregiver and vet clear guidance and some discretion.
- Reporting: whether the trustee will visit or ask for vet records once or twice a year.

Aim for instructions a loving stranger could follow, not rules so strict that the caregiver cannot meet them.

## What are the alternatives to a full pet trust?

A pet trust is not always necessary. Which tool fits depends on the pet and the people.

| Option | How it works | Best when | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gift to a person in your will | Leave the pet and money to a named person | Healthy pet, trustworthy friend | Not enforceable; probate delay |
| Gift with a condition | Gift is conditional on caring for the pet | You want some leverage | Hard to enforce without a trustee |
| Pet trust | Trustee holds money, pays caregiver, enforceable | Long-lived or costly pets, larger sums | Cost to set up and administer |
| Written agreement with a caregiver | Signed agreement, perhaps with money placed now | You want certainty during life | Needs careful drafting |
| Sanctuary or rescue arrangement | Pre-arranged placement with a donation | No suitable family member | Place may change; confirm in writing |
| Pet insurance | Covers vet bills | Supplement only | Does not provide a home |

Many owners combine a modest gift to a caregiver with a backup, and add a trust for animals that need more.

## What if something happens to you while you're alive?

Plan for emergencies and incapacity, not just death. Many pets end up in shelters because an owner was hospitalized and no one knew who to call. Cover these steps:

1. **Carry a wallet card** that says you have pets at home and lists an emergency contact.
2. **Give a neighbor or friend a key** and written instructions, and tell them where the pet's food, medicine and records are.
3. **Name an emergency caregiver** and keep that name on your pet's microchip registration, along with your own.
4. **Include the pet in your incapacity plan.** A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) can authorize your agent to pay for pet care, and some people name a successor trustee to take over a pet trust if they become unable to act.
5. **Think ahead about a move to assisted living.** Some facilities allow pets, and some do not. A written plan that says what happens to the pet then is kind to everyone.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Naming a caregiver without asking.** Confirm they are willing, and name a backup.
- **Leaving the entire estate's residue to the pet's caregiver "to take care of the pets."** That is a gift, not a trust.
- **Choosing a trustee who cannot say no to the caregiver,** or a caregiver who cannot stand the trustee.
- **Forgetting multiple pets.** Say whether they stay together and what happens when one dies.
- **Not updating.** A new pet, a moved caregiver or a lapsed trustee leaves a gap.
