The funeral is over, the relatives have gone home, and the house is dark. Someone has the keys. The mail is stacking up inside the door. Probate has not started, or it has and the court has not issued letters yet. Meanwhile an empty house is a bill and a risk, and it gets worse every month.

This page is for the family member who is suddenly responsible for an empty house, and for the person who wants to avoid leaving their own family in that spot. It sits in our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets), and it complements our broader page on [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house).

## Who is in charge of the house right after a death?

Legally, the house belongs to the estate, and the person with authority over it is the executor named in the will or the successor trustee if the house was in a living trust. An executor generally cannot act with full power until the court issues letters testamentary, though in many places a named executor can take urgent protective steps right away, like securing the property and calling the insurer.

If there is no will, a relative can petition to become administrator. If there is a trust, the successor trustee may act as soon as they accept the role and show the death certificate, with no court. Our page on [what happens if there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) covers the gap.

Until authority is clear, heirs should do only what any careful family member would: lock up, photograph, make safe and tell the insurer. Do not give away belongings, sell items, make big repairs or let anyone move in. For the longer picture, see [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties).

## What should I do in the first week?

1. **Secure the house.** Change the locks if you cannot account for every key. Lock windows, garage and outbuildings.
2. **Photograph every room and the exterior.** The dated photos show the condition at death, which matters for insurance, taxes and any later dispute among heirs.
3. **Call the insurance company.** Give notice, ask about vacancy limits and request the policy in writing. This is the most time-sensitive call on the list.
4. **Forward the mail.** File a change of address with the post office as the executor or representative so that tax bills, notices and bank mail are not lost.
5. **Keep the utilities on or winterize.** Heat to a safe minimum in cold climates; if you shut off water, drain the lines. A frozen pipe is a very common and expensive loss in empty houses.
6. **Remove perishables and clear obvious fire hazards.**
7. **Remove or secure valuables, papers and firearms.** Documents and cash can disappear, and guns need a lawful plan. See [firearms in estate planning](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning).
8. **Tell neighbors and the police non-emergency line** that the house is unoccupied and who to call.

## What does vacancy do to homeowner's insurance?

It can quietly remove your coverage. Many standard homeowner policies treat a house as vacant after it has been empty for a stated period, commonly 30 to 60 days, and then limit or exclude losses such as vandalism, glass breakage, water damage from frozen pipes and sometimes theft or fire. A house with furniture in it is unoccupied, not vacant, and policies often treat the two differently. The wording is in your policy, and it is worth reading.

Ask the insurer these questions, in writing if you can:

- Does the policy still cover the house now that the owner has died, and who is the named insured?
- What counts as vacant, and when does the limitation start?
- Can we add a vacancy permit or switch to a vacant dwelling policy?
- Does coverage include liability if someone is hurt on the property?

A vacant dwelling policy costs more than normal coverage. It costs far less than a fire. If the house is leaving the family in a few months, the extra premium is easy to defend as an expense of the estate.

## Who pays the taxes, utilities and repairs?

The estate does, from its own funds, if it has any. Property taxes, insurance, utilities and urgent repairs are ordinary expenses of administering the estate, and the executor can pay them once an estate account is open. Before then, a family member often advances the money. That is fine, as long as it is documented: keep receipts, tell the other heirs and ask the executor to reimburse you from the estate.

Mortgage payments need attention. The loan does not vanish at death, and lenders move to foreclose after missed payments. In general, the lender must deal with heirs who want to keep the home and cannot simply call the loan due when the house passes to a close relative. Call the servicer early, give them the death certificate, and ask for a contact in the department that handles successors. Our guide to [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) discusses the loan in more detail.

Heirs sometimes disagree about who should be paying. If one sibling lives nearby and handles everything while another says "sell it," the arrangement deserves a written plan. See [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree).

## What if the house is in bad shape?

Triage it. Fix whatever threatens the structure or safety first, and leave the cosmetic work alone unless it affects a sale.

| Problem | Why it matters | Usual response |
|---|---|---|
| Leaking roof or burst pipe | Damage grows daily | Emergency repair, and file the insurance claim. |
| Mold, pests or rot | Health issue and lower sale price | Professional inspection. |
| Unsafe stairs, deck or wiring | Liability if someone is hurt | Repair or block off access. |
| Overgrown yard, junk | Code citations and fines | Cut and clear regularly. |
| Cosmetic wear | Does not threaten value | Leave for the buyer or later. |

Selling "as is" is often reasonable when the cost of repairs exceeds what the repairs would add to the price. Investors and cash buyers purchase damaged houses all the time. If the house must be sold and the estate has no cash, see the guide on [whether heirs can sell the house during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate).

## What if squatters move in?

Treat this as a legal issue, not a confrontation. Squatters are people who occupy a property without permission. The law protects even unwelcome occupants from self-help removal in most places, which means changing the locks, cutting utilities or throwing out their belongings can expose you to liability.

A cautious sequence:

1. **Do not go inside alone** if you suspect someone is there.
2. **Call the police** and report an unauthorized person on the property. If they leave or are charged with trespass, that is the quickest outcome.
3. **Document the situation.** Photographs, dates, and notes of who you spoke with.
4. **Do not accept rent or let them stay informally.** Doing so can create a tenancy that requires a formal eviction.
5. **Contact an attorney** about the removal procedure in your state, which usually requires a court order when the occupant claims a right to be there.

Laws on squatters differ widely, and some recently changed to speed up removal when an occupant has no lease or claim. The executor with court authority is the right person to start the process. A fast response counts: the longer someone stays, the more complicated the process is likely to be.

## What if the heirs cannot agree what to do?

Then the house costs money while the disagreement continues. Property taxes and insurance keep running whether or not anyone agrees. Set a deadline, get a broker's opinion of value, and choose among keeping, renting, selling or buying out a sibling. Our page on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) explains what can happen when ownership is split among relatives who cannot agree.

Anyone planning ahead can reduce this risk. A will or trust that says who may live in the house, who pays, and when it should be sold removes the guesswork for the people who come next.
