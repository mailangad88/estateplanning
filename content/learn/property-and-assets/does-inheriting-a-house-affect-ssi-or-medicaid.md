Marcus, 47, receives SSI because of a disability. His aunt died and left him her bungalow in Rockford. He never expected anything. Now he has a deed, a tax bill, and a letter from a neighbor who wants to buy the place. He is also afraid that if he says yes to any of it, his check and his health coverage will stop.

The fear is reasonable. Means-tested benefits count what you own, and a house is the largest thing most people own. This page, part of our [property and assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets), explains how the main rules apply, what your options are, and what to do before you sign anything. Benefit rules change and differ by program, so use this as orientation and confirm with the agency or an attorney.

## How does an inherited house count for SSI?

SSI is a needs-based program with a resource limit: $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple as of 2026. Most real estate you own counts toward that limit. There are two separate effects to understand.

- **Income in the month received.** SSI generally treats an inheritance as income in the month you get it. That can reduce or stop that month's payment.
- **A resource afterward.** In the following months it counts as a resource, and if you are over the limit you lose eligibility until you are back under.

Two exceptions matter most. The home you live in is excluded, no matter its value. And non-home real estate you are actively trying to sell can be excluded for a limited period, generally measured in months, while you make reasonable efforts to sell at a fair price. When it sells, the cash counts.

## Does it matter whether I live in the house?

Very much. This is the single biggest lever.

| Situation | How SSI generally treats the house |
|---|---|
| You inherit it and move in as your main home | Excluded as your home |
| You inherit it and keep living elsewhere | Counts as a resource, unless an exclusion applies |
| You list it for sale at a reasonable price | May be excluded while you try to sell it, for a limited time |
| You and siblings inherit it together | Depends on whether you can sell your share without their consent |
| A life estate lets you live there | May be excluded as your home, but terms vary |

Marcus rents a small apartment. If he sells the house, the proceeds will push him far over $2,000. If he moves in, the house is exempt, but he must pay taxes, insurance and repairs from his own limited income. Neither choice is obviously right. A third option is to place the proceeds in a trust or ABLE account, discussed below.

## What about Medicaid?

Medicaid is several programs under one name. In Illinois and New Jersey, as elsewhere, adults who qualify because they are aged, blind or disabled face an asset test, while some other categories are judged by income only. Long-term care Medicaid has its own rules, including a look-back on gifts and transfers (60 months in most states). A second property is generally countable for these programs, with exceptions for a home a spouse or dependent relative lives in.

If you are already on Medicaid, an inheritance may need to be reported and can affect eligibility in the same way as for SSI. See [what counts as a countable asset for Medicaid](/learn/elder-care/what-counts-as-a-countable-asset-for-medicaid) for categories, and [the Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) before making any transfer.

## Can I disclaim, sell, gift or retitle the house?

Each of these has a trap.

- **Disclaiming** (refusing the inheritance) passes the house to someone else. For SSI and Medicaid, an agency may treat that as giving away an asset. The rules are technical, so ask before you sign.
- **Giving the house to a relative** can produce a penalty period that delays Medicaid for long-term care.
- **Selling for less than value** has the same problem.
- **Putting the house in a trust you created with the inheritance** is allowed only in certain forms. A first-party special needs trust holds your own money, requires a payback to the state when you die, and has age and other limits. See [first-party vs. third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust).

## What are the options that keep benefits?

Here is what attorneys commonly consider, in rough order of how often it fits.

1. **Move in** and treat the house as the home. Works if you want it and can afford upkeep.
2. **Sell within the allowed window**, then spend the proceeds on exempt items: a home to live in, a vehicle, medical equipment, education, debts, home repairs. Spending on yourself is not a disqualifying transfer if you receive fair value.
3. **Place the proceeds in a first-party special needs trust**, if you qualify, to pay for extras the benefits do not cover.
4. **Use an ABLE account** for the part that fits within the annual limit. See [ABLE accounts](/learn/special-needs/able-accounts).
5. **Let a co-owner buy you out** only if you will receive the cash in a form that fits the plan above.

A mistake we see: someone sells the house and deposits the check in a regular bank account, planning to sort it out later. The next month's review shows the balance and the SSI stops. Plan the deposit first.

## An example with numbers

These figures are invented to show the mechanics. Marcus's aunt's bungalow is worth $180,000 and has no mortgage. Marcus has $900 in the bank. He lists the house, and a buyer closes in four months for $175,000 after $12,000 in selling costs, leaving $163,000.

If he deposits that in his checking account, he is $162,000 over the SSI limit and loses benefits until the balance falls. If instead his attorney sets up a first-party special needs trust before closing, the proceeds go to the trust, Marcus keeps SSI, and the trust pays his rent, a used car and dental work. When Marcus dies, the state is repaid for Medicaid it provided before anything goes to his family, which is the price of this kind of trust.

The same house, moved into as a home, would have cost him nothing in benefits but put upkeep on his own shoulders. The right answer depends on whether he wants to live there.

## What should I do first if I have just inherited?

1. **Do not sign a deed, a disclaimer or a sale contract yet.**
2. **Tell the agency** (SSA or the state Medicaid office) about the inheritance within the required time. Keep a copy.
3. **Get the facts about the house**: its value, who else owns it, any mortgage, back taxes or liens.
4. **See an attorney** who handles benefits planning, with the numbers in hand.
5. **Choose an option** and document the date and reason, because you may need to show it later.

## How does the house itself get to you?

Before any of this you need clear title. A house may sit in probate for months, and you cannot sell or move in until the court or a trust gives you the deed. Read [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) for the general process, and [heirs property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) if several relatives share it.

For cash, investments and other inherited property, see [how an inheritance affects Medicaid or SSI](/learn/after-a-death/does-an-inheritance-affect-medicaid-or-ssi).
