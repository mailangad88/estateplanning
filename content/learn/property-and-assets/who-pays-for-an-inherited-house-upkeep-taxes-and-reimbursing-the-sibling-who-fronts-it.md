The funeral is over, the house is empty or half-empty, and the bills have not stopped. The property tax is due. The insurance renews next month. The furnace needs repair. Somebody has to pay, and in many families the answer becomes whoever is closest and most responsible, usually one child.

That can be fine, until the siblings later argue about what that child is owed. This article explains who is responsible at each stage and how to keep a record that holds up. It is part of our [property and assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## The short version

- During probate, the estate pays. The executor writes the checks.
- After the house is distributed, the heirs pay in proportion to ownership.
- An heir who fronts more than their share should document it as it happens.
- Taxes and insurance get priority, because a lapse hurts everyone.
- Agree in writing who lives there, who pays what, and how it is settled when the house sells.

## Who pays while the estate is in probate?

The estate does. Property tax, insurance, utilities, lawn care and necessary repairs are expenses of administering the estate. The executor pays them from estate accounts, and those payments come ahead of what heirs receive. The executor's duty is to preserve the property, which includes keeping it insured and heated. See [what an executor does](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) for the broader job.

The difficulty is cash. The estate may not have an account open yet, or the only large asset is the house. Letters from the court are usually needed to open one. Until then, someone often pays from their own pocket. That is fine, but do it the right way:

1. Pay the bill and keep the receipt.
2. Tell the executor and the other heirs by email the same week.
3. Have the executor record it as an advance to be repaid by the estate.
4. Repay it from estate funds as soon as there is an account, and note it in the accounting.

Nadia's father died in May. Her brother Anand paid the June insurance premium and the spring tax installment, about $4,000 together, out of his savings. Because Nadia, the executor, logged both and repaid him from the estate account in August, no one ever argued about it. Without the log, the same amount could have become a three-year grudge.

An empty house also raises an insurance issue: many standard policies restrict coverage when a home is vacant for a set period. Our article on [vacant inherited houses](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters) covers the policy, repairs and security.

## Who pays after the heirs own the house?

Each co-owner is responsible for the costs in proportion to their ownership. If three siblings inherit equally, each owes a third of the property tax, insurance and any mortgage payments. That is the legal default, whether or not the family discusses it.

Practical splits tend to be looser. A sibling who lives in the house may cover utilities and routine upkeep. Siblings who live far away may send a monthly payment into a shared account. What matters is that it is written down. A text thread that says "I'll handle it for now" is not an agreement about repayment.

If the house has a mortgage, the payments continue. The loan does not disappear at death, and the lender can generally not call it just because heirs inherited. We explain this in [what happens to the mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).

## Can the heir who pays be reimbursed?

Often, yes, with limits. Many states let a co-owner who pays more than their share of carrying costs recover the excess from the others. This is usually decided when the house is sold or in a partition case, not as a bill sent each month. The costs most reliably recoverable are:

- Property taxes
- Homeowner's insurance
- Mortgage payments (principal and interest)
- Emergency or necessary repairs to preserve the property

Improvements are different. A new kitchen, even if it raised the value, is less certain to be reimbursed, because the other owners did not agree to it. Some states credit the added value at sale, and some do not.

| Expense | Usually reimbursable? | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| Property tax | Yes, other owners' share | Payment receipts |
| Insurance | Yes | Policy and invoice |
| Mortgage payments | Often, as to the others' share | Bank records |
| Necessary repairs (roof leak, furnace) | Often | Invoices, photos |
| Cosmetic upgrades | Uncertain | Written agreement beforehand |
| Your own travel and time | Rarely | Do not count on it |

A short written note signed by everyone, naming who will pay what and how it will be repaid at sale, converts a vague hope into something enforceable. Our article on [what happens when siblings inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) goes through the situations where the note fails to appear.

## What if one sibling lives in the house rent-free?

This is the most common source of resentment. The law's starting point is that every co-owner has a right to use the whole property. So a sibling who simply lives there often owes nothing, even though the others own two-thirds.

That changes if the occupant keeps the others out, changes the locks, or rents to a third party and keeps the money. Then many courts require an accounting. And the family can always decide differently. Common compromises are a modest rent paid into the shared account, or a credit against the occupant's share if they later buy the others out.

If you are the sibling living in the house, offering to pay something, even less than market rent, usually costs less than a lawsuit later. If you are one of the siblings who live elsewhere, say what you expect early and in writing, before the arrangement has run for five years. For ways to buy a share, see [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property).

## What if the heirs cannot afford the carrying costs?

Don't wait. Property tax delinquency leads to penalties and interest, then to a lien, and in time to a tax sale. Insurance lapses mean one storm can wipe out everyone's share. In some regions, heirs who inherited informally without a deed lose eligibility for tax relief and end up with nowhere to turn. That is the pattern we describe in [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property).

Put the options on the table:

1. Sell and divide the money.
2. One heir buys the others out, perhaps over time.
3. Rent the house and use the income for the bills.
4. Contribute equally to a shared account each month.
5. Ask the county about payment plans, senior or hardship deferrals, and exemptions that new owners must claim.

The last point links back to a tax topic: reassessment and exemptions change who owes what, and we go through those in [property tax reassessment and transfer tax](/learn/property-and-assets/property-tax-reassessment-and-transfer-tax-when-a-house-passes-to-family).

## How do you keep the peace?

Hold a call within the first few weeks. Agree on who is the point person. Open a shared account for the house, fund it, and pay everything from it. Keep one spreadsheet. Decide a date by which the family will decide to keep or sell the house. Houses that nobody decides about tend to cost more every year, and relationships do not improve with age.
