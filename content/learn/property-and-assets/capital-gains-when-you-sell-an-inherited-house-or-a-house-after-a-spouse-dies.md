A parent dies, the house is worth far more than the $90,000 they paid for it in 1984, and you are about to list it. Or you are a widow in your seventies thinking about downsizing. In both cases the first question is the same: how much of the profit goes to the IRS?

The answer for most families is less than they fear, because of one rule. This page shows how that rule works for an heir and for a surviving spouse, with sample numbers, and points out where Illinois and New Jersey add something extra. It is part of our [property and assets series](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Do you pay capital gains on a house you inherit?

Only on growth after the owner's death. When you inherit property, your cost basis is generally reset to its fair market value on the date of death. That is called the [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis). Your gain on a sale is the selling price, minus selling costs, minus that new basis.

Two other rules help heirs. Inherited property is treated as held long term no matter how soon you sell, so you do not face short-term rates. And the owner's original purchase price and any improvements they made drop out of the picture for you.

### Example: a house inherited by a daughter

Example only. Elaine's father bought a house in Evanston for $90,000 and it was worth $540,000 on the day he died. Elaine inherits it. Her basis is $540,000, not $90,000. Nine months later she sells for $555,000 and pays $33,000 in agent fees and closing costs.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sale price | $555,000 |
| Selling costs | $33,000 |
| Net proceeds | $522,000 |
| Basis (date-of-death value) | $540,000 |
| Result | No taxable gain ($18,000 below basis) |

She has no taxable gain. Had her father sold it himself before he died, the taxable gain would have been far larger. That is the practical benefit of holding appreciated property until death, and it is why an attorney will often talk a parent out of deeding the house to a child while the parent is alive. Our article on [giving your house to a child](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs) covers that decision, and the general rule is in [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## What if I sell years later?

Then the basis is still the date-of-death value, and you are taxed on the growth since. Add the cost of capital improvements you make after inheriting. Subtract depreciation if you rent the house out, which is explained in our article on [inherited rental property taxes](/learn/property-and-assets/inherited-rental-property-taxes). Keep your records. The IRS will ask how you got the number.

## Can an heir use the home sale exclusion?

Only by living in the house first. The exclusion for a main home, up to $250,000 of gain for a single person and $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, requires that you owned the home and used it as your main residence for at least two of the five years before the sale. Heirs who list the house right away do not meet the test. Heirs who move in and stay for two years do.

For most heirs it makes no difference, because the step-up has already removed the gain. The exception is a house that is appreciating fast, or one you plan to hold for many years.

## What about a surviving spouse who sells the house?

A surviving spouse gets two separate benefits, and people often mix them up.

**The partial step-up.** In a state that is not a community property state, which includes Illinois and New Jersey, a house the spouses owned together generally gets a new basis only for the deceased spouse's half. The survivor's half keeps its old basis. In a community property state, both halves can step up. Our article on [step-up in basis for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis-for-married-couples-and-joint-property) goes through the exceptions.

**The larger exclusion.** A surviving spouse who has not remarried can generally still use the $500,000 exclusion if the sale happens within two years of the date of death and the use and ownership tests were met just before the death. After two years, the limit falls back to $250,000 for one person.

### Example: a widow who sells within two years

Example only. Walt and Nora bought their Montclair home in 1988 for $210,000, joint owners. Walt died in March. The home was appraised at $700,000 on the date of death. Nora sells in November of the following year for $720,000 with $40,000 of selling costs.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Walt's half: basis stepped up to half of $700,000 | $350,000 |
| Nora's half: original basis | $105,000 |
| Total basis (ignoring improvements) | $455,000 |
| Net sale proceeds ($720,000 minus $40,000) | $680,000 |
| Gain | $225,000 |
| Exclusion, if sale is within two years | up to $500,000 |
| Taxable gain | $0 |

If Nora had waited three years, the exclusion would have dropped to $250,000, and the result would have been similar here only because the gain is small. A bigger gain would have created a tax bill. If the house had been in Walt's name alone, the whole house would have stepped up and the gain would have been smaller still.

## What documents do I need to prove the number?

Gather them before closing, not at tax time.

1. A date-of-death appraisal by a licensed appraiser. Ask for one in writing with the date stated.
2. The deed and the closing statement from the sale, showing costs.
3. Records of improvements, such as a new roof or kitchen.
4. For a house held by spouses, the original purchase documents, to show the survivor's half.
5. The estate's inventory, if the executor filed one, and Form 706 if an estate tax return was required.
6. IRS Form 1099-S from the closing agent, and your own Form 8949 and Schedule D.

The executor usually has the appraisal. If the house is in a trust, the trustee does. See [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) for the full sequence.

## What do Illinois and New Jersey add?

Both states tax capital gains as ordinary income on the state return, so a gain above the stepped-up basis is taxed twice, federally and by the state. Neither state has a separate capital gains rate for homes.

New Jersey adds a step before the sale. The Division of Taxation must issue a real property tax waiver before a dead owner's house can be transferred, unless it is held as tenants by the entirety or in a trust. Our guide to [inheriting a house in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/inheriting-a-house-in-new-jersey) explains Form L-9 and the timing. That waiver is about inheritance tax, not capital gains, but it can delay closing if no one starts early.

Illinois has no inheritance tax, and its estate tax applies only above a threshold that most homeowners are well under. The details are in [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).

## Should I sell now or wait?

That is a decision about carrying costs and market timing more than tax. Property tax, insurance, utilities and repairs run month by month, and a delay of a year can cost more than the tax you are trying to avoid. Our [keep-or-sell worksheet](/learn/property-and-assets/should-you-keep-or-sell-an-inherited-house-a-carrying-cost-and-timing-worksheet) puts numbers on that. For widows and widowers, the broader question is in [should you sell the house after your spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/should-you-sell-the-house-after-your-spouse-dies).
