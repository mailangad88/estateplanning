Once a house is in a trust, many owners worry that it is stuck. It is not. A revocable trust is built to be changed, and you can sell, borrow against or rent out the house just as before. The question is how the paperwork changes, because the seller on the deed is now the trustee, not you personally.

This page covers selling, refinancing and moving out of a trust-owned house. If you are still deciding whether to deed your house to a trust, start with our guide to [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust). This page belongs to our [property and assets series](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Can I sell a house that is in my living trust?

Yes. The trustee has the power to sell trust property, and in a revocable trust you are the trustee. Nothing about the trust prevents a sale during your lifetime unless you wrote in a limit.

What changes is who signs and what documents are used.

1. **List the house as normal.** A buyer does not care whether the owner is a person or a trust.
2. **Give the title company a certification of trust.** It is a short document, signed by the trustee, that confirms the trust exists, names the trustee and states that the trustee has authority to sell. It avoids handing over the full trust, which contains private family terms.
3. **Sign the deed as trustee.** The deed reads something like "Marianne Ortiz, as Trustee of the Ortiz Family Trust." Your attorney or the title company drafts it.
4. **Put the proceeds in the trust.** Deposit the money into a trust account, or into an account titled in your name as trustee. Money that goes into your personal account is outside the trust unless you retitle it, which leaves part of your plan unfunded. Our article on [how to tell if your trust is actually funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded) explains why that matters.
5. **Record and keep copies.** The county recorder gets the deed; you keep the closing statement for tax records.

### Who must approve?

You alone, if you are the sole trustee and the trust gives you the power. If the trust names co-trustees, all or a majority may need to sign, depending on the document. If you have become incapacitated, the successor trustee acts. See [co-trustees and changing a trustee](/learn/trusts/co-trustees-and-changing-a-trustee).

The beneficiaries who will get the house at your death do not have a say. They have a hope, not a right, until you die.

## Can I refinance a house that is in a trust?

Usually, and lenders see it all the time. A mortgage lender will want to confirm that the trust is revocable and that you, the borrower, are the trustee. The lender then has the trustee sign the loan documents and often the deed of trust or mortgage.

| Lender asks for | Why | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Certification of trust or trust excerpts | Confirms the trustee's authority | Ask your attorney to prepare one in advance |
| Title insurance commitment | Confirms ownership and liens | Ask the title company whether coverage continues after your transfer |
| Trustee signature, sometimes your personal signature as well | Binds the trust and you | Sign both where asked |
| A deed back into your name before closing | Some lenders still prefer this | Ask at application, not at closing |

If a lender insists on a deed back, the transfer out and back in has to be recorded. After closing, you or your attorney should record a new deed putting the house back in the trust. Skipping that step is a common way for a trust to become unfunded by accident. The federal Garn-St Germain Act protects many owner-occupants from a lender calling the loan when the house goes into the owner's own trust, as described in our article on moving a house to a living trust. Refinancing is a new loan, not a transfer, so it follows the lender's own rules.

## What happens if I move out of a house in my trust?

The trust keeps owning it, and you keep control. Several side effects depend on what you do with the house next.

**Insurance.** Homeowners policies often limit coverage for homes that sit empty for 30 to 60 days or are rented. Tell your agent before you move and ask whether you need a vacant-home or landlord policy. Add the trustee as an insured if you have not.

**Property tax exemptions.** A homestead or owner-occupied exemption generally depends on living there. In Illinois, the general homestead exemption is tied to the owner's primary residence, and a trust can qualify if a beneficiary lives in the home. Our article on [whether a living trust affects homestead and property taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-affect-homestead-exemption-and-property-taxes) covers the rules and the Illinois angle. In New Jersey, the property tax relief programs have their own eligibility rules; check with the municipality.

**Mortgage terms.** Most mortgages say you will live in the house as your main home, at least for the first year. Moving out for a medical reason or a move to assisted living is common, but tell the servicer if you are going to rent it out.

**Sale tax treatment.** The home sale exclusion requires two years of use as your main home in the five years before the sale. Because a revocable trust is ignored for income tax, moving out and selling within the window still qualifies. Move out for longer and you may lose some or all of the exclusion. See [capital gains when you sell a house](/learn/property-and-assets/capital-gains-when-you-sell-an-inherited-house-or-a-house-after-a-spouse-dies) for how the numbers work.

### Example: moving to assisted living

Example only. Joyce, 81, of Joliet, transferred her house to her living trust five years ago. She moves to an assisted living apartment in January and plans to sell by summer. She calls her insurer, who tells her to add a vacancy endorsement for the months the house will sit empty. She tells her mortgage servicer. Her daughter, the successor trustee, has a certification of trust ready. In July Joyce signs the deed as trustee, the sale closes, and the proceeds go into the trust account that pays her assisted living bills. Because she lived in the house for most of the last five years, she can use the main-home exclusion.

That outcome came from a few calls made early. Our guide to [updating your plan when you move to assisted living](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-when-you-move-to-assisted-living-or-your-health-declines) lists the others.

## What if I am the only trustee and can no longer act?

Then the successor trustee named in the trust takes over, after a doctor's letter or another proof of incapacity that the trust specifies. This is one of the reasons people use trusts. The family does not need a court-appointed conservator to sell a house for care costs. Without a trust, a sale can require a court order if you cannot sign. See [what happens to a living trust when you die or become incapacitated](/learn/trusts/what-happens-to-a-living-trust-when-you-die) for the steps.

## Should I take the house out of the trust to sell it?

No, usually it is unnecessary. A trustee's deed from the trust is a normal way to sell. Retitling to yourself first adds a recording step and can sometimes disturb title insurance. Ask your attorney if a particular buyer or lender is asking for it.

If the owner has died and a successor trustee is selling, see [selling a house held in a trust after a death](/learn/after-a-death/selling-a-house-held-in-a-trust-after-death).
