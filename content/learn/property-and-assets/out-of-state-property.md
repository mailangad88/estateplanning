Many families end up owning property across state lines: a lake cabin, a beach condo, a rental, a parent's old house, or land inherited from a relative. At death, each of those can add a second set of court costs and paperwork unless planned for.

This page explains how out-of-state property is treated, what it costs your family if you do nothing, and the practical fixes. It is part of our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Why does property in another state need its own probate?

Real estate is controlled by the courts and laws of the state where it is located. Your home state's probate court can handle everything you own in that state and your personal property wherever it sits, but it has no power to transfer title to land in another state. The other state's court does that, and the process is called [ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate).

Imagine Walter, 71, a Minnesota resident who owns his home plus a cabin in Wisconsin. He dies with a will. His executor opens probate in Minnesota. To transfer the cabin to Walter's daughters, someone must also open a case in Wisconsin, typically with a Wisconsin attorney. Two courts, two sets of filing fees, two timelines, and possibly two attorneys.

Our page on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains the procedure in detail, and [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) covers the basics.

## What does ancillary probate cost?

The cost is the second proceeding, and it can be disproportionate to the property's value. Expect these:

- **Local counsel.** Most executors cannot appear in a court in another state without a local attorney.
- **Court fees and publication costs.** They vary by state.
- **Delay.** The out-of-state case may take months, and the heirs cannot sell or refinance until it ends.
- **Extra paperwork.** Certified copies of the home-state court documents are generally required.
- **Ongoing carrying costs.** Taxes, insurance and upkeep continue during the case.

Some states let a nonresident executor use a simplified procedure for small or uncontested matters. Don't count on it.

## How can you avoid ancillary probate?

You avoid it by making sure the property does not pass through your will. Four tools do that.

### A revocable living trust

You sign a deed transferring the out-of-state property to the trustee of your [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust). At death, the successor trustee transfers or sells it under the trust's instructions, with no court case in either state. This is the most widely used solution and works in all states. The catch is that the deed must be prepared and recorded under the laws of the state where the property sits, so you may need local help. See [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

### A transfer-on-death deed

If the state allows one, a [TOD deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) names the person who receives the property at death. It is low cost and easy to revoke. The law of the state where the land is located governs, so check that state's requirements. It can be rigid and does not manage a property shared among several children.

### Joint ownership

Titling the property jointly with a spouse or another person with right of survivorship passes it to the survivor, without ancillary probate. Between spouses this is common. Adding an adult child carries the hidden risks in our article on [joint tenancy](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

### An LLC or other entity

Placing the property in a limited liability company means you own business interests, not land. Personal property interests are generally governed by your home state's law, so they pass with your home-state estate. That may avoid a second probate. But entities involve annual filings, can complicate mortgages and insurance, and may change property tax treatment. Use only on advice from an attorney.

## Can another state tax your estate?

Yes, in some cases. The federal estate tax applies to your total estate wherever it sits, and as of 2026 only estates above $15 million per person are subject to it. A dozen or so states have their own estate tax, and some states have an inheritance tax. A number of these states tax real estate located within their borders owned by nonresidents, and the thresholds are often much lower than the federal one.

That means an owner who lives in a state with no estate tax but owns a cabin in a state that has one might owe tax there. The rules differ by state, so check the [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) page and the state guides, such as New York, Washington or Oregon, for the states where you own land. Putting a property in a trust does not generally change the state estate tax result, but placing it in an entity sometimes does, which is one more reason to talk to a lawyer.

## What if property is owned in a community property state?

If you live in a community property state, or you move between the two kinds of states, how the property is characterized can change. Property acquired during marriage in a community property state typically stays community even if you move, but real estate in a common-law state follows that state's rules. Surviving spouses may have different rights in each. This is an area where local advice matters, particularly for couples who relocated after retirement. See community property.

## How do you plan for a vacation home that several children will inherit?

A shared vacation property is a common source of family conflict, often because nobody discussed the costs. Decide these questions now:

1. **Who will own it?** All children equally, or one child who buys out the rest.
2. **How will it be funded?** A cash reserve or a separate trust account for taxes, insurance, and repairs.
3. **Who gets to use it, and when?** A calendar and rules for guests prevent resentment.
4. **How do decisions get made?** A majority vote, a trustee, or an LLC operating agreement.
5. **What is the exit?** A buyout formula, a right of first refusal for siblings, and a sale if a set number of owners want out.
6. **What if a child dies, divorces or goes bankrupt?** Trust or LLC provisions can keep the property in the family.

A trust or LLC can document these rules. Without them, any co-owner can ask a court to force a sale.

## What is a practical plan, step by step?

1. **List every property** with its location, how it is titled and whether there is a mortgage.
2. **Pull the current deed** from the county recorder. Many are searchable online.
3. **Decide who should receive each property** and on what terms.
4. **Choose a tool** for each, usually a trust deed, or a TOD deed where allowed.
5. **Hire local counsel for the deed if needed.** Recording rules differ.
6. **Check state taxes** for each state.
7. **Notify the lender and insurer** and update the coverage.
8. **Keep a record** of the deeds and instructions with your plan, and tell your executor or trustee where they are.

Mineral rights, timeshares and farmland have similar issues, and each may need its own deed or documents. If you own property abroad, the foreign country's law governs it, and you should get advice there.
