A car outlasts its owner by months or years in the driveway, and the family usually realizes the problem when the registration comes due. Cars, motorcycles, boats and RVs are titled property. Whoever the title says owns it, owns it, and nothing else you can show the motor vehicle office, including a handwritten note, changes that.

This page explains how a vehicle changes hands after a death in each common situation, and how to leave a vehicle on purpose. It is part of our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Who gets the vehicle when the owner dies?

The answer depends on how the title reads, so find the title first. The title is usually kept in a file at home, with the lender if there is a loan, or on record with the state motor vehicle agency, which can issue a copy to the right person.

| What the title says | Who gets the vehicle | Court needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Two names joined by "or" or "with right of survivorship" | The surviving co-owner | Usually no. |
| Two names joined by "and," no survivorship | Depends on state law; often the deceased owner's half goes through the estate | Possibly. |
| One name plus a transfer-on-death beneficiary | The beneficiary named | No. |
| One name, no beneficiary | The estate, then whoever the will or state law names | Often yes, unless a small estate procedure applies. |
| In a living trust | The trust, under its terms | No, the successor trustee acts. |

The deciding word on a joint title is often a tiny one. Many people sign a title as "Sam and Lena" without understanding that it may not give Lena the car. If you are the surviving owner, bring the title and a death certificate and ask the office how it reads before assuming.

## How does a car pass when there is a will?

A vehicle titled in the deceased person's name alone is a probate asset. The executor named in the will asks the probate court for letters testamentary, which is the document proving authority, and then signs the title over to the person the will names. The motor vehicle agency typically asks for the original title, the death certificate, the letters, an application for a new title and any odometer disclosure form for the transfer. Requirements differ from state to state, so call the office or check its website before going in.

If the will does not mention the vehicle, it passes with the rest of the estate under the residuary clause. That is how a car ends up owned by four siblings. Someone has to buy out the others or sell it. A one-line specific gift avoids the argument: "I give my 2019 Subaru Outback to my nephew Ben." Our page on [leaving jewelry, heirlooms and sentimental items](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-jewelry-heirlooms-and-sentimental-items) shows how to word these gifts.

## Can a vehicle transfer without probate?

Often, yes. Vehicles are the easiest probate asset to move without a full court case. Three routes are common:

- **Survivorship or joint title.** The survivor retitles with a death certificate.
- **Transfer-on-death title.** A number of states let an owner name a beneficiary on the title itself, the way a bank account has a payable-on-death designation. States that allow it have a specific form, and the beneficiary retitles by showing a death certificate and ID. Ask your state's motor vehicle agency whether it offers this.
- **Small estate procedures.** Many states let an heir use an affidavit or a short form to retitle a vehicle without opening probate, sometimes when the vehicle is the only asset, and sometimes with a waiting period after the death. Our page on the [small estate affidavit](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) explains the general idea, and the [small estate checker](/tools/small-estate-checker) can show whether your estate may qualify.

Some states also have a special rule for a surviving spouse that lets them retitle the family car with a simple form. Check before paying for a court filing you may not need.

## What are the steps to retitle a vehicle after a death?

Here is a workable order of operations for an heir or executor.

1. **Locate the title and any loan papers.** If the title is lost, request a duplicate from the state.
2. **Get certified copies of the death certificate.** Order several. Vehicles, banks and insurers each want one.
3. **Keep insurance active.** Call the insurer, tell them the owner has died and ask what is required to keep coverage while the vehicle is parked or being transferred.
4. **Decide who is entitled.** Read the title, then the will, then state law. If there is a lender, find out the balance.
5. **Gather the transfer papers.** Typically the original title, the death certificate, proof of authority such as letters or an affidavit, an application for title and an odometer statement.
6. **File with the motor vehicle agency and pay the fees.** Ask about sales tax or title tax exemptions for transfers to family members or heirs, because many states waive or reduce them.
7. **Register and insure in the new owner's name.** Do this before driving the vehicle on public roads.

## What if there is a loan on the vehicle?

The debt follows the vehicle. If the new owner keeps making payments, the lender generally does not mind. If payments stop, the lender can repossess the vehicle, regardless of who has the title. The estate is responsible for paying valid debts before heirs receive what is left, so an heir who inherits an underwater loan may be better off asking the executor to hand the vehicle back to the lender or to sell it. See [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

If the car is worth less than the loan, nobody is required to keep it. Surrendering a vehicle voluntarily is usually better for the family's credit than a repossession after months of missed payments.

## What about boats, motorcycles and RVs?

They follow the same logic with a few differences.

- **Motorcycles and RVs** are titled with the state like cars. The same options apply.
- **Boats** may be titled with the state or, for larger vessels, documented with the U.S. Coast Guard. The registration papers show which. A documented vessel has a separate documentation record, and a new owner must file the right form. Boat trailers have their own titles in many states.
- **Collector vehicles and valuable boats** can be worth more than the whole house contents. Get an appraisal, keep the insurance and consider whether a revocable trust is cleaner for these. Our page on [funding a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) lists what usually does and does not go in.

Marisol's father died owning a 2004 pickup truck and a fishing boat, both in his name alone, with no co-owner. He had a short will leaving everything to Marisol and her brother. They could not sell either one until Marisol, as executor, received letters from the probate court. The estate was small, but because the state has a threshold for vehicles and the boat pushed the total over it, a simple affidavit was not available. The truck sat for four months. A transfer-on-death title would have solved this in a single trip to the motor vehicle office.

## How do I leave a vehicle in my will, or avoid probate for it?

Name the vehicle and the person, or use a title feature that passes it automatically. The options, from least to most work:

- A specific gift in your will, with a backup person. Easy, but probate is needed unless a small estate procedure applies.
- Joint title with a spouse or partner who will own it at your death. Fine between spouses; riskier with a child, for the reasons in our page on [joint ownership risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).
- A transfer-on-death title, where your state offers it. The cheapest way to skip probate for one vehicle.
- A revocable living trust, for valuable or collectible vehicles. See [what is a living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).

Whatever you pick, tell the person. The most common failure is a vehicle that nobody knew was titled in the deceased person's name alone.
