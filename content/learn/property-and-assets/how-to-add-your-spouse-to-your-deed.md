Maybe you bought the house before you married. Maybe you refinanced and your spouse's name never made it onto the deed. Maybe a friend told you that if you die first, your spouse will "have to go through probate." You want to know how to add your spouse to the deed and whether you should.

This article gives the steps, the wording that matters in Illinois and New Jersey, and the situations where adding a spouse backfires. For the broader choices on how to hold a home, see our [property and assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## How do you add a spouse to a house deed?

You sign a new deed, you do not amend the old one. The new deed names you as the grantor and you and your spouse as the grantees. It is the same process as any deed, and the details matter.

1. **Get your current deed.** Find the vesting deed in your closing papers or request a copy from the county. You need the exact legal description and the way your name is written.
2. **Decide how you will hold title.** For a married couple the usual choice is tenancy by the entirety. The alternative is joint tenancy with right of survivorship.
3. **Choose the deed form.** A warranty deed or a quitclaim deed both work between spouses. See [types of deeds](/learn/property-and-assets/types-of-deeds-quitclaim-warranty-grant-and-trustee-deeds) for how they differ.
4. **Draft the deed with the survivorship wording.** The language that creates the entireties tenancy is not optional in Illinois.
5. **Sign before a notary.** The owner on the old deed must sign. A spouse who is not an owner does not need to sign to receive it, but many attorneys have both sign to waive other claims.
6. **Complete the transfer-tax form.** A gift between spouses is exempt, but the exemption has to be claimed on the form the county or state requires.
7. **Record the deed** with the county recorder in Illinois (in Cook County, the County Clerk's recordings division) or the county clerk in New Jersey. Pay the recording fee listed in the county schedule.
8. **Tell the lender, the insurance company and the assessor.** Ask the insurer to add your spouse as an insured.

A deed that is signed but never recorded can fail against a later buyer or creditor. Check that the recorded copy arrives and keep it with your estate documents.

## What does the deed have to say in Illinois and New Jersey?

The wording differs, and a deed that follows the wrong state's habits can give you a result you did not intend.

In **Illinois**, a conveyance to two people creates a tenancy in common unless the deed expressly declares otherwise. To get survivorship, the deed must say the two hold "in joint tenancy" or, for spouses holding their homestead, "as tenants by the entirety." Without that wording the house does not pass to the survivor automatically. Read more in our article on [Illinois joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety).

In **New Jersey**, a deed to a husband and wife is treated as a tenancy by the entirety by statute, and neither spouse can sever it alone. Even so, a good deed states the tenancy outright so a title company has nothing to question. The rules are in [joint property and tenancy by the entirety in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/joint-property-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety-in-new-jersey).

## What happens to the mortgage and title insurance?

Adding a spouse does not normally disturb either one, but you should confirm. The federal Garn-St Germain Act generally prevents a lender from using a due-on-sale clause when a borrower transfers the home to a spouse. The mortgage remains in your name until you pay it off, and your spouse becomes an owner without becoming a borrower.

Title insurance is less settled. Many policies keep covering a spouse who receives the property from the insured, but older policies and some endorsements read differently. Call the insurer, or the title company that wrote the policy, and ask for confirmation in writing. If you are refinancing anyway, add your spouse to the deed through the refinance closing, and the new lender's title company will insure the result.

Homeowner's insurance is simpler. Add your spouse as a named insured. If a claim is paid for damage, the check should cover both owners.

## Are there tax consequences?

For most married couples, the answer is no tax today and a small one later. Adding a spouse is a gift of half the house, but a gift to a US citizen spouse qualifies for the unlimited marital deduction, so no gift tax applies. If your spouse is not a US citizen, a capped annual limit applies instead. See [marital deduction explained](/learn/estate-tax/marital-deduction-explained).

At the first death, the tax basis of the house steps up on the deceased spouse's half. The survivor's half keeps its old basis. That is a smaller adjustment than if the house had been held solely by the deceased spouse and bequeathed. Our article on [step-up in basis for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis-for-married-couples-and-joint-property) shows an example with figures.

Both states generally exempt a no-payment transfer between spouses from their transfer taxes, but the exemption has to be claimed on the transfer form. In practice, the title company or attorney completes it.

## What are the benefits of adding a spouse?

There are three real benefits.

- **Probate avoidance.** The survivor owns the house automatically and records the death certificate to clear title. No court is needed for that asset.
- **Creditor protection.** In both states, entireties ownership generally protects the home from creditors who have a claim against only one spouse, though not from debts you owe jointly or from federal tax liens.
- **Clear title for the survivor.** The surviving spouse does not need letters of office or an heirship finding to prove ownership. A death certificate and a short affidavit are normally enough for the recorder and a future buyer.

## When should you not add your spouse?

Hold off if any of these apply, and talk to an attorney first.

- **It is a second marriage and you have children from before.** The survivor takes the whole house, and your children can end up with nothing. A trust or a life estate for the spouse with the remainder to your children does a better job. See [the family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage).
- **Your spouse is in poor health or you may need long-term care.** Transfers between spouses are exempt from Medicaid penalties, but putting the house in the ill spouse's name changes the planning. Check [Medicaid for married couples](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-for-married-couples) first.
- **Your marriage is shaky.** A spouse on title has real rights, and unwinding it needs their cooperation or a court.
- **The house was a gift or inheritance to you alone.** Putting it into joint names may turn separate property into marital property in a divorce. Our article on [whether an inheritance is marital property](/learn/after-a-death/is-an-inheritance-marital-property) explains why.

## Example: Ruth and Daniel

Ruth, 63, owns a house in Naperville worth $420,000 that she bought before she married Daniel, 66, five years ago. Her only child, Alicia, is from her first marriage. Ruth wants to add Daniel because she worries he will have a hard time if she dies first.

If Ruth adds Daniel as tenants by the entirety, Daniel becomes the sole owner on her death. When Daniel dies, the house goes where Daniel's will sends it, and that might not be to Alicia. If Ruth wants Daniel to keep living there but wants Alicia to end up with the house, she should not add him. A revocable trust that gives Daniel the right to live in the house for life, with the remainder to Alicia, does both. Adding Daniel to the deed would be an accidental decision to disinherit her daughter.
