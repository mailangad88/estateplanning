Your grandmother's farm, a family house in town or a few wooded acres has been in the family for decades. The deed may still carry the name of someone who died in 1985. Several cousins pay taxes, a few use the land, and one has just said that he wants to sell his share.

That situation has a name: heirs' property. It is common, quiet and fragile. This article explains how it happens, the risks, how courts handle partition, and the practical steps to put the land on solid ground.

## What is heirs' property and how does it happen?

Heirs' property is land owned by the relatives of someone who died without a will or without anyone opening probate. When a person dies without a will, state law gives the property to the heirs in set shares. If no one files in court and no new deed is recorded, the heirs become co-owners (usually tenants in common) without any paperwork. If one of them also dies without a will, their share splits among their heirs, and so on.

Here is how it grows. Ruth owned 40 acres and died in 1978 with no will. Her five children inherited equally, but no one opened an estate. Two of the children have since died, one with three children and one with two. The land now has ten owners who may not know each other, and each owner's share is a fraction of an undivided whole. No one can point to a single deed that shows all ten.

Common causes include:

- Families who distrust the courts or cannot afford a lawyer.
- A belief that "everyone knows whose land this is," so no record is needed.
- Informal arrangements, such as one sibling living on the land and paying the taxes.
- No will, or a will that was never probated.

## Why does it matter?

Heirs' property matters because unclear title blocks ordinary things and makes the land easy to lose. These are the main risks:

- **Forced sale.** A co-owner, or someone who buys a share from one, can ask a court to partition the property, and the court may order it sold.
- **No loans.** Lenders generally will not make a mortgage or home improvement loan against land with unclear title.
- **Insurance and aid problems.** Insurers or disaster programs may ask for proof of ownership, and a family without a recorded deed can find it difficult to qualify.
- **No clear right to sell.** Buyers and title companies will not close without every owner's signature.
- **Tax and eligibility issues.** Property tax bills may go unpaid when no one knows who is responsible, and tax sales can follow. Some agricultural and homestead programs also require proof of ownership.

## What is partition, and how does the Uniform Act change it?

A partition action is a lawsuit in which a co-owner asks the court to divide the land or sell it and divide the money. Courts can divide land into parcels (partition in kind), or order a sale and split the proceeds (partition by sale). For a century, many courts treated sale as the easy answer when the land could not be divided evenly, so a speculator who bought one small share could force a sale of the whole property, often at a low price at auction.

The Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act, drafted by the Uniform Law Commission, gives co-owners more protection. In a state that has adopted it, the process generally works like this:

1. **Notice.** All co-owners are notified when a partition is filed.
2. **Appraisal.** The court orders an appraisal of the property's fair market value.
3. **Buyout.** Co-owners who do not want to sell get the first chance to buy the shares of the co-owner who asked for the partition, at a price based on the appraisal.
4. **Preference for division.** The court considers whether partition in kind is practical and fair, and weighs factors such as family use, sentimental value and the owners' histories with the land, before ordering a sale.
5. **Open-market sale.** If a sale is still ordered, it is generally conducted on the open market, not at a rushed auction.

According to the American Bar Association's summary of the 2025 legislative year, 24 states plus the District of Columbia and the U.S. Virgin Islands had enacted the act. Your state may or may not be one of them, so check it. If not, the older rules apply, and the risk of a forced sale is higher.

## How do you clear title?

The usual way to clear title is to settle each estate in the chain and then record deeds that show who owns what. The right route depends on state law and the facts, but the common tools are:

- **Probate or administration.** Open an estate for each person who died owning an interest and had no estate opened. A court appoints someone to transfer title to the heirs. Several estates may be handled together in some states. See [how to file probate](/learn/probate/how-to-file-probate).
- **Affidavit of heirship.** Some states allow family members to sign a sworn statement of who the heirs are and record it in the county land records. It can help a title company, but it does not always settle title on its own. Whether and when an affidavit works depends on state law.
- **Quiet title action.** A court case that asks a judge to declare who owns the land. It is slower and more costly but produces a clear order.
- **Deeds among heirs.** When all the heirs are identified and agree, they can sign deeds to one person or to a group, such as a family LLC or trust.
- **A family LLC or trust.** Heirs can transfer the land into an entity that holds title and sets rules for use, taxes and buyouts. See [leaving your home to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) for how trusts and deeds compare.

Start with a family tree. List every person who died owning a share, their spouse, and their children. Dates, places and certified copies of death certificates are the raw material. Missing or distant relatives are the hardest part, and an attorney can help find them or give notice by publication when they cannot be found.

## How do you protect the land going forward?

You prevent the next heirs' property problem with a plan made while the owner is alive. The tools are familiar:

- **A will** that names who inherits and an executor to open probate. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).
- **A revocable living trust** that holds the land and names a successor trustee, so the land passes without probate.
- **A transfer-on-death deed**, in states that allow it, naming a beneficiary. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).
- **A written agreement among heirs** that covers who uses the land, who pays taxes and insurance, how to vote on decisions and how a share can be bought out.

Be careful with shortcuts. Adding a child to the deed during life can create other problems, from creditor exposure to losing the stepped-up basis. See [adding a child to your deed: the hidden risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks). The sorting rules for which assets go through court are in [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## Is there funding to help heirs resolve ownership?

Yes, in some cases. The U.S. Department of Agriculture created an Heirs' Property Relending Program, which makes loans to eligible lenders that then relend to heirs to help resolve ownership and succession issues. Program details, funding and eligible lenders change, so check with the Farm Service Agency for the current status. Some nonprofit legal aid groups, law school clinics and state bar programs also help families clear title for little or no cost.

## How should the family decide?

Decide the practical questions in writing before they turn into a fight:

| Situation | Risk | First step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One heir lives on the land and pays the taxes | Others may claim a share of the value or dispute who pays | Written agreement on use, taxes and credit for improvements |
| An heir wants to sell a share to an outsider | Forced sale by the buyer | Offer the other heirs a first right to buy |
| No one knows who owns it | Unclear title, tax sale risk | Build a family tree and check the county land records |
| Heirs have scattered and cannot be found | A court case may be needed | Ask an attorney about notice and quiet title |
| Someone offers cash for a quitclaim deed | Selling for far below value | Get an appraisal and legal advice before signing |

Common mistakes to avoid: paying the property taxes without any written agreement, since later disputes about repayment are common; selling a share to an outsider; and signing a quitclaim deed for cash from a buyer you do not know.

If an heir wants to sell, see [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) for who can sign and how the sale is taxed. Heirs who receive land generally get a stepped-up basis to its value at the date of death, which can reduce tax on a later sale.
