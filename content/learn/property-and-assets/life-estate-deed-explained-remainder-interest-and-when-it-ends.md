A life estate deed has a simple pitch. You keep living in your house until you die, and your daughter gets it afterward without probate. People hear it from a neighbor or a financial planner and assume it is the clean version of adding a child to the deed.

It is cleaner in some ways and riskier in others. This article explains the vocabulary, how the deed works day to day, when it ends, and what it does to taxes and Medicaid. For other ways to keep a house out of court, start with our [property and assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## What is a life estate deed?

A life estate deed divides one piece of property into two interests. The first is the life estate, which belongs to the life tenant. The second is the remainder interest, which belongs to the remainderman. Both are created on the same day by one deed.

Say Walter, 78, owns his house in Peoria, Illinois, and signs a deed granting it "to my daughter Colleen, reserving a life estate to myself." From that day, Walter is the life tenant and Colleen is the remainderman. Walter keeps the right to live in the house, rent it and collect the rent. Colleen holds a future interest that becomes full ownership when Walter dies.

The deed is recorded with the county, like any other deed. The wording matters. A deed that says only "to Colleen" gives away the whole house. The reservation of the life estate is what lets Walter stay.

## What can the life tenant do, and what can't they do?

The life tenant has a strong right to live in the property and a weak right to do anything else. This is the part people underestimate.

| Action | Life tenant alone | Needs the remainder owner |
|---|---|---|
| Live in the house | Yes | No |
| Rent it and keep the income | Yes | No |
| Pay taxes, insurance and ordinary repairs | Required | No |
| Sell the whole house | No | Yes, both must sign |
| Mortgage or refinance | No | Yes, lenders ask both to sign |
| Make major changes or tear down | No (this is called waste) | Yes |
| Change who gets the remainder | No | Yes, only the remainderman can give it up |

A life tenant who stops paying taxes or lets the roof collapse can be taken to court by the remainder owner. A life tenant who wants to move to assisted living can sell only if the child agrees, and then the proceeds are divided by the value of each interest under actuarial tables, not simply handed over to the owner. For many families, that is the moment they wish they had picked something more flexible.

## When does a life estate end?

Most end at the life tenant's death, and at that moment the remainderman becomes the full owner. There are no estate proceedings to open for the house. The new owner usually records the death certificate and files an affidavit with the recorder, and the property is theirs. Debts and a mortgage stay attached to the house.

Other endings are less common but worth knowing:

1. **Both owners sign a sale deed.** A buyer gets the whole property and the sale price is split between the two interests.
2. **The life tenant releases the interest** to the remainderman, which merges the two into full ownership.
3. **One person acquires both interests**, for example if the remainderman inherits the life estate.
4. **The life estate is measured by another person's life**, such as a spouse or a sibling, and ends when that person dies.

A life estate does not end because the life tenant moves out. If Walter goes into a nursing home, he still holds the life estate. It does not end by divorce or a remarriage either, and the remainder owner cannot step in early.

## What happens to taxes?

A life estate has two tax effects that surprise people: one at the time of the deed, one at death.

**At signing.** The gift of the remainder interest is a gift, even though Walter lives in the house. Its value is calculated from IRS tables based on Walter's age and the value of the house. A gift of a future interest does not qualify for the annual exclusion of $19,000 per recipient in 2026, so the donor generally files a gift tax return (Form 709). No tax is due unless lifetime gifts exceed the $15,000,000 exclusion in 2026. See our article on [Form 709](/learn/estate-tax/form-709-gift-tax-return-gift-splitting-and-net-gifts).

**At death.** Because Walter kept the right to live in the house, its full value is included in his estate for federal estate tax purposes. That inclusion gives Colleen a stepped-up basis equal to the value at his death. If Colleen sells for $300,000 and the date-of-death value was $295,000, her taxable gain is $5,000, not the larger gain measured from what Walter paid decades ago. A plain gift of the house, with no life estate, would have given her Walter's old basis. Our article on [giving your house to a child](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs) covers that trade-off.

Property tax is a local matter. Ask the county assessor whether a senior or homestead exemption survives the deed before you sign, because the rules turn on who owns and who lives in the house.

## What about Medicaid and long-term care?

A life estate deed is a transfer for Medicaid purposes. In most states, giving away the remainder interest starts the 60-month look-back clock, and the value of the gift can lead to a penalty period if you apply within five years. After the five years, the house is no longer counted as yours if you are applying for nursing home Medicaid.

The catch is estate recovery. Some states pursue the house after death even though it passes outside probate. New Jersey is one of the states with a broad definition, which is covered in [New Jersey estate recovery](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-estate-recovery). Illinois has its own rules, summarized in [Illinois Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/illinois/illinois-medicaid-estate-recovery). Do not sign a deed meant to protect a house from Medicaid without current advice for your state.

## What are the risks of a life estate?

The remainder owner's life is the biggest risk, because the interest is theirs from the day you sign. A few things can happen.

- **Their creditor can attach the remainder.** A child with a judgment or a bankruptcy puts the future interest in play, which can cloud the title.
- **Their divorce can pull it in.** A spouse may claim part of the value in a property settlement.
- **They can die first.** If Colleen dies before Walter, her remainder goes to her heirs or under her will. Her spouse or children could end up owning it, and Walter has no say.
- **They can disagree.** Without their signature you cannot sell, refinance or borrow.
- **The lender can notice.** Federal law generally protects a transfer to a child when the borrower keeps living in the home, but you should tell the lender and the title insurer before recording.

The same issues appear in our article on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed), and the problems of joint title are in [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).

## How does a life estate compare with other tools?

If your main goals are to stay in your house, avoid probate and keep control, other tools often do the job better.

| Tool | Keep control to sell? | Avoids probate? | Stepped-up basis at death? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Life estate deed | No, needs the child | Yes | Yes |
| Lady bird (enhanced life estate) deed | Yes, in states that recognize it | Yes | Yes |
| Transfer on death deed or instrument | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Revocable living trust | Yes | Yes, if the house is retitled | Yes |

Illinois does not have an enhanced life estate in common use, but it does have a transfer on death instrument, covered in [our Illinois TODI article](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument). New Jersey has no TOD deed, so families there usually weigh a trust against a life estate, as described in [transfer on death options in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-on-death-options-in-new-jersey). A spouse who needs to be protected after the owner dies may be a good candidate for a life estate, which is the subject of [the life estate for a surviving spouse](/learn/blended-families/life-estate-for-a-surviving-spouse).
