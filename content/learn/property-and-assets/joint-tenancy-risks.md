Adding a child to a deed or bank account is one of the most common do-it-yourself estate plans. The reasoning is sensible: the child can help with bills, and when you die, the asset goes to them without probate. Many people do it after a friend or a bank teller suggests it.

The shortcut works in a narrow sense, and it has costs that rarely come up until something goes wrong. This page lists them and shows the alternatives. It is part of our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## What does adding a child to your account or deed actually do?

It makes your child a legal owner today. For a bank account, a joint owner can usually withdraw any amount, at any time, without telling you. For a deed, the child owns an interest in the real estate and must sign to sell or refinance it. Most joint accounts and many deeds also carry a right of survivorship, so the asset passes to the surviving owner at the first owner's death and your will does not apply.

That is different from naming a child as a beneficiary. A beneficiary or transfer-on-death designation gives a child nothing while you are alive. A joint owner owns something right now. Our glossary entry on [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy) explains the legal form.

## What are the main risks?

### 1. The child's creditors and ex-spouse can reach it

Once your child is a co-owner, their personal problems can touch your asset. If your son is sued after a car accident, a judgment creditor may be able to put a lien on his share of the house or freeze a joint account. If he divorces, a court may treat his interest as marital property. If he files for bankruptcy, the trustee may claim the interest. These situations are uncommon until they are not.

### 2. A gift of a house can cost your child the step-up in basis

When you put a child on a deed, you are giving them part of the home. For the share you give, the child takes your original tax basis. Sophie, 78, bought her house for $80,000, and it is now worth $400,000. If she adds her daughter Dana to the deed as a half owner, Dana's basis in her half is $40,000. If Dana later sells her half, she may owe capital gains tax on much of the $160,000 of appreciation on that half. If Dana had simply inherited the house, her basis would have been the full $400,000 and the gain would likely be zero.

How much of a jointly owned property is included in your estate at death, and so how much receives a step-up, depends on the type of ownership and on who paid for it. The rules are technical and easy to get wrong, and the losses can be large. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

### 3. Gift tax reporting

A transfer to a child of a share worth more than the annual exclusion, $19,000 per recipient in 2026, is a taxable gift that must be reported on a gift tax return. You will almost certainly owe no tax because of the $15 million lifetime exclusion, but the return is required, and the gift reduces your remaining exclusion. A bank account is different: putting a child on the account is not usually a completed gift until the child withdraws money for their own use. See [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).

### 4. Medicaid and the look-back

If you might need nursing home care, a transfer to a child can be counted as a gift under the [Medicaid look-back](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period), which in most states is 60 months. A gift of half a home can trigger a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay. Before changing title for any reason, read [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

### 5. Unequal results between children

Suppose you have three children and add only the one who lives nearby. When you die, the house passes to that child by survivorship, and your will leaving the estate equally to all three does not reach it. Sometimes that is what a parent wants. More often it is an accident that the other children discover later. Even where the child is expected to share, the law may treat the asset as theirs, and nothing makes them do so.

### 6. Loss of control

You can no longer sell, refinance or take a reverse mortgage without the child's consent, and a child who disagrees can block it. Homestead or senior property tax exemptions that you qualify for may also change when title changes, so check locally.

### 7. The child may die first

If your child dies before you, their share can pass to their spouse or children, and you may end up co-owning with people you barely know. A survivorship deed can avoid this, but only if the form is right and the child's creditors do not intervene first.

## Convenience accounts and joint accounts: which is which?

Many older adults put a child on an account only so the child can pay bills. Some states treat such accounts as convenience accounts, in which the helper may deposit and withdraw but has no ownership interest. Whether an account counts as one depends on its terms and state law, and when there is a dispute, courts look at intent. Do not leave it to a judge. Ask the bank for a convenience signer or agent form that says clearly that the helper has no ownership rights.

## What are the better alternatives?

| Goal | Better tool | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Child can pay your bills | [Durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), or bank agent form | Authority without ownership |
| Money goes to child at death | [Payable-on-death designation](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) | No ownership, no probate, easy to change |
| House goes to child at death | [Transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) or revocable trust | You keep control; child gets a step-up in basis |
| Several assets, several children | [Revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) | One document, customizable |
| Child helps if you cannot act | Successor trustee | Takes over without a court |

For a house, see [leaving your house to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) for how each option compares.

## When is joint ownership actually fine?

Joint ownership is often sensible between spouses, because the survivor naturally inherits and there is no third-party creditor risk. [Tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety), available to married couples in some states, adds protection from one spouse's separate creditors. It may also be reasonable for a small checking account where the risk is low. Even then, be deliberate about it.

## I already added my child. How can I fix it?

Start by finding out what you have.

1. **Get a copy of the deed or account agreement.** Look at how title is stated: joint tenants with survivorship, tenants in common, or something else.
2. **Ask whether the arrangement still serves your goals.** Consider the creditor, tax and fairness issues above.
3. **Talk to your child.** Unwinding requires their cooperation, and an open conversation helps.
4. **Use a new deed or a new account.** A new deed can put the property into a trust with your child's signature, and a joint account can be closed and reopened as a POD account.
5. **Check for a gift tax return.** If you added your child to a deed in a prior year without filing one, an attorney or tax preparer can advise you on correcting it.
6. **Watch for Medicaid timing.** A change made now might start a new look-back clock, so get advice first if care is likely.
