Ask someone what they own and they will list the house, the car and the accounts. The things that cause trouble are the ones not on the list: a timeshare in Florida that Dad bought in 1998, a storage unit nobody has opened in three years, a safe deposit box whose key is somewhere in a drawer.

A will can reach all of these, but only if your executor knows they exist and can get to them. This page goes asset by asset. It supports our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## What does a will actually cover?

A will controls property you own in your name alone, with no beneficiary and no co-owner. That group is called the probate estate. Everything else passes by its own paperwork: jointly owned property, life insurance and retirement accounts with beneficiaries, and assets in a trust. Our page on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) explains the line.

The four assets below usually sit inside the will's reach. The question is what is attached to each.

| Asset | Covered by your will? | Common catch |
|---|---|---|
| Timeshare | Usually, if in your name alone | Ongoing fees; contract may restrict transfer |
| Storage unit contents | Yes, as personal property | Rent due; auction if unpaid; nobody knows it exists |
| Safe deposit box contents | Yes, if the box is in your name | Access delays; box may hold the original will |
| Farm equipment and livestock | Yes, if owned individually | Liens, business ownership, brand and title records |
| Property in another state | The gift, yes; the transfer, not always | Second probate unless in a trust |
| Property abroad | Maybe not | Local law decides |

## Does my will cover a timeshare?

Yes if you own it alone, but a timeshare is as much a contract as an asset. Some are deeded real estate: a week or a share of a unit, recorded in the county like a house. Others are a right-to-use membership that does not involve a deed at all. Either way, the annual maintenance fee and any special assessments keep coming, and unpaid fees can lead to collection or foreclosure.

Many people are astonished to learn that the heirs may want nothing to do with it. The practical answer is to find out what the resort allows before you write the gift. Some developers take back a timeshare when the owner dies or when heirs ask. Others will not. If yours will not, selling it, or leaving it to a family member who actually uses it, is the better plan. Include the contract number, resort name and week or points in your will or inventory, and consider adding a sentence that frees the heir from any duty to keep it.

Beware of resale companies that charge large upfront fees to "sell" timeshares. Start with the resort and your state attorney general's consumer office.

## Does my will cover the contents of a storage unit?

The contents are personal property that belongs in your estate. The unit itself is a rental agreement. Both need attention.

Executors lose track of storage units because they are in nobody's paperwork. The facility bills a credit card or bank account, and when the owner dies, the payments stop and so does the facility's patience. After a period set by state law, it may auction the contents.

What to do:

1. Write the facility name, address, unit number and access method in your [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction).
2. List what is in it, especially anything valuable, sentimental or irreplaceable.
3. Name who is authorized to enter. Some facilities require the account holder, and a will alone may not be enough.
4. Tell your executor to keep the rent current until the contents are sorted.
5. Do not leave the key to one person with no explanation. Say who gets what, or that the executor decides.

If you are the executor and find an unpaid unit, call the facility at once, send letters testamentary, and ask for a short extension. See [what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) for the wider list.

## Does my will cover a safe deposit box?

The contents are yours, so they pass under your will, but getting in can take time. The bank generally will not open a box to heirs without proof of death and proof of authority, typically court-issued letters. Many states let a family member make a supervised visit to look for a will, a burial plan or a deed, and nothing else may be removed.

Some practical points:

- Do not keep your only original will in a safe deposit box if the people who need it cannot get in without it. Our page on [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) compares the options.
- A co-renter or a named deputy can usually access the box right away. Weigh the convenience against the risk of giving someone else access.
- If you have an unfunded or forgotten box, banks report abandoned contents to the state after a period, and the state holds them as unclaimed property.
- A written list of what is in the box helps your executor see whether anything is missing.

## Does my will cover farm equipment and livestock?

It covers what you own individually. Farms are usually messier. Tractors and combines may be financed, and the lender holds a lien. Vehicles and trailers have titles that must be transferred. Livestock may be registered, branded or part of an operating business, and crops in the field raise questions of who gets the harvest.

If the farm is run through a partnership, LLC or corporation, your will covers only your ownership share, and the entity's own agreement may control what happens next. A working farm should have a plan that goes well beyond a will, which our article on [farm succession planning](/learn/business-owners/farm-succession-planning) covers. At a minimum, a will for a farm should name who gets the equipment, who is responsible for the animals until the estate settles, and who has authority to keep operating.

Think about a bridge as well. Cattle need feeding the day after a funeral. Name a person who can act now, and give your executor the authority to run or wind down the operation.

## Does my will cover property in another state or country?

Your will can leave the property to someone, but the transfer of real estate is governed by the state where it sits. An out-of-state house, cabin or land typically needs a second probate in that state unless you hold it in a living trust or use a transfer-on-death deed where the state allows one. See [property in another state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) and [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

Property abroad is more complicated. Many countries apply their own inheritance rules, including forced shares for children, to real estate within their borders, no matter what a U.S. will says. If you own a vacation home in Mexico or Canada, ask a local lawyer how it passes and whether a separate local will is worthwhile. Coordinate the two so they do not contradict each other. Our page on [moving states or living abroad](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) addresses the wider issue.

## How do you fix gaps before they matter?

Start with a one-page inventory of the unusual assets.

1. List each item, where it is, who has the key or code, and what it costs each month.
2. Note whether it is in your name alone, shared or in a business.
3. Say whom you want to receive it, and add a backup.
4. Check for a lien, a contract restriction or a fee that comes with it.
5. Give copies to your executor and keep one with your will.

If you hold many such assets, a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) paired with a revocable trust is cleaner. Our guide on [how to inventory your assets](/learn/basics/how-to-inventory-your-assets-and-value-your-estate) walks through the list.
