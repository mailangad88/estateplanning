People say "I'll just deed the house to my daughter," as if there were one kind of deed. There are several, and they promise different things. Picking the wrong one rarely makes the transfer fail, but it can leave a child with a title problem, a bigger tax bill or a lender who calls the loan.

This page explains the common deeds in plain terms, when each fits, and what has to happen after you sign. It belongs to our guide to [property and assets in your estate plan](/learn/property-and-assets), and it pairs with the article on [removing a name from a deed](/learn/property-and-assets/remove-a-name-from-a-deed).

## What does a deed actually do?

A deed is the document that transfers ownership of real estate from one person, the grantor, to another, the grantee. Your will does not do that during your life, and a beneficiary form does not apply to a house the way it does to a bank account. The deed is what a county clerk files in the land records, and that public record is what shows who owns the property.

A valid deed generally has five parts: names of grantor and grantee, words that transfer the interest, a legal description of the land (not just the street address), the grantor's signature, and a notary's acknowledgment. Then it has to be delivered to the grantee and recorded. Unrecorded deeds can still be valid between the parties, but they leave the public record wrong, and that is how title problems start.

## What are the main types of deeds?

The names differ from state to state, so treat the table as a guide and read the heading on your own form.

| Deed | What the grantor promises | Common use |
|---|---|---|
| General warranty deed | The title is clear back through all prior owners, and the grantor will defend it | Sales to buyers, in many states |
| Special warranty deed | The grantor did nothing to harm the title during their own ownership, but says nothing about earlier owners | Sales by businesses, banks and estates |
| Grant deed | Narrow implied promises: no other sale, no hidden liens | Standard in some western states |
| Quitclaim deed | Nothing. It passes whatever interest the grantor has, even if that is none | Family transfers, trust transfers, divorce, fixing names |
| Trustee's deed | Depends on the trust. Signed by a trustee, not the owner | Distributing a house from a trust, or a foreclosure sale in some states |
| Personal representative's deed | The executor has court authority and is conveying estate property | Selling or distributing a house after probate |
| Transfer-on-death deed | Nothing during life. Ownership passes at death to a named person | Avoiding probate on a house, in states that allow it |
| Life estate or lady bird deed | Keeps the owner's right to live in and control the house, with a named person receiving it at death | Probate avoidance while keeping control |

## When is a quitclaim deed the right choice?

A quitclaim deed fits when the grantor and grantee know each other, the title history is not in doubt and the point is to change names, not to sell. Typical cases are moving a house into your own living trust, adding or removing a spouse, and settling a divorce.

It is also the wrong deed in several situations:

- **A sale to a stranger.** A buyer's lender and title insurer will want a warranty or grant deed, because they need your promise.
- **A transfer you are not sure about.** If a cousin might also have a claim, a quitclaim gives the child nothing to point to later.
- **A property with a title insurance policy you want to keep.** Some policies can be affected by a transfer, so check with the insurer before recording. Our guide on [moving a house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) covers that.

Think of Raymond, 71, who wants his house in his living trust. He signs a quitclaim deed to himself as trustee. That is a normal and sensible use. The trust receives whatever Raymond owns, which is the house. Nobody is buying anything, so nobody needs a warranty.

## When do you need a warranty or grant deed?

Use one when the other side is relying on your title. Selling to a buyer is the obvious case. A warranty deed also fits when you are conveying to a family member who wants protection, for instance, a son paying you fair price for the house, or one sibling buying out another.

If something later turns out wrong, such as an old lien or a missing heir from a prior owner, the person holding a warranty deed can sue the grantor. The practical value comes from title insurance, which pays for most of these problems so the buyer does not have to chase you. That is also why a warranty deed matters less in a loving family transfer than in a sale.

## What about trustee's deeds and executor's deeds?

These come up after a death. When a house sits in a revocable trust, the successor trustee signs a trustee's deed to transfer it to a beneficiary, or to a buyer if the house is sold. When the house is in the probate estate, the executor signs a personal representative's deed using court-issued letters. Both deeds are signed by someone acting in a role, not by the owner, so the buyer will want the trust certificate or the letters recorded along with them.

If the heirs have found an old or incorrect deed, a different set of steps applies, covered in our article on [fixing title problems on inherited property](/learn/property-and-assets/fixing-title-problems-on-inherited-property).

## How do TOD and lady bird deeds differ from a regular deed?

A regular deed transfers ownership now. A [transfer-on-death deed](/glossary/transfer-on-death-deed), available in many but not all states, transfers ownership only at your death, and you keep full control while alive. You can sell, borrow, or revoke the deed. It avoids probate for that house if recorded correctly during your life. See [transfer-on-death deeds for real estate](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

A lady bird deed, or enhanced life estate deed, is a similar tool used in a smaller group of states. It is explained in our article on [lady bird deeds](/learn/probate/lady-bird-deed-explained).

Both save a child from a probate case. Neither does everything a trust does if you become incapacitated, since a deed to a beneficiary does not tell anyone who manages the house in the meantime.

## What has to happen after you sign?

1. **Get the legal description right.** Copy it from your current deed, not from a tax bill.
2. **Sign before a notary.** Some states also require witnesses.
3. **Check for a transfer tax or a required form.** Many states and counties require a declaration of value or a similar form, and some charge a tax, with exceptions for family or trust transfers.
4. **Record it with the county.** Usually the recorder, register of deeds or county clerk. Pay the fee.
5. **Tell the insurer and lender.** Update the homeowner's policy and ask the lender whether it needs notice.
6. **File a gift tax return if needed.** A transfer for less than full value is a gift. A gift above the 2026 annual exclusion of $19,000 per recipient generally needs a Form 709, though it rarely means tax is owed.
7. **Keep the recorded copy** with your estate papers.

## What problems can a deed cause?

The deed is simple. The consequences are not.

- **Lost step-up in basis.** A house you give a child during life keeps your old tax basis. A house the child inherits generally steps up to its value at your death. Our article on [giving your house to a child](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs) compares these.
- **Medicaid look-back.** A deed to a child can count as a gift within the 60-month look-back used in most states.
- **Exposure to your child's creditors or divorce.** The house now belongs to them.
- **Property tax changes.** Some states reassess on transfer and exempt certain family or trust transfers.
- **Wrong tenancy.** A deed that says "to my children" without the right language can leave several people owning the house in a way you did not intend.

These are the reasons attorneys review deeds before they are recorded, and why a deed that looks like a form can be a major decision.
