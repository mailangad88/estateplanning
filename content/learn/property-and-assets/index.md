Your home, a second property, a joint bank account, a gun collection and a dog may not seem to belong in the same conversation. In estate planning they do, because all of them pass by rules that are separate from your will, and each one can cause trouble when nobody planned for it.

This guide covers how ownership works, the tools used to pass real estate and other property, and what to do about assets that need special handling. It points to five detailed articles for the specific situations.

## Why does ownership matter more than your will?

Ownership matters more because the way an asset is titled usually decides who receives it at death, and that happens before your will is read. A will controls only property that is in your name alone with no other instructions, which lawyers call the [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate). Everything else passes automatically by its own terms.

That is why a parent with a carefully written will can still leave a house to the wrong person. If the deed says the house is held in [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy) with one child, that child owns it on the parent's death, and the will is ignored. Our article on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) lists the assets that bypass a will, and [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) shows how to sort yours.

Before changing anything, find out how each major asset is titled. For real estate, get a copy of the deed from the county recorder. For accounts, ask for the account agreement or look at the beneficiary form.

## What are the main ways to hold title to property?

The common forms of ownership each have different results at death. Terms vary slightly by state, and not every state offers every form.

| Form of ownership | Who owns it | What happens at one owner's death |
|---|---|---|
| Sole ownership | One person | Passes by will, or by intestacy if no will; goes through probate |
| [Joint tenancy with survivorship](/glossary/joint-tenancy) | Two or more, equal shares | Share passes automatically to the surviving owner or owners |
| Tenancy in common | Two or more, any shares | Each owner's share passes by their own will or intestacy |
| [Tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety) | Married couples only, in some states | Passes to the surviving spouse; protects from one spouse's separate creditors |
| [Community property](/glossary/community-property) | Spouses, in about nine states | Each spouse owns half of marital property; rules vary on survivorship |
| Trust ownership | A trustee for the beneficiaries | Passes as the trust directs, without probate |
| Transfer-on-death deed | One owner, with a named beneficiary | Passes to the named person at death, without probate |

Choose a form deliberately. The wrong one can send a house to a person you did not intend, or leave it stuck in court.

## How do you leave your home to your children?

The main options for passing a house to children are a will, a revocable living trust, a transfer-on-death deed, and a lifetime transfer. They differ in probate, control and taxes. Our article [leaving your house to your children: deed, trust or will?](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) compares each in detail, and it covers the issues that surprise families most.

The biggest of these is the income tax basis. A child who inherits a home takes its value on the date of death as the starting point for any later gain, so a house bought for $90,000 and worth $450,000 can be sold with little or no tax. A child who is given the home during life takes the parent's original cost and may owe tax on a $360,000 gain. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) for the rule and its limits.

Other points from that article:

- A transfer-on-death deed is simple, cheap and revocable, but it is not available in every state, and it cannot hold property for a minor or a person with a disability. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).
- A [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) avoids probate for the home and gives you the most control over the terms, but the house has to be retitled to the trust. See [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
- Mortgages and homeowner's insurance need attention whenever title changes.
- Siblings should have written rules for keeping, selling or buying out the house.

## What if you own property in more than one state?

If you own real estate in more than one state, your estate may need a second probate, called [ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate), in each state where you hold land. Each state's court has authority over the real estate within its borders. That means separate filings, local attorneys and extra delay. Our article on [owning property in more than one state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) explains how to avoid it with a trust, a transfer-on-death deed or joint ownership, and how some states tax a nonresident's real estate. The probate cluster covers the court process in [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

Vacation homes need more than a title change. If several children will inherit a cabin or beach house, decide who pays the carrying costs, who uses it when, and how someone can leave the group. Without answers, a co-owner can ask a court to force a sale.

## Is it a good idea to add a child to your deed or bank account?

Usually not, because it makes the child an owner today and exposes the asset to their problems. Parents add a child so the child can pay bills or so the asset passes at death without probate. The article [adding a child to your deed or bank account: the hidden risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) explains why this shortcut is risky:

- The child's creditors, divorce or bankruptcy can reach the asset.
- A gift of part of a house gives the child your tax basis, not a stepped-up one, and may require a gift tax return.
- A transfer can count against you under the Medicaid look-back, which in most states is 60 months.
- Your other children may be left out, because survivorship overrides your will.

The same goals can be met more safely. A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets a child manage bills without owning anything. A [payable-on-death or transfer-on-death account](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) passes money without probate and without giving the child present rights. See also [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) if long-term care is a concern, and [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs).

## How do you plan for firearms?

Firearms can be left by will or trust, but each recipient must be legally allowed to own them. Federal law prohibits certain people from possessing guns, items regulated by the National Firearms Act need ATF approval to transfer, and states add their own permit, registration and background-check rules. Our article on [passing on firearms safely and legally](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning) explains how to make an inventory, name recipients and alternates, use a sale as the fallback for an heir who cannot receive a gun, and keep the collection secure until the transfer is done.

The executor has a hands-on job here that is different from other assets, which is a reason to think about [who you choose as executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).

## How do you provide for a pet?

You cannot leave money to a pet, because the law treats animals as property. A gift of the pet and money to a person is the simple route, but it is not enforceable, and a pet trust gives more protection. The article on [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) covers how a pet trust works, how to estimate the amount to set aside, what care instructions to write, and what to do about emergencies and incapacity.

## What other special assets deserve a plan?

Some assets do not fit neatly into one tool and deserve a line in your plan.

- **Tangible personal property.** Furniture, jewelry, art, tools and keepsakes cause more family arguments than money does. Many states allow a signed list that refers to a will or trust and names who gets which items. Check that your state recognizes it, and keep the list current. Valuable pieces may need an appraisal.
- **Vehicles, boats and recreational vehicles.** They have titles. Some states allow a transfer-on-death registration for vehicles, and others require probate or a small-estate affidavit. See [small estate procedures](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit).
- **Digital property.** Accounts, photos and cryptocurrency need their own instructions. See our [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets).
- **A business interest.** Ownership of a company has its own rules and agreements. See our [business owners guide](/learn/business-owners).
- **Real estate used as a rental.** Think about management, liability and income. An LLC may help in some cases but carries costs.
- **Land with minerals, farmland or timber.** These raise questions about leases, rights and valuation that need local advice.
- **Property abroad.** The country where the land sits controls it. Get advice there.

## How do taxes affect property decisions?

For most families, property decisions are driven by income tax basis and state rules, not the federal estate tax. As of 2026 the federal estate tax applies only to estates above $15 million per person, so most homeowners will never owe it. See the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption). But a few things still deserve attention:

- **Capital gains and basis.** Gifts during life keep your basis; transfers at death usually step up.
- **Gift tax reporting.** A gift above $19,000 per recipient in 2026 needs a return, even if no tax is due. See [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).
- **State taxes.** Some states have an estate or inheritance tax with lower thresholds. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).
- **Property tax.** Transfers may trigger reassessment, and exemptions for seniors, veterans or homesteads may change if you change title.

## What mistakes cost families the most?

1. **Never checking the deed.** The title may not say what you think it says.
2. **Naming a trust and never funding it.** A trust does not control assets it does not own.
3. **Adding a child to a deed for convenience.** It can cost basis, expose the home to creditors and cause a Medicaid penalty.
4. **Ignoring property in other states.** A second probate is an avoidable cost.
5. **Leaving guns to someone who cannot own them.** It creates legal trouble for the heir and the executor.
6. **Assuming a friend will take the pet.** Without a plan, the animal may end up in a shelter.
7. **Skipping the conversation.** Heirs fight over houses and keepsakes when they were surprised.

## Who should handle the property after you are gone?

Real estate and special assets need a hands-on administrator, and the person named in your will or trust is not always the right fit. Someone must secure and insure vacant property, keep paying the mortgage and taxes, deal with tenants, appraise heirlooms, and decide when to sell. An executor or successor trustee who lives nearby, handles paperwork well and communicates openly with the family will do better than one chosen only by birth order. Consider naming a co-fiduciary or a professional for complex holdings, and give the person written authority to hire help, such as a property manager or an appraiser. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) and [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) for what the job involves.

## A step-by-step way to start

1. **Make an asset list** with how each is titled or designated.
2. **Pull the deeds** for every property.
3. **Decide who should get each major asset,** and who gets it if that person cannot.
4. **Pick the tool:** trust, deed, designation, will or a combination.
5. **Fix the titles and forms** so they follow the plan.
6. **Write instructions** for special assets: firearms, pets, keepsakes, digital property.
7. **Tell your executor or trustee** where everything is.
8. **Review** after a move, a purchase or sale, a death or a birth. See [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).
