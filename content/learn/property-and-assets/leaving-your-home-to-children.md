For most families, the house is the largest asset and the one parents most want to go to their children without a fight, a tax bill or a court case. There are several ways to do it, and the differences in cost and risk are bigger than most people expect.

This page compares the options, explains the tax and mortgage issues, and gives a way to choose. It is part of our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## What are the ways to leave a house to your children?

There are five common methods, and they differ mainly in whether the house goes through probate, whether you keep control, and how the children are taxed.

| Method | Probate? | You keep control? | Children's tax basis | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Will | Yes | Yes | Stepped up at death | Low to write, higher at death |
| Revocable living trust | No | Yes | Stepped up at death | Moderate up front |
| Transfer-on-death (TOD) deed | No | Yes | Stepped up at death | Low |
| Add child to deed now | No | Partly lost | Gifted share keeps your basis | Low, with hidden costs |
| Life estate deed | No | Partly lost | Generally stepped up at death | Low to moderate |

## Is a will enough to leave a house to your children?

A will works, but the house must go through probate first. Your will names the children, the executor files it with the court, and the court issues authority to transfer the deed. Depending on the state, this may take several months to over a year and cost thousands in fees. See how long probate takes and probate costs.

A will is the right base document even if you use another tool, since it handles anything left over. A will alone is a reasonable choice if the estate is simple, probate in your state is quick, and you do not mind the delay. Some states also have simplified procedures for small estates, but a house often exceeds those limits.

## How does a living trust handle the house?

With a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), you deed the house to yourself as trustee. You live there, sell it, or refinance as before. At your death, the successor trustee transfers it to your children as the trust directs, with no probate.

The main advantage is flexibility. A trust can hold the house for a child with special needs, keep it for a surviving spouse first, require siblings to sell by a set date, or give one child the option to buy out the others at appraised value. The tradeoff is cost and one chore: the deed must actually be changed. A trust that does not own the house does not control it. Our guide to [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) shows how.

## What is a transfer-on-death deed?

A [transfer-on-death deed](/glossary/transfer-on-death-deed) is a recorded deed that names who gets the house at your death. Until then you own it fully and can revoke or change it. Not every state allows one, and the requirements for signing and recording differ. See our page on [transfer-on-death deeds for real estate](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

It is simple and cheap, which makes it popular. The weakness is that it is rigid. If a named child dies before you, the deed may not say who takes the share, and it cannot hold the house in trust for a child who is a minor or has special needs. If all your children share equally and are adults in good standing, it may be all you need.

## Why is deeding the house to a child now often a mistake?

Giving the house to your child today has real costs, so think hard before doing it. There are four.

**Lost step-up in basis.** A child who inherits a house takes its value at your death as their basis, which can erase tax on decades of appreciation. A child who is given the house takes your original cost. Suppose you bought the home for $90,000 and it is worth $450,000. A child who inherits it and sells it for $450,000 has no taxable gain. A child who received it as a gift and sells it for the same price has a $360,000 gain, subject to capital gains tax, except for any home sale exclusion they qualify for. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

**Exposure to the child's problems.** The house now belongs to someone else. A lawsuit, a bankruptcy, a divorce or a tax lien against the child can reach it.

**Loss of control.** You cannot sell or refinance without the child's signature, and a child could force a sale.

**Gift tax and Medicaid.** A gift of a house worth more than the annual exclusion ($19,000 per recipient in 2026) requires a gift tax return, though tax is rarely due because of the $15 million lifetime exclusion. A gift can also trigger a Medicaid penalty period under the five-year look-back. See [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) and [the Medicaid look-back](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period). Our article on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) goes into these in detail.

A life estate deed, in which you keep the right to live in the house and the children receive it at your death, avoids some of these, and property held in a life estate usually still qualifies for a step-up in basis. But the deed is hard to undo, and the children's interest can still complicate a sale. Ask an attorney before using one, especially if Medicaid may matter.

## Can you give the house to children and still protect it from nursing home costs?

Sometimes, but not simply and not late. The look-back period in most states is 60 months, and some tools, such as an irrevocable trust or life estate, may help if done well in advance. They come with real tradeoffs. Read [can you protect your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) before moving a house for this reason.

## What about the mortgage and insurance?

Check the loan and the policy before you retitle anything.

- **Mortgage.** Federal law generally bars a lender from calling the loan just because you put a home with fewer than five units into your own revocable trust, or because the house passes to a relative at your death. Children who inherit still need to keep paying. They can often assume the loan or refinance.
- **Homeowner's insurance.** Ask the insurer to add the trust as an insured or confirm in writing that coverage continues.
- **Title insurance.** A deed to a trust normally does not void the policy, but ask the title company if you are unsure.
- **Property tax.** Many places reassess property on a transfer but exempt transfers to a trust you control or between parents and children. Check your county's rules before signing.

## How do you decide among the options?

Work through these questions in order.

1. **Do you want to keep control while you live?** If yes, rule out lifetime gifts and choose a trust, TOD deed or will.
2. **Do you want to avoid probate?** If yes, choose a trust or TOD deed.
3. **Will the house pass to someone who needs protection?** A minor, a person with a disability, or a child in a shaky marriage points toward a trust.
4. **Are there other assets in other places?** A trust pulls the plan together, and a TOD deed handles only the one deed.
5. **Does your state allow a TOD deed?** Check the state guides, such as Texas, California and Florida, which differ on deed options.
6. **Is Medicaid a realistic concern in the next five years?** If yes, see an attorney before doing anything.

## How can you prevent fights between siblings?

Plan for the house to outlast your wishes. Siblings often disagree about whether to keep, sell or rent it. Spell out the process in writing:

- Who may live there, and for how long.
- How the house is valued and who pays for an appraisal.
- Whether a child may buy out the others, and on what terms.
- How the carrying costs (tax, insurance, repairs) are shared.
- What happens if no one agrees after a set period, often a sale and equal division.

Siblings of unequal means may also need an equalizing gift, such as a larger share of other assets for the child who does not get the house. See [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) to understand what your children will face and talking to family about your estate plan to start the conversation.

Land that was never put into anyone's name after a death is a different problem, covered in [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property).
