People ask to take a name off a deed for three very different reasons. A couple divorced, and one of them wants the other off the house. An owner died, and the surviving owner wants the records to say so. A parent added a child years ago and now wants to undo it. The paperwork looks similar. The legal route does not.

This page walks through each case, explains the choice between a quitclaim and a warranty deed, and flags the mortgage problem that catches many people. It belongs to our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## What does a deed actually do?

A deed is the document that transfers ownership of real estate. The names on the most recent recorded deed, and the way they are written, are what the world, including your lender, your title insurer and any buyer, relies on. That is why a handshake agreement, an email or even a court judgment does not change the record by itself. Someone has to record the right document with the county.

How the owners hold title matters too. Joint tenants with survivorship and spouses holding as [tenants by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety) pass the whole property to the survivor. Tenants in common each own a separate share that goes to their own heirs. Our glossary entry on [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy) gives the details. Which kind you have is written in the deed, so pull the deed before anything else.

## How do I remove a living person from a deed?

The person leaving signs a new deed, and it gets recorded. That is the whole principle, even if the forms vary by state.

1. **Get a copy of the current recorded deed.** Request it from the county recorder or register of deeds, or from an online land records search. You need the exact legal description and the exact names as they appear.
2. **Choose the deed type.** Usually a quitclaim deed, sometimes a warranty or grant deed (see the next section).
3. **Draft the new deed.** The person leaving (the grantor) conveys their interest to the person keeping the property (the grantee). Use the legal description from the old deed.
4. **Have it signed and notarized.** Most states require the grantor's signature to be acknowledged before a notary. Some require witnesses.
5. **Complete any transfer forms.** Many counties want a transfer declaration, a property tax change form, or an affidavit of consideration. Some states charge transfer tax, with exemptions for family or divorce transfers.
6. **Record the deed.** File it at the county recorder's office, in person, by mail or electronically where available, and pay the recording fee.
7. **Check the property tax effect.** In some places a transfer triggers reassessment unless an exclusion applies, such as for spouses or for a parent and child. Ask the assessor.

Our page on [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed) covers the reverse move and the risks, which are worth remembering if you are fixing an old arrangement.

## Quitclaim deed or warranty deed?

A quitclaim deed passes whatever interest the signer has, if any, with no promises. A warranty deed promises that the signer owns the property and that it is free of undisclosed claims. A special warranty or grant deed sits in between, promising only against problems that arose during the signer's own period of ownership.

| Situation | Typical deed | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse leaving the title in a divorce | Quitclaim or a deed required by the decree | The spouse is giving up an interest, not selling. |
| Co-owner moving off title to another co-owner | Quitclaim | The remaining owner already holds the claim. |
| Sale to a new buyer | Warranty or special warranty | The buyer and lender expect assurances. |
| Parent to child | Often quitclaim, sometimes warranty | Depends on title insurance and the lender. |
| Moving into a living trust | Often warranty or a trust transfer deed | Can preserve title insurance in some cases. |

The low cost of a quitclaim deed makes it popular, and it is generally fine when the person remaining on title wants nothing more than to be rid of a co-owner. Its weakness is that it does not bring title insurance coverage along or give the grantee any recourse if a defect appears later. For a significant transfer, check whether the existing title insurance policy still protects the remaining owner, and read our guide on [transferring a house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) for the related question.

## How do I remove a deceased owner from the deed?

It depends on how title was held.

**Survivorship title.** If the deceased person and the survivor held title as joint tenants with right of survivorship, or as spouses in a state that recognizes entireties ownership, the survivor became the owner at the moment of death. The record still needs updating. The survivor typically files a certified copy of the death certificate, often with an affidavit of survivorship or a similar county form. No court is needed in most places.

**Transfer-on-death deed.** If the deceased person recorded a transfer-on-death deed, the named beneficiary records an affidavit with the death certificate. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).

**Tenants in common or sole owner.** The deceased person's share passes to heirs under the will or state law, which generally means probate unless a simplified procedure applies. See [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for the sorting logic.

Lena and Sam bought their home in 1999 as joint tenants with survivorship. When Sam died last spring, Lena assumed the house was hers and did nothing. Two years later she tried to refinance, and the lender found Sam's name still on the county record and refused to proceed until she recorded the death certificate and affidavit. It took one afternoon and a modest fee. The delay cost her a rate lock.

## What about an ex-spouse?

A divorce decree divides property between the spouses, but the deed is a separate record. The decree may order one spouse to sign a deed, and if the person refuses, the court can enforce the order or in some states let the decree itself act as the transfer. In either case, get the deed signed and recorded as part of closing the divorce. Leaving a former spouse on the deed is a trap: if they die, their share could pass to their new spouse or children, and if they run into debt, a creditor can lien their interest. For the other parts of an estate plan after a divorce, see [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce).

Transfers between spouses incident to divorce are generally not taxable gifts and often avoid transfer tax and reassessment, but the details depend on your state and on the wording of the decree.

## What happens to the mortgage?

Nothing, unless the lender agrees. A deed controls ownership. The mortgage is a separate contract between the borrower and the lender, and signing a deed does not remove anyone from it. If your ex signed the note, they remain liable for the debt even after giving up the house, and a missed payment hurts both credit records.

To change who owes the loan, you can:

- **Refinance** in the remaining owner's name alone, which pays off the old loan.
- **Ask for an assumption**, where the lender allows one owner to take over the loan.
- **Request a release of liability**, which lenders grant rarely and only after reviewing income.

Lenders can sometimes call a loan due when title transfers, under a due-on-sale clause, but federal law bars them from doing so for certain transfers, including to a spouse, to a child, or after a borrower's death in some situations. Call the servicer before you record.

## What if the other owner will not sign?

Then the deed route is closed and a court is the next option. A divorce court can order a transfer. A partition action asks the court to split or sell co-owned property. A quiet title suit settles who owns what when records are unclear. Our page on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) explains how partition plays out among relatives. These cases cost far more than a deed, so a calm conversation and a fair buyout are usually better.
