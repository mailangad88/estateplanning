Three families, one problem. Dana's mother put $60,000 toward the down payment but never signed a deed. Luis bought his house at 30 and married at 38, and now they are drafting a will. Carol and Ben have lived together for 14 years in a house that is only in Ben's name. Each family assumes the answer to "who owns the house" is obvious. In each case the obvious answer may be wrong.

This page works through those three situations. It is part of our [property and assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## The short version

- Read the deed. That document says who owns the house, and it controls unless a court is persuaded otherwise.
- Who made the mortgage payments is evidence, not title.
- Help from a parent should be labeled in writing: gift, loan, or ownership share.
- A premarital house and a premarital will can both be undone quietly by retitling.
- Partners who are not married need documents because the default rules do not recognize them.

## What happens when a parent helps with the down payment?

A parent's money can be one of three things, and the paper decides which.

| Label | What it means | What you need |
|---|---|---|
| Gift | The money is yours. Parent has no claim. | A gift letter (lenders ask for one) |
| Loan | You owe repayment, possibly with interest. | A signed promissory note, ideally secured by a mortgage |
| Ownership share | The parent owns a percentage of the house. | The parent on the deed, or a written co-ownership agreement |

Without paper, courts and heirs treat it as a gift. That is where families get hurt. Dana's mother, now in her eighties, tells her other two children that the $60,000 was "really a loan." When the mother dies, her will splits everything three ways. Dana says it was a gift. Her siblings say it should be counted against her share. With no note and no letter, nobody can prove either version.

Gifts also have a tax side. A gift above $19,000 per recipient in 2026 calls for a gift tax return from the giver, though tax is rarely owed given the $15 million lifetime exclusion. Our article on [taxable gifts, loans and down payments](/learn/estate-tax/is-it-a-taxable-gift-cars-loans-and-down-payments) covers the filing.

If you are the parent and want your child to repay, a note secured by a lien is the cleanest route. If you want the child to have it free and clear but your other children treated equally, put it in your will or trust as an advance on their share. If you want a real ownership interest, be on the deed.

## Who owns a house bought before marriage?

Usually the person who bought it, and it generally remains separate property after marriage. But "separate" is only a starting point.

Several things can pull a share toward the marriage:

1. **Marital money pays the mortgage.** A spouse can claim a share of the equity built with joint earnings, even though the deed names only one spouse. The rules vary a great deal by state, and community property states approach it differently from the rest.
2. **Improvements.** A new kitchen paid from a joint account can create a claim.
3. **Retitling.** If Luis adds his wife to the deed as joint tenants, the house typically becomes shared. Lawyers call this transmutation, and a deed signed to "make her feel secure" cannot be undone casually.
4. **Mixing in a refinance.** A new loan taken by both spouses sometimes comes with a new deed. Read what you sign.

On death, a surviving spouse has protections regardless of what the will says: an elective share in most common-law states, a community property interest in others, and homestead rights in many. We explain those in [homestead rights at death](/learn/property-and-assets/homestead-rights-at-death-surviving-spouse-and-the-family-home). For how divorce treats separate property, see [whether an inheritance is marital property](/learn/after-a-death/is-an-inheritance-marital-property).

If you want the premarital house to go to your children from a first marriage, put it in writing: a prenuptial agreement, a trust, or at least a will that matches your intent and an honest conversation with your spouse. Our article on [the family home in a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/family-home-in-second-marriage) goes through the options.

## What rights does an unmarried partner have to the house?

Fewer than most couples assume. Marriage brings a set of default protections that an unmarried partner does not receive.

If the house is in one partner's name alone:

- The other partner generally has no inheritance right under intestacy laws in most states.
- The heirs, who may be adult children or siblings, can inherit the house and ask the partner to leave.
- Years of living there and paying bills do not usually create ownership, though in a few situations a court may recognize a claim for contributions.
- A handful of states recognize common-law marriage, which would change the answer. Most do not, and many that once did have ended it.

Carol, in our example, pays half the bills and has painted every room. If Ben dies without a will, his two adult children from a prior marriage inherit the house. They can be gracious, or they can send a letter.

There are straightforward fixes:

1. **Put both names on the deed with right of survivorship.** The survivor owns it automatically. See [joint tenants, tenants in common and tenancy by the entirety](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenants-vs-tenants-in-common-vs-entirety) for the differences. The downside is that the first to die cannot leave their half to their own children.
2. **Use a trust.** Ben's living trust can give Carol the right to live in the house for life or for a period, then pass it to his children.
3. **Use a life estate.** A deed that reserves a life interest for Carol and names the children as remainder owners, which we discuss in [what a lady bird deed does](/learn/probate/lady-bird-deed-explained).
4. **Sign a cohabitation agreement.** It records who contributed what and what happens if you separate.

## Does a stepchild inherit the house?

Not unless the owner names them. Intestacy laws favor spouses, children and blood relatives, and most states do not treat stepchildren as heirs. A stepchild who lived in the home for years has no more claim than a stranger, unless a will, trust or beneficiary designation says otherwise. If you want a stepchild to inherit, name them. For the broader picture, see our [blended family planning guide](/learn/blended-families).

## What if the paper already says something different from what everyone remembers?

It happens often. A deed was signed in a rush at closing, or a refinance quietly added a name, or a handwritten note exists from twenty years ago. Do not guess. Order a copy of the recorded deed and any later deeds, and read them with the dates in mind. If the deed is wrong, it can usually be corrected while everyone is alive and agreeable. After a death, the same correction may need a court. Our article on [fixing title problems on inherited property](/learn/property-and-assets/fixing-title-problems-on-inherited-property) shows what that looks like.

## What should you do now?

A short action list works for all three situations.

1. Pull the current deed from the county recorder's website and read the names and the form of ownership.
2. Gather any evidence of loans, gifts or shared payments.
3. Decide what you actually want to happen, in plain words.
4. Match the paper to the wish with a deed change, an agreement, a will or a trust.
5. Tell the people affected. Surprises at a funeral are the costly kind.
