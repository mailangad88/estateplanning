You inherit a house. It is not yours to decide about on the first day, and the first call you get will probably be from a neighbor, a realtor or a cousin with an opinion. The better move is to make a decision with numbers, not feelings, and to give yourself a short, defined window to do it.

This worksheet-style page sets out what holding the house costs, what selling would net, and what changes if you share it with siblings. It supports our guides to [inheriting a house](/learn/after-a-death/inheriting-a-house) and [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property), and sits in the [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## First question: can you decide yet?

Only the person with authority can sell, so confirm who that is. If the house is in probate, the executor controls it, and heirs generally cannot sell until the court appoints one. Our page on [whether heirs can sell during probate](/learn/probate/can-heirs-sell-the-house-during-probate) covers this. If the house is in a living trust, the successor trustee holds the power. If title has already passed to you, the decision is yours, together with any co-owners.

Use the waiting time to gather facts. You can do most of the worksheet below before you have any power at all.

## What does it cost to hold the house each month?

Add these up for a typical month, then multiply by twelve. Most families underestimate this number.

| Cost | Notes |
|---|---|
| Mortgage or home equity payments | Ask the servicer how to keep the loan current |
| Property tax | Divide the annual bill by twelve; check for a reassessment |
| Homeowners insurance | A vacant house may need a different policy |
| Utilities | Keep heat on in winter to protect the pipes |
| Lawn, snow and basic maintenance | Local services or a neighbor you pay |
| Repairs and a reserve | A roof or boiler can arrive without warning |
| HOA or condo fees | Often due even if no one lives there |
| Your travel time | Real, even if it does not show on a bill |

Priya, who lives four hours from her late father's house, found the monthly total was larger than the rent she could expect from a tenant. That one comparison told her what to do. Our page on [vacant inherited houses](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters) explains the insurance clause that catches many heirs: policies often limit coverage if a house sits empty for 30 to 60 days.

## What would selling net you?

Start with a market opinion or appraisal, then subtract the agent's commission, closing costs, any mortgage payoff, and repairs a buyer will demand. That is your gross check. Now think about tax.

Inherited property generally gets a new tax basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death. That is the step-up in basis, explained on our [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) page. If you sell soon after the death for about that value, the gain is small. If you hold for five years and the house rises by $80,000, that increase is your taxable gain. Inherited property counts as held long term whether or not you owned it for a year.

The home sale exclusion of $250,000 per person generally requires you to have owned the house and lived in it as your main home for two of the last five years. An heir who never lived there cannot use it. An heir who moves in and stays two years may qualify later. A surviving spouse has a special two-year window that is covered on the sale page.

Keep the date-of-death appraisal. It is the most important tax document you will have.

## Which situations point to keeping the house?

Keep it when the house has a job to do. These are the cases where it usually pays:

- **You will live in it.** You avoid a second set of housing costs, and after two years you may qualify for the exclusion on a later sale.
- **It rents at a profit.** After all costs, including vacancy and management, the rent is positive. You also take on landlord duties, and our page on [inherited rental property taxes](/learn/property-and-assets/inherited-rental-property-taxes) shows how depreciation and income are handled.
- **The family has a real plan.** One sibling buys the others out, or all of them share use under a written agreement.
- **The house is land with long-term value.** A farm lot, a lakefront parcel or a family place that matters to the whole clan. See [keeping family land in the family](/learn/property-and-assets/keeping-family-land-in-the-family).

## Which situations point to selling?

Sell when holding costs exceed benefits or when ownership is shared without a plan.

- The monthly cost is higher than anything the house returns.
- It needs major repairs you cannot fund.
- Heirs live in different places and cannot manage it.
- Heirs disagree and a forced sale is the likely end anyway.
- The estate needs cash to pay debts, taxes or equalize shares.
- You will inherit a large amount of property at once and cannot manage it all.

Selling early also keeps the tax simple, because the basis is close to the sale price. In a high-tax area, check whether a transfer or reassessment applies if you keep the house instead. Our page on [property tax reassessment when a house passes to family](/learn/property-and-assets/property-tax-reassessment-and-transfer-tax-when-a-house-passes-to-family) explains.

## What if there are several heirs?

Then the decision is a family one, and money questions come first. Decide who pays carrying costs before you decide whether to keep. Keep records, since an heir who pays more may be entitled to credit later, as our page on [who pays for upkeep and reimbursing the sibling who fronts it](/learn/property-and-assets/who-pays-for-an-inherited-house-upkeep-taxes-and-reimbursing-the-sibling-who-fronts-it) describes.

If one heir wants to keep and the others want cash, a buyout is the usual answer. Our guide to [buying out a sibling or co-owner](/learn/property-and-assets/buying-out-a-sibling-or-co-owner-of-an-inherited-house-or-forcing-a-partition) covers pricing, financing and what a court partition would cost.

## A step-by-step way to decide

1. **Confirm authority.** Learn who can sign a deed and when.
2. **Secure the house.** Lock it, insure it, and photograph its condition.
3. **Order a date-of-death appraisal.** You need it for both the keep and sell paths.
4. **Fill in the monthly carrying costs** from the table above.
5. **Estimate net sale proceeds** after commission, closing costs, loan payoff and repairs.
6. **Estimate rent, if you would rent,** and subtract all costs, not just the mortgage.
7. **Set a decision date,** commonly within six to twelve months of the death.
8. **Put the decision in writing** if more than one person owns it, and update insurance to match.

## How state law changes this

State rules affect timing and cost. Probate length, whether a court must approve the sale, transfer taxes, and property tax rules differ. In New Jersey, deeds from an executor to an heir under a will or intestacy are among those the Division of Taxation lists as exempt from the realty transfer fee, and our page on [inheriting a house in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/inheriting-a-house-in-new-jersey) has the details. In Illinois, the homestead exemption rules after a death are explained in our [Illinois homestead page](/learn/illinois/illinois-homestead-exemption-after-a-death).
