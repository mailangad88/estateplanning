Firearms are personal property, like jewelry or furniture, but they come with legal limits that other heirlooms do not. A family member who inherits a gun they are not allowed to own can face serious criminal charges, and a well-meaning executor who hands a restricted item to the wrong person can too. Planning ahead protects your family and keeps the transfer lawful.

This page is general education about how firearms fit into an estate plan. The law changes and differs by state, so confirm the details for your situation with an attorney. It is part of our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Can firearms be passed down in a will or trust?

Yes, firearms can be left by will or trust, but the recipient must be someone who may lawfully possess them. A will can make a specific gift, such as "my Remington shotgun to my son Daniel." A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can do the same and is also a useful place to give instructions for storage and interim care. A firearm left to someone who is legally prohibited cannot be delivered to them.

Some collections are valuable, and some are only sentimental. Treat them like any asset that needs identification, valuation and a named recipient.

## Who cannot legally receive a firearm?

Federal law prohibits some categories of people from possessing firearms, and an heir in any of those categories cannot take them. Generally, the federal list covers people who:

- Have been convicted of a felony, or of certain domestic violence misdemeanors.
- Are subject to certain domestic violence protective orders.
- Are unlawful users of, or addicted to, controlled substances.
- Have been involuntarily committed or found by a court to be mentally incompetent, in the circumstances the law describes.
- Are fugitives from justice, or are in the country unlawfully or on certain visas.
- Were dishonorably discharged from the military.

The details of each category are technical and change through legislation and court decisions, so do not assume. States add their own prohibited categories, and some restrict people under a certain age or with certain convictions.

Minors are a special case. Federal law restricts handgun possession by anyone under 18 with limited exceptions, and states have their own age rules. A minor beneficiary should not receive a firearm directly. The executor or a trustee should hold it until the child is old enough and meets every legal requirement.

If a family member is prohibited, name an alternate recipient, or tell the executor to sell the firearm through a licensed dealer and give that person the proceeds. This is the safest way to treat everyone fairly without breaking the law.

## What are NFA items, and why do they need extra care?

The National Firearms Act regulates certain items, including machine guns, suppressors (silencers), short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and a few other categories. They must be registered with the ATF, and transferring one requires an approved ATF application. A transfer without approval can be a felony.

When an NFA owner dies, the item does not simply pass to an heir. The general path is:

1. The executor secures the item and does not let anyone else take possession.
2. The executor, or the trustee if a trust owns it, reviews the ATF's current guidance with an attorney who knows NFA rules.
3. An ATF application, commonly Form 5 for a transfer without payment of tax to a lawful heir, is filed and approved before the item goes to the new owner.
4. Only after approval does the heir take possession.

The tax picture for NFA items has changed in recent years, and as of 2026 some items may carry a reduced federal transfer tax while still requiring registration and approval. Check current ATF rules before acting. Machine guns made after a certain date generally cannot be transferred to private owners at all, which limits what an executor can do with them.

Some owners hold NFA items in a trust, often called a gun trust, so that named trustees can possess them lawfully and a successor takes over at death. A gun trust is not a shortcut around approval or prohibited-person rules. Everyone named must be legally able to possess the items, and an attorney who understands the area should draft it.

## How do state laws change the picture?

States vary widely, and your plan has to fit the state where the heir lives, not just where you do. Differences include:

- **Permits and background checks.** Some states require a permit to purchase or possess, or a background check on any transfer, including between family members. Others have few requirements.
- **Registration.** A few states or cities require registration of firearms, and some require inherited firearms to be registered within a set period.
- **Restricted firearms and magazines.** Certain states ban particular models, features or magazine sizes. A firearm that is lawful where you live may be prohibited where your heir lives.
- **Waiting periods and safe storage laws.** Some states require specific storage or locking devices, particularly when minors may have access.
- **Out-of-state heirs.** Federal law generally allows a person to take possession of a firearm by inheritance in another state when both states' laws allow it, but the heir must still follow their own state's rules. Mailing handguns or driving firearms across state lines has its own restrictions.

Our state guides, such as [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), [California](/estate-planning/california) and [New York](/estate-planning/new-york), show how much estate rules differ in general. Confirm the specific firearms rules with an attorney in the relevant state.

## How should you plan for firearms, step by step?

1. **Make an inventory.** List each firearm with make, model, caliber, serial number, how and when it was acquired, and whether any are NFA items. Keep photos and any purchase records.
2. **Decide who gets what.** Name recipients and alternates in your will or trust, and make sure each is legally eligible and willing.
3. **Plan for someone who cannot receive them.** Give the executor authority to sell through a licensed dealer and share the proceeds.
4. **Address storage.** Say where the firearms are kept and who can access them. Keep the safe combination or key location in a separate document, because a will becomes public in probate.
5. **Appoint a capable executor or trustee.** Choose someone comfortable securing guns and following instructions. See [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
6. **Give instructions for NFA items.** Say how they are held and whom the trustee or executor should ask for help.
7. **Update the plan.** Review after a purchase, a sale, a move, or a change in a beneficiary's circumstances.

## What should an executor do right after a death?

The first job is to prevent a loss or an unlawful transfer. A reasonable approach:

- Lock the firearms in a safe or secure room, or use trigger locks and remove ammunition if no safe is available. Change locks on a vacant home, since empty houses are a known target for theft.
- Do not let heirs take items home before the estate is ready, even if the will names them.
- Make or confirm the inventory and note any NFA items.
- Check the will or trust for instructions, and consult an attorney about transfer rules in the heir's state.
- Keep records of who received what and when, including receipts for any sale.
- If a firearm is stolen or lost, report it to local law enforcement.

Our overview of [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) covers the wider job.

## What about firearms during illness or memory loss?

Safety issues often arise before death. If a gun owner develops dementia, family members may need to arrange secure storage or removal of firearms. A conversation while the owner has capacity, with instructions in writing, makes that easier. Do not assume a financial power of attorney lets an agent possess or transfer firearms, because the agent must still follow the same federal and state rules. See [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) for planning the broader picture.

## What if an heir does not want the firearms?

No one is required to accept an inheritance. A beneficiary who does not want a firearm can [disclaim](/glossary/disclaimer) it, which means it passes as if that person had died first, or the executor can sell it through a licensed dealer, an auction house or a consignment sale. State and federal rules apply to the sale, so use a licensed intermediary rather than a private sale unless an attorney confirms a private sale is allowed.
