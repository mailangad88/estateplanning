Owning a house with someone is easy at the closing table and confusing a decade later. The deed says something like "Elena and Marcus, as joint tenants with right of survivorship," and nobody read it closely. Then one dies, or they split up, or one files for bankruptcy, and the three lines on the deed become the whole story.

This page compares the main ways two or more people can hold title to real estate, what each does at death, and how to choose among them for a spouse, an unmarried partner or a sibling. It belongs to our [real estate and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## How do the three forms compare?

| Feature | Joint tenancy (survivorship) | Tenancy in common | Tenancy by the entirety |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who can hold it | Anyone | Anyone | Married couples only, in states that allow it |
| Shares | Equal | Any split, equal or not | Treated as one unit |
| At one owner's death | Survivor takes everything | Share goes to heirs or will beneficiaries | Survivor takes everything |
| Can one owner sell alone? | Yes, which ends the survivorship | Yes | Generally no |
| Can the owner's will leave the share? | No | Yes | No |
| Creditors of one owner | May reach that owner's share | May reach that owner's share | Often protected from one spouse's separate debts |
| Probate for the first death | Avoided | Needed for the deceased share | Avoided |

The pattern is simple. Survivorship forms pass the property automatically and ignore your will. Tenancy in common keeps each share separate and subject to your will.

## What is joint tenancy with right of survivorship?

It is co-ownership in which the surviving owner automatically takes the whole property. The classic requirements are that the owners take title at the same time, in equal shares, with the same deed, and with equal rights to use the whole. The deed must say survivorship, or words such as "as joint tenants with right of survivorship," because several states presume otherwise.

The benefit is simplicity: no probate for the first death. The costs are the ones our page on [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) explains: a creditor of one owner may attach that owner's interest, a lawsuit or bankruptcy can ripple through, and the survivor can leave the property to anyone, including people the first owner would never have chosen. One owner can also end the survivorship without the other's knowledge by deeding their interest away, in many states. See our glossary entry on [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy).

## What is tenancy in common?

Each owner holds a separate share, equal or not, and can sell, mortgage or leave it by will. There is no survivorship. If Dev owns 60% and his sister Anika owns 40%, and Dev dies with a will leaving everything to his wife, the wife now owns 60% with Anika.

Tenancy in common is the usual default when unrelated people buy together and the deed says nothing, in most states. It suits situations where each owner wants their own heirs to receive their share: a second marriage with children from each side, business partners, or friends buying together. The downsides are that the deceased share must go through probate (or a trust), and you may end up co-owning with a stranger. Our article on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) shows how that goes wrong over generations.

## What is tenancy by the entirety?

Tenancy by the entirety is a form of survivorship ownership available only to married couples, and only in some states. The law treats the couple as a single owner. Neither spouse can sell or mortgage the property alone, and in many states the property cannot be reached by a creditor who holds a judgment against just one spouse. The protection usually ends if both spouses owe the debt, if they divorce, or if federal tax claims are involved.

Whether your state allows it varies. Illinois, for example, recognizes it only for homestead property, as described in our [Illinois guide](/learn/illinois/illinois-joint-tenancy-and-tenancy-by-the-entirety). Check how the form works where you live, and whether it applies to property bought before or after you married. See also our glossary entry on [tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety).

## What about community property states?

In community property states, married couples may hold title as community property, and some states allow community property with right of survivorship, which combines community treatment with automatic survivorship. Community property can also give both halves a full step-up in basis at the first death, which matters for taxes. We explain it in [community property with right of survivorship](/learn/probate/community-property-with-right-of-survivorship). Our glossary page on [community property](/glossary/community-property) has the basic idea.

## What if you own a house with an unmarried partner?

Be deliberate, because the law gives unmarried partners almost nothing by default. If the deed names only one partner, the other has no ownership, and without a will the other may have no inheritance at all. Our scenario page on [an unmarried partner who dies without a will](/learn/what-if/unmarried-partner-dies-without-a-will) shows how that ends.

If both names are on the deed, you have two choices.

- **Joint tenants with survivorship.** The survivor keeps the home, which suits a couple with no children from other relationships. Adding a partner to a deed may be a taxable gift to the extent you give them value, and unlike a spouse, there is no unlimited marital deduction for a gift to an unmarried partner.
- **Tenants in common.** Each can leave their share to their own children or family. Back it with wills or a trust, and a written agreement about who lives there, who pays, and what happens if the relationship ends.

A cohabitation or property agreement is the piece people skip. It should state each person's contribution, the buy-out price formula, and how long the survivor may stay if the share goes to the other's family. See [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples).

## What if you own a house with a sibling?

Siblings often inherit or buy a parent's house together. Treat it like a business partnership. Decide first whether you want survivorship, which gives the longer-lived sibling the whole house and may cut out nieces and nephews, or tenancy in common, which keeps each family's share separate. Then agree on expenses, use, and a right of first refusal. If one sibling stops paying or wants out, a partition lawsuit is the legal fallback, and it is expensive. Our page on [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) describes the usual fights.

## How is the tax treated at death?

For spouses, the rule is generally simple: half the property is included in the first spouse's estate, and that half gets a step-up in basis. For co-owners who are not spouses, the amount included depends on the form of ownership and, for joint tenancy, on who contributed to the purchase. For tenancy in common, the decedent's share is included, and that share gets a step-up. These rules are technical, and a wrong assumption can cost the survivor. Read [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) and ask a tax professional about your numbers.

## How do you change the way you hold title?

You sign and record a new deed. Converting from joint tenancy to tenancy in common is usually done by either owner deeding their share to themselves as tenants in common, and in many states that works without the other's consent. Going the other way requires both signatures. Some forms have special rules about how the deed must be worded, so use a lawyer-drafted deed. Check mortgage and title insurance effects before you record, and see [how to add or remove a name on a deed](/learn/property-and-assets/remove-a-name-from-a-deed).

Name an agent too. If one of you becomes unable to sign, a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets the other keep the property moving.
