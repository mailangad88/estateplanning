Three children inherit their mother's house. One, Priya, wants to keep it and move in. Her brothers, Dev and Anil, would rather have cash. Nobody is angry, but no one knows how a buyout actually works, and the house now belongs to all three of them in equal shares. That is the usual starting point, and the good news is that most of these end with a signed agreement and a deed, not a lawsuit.

This page walks through the buyout, in order, and then describes what happens if a co-owner will not cooperate. For the family-fight side of this, read our page on [siblings who inherit a house and disagree](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree). For the wider land picture, see [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) and our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## First, who actually owns the house?

You cannot buy a share from someone who does not yet hold it. If the house is going through probate, the executor controls it until a deed or court order puts it in the heirs' names. If it sat in a trust, the successor trustee deeds it out. Get a title search or a copy of the recorded deed before you negotiate, because a buyout built on the wrong names has to be redone. Our page on [fixing title problems on inherited property](/learn/property-and-assets/fixing-title-problems-on-inherited-property) covers the common snags.

## How do you price a sibling's share?

You price it from the value of the whole house, then divide. A written appraisal from a licensed appraiser is the most defensible number, because nobody can say the buyer picked it. Two real estate agents giving an opinion also works when everyone trusts them.

A worked example, with invented numbers. The appraisal is $360,000. There is a $60,000 mortgage left. Equity is $300,000, so each child's third is $100,000. Dev paid $6,000 of property tax and insurance out of pocket during probate, so his buyout is $106,000. Anil, who paid nothing, gets $100,000. Priya covers both, and she owes the mortgage afterward.

| Step | Example |
|---|---|
| Appraised value | $360,000 |
| Less mortgage | $60,000 |
| Equity | $300,000 |
| Each one-third share | $100,000 |
| Adjust for expenses one sibling paid | +$6,000 to Dev |
| Priya's total payment | $206,000 |

Sellers sometimes argue for a market-value test and buyers for a quick-sale discount. Skip the debate by splitting the cost of one appraisal and treating it as final. For the expense side, our guide to [who pays for upkeep and reimbursing the sibling who fronts it](/learn/property-and-assets/who-pays-for-an-inherited-house-upkeep-taxes-and-reimbursing-the-sibling-who-fronts-it) shows how to document it.

## How do you pay for it?

There are three common ways, and many families mix them.

1. **Cash or other inheritance.** If Priya has savings, or if her share of other assets can be offset (she takes less of the investment account), there is no loan at all. This is often the cleanest route, and an executor can build it into the estate's distribution.
2. **A refinance.** Priya takes a new mortgage in her name only, pays off the old loan and uses the extra to pay her brothers. The lender will want the title in her name or arranged to close at the same time.
3. **A promissory note.** The brothers accept payments over time, secured by a mortgage on the house. It works for family, but it makes them lenders. Put the interest rate, the due date and the default terms in writing.

If the old mortgage stays in place, talk to the servicer before you sign anything. Federal law generally stops a lender from calling a loan due just because a home passes to a relative on a death, as our page on [mortgages when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies) explains, but a buyout is a separate transfer and the servicer should hear about it.

## What documents close the buyout?

Keep it short and signed, in this order:

1. A written buyout agreement stating the price, the date, who pays what, and who bears taxes and utilities until closing.
2. The deed from each selling sibling to the buyer. A quitclaim or special warranty deed is common, and our page on [types of deeds](/learn/property-and-assets/types-of-deeds-quitclaim-warranty-grant-and-trustee-deeds) explains the difference.
3. Payment, ideally through a title company or attorney's escrow, so nobody hands over a deed before the money lands.
4. Recording of the deed with the county recorder. In New Jersey, deeds that are part of a partition are among those the Division of Taxation lists as exempt from the realty transfer fee, so confirm the exemption wording on the deed. In Illinois, ask the county recorder about transfer declarations.
5. Updated homeowners insurance and, if needed, title insurance for the new owner.

A title company is worth the fee here. A mistake in a deed between siblings is easy to make and hard to fix once someone has died.

## What are the tax effects?

For the seller, small. Inherited property generally receives a new basis equal to its value at the date of death, so a sibling who sells soon after the death usually has little or no gain. Our page on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains the rule. If the house has risen in value since the death, the seller reports that difference as a long-term gain. The buyer's basis for the bought portion is the price paid, and the inherited portion keeps its stepped-up basis.

A buyout at far below value can be treated as a partial gift by the seller, which is a reason to use a real appraisal. The annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, and larger gifts need a gift tax return, though not necessarily any tax. Speak with a tax professional if the numbers are lopsided.

## What if a co-owner will not sell, or will not buy?

Then you are choosing between waiting, negotiating through a mediator, and a court partition action. Mediation is often worth its modest cost, because a neutral person can say what the siblings cannot.

Partition is the legal tool for ending co-ownership. In Illinois the statute is Article XVII of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/17-101 and following), and in New Jersey partition begins at N.J.S.A. 2A:56-1 and following, brought in the Chancery Division of Superior Court. A court can divide land physically, which rarely suits a single house, or order a sale and split the proceeds. Many states also give the owners who want to stay a chance to buy first, but the rules differ, so ask a local attorney about yours.

What drives the cost:

- Court filing fees and service on every co-owner, including heirs who have died.
- An attorney for each side, or a shared one if everyone agrees.
- An appraiser, and sometimes a court-appointed referee or commissioner to run the sale.
- Carrying costs while the case drags on: taxes, insurance, utilities and repairs.
- The sale itself, including agent commission.

Because those costs usually come out of the proceeds, a forced sale shrinks everyone's check. That fact alone is a good line to use in a family conversation. Our page on [selling inherited property](/learn/after-a-death/selling-inherited-property) covers the sale side.

## Can you prevent this before it starts?

Yes. A parent who wants one child to keep the house can say so in the will or trust, with an equalizing gift to the others, or give the trustee power to offer the house to a named child at appraised value.
