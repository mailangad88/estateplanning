Ruth, 76, bought her home in 1984 for $62,000. It is worth about $410,000 today. Her son Paul suggests she sign it over to him now, "so we don't have to deal with probate." It sounds practical. She asks whether there is a tax, and the honest answer is: not the one you are thinking of, and several you are not.

This page lays out what a lifetime gift of a house actually triggers, compares it with a sale and with leaving the house at death, and describes the lower-risk ways to do the same job. It belongs to our [guide to real estate and special assets](/learn/property-and-assets).

## What happens when you deed a house to your child?

You make a gift of its full value on the day the deed is signed. Your child becomes the owner, you lose control, and four separate tax and benefit systems notice: federal gift tax, income tax basis, local property tax, and Medicaid. Each one is covered below.

If you keep living there, you are a guest in what is now your child's house. They can sell it, borrow against it, or lose it to a lawsuit, and you cannot stop them.

## Do you owe gift tax?

Almost never in cash, but you do have paperwork. The gift tax annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient in 2026. A house is far above that, so you file a federal gift tax return (Form 709) for the year of the gift. The value above the exclusion is subtracted from your lifetime exclusion of $15,000,000, and tax is owed only if you ever exhaust it. Few families do.

Married couples can treat a gift as made half by each spouse, which doubles the annual exclusion, but the return is still needed. An appraisal supports the value you report. For the full mechanics, read [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) and [whether lifetime gifts count toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax).

## What is the basis trap?

The basis trap is the biggest cost most families do not see. A child who receives a house as a gift takes over the parent's original basis, the price paid plus improvements. A child who inherits the house at death generally takes a basis equal to its value on the date of death, a step-up that can erase the gain.

Look at Ruth's numbers.

| | Gift now | Inherit at death |
|---|---|---|
| Value at transfer | $410,000 | $410,000 |
| Paul's basis | About $62,000 | Value at death, say $410,000 |
| Gain if Paul sells at $410,000 | About $348,000 | About $0 |
| Federal tax on that gain at a 15% rate | Roughly $52,000 | Roughly $0 |

The 15% figure is an illustration, not a prediction, since rates depend on income, and Paul could owe more. Paul might reduce the gain if he lives in the home as his main residence for two years and uses the home sale exclusion of up to $250,000, but that only helps if he lives there. Many states add their own tax on the gain too. The step-up does not apply to gifts, which is why most estate planners prefer to keep the house in the parent's name until death. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## Will property taxes go up?

Possibly. Property tax rules differ widely. In many places, the assessed value changes only when the home is sold. In others, a transfer of ownership, including a gift, can trigger reassessment to current market value, wiping out the long-owner discount that years of capped increases created. Some jurisdictions exempt transfers between parents and children, but a number of them have narrowed that exemption in recent years, for instance to a home the child will use as a main residence, or up to a value cap.

You may also lose a homestead or senior exemption. These often attach to the owner who lives in the home, and a deed to a child can disqualify you. Call the county assessor and ask what happens on a parent-to-child deed before you sign anything. A letter back is better than a phone answer.

## Is there a transfer tax or recording fee?

Recording a deed costs a fee almost everywhere. Some states and cities also charge a transfer tax based on value. Many exempt gifts and transfers with no payment, or transfers between family members, and require you to file an exemption form with the deed. Some charge a smaller amount anyway. Our article on [removing or adding a name on a deed](/learn/property-and-assets/remove-a-name-from-a-deed) includes the recording steps.

## How does a gift affect Medicaid?

A gift is a transfer for less than fair value, and Medicaid looks at it. In most states, any transfer within 60 months before you apply for long-term care Medicaid can trigger a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay. A house worth $410,000 could create a long penalty. Some transfers are exempt, such as one to a disabled child or to a child who has lived in the home and cared for you for two years or more. These rules are narrow and specific. Read [the Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) and [protecting your home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) before any transfer.

## Should you sell the house to your child instead?

A sale makes you the seller, with a tax bill if the gain exceeds your exclusion. If you lived in the house for two of the last five years, a single person can exclude up to $250,000 of gain and a married couple up to $500,000. Ruth's gain of about $348,000 would be partly sheltered.

A sale at a discount is half gift and half sale. You report the gift portion, the child's basis becomes a blend, and Medicaid treats the discount as a transfer. A sale also works against the family's usual goal, which is to avoid cost and keep it simple. Lenders and title companies may also treat a family sale carefully. If it is done for a good reason, such as the child paying with a note, it needs proper documents.

## What are the safer alternatives?

Four tools can let the house reach your child without the downsides above.

1. **Transfer-on-death deed.** You keep ownership and control, and the house passes at death outside probate in the states that allow it. The child still gets the step-up. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).
2. **Enhanced life estate (lady bird) deed.** Available in a few states, it keeps your right to sell or change the beneficiary and gives a step-up. See [lady bird deeds](/learn/probate/lady-bird-deed-explained).
3. **Revocable living trust.** It avoids probate, handles incapacity, and works in every state. See [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).
4. **A will.** It works but requires probate, which is a cost and a delay. Our page on [leaving your home to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) compares all of them.

## When does giving the house now make sense?

There are good reasons to give a house in life: a parent in good health who has other assets and is moving into assisted living, an estate large enough that removing appreciation matters for tax, or a house with little gain because the parent bought it recently. A deliberate gift made with tax advice, five or more years before care is likely, with a signed agreement about who pays the taxes, insurance and repairs, can work. A gift made in a panic when a diagnosis arrives usually does not.

Talk to the other children before you decide. Giving the house to one child, even with the best intent, is how many sibling disputes start.
