Walt, 72, owns 140 acres and a hunting cabin that his grandfather bought. He has four children, two of whom live within an hour and two of whom live across the country. His will says "to my children equally." He is quietly hoping that means the land stays in the family. It does not mean that, and he knows it.

Land has an emotional pull that a bank account never has, and the law treats it with less patience than families do. This page covers practical ways to keep a farm, a lake cabin or a piece of ancestral ground together. It belongs to our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## Why does family land get sold?

Most land that leaves a family does so in a handful of familiar ways.

- **Equal shares, no rules.** Four children inherit a quarter each as tenants in common. Each can sell, borrow against, or be sued over their share.
- **A partition lawsuit.** Any co-owner can ask a court to divide or sell the property. If it cannot be divided fairly, the court may order a sale. Our article on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) shows how this works.
- **Carrying costs with nobody in charge.** Property tax, insurance, repairs and road maintenance continue. One child pays and resents it.
- **Taxes at the death.** A federal estate tax is unlikely for most families because the 2026 exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, but a state estate or inheritance tax may apply, and land rich families can be cash poor.
- **A spouse or creditor of an heir.** A share owned by a child can be reached in that child's divorce or bankruptcy.

Every tool below is a way to remove one of those causes.

## How do you choose the right owner: trust, LLC or individuals?

Land should usually be owned by something that does not die, with rules written down. The two main choices are a trust and a limited liability company.

| Feature | Trust | LLC |
|---|---|---|
| Avoids probate | Yes, if the land is deeded in | Yes, interests pass by the agreement or a trust |
| Written rules for sharing | Can be added to the trust terms | Built into an operating agreement |
| Voting and management | A trustee decides | Managers or members vote |
| Buy-out of a relative | Possible, but has to be drafted | Usual feature |
| Liability protection | Limited | Often useful for rental or recreational use |
| Cost to set up and run | Lower | Higher, with annual filings in many states |

A common design is a revocable living trust during your life that becomes a continuing trust, or an LLC owned by the trust, after your death. Our page on [what assets go in a living trust](/learn/trusts/what-assets-go-in-a-living-trust) covers the basics of what belongs in a trust, and [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) covers the deed step that people skip. A trust that is never funded does nothing, as the [unfunded trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) page shows.

Some states limit how many years a trust can last, while others allow trusts that run for a very long time. If you want a plan that spans several generations, an attorney in your state needs to confirm what is allowed.

## What should the family agreement say?

The agreement is where land stays or goes. A good one answers these questions before anyone is angry.

1. **Who may use the property, and when?** A calendar for the cabin, or a rule for who may farm which fields, prevents most squabbles.
2. **Who pays what?** Split property taxes, insurance and repairs by share or by use, and set a reserve fund.
3. **How are decisions made?** Majority vote for routine matters, supermajority for selling or borrowing.
4. **How does someone leave?** A right of first refusal lets the family buy a departing member's share at a formula price before an outsider can.
5. **What happens in a divorce, bankruptcy or death of a member?** Transfers to spouses and creditors can be blocked or bought out.
6. **Who steps in as manager?** Name a successor and a backup, and say how they are removed.

Think about fairness too. A child who farms the land, and one who lives abroad, have different needs. Some families give the working child the land and leave other assets to the others. See [how to leave a house to one child and money to another](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children) for that trade-off.

## Should you leave cash to carry the land?

Yes, almost always. Land can be worth millions and produce no income. Leave a fund, often in the same trust, to pay taxes, insurance and maintenance for a few years, and a source of cash for buy-outs. Life insurance owned by an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) is a common way to fund this when the estate would otherwise have to sell land to pay a tax bill.

## What is a conservation easement and does it fit?

A conservation easement is a permanent agreement with a land trust or government body that limits development. You continue to own the land. You can usually still farm it, hunt on it and leave it to your family. The easement stays with the land forever and binds every later owner.

It can reduce the land's appraised value, which may reduce estate tax and, if it meets federal requirements, can qualify for an income tax deduction. The costs are real: the family gives up future development value and the restrictions are very difficult to undo. It suits owners whose main goal is that the land stays open, and it is a poor fit for those who might one day need to sell lots. Because the tax rules are technical, have a tax advisor review the plan before you sign.

## What about mineral rights, water and access?

Check these early. They are separate property interests.

- **Mineral rights** can be owned apart from the surface. You may own them, you may have sold them decades ago, or a relative may hold a share. Look in the county records for deeds that "reserve" or "except" minerals. If you do own them, they can be left by will or trust, and ones in another state can require a second probate. See [property in another state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).
- **Water rights** are important in the western states, where they can be worth more than the land. They often have their own paperwork and use requirements.
- **Road access** matters. A landlocked parcel is hard to insure, finance or sell. Look for a recorded easement in the deed. If there is none, your state may recognize an easement by necessity, but proving it may take a court case. Settle it while a neighbor remembers the handshake.

## What about taxes when land passes to the next generation?

Land left at death generally gets a stepped-up basis to its value on the date of death, which can wipe out decades of gain. Land given away in your lifetime generally keeps your old basis. That is a strong reason not to deed land to children early just to keep it in the family; a [trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) holds it for the family while preserving the step-up. For the details, see [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). Farms can have special valuation and installment rules, covered in [farm succession planning](/learn/business-owners/farm-succession-planning).

## How do you start?

Write down what "keeping it in the family" means to you. For Walt it was: the cabin stays, any child can use it, no outsider owns a piece, and the farm fields can be rented. That statement drives the entity, the agreement, and the funding. If you cannot yet say which child will care for the land, write the rules so that a fair process, not a lawsuit, answers the question.
