Many families learn about mineral rights only after a death. A letter arrives from an energy company with a small check, or a landman calls about leasing land nobody in the family has visited in thirty years. Sometimes the discovery is a pleasant surprise. Often it is a tangle of old deeds and a dozen relatives.

This page explains how mineral rights and royalties are passed on, how heirs claim them, and what an owner can do now to make the process easier. It is part of our guide to [property and assets](/learn/property-and-assets) and often overlaps with [property in another state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

## What are mineral rights, and why can someone else own them?

Land ownership can be split into layers. The surface is one layer. The oil, gas, coal or other minerals below are another, and in many states they can be sold or kept separately. When an earlier owner sold a farm and held back the minerals, the buyer got the surface and the seller kept what lay beneath. Those mineral rights then passed down in the seller's family for generations.

That explains why a deed to a house or farm may say nothing about minerals, and why the person who owns the field may not receive the royalty. It also explains why your parent's will might leave "the farm" to one child, while the minerals pass differently if they were never mentioned.

Three terms come up often:

- **Mineral interest.** Ownership of the minerals themselves, usually including the right to lease them.
- **Royalty interest.** The right to a share of the money from production, with no cost to the owner.
- **Working interest.** The right to explore and produce, which also carries the costs and risks. This is more common for operators than for family owners.

## Do mineral rights pass by will, trust or state law?

By any of the three, the same as other real estate. They are real property, so if you leave a will they pass under it, and if you have a living trust and it owns them, the trustee distributes them. If neither applies, state intestacy law decides who inherits, in the state where the minerals are located.

Here is how the paths differ.

| How the rights are held | What happens at death | What the heirs do |
|---|---|---|
| Your name, will in place | Probate in the state and county of the minerals | Letters, then a recorded executor's deed or order of distribution |
| Your name, no will | Same, with heirs set by state law | Court determines heirs, which can include distant relatives |
| Living trust | Passes under the trust, no probate | Successor trustee records a certificate of trust and a deed |
| Joint with right of survivorship | Passes to the survivor | Record the death certificate where required |
| TOD deed (where allowed for minerals) | Passes to the named person | Record the death certificate and affidavit |

Tom, 79, lives in Ohio and owns a quarter interest in the minerals under a ranch in another state that his father held. He never put them in his trust. When he dies, his executor must open a second probate in that state to transfer title, even though the rest of Tom's estate goes through an Ohio court. The second case costs a local attorney's fee and takes months. If Tom had signed a deed assigning the minerals to his living trust, the trustee would have recorded a document and been done. Our article on [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) explains why this happens.

## How do heirs collect royalties?

Companies that pay royalties will not simply redirect a check because a relative asks. They need proof, and they get it from the county records. The usual path:

1. **Find every interest.** Look for old leases, division orders and 1099-MISC or 1099-R forms. Ask the operator for a statement showing the decimal interest.
2. **Open probate or use the trust.** In the state where the minerals sit, if needed.
3. **Record the proof.** An order from the court, or a trust certificate and trustee's deed, is filed in the county land records.
4. **Send it to the payor.** The operator or purchaser will want a certified copy, a death certificate, a W-9 and updated payment details.
5. **Expect a delay.** Payment may be held in suspense until title is cleared, then released with back royalties.

Operators sometimes cannot locate the owner of record, so unpaid royalties may be held, or sent to the state as unclaimed property. If you suspect your parent had an interest, searching your state's unclaimed property database and asking the operator is free. Our article on [how to find a deceased person's accounts and assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-find-a-deceased-persons-accounts-and-assets) lists other places to look.

## Why do mineral interests get so complicated?

Because they divide. If a grandfather leaves minerals to four children and each of them leaves theirs to three, nine grandchildren own fractions of a fraction. Each fraction is real property that needs its own paperwork. Some heirs live in other states, some cannot be found, and some do not want the headache.

This looks a lot like the situation with family land, which our article on [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) describes, and the same lesson applies. A clear plan in one generation saves five generations of paperwork.

Heirs often have several practical options. They can keep the interest and collect the income. They can sell it, sometimes to a company that has been asking, in which case a valuation by an independent professional is worth the cost. They can also combine interests in an LLC or trust so decisions are made once. Selling to the first caller is rarely a good idea, since offers are often well below what the interest is worth.

## How should an owner plan for mineral rights?

- **Make a list.** Where the minerals are, which counties, what fraction you own, who the operator is, and your lease status.
- **Put them in a living trust.** This avoids probate in each state. Funding means signing and recording a deed or assignment for each county, as explained in [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
- **Say who gets what.** Decide whether the minerals go with the surface land or are shared separately, and whether to leave unequal shares. Name them in the document.
- **Name a manager.** Pick one person to deal with the operator, so heirs do not each sign different leases.
- **Keep records together.** Leases, division orders and royalty statements in one folder, with a note on where the original deeds are.
- **Think about a family entity.** Some owners transfer the minerals to a family LLC while alive, a step with tax and valuation effects worth reviewing first.

## What are the tax issues?

Heirs generally receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) on inherited minerals, which resets their tax cost to the value at death. Royalty income is taxable income each year, and heirs may be able to claim depletion deductions, a tax rule for resources that run out. The estate or trust reports income received before it is distributed.

Valuation is the hard part. A producing well's value depends on its production history and current prices, and an appraiser familiar with oil and gas interests usually does the job. For the estate tax rules, see our article on [how assets are valued](/learn/estate-tax/how-assets-are-valued-for-estate-tax). A tax preparer who handles royalty returns is worth finding, since the forms and state filings can be unfamiliar.
