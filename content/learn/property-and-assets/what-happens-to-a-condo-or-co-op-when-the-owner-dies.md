A condo looks like a small house, but legally it is a bundle. You own the inside of a unit, a share of the common areas, and a duty to the association that runs the building. A co-op is even further from a house. When the owner dies, those extra pieces are what slow families down.

This page explains what happens to a condo or co-op apartment when the owner dies, what the association can and cannot do, and the steps for heirs. It belongs to our [property and assets](/learn/property-and-assets) series.

## How is a condo different from a co-op?

The names get used loosely, so start with the difference.

| | Condo | Co-op |
|---|---|---|
| What you own | The unit itself, plus a fractional interest in common areas | Shares in a corporation and a proprietary lease |
| Legal type | Real estate | Generally treated as personal property |
| Transfer paper | Deed | Stock certificate and lease assignment |
| Who approves a new owner | Usually nobody, though there may be a right of first refusal | The board, almost always |
| Monthly bill | Common charges, also called HOA fees | Maintenance, which includes the building's taxes and mortgage |
| Probate route | Same as any real estate | Often handled as personal property |

Both exist in Illinois and New Jersey. A co-op share can be easier to pass in probate because it is personal property, but the board adds a step.

## What happens to a condo when the owner dies?

The unit passes under the owner's will, trust or deed. If the condo is titled in a living trust, the successor trustee can act within days. If it is in the owner's name alone, the executor needs letters from the court to sign a deed, and a no-will estate means an administrator. Read our guide to [putting a house in a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) for how the trust route works, which applies to condos too.

Joint ownership changes the result. If two people own with right of survivorship, the survivor takes the whole unit by operation of law and the file is just a death certificate and a new recording. Illinois and New Jersey both have forms of this, including [tenancy by the entirety](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenants-vs-tenants-in-common-vs-entirety) for married couples.

Meanwhile the association keeps sending bills. Common charges do not pause for a death.

## What should the executor do in the first week?

1. **Call the property manager or association.** Ask for the account balance, any special assessments voted on or pending, and the documents on file. Say you are the family and that letters are being sought.
2. **Check the declaration or bylaws.** They set rental limits, resale rules and any right of first refusal for the association.
3. **Keep the insurance.** The association's master policy covers the building, but the owner's own policy covers contents and the interior. A vacant unit may need a notice to the insurer.
4. **Pay or arrange the fees.** Falling behind lets the association record a lien. The estate pays out of the owner's accounts when it can.
5. **Secure the unit.** Change locks if needed, and give the manager the contact for the family.
6. **Find the mortgage lender.** Federal law protects family members who inherit a home from a due-on-sale demand in many cases, as discussed in [what happens to the mortgage when the homeowner dies](/learn/property-and-assets/mortgage-when-the-homeowner-dies).
7. **For a co-op, ask the managing agent for the process** to approve a new shareholder or to sell the shares.

## What can the condo association actually do?

An association can charge fees, assess for repairs, and enforce its rules. If fees go unpaid, it can generally record a lien against the unit, and in many states it can foreclose on that lien. That can happen even if there is no mortgage balance owed.

It cannot take the unit for no reason or block an heir from owning it just because it dislikes them, unless the declaration gives it a right of first refusal. A right of first refusal lets the association match a sale offer within a stated period. It usually applies to sales, not to transfers by inheritance, but read the document, because wording varies.

For assessments, timing is the issue. Charges due before death are debts of the estate. Charges that come due after death fall to whoever holds title then, which may be the estate or the heir. Ask the association for a written statement of what is owed and when the next big charge is expected.

## What happens to a co-op when the owner dies?

The shares and the proprietary lease belong to the estate. Heirs do not automatically become shareholders. The co-op's bylaws usually say that a new holder needs board approval, and some require a financial review like the one a buyer would face.

In practice, boards often approve a spouse or child who will live there, and often allow the estate time to sell to a third party. The board normally cannot force the estate to sell on the spot, but it can insist on timely payment of maintenance. If the heir is not approved, the shares are sold and the money goes through the estate.

### An example

Gloria, 71, owned a one-bedroom condo in Evanston with $410 in monthly fees, and no will. Her son Marcus lives in New Jersey. The unit would have a market value of around $300,000. She had fallen three months behind on dues after an illness, and the association had voted a $6,000 roof assessment the week before she died.

Marcus asked a court to appoint him administrator. Meanwhile the association's attorney warned that a lien would be recorded unless the arrears were paid. The estate paid them from Gloria's savings account once it had letters. Marcus sold the unit for $300,000, a price close to the value at death, so his gain was close to zero because of the step-up in basis. The estate paid the $6,000 assessment at closing. A trust would have let him act weeks sooner.

## Will there be tax on a sale?

An heir generally takes a basis equal to the unit's value on the date of death. Selling soon after for about that figure usually produces little taxable gain. This is the same step-up described in [capital gains when you sell an inherited house](/learn/property-and-assets/capital-gains-when-you-sell-an-inherited-house-or-a-house-after-a-spouse-dies). Gifts made during life do not get the step-up.

Estate tax is the other question. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026. Illinois taxes estates over $4 million. New Jersey has an inheritance tax rather than an estate tax, and a spouse or child does not pay it.

## Should heirs keep or sell the unit?

Carrying cost decides it more often than sentiment. Add the fees, taxes, insurance and utilities, then compare them to what the unit would rent for, if rentals are allowed. Our [keep-or-sell worksheet for an inherited house](/learn/property-and-assets/should-you-keep-or-sell-an-inherited-house-a-carrying-cost-and-timing-worksheet) walks through the math. If several siblings inherit together, see [heirs property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) for the problems co-ownership can cause.

## How can an owner plan ahead?

Put the declaration, the latest budget and the manager's contact in one folder. Decide whether the unit should sit in a living trust. For a co-op, ask the managing agent in advance how the board treats shares passing to family. Name an executor who lives close enough to show up.
