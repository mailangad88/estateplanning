Ask an estate attorney what families actually fight about and the answer is rarely the bank accounts. It is the wedding ring, the piano, the cast iron skillet, a father's watch. Money can be divided on a spreadsheet. A single ring cannot, and nobody feels fairly treated when someone else simply takes it.

This page covers how to leave particular objects to particular people, what happens to everything you do not mention, and how to divide belongings when a loved one left no instructions. It belongs to our [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## How do I leave a specific item to a specific person?

Write it down in a signed document that the law treats as part of your estate plan. The most direct way is a specific gift in your will: "I give my mother's engagement ring to my niece, Claire." The will is a document the court will follow, so a clear sentence works.

Clarity is the whole job. "My jewelry to my daughter" sounds simple until you own three boxes of it, including pieces your sister expects. Name the item, say who gets it, and say what happens if that person dies first. Without a backup, the gift can fail and the item falls into the residue, the leftover pile described in our glossary entry on the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate).

| Wording | Problem | Better wording |
|---|---|---|
| "My good jewelry to Anna" | Which pieces are good? | "The pearl necklace and the gold watch in my bedroom dresser, to Anna". |
| "The piano to my son" | What if he cannot take it? | "The Baldwin upright piano to Marcus, or if he declines, to his sister Teresa". |
| "My art to my children" | Equal shares of unequal paintings | "The two oil paintings in the hallway to Teresa; the rest of my art to be divided by the process in this paragraph". |
| "Dad's tools to the family" | No one is responsible | "My father's workbench tools to Marcus, with the right to share them with his siblings". |

## Should I put the list in my will or in a separate memorandum?

A separate memorandum is easier to update, but only if your state gives it legal effect. Many states allow a will to refer to a signed, dated written list of tangible personal property, such as furniture, jewelry, art, vehicles and household goods, and treat the list as binding even though it was written after the will. Some states set limits on how much the list can cover or require that it exist when the will is signed. Other states do not honor it at all, in which case the list is only a request.

That uncertainty is the reason this question deserves a local answer. If you want flexibility, ask the attorney who drafts your will to include the right reference and confirm your state's rule. If you live in a state that does not honor lists, the practical options are a codicil each time something changes or a revocable trust, where the trust document can be amended more easily.

Whatever you choose, a memorandum should include:

1. The date and your signature.
2. A description of each item specific enough to identify it.
3. The name of the person who receives it, with a contingent name if you wish.
4. A note on where the item is kept.

Keep the memorandum with your will, tell your executor where it is, and do not post a copy on a refrigerator for relatives to read and debate. Our guide to the [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) is the right place for the softer, non-binding notes: why you chose each person and how you hope they will use the object.

## What happens to items I do not name?

They pass under the residuary clause of your will, which usually means they are shared among the same people who receive the rest of your estate. If you have no will, state intestacy rules decide, and personal belongings are treated like any other property. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) for how that works.

Take Ruth, 81, who has four children and a house full of things. Her will says "everything to my four children equally" and names no one for the china, which three children want and one does not. Ruth meant to talk it over and never did. Her executor, who is one of the children, now has to inventory the house, and each child suspects the others of taking a favorite thing. A single page of instructions would have cost Ruth an afternoon.

## How do I divide belongings fairly when there are no instructions?

Use a process that every heir can see is neutral. The executor, who controls the belongings until the estate closes, should make this part of the job and not leave it to whoever lives closest. See what [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) cover.

1. **Secure the house and take an inventory.** Photograph each room. Ask nobody to remove anything yet.
2. **Set aside the valuable items and get them appraised.** Jewelry, art, firearms, coins and collectibles can be worth more than they look.
3. **Let each heir list the items they would want most.** Often the wish lists overlap less than people fear.
4. **Resolve overlaps by turns.** Draw numbers, then choose in rounds, reversing the order each round. Some families use sticky notes in a color for each person.
5. **Handle disputes by value.** If two heirs want the same item, one can take it and the other can receive an equal credit in cash or in another item.
6. **Sell or donate the rest.** Take photographs and keep a record, so the executor can show the accounting if anyone asks.

Do not let an heir carry items out in the first week "for safekeeping." It looks like taking, even when it is not, and it is the move that most often turns grief into a lawsuit. If the estate is still in probate, items belong to the estate until the executor distributes them.

## Are some items worth more than they look?

Yes, and it is worth checking before you assign them. Fine jewelry, antiques, signed art, vintage instruments, watches and coin or stamp collections can have substantial value that the family does not recognize. A guitar that looks worn may be a collectible. A diamond ring may be worth far less than its sentimental weight, which is also useful to know.

For an heir who inherits an item, the tax rule is generous. Most inherited property receives a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) to its value at the date of death, so an heir who sells an inherited painting usually owes tax only on appreciation since then. Collectibles are an exception to the favorable long-term rates in some situations, so an heir who plans to sell a large collection should speak to a tax adviser.

## How do I handle fairness when the items are unequal in value?

Decide what fairness means in your family and say so. Equal value is one answer. Another is that the child who has been caring for you gets first choice, which is fine when it is written down and explained. A third is to leave the more valuable objects to one child and balance it with a larger share of cash for the others. Our page on [unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) covers how to document this so it holds.

Two practical ideas help. First, give heirs a chance to speak up while you are alive. Many parents find that the child they assumed wanted the grandfather clock does not, and the one who does has never said so. Second, record the story. A short video or a note taped to the back of a photograph that says where a piece came from will matter to the person who receives it.

## What about items I want to go to someone outside the family?

Name them. Friends, godchildren and caregivers are not heirs by default, and a verbal promise to give a friend your late husband's watch has no force. A specific gift in your will or in a properly drafted memorandum does. If you have a [partner you are not married to](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples), written instructions matter even more, because the law may favor relatives over them.

For pets, vehicles and firearms, separate rules apply. See [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) and [firearms in estate planning](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning).
