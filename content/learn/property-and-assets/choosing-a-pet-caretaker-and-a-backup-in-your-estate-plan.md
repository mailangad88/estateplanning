Tomas, 62, lives alone with an eleven-year-old retriever named Biscuit. His will leaves everything to his sister, who lives two states away, is allergic to dogs and has not been told. If Tomas collapsed tonight, Biscuit would spend the weekend in the apartment with no one who knew to come.

Most people with a pet have a rough idea of who would take it, and no paper that says so. This article is about the person, not the money: how to choose a caretaker, how to ask, what to do if the answer is no, and how to cover the stretch between your death and the day the will does its work. If you want to set up a dedicated fund, our [pet trust guide](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) covers that, and the wider [home, real estate and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets) places pets among your other property.

## The short version

- Legally, a pet is property. It goes where your will or trust sends it.
- Pick a caretaker who wants the pet, not one who feels obliged.
- Name a backup, and a third choice if you can.
- Plan the first two weeks separately from the long term.
- Leave care instructions in writing, and enough money to make the job realistic.

## Who gets my pet if I die with no plan?

Your heirs do. When there is no will, state intestacy law divides your property, pets included, among relatives in a set order, usually starting with a spouse and children. Nobody is required to take the animal. An heir who does not want it can hand it to a friend or surrender it to a shelter, and the other heirs have no legal say.

With a will that does not mention the pet, it falls into the residuary estate, which is whatever remains after specific gifts. The person who receives the residue owns the pet and can do as they like. That is how an animal that spent twelve years on a couch ends up in a county shelter. The mechanism is the same one described in our page on [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts).

## How do you pick a caretaker?

Choose someone who already knows your animal and wants the job. Willingness matters more than affection for you. Think through the practical side before you think about the family tree.

- **Does the animal know them?** An older cat that has met a person a dozen times copes better.
- **Can they take on the daily work?** A young dog needs hours of attention. A parrot may live fifty years.
- **Is their home suitable?** Check rental pet rules, other animals, children, allergies and space.
- **Do they have the money?** Even a generous gift may not cover a surgery. Plan for who bears the overflow.
- **Will they still be around?** A friend your own age may not outlive a young pet.

The obvious choice, a child, is not always the right one. A grown daughter with two toddlers and a small apartment may say yes out of love and regret it in a month. A neighbor who walks the dog every day may be better.

## How do you ask, and what do you say?

Ask in person, and give the person an easy way to say no. A conversation like this works:

> "I have written you into my will as the person I would like to take Biscuit. Before I leave it that way, I want to know if you would be willing, and I want you to feel free to say no. If yes, here is what I plan to leave to cover his care. If no, I will find someone else."

Then write it down. Note the date, the name and the answer. Do the same with the backup. Someone who has been asked and has said yes is far more likely to follow through than someone who learns about it from an executor.

## What if the person says no later, or never answers?

A will gift can be refused, and an unsuitable owner is a worse outcome than a different one. This is why a backup matters. A workable will lists a first choice and a second choice. It also gives the executor a final instruction, such as this one. "If neither can take my cat, my executor shall place her with a qualified rescue. Up to this amount may be paid from the estate."

Rescue groups, breed clubs and some sanctuaries accept animals from estates, often with an advance agreement and a donation. Call before you name one. A group that has never heard of you may not take the animal, and a will cannot oblige it to.

## Who looks after the pet in the first days?

The pet needs a person on day one, and the will cannot supply one. A will does not take effect in a way anyone can use until the court appoints the executor, which can take days or weeks. The pet is alone, or with whoever happens to be there.

An emergency plan covers that gap. It should work for a sudden death, a hospital stay and an incapacity, because each leaves the pet without you.

1. **Name an emergency contact in writing.** Put the name and phone number on a card in your wallet and on the inside of the front door.
2. **Give them a key.** And tell them where the leash, carrier, food and medications are.
3. **Tell your vet.** Ask the clinic to note your emergency contact on the file and to release records to them.
4. **Sign a financial power of attorney that allows it.** A [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) lets your agent spend your money on the animal if you are in the hospital.
5. **Give your executor a copy of the plan.** The executor will have the authority to pay boarding from the estate.

A short term arrangement does not have to be your long-term choice. The emergency person might hold the animal until the permanent caretaker arrives.

## How do you leave money for the pet's care?

You have three realistic options, and each gives the caretaker a different amount of obligation.

| Method | What it does | Enforceable? |
|---|---|---|
| Gift to the caretaker with a request | Outright cash gift, with a note that it is for the pet | No. The caretaker owns the money |
| Written caretaker agreement | A contract setting duties and payments, sometimes funded in installments | Yes, as a contract, but only by the other party |
| Pet trust | A trust holds the money and a trustee pays the caretaker | Yes. A named person can enforce it |

An outright gift is the simplest, and it works well when you trust the person completely. A trust costs more to set up and makes sense for a long-lived animal or a large sum. Illinois and New Jersey both allow pet trusts, described in [pet trusts in Illinois](/learn/illinois/pet-trusts-in-illinois) and [pet trusts in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/pet-trusts-in-new-jersey).

Think about the amount in terms of years and routine costs: food, annual exams, vaccines, medication, grooming and boarding, plus a cushion for emergencies. Ask your vet for a realistic yearly figure for the species and age, and multiply by a reasonable life expectancy. A gift far larger than that can look like a payment for something else, and relatives may challenge it.

## What should the care letter say?

Write one or two pages in plain words and attach them to your estate plan. This is a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction), and it is not legally binding, but it is the document the caretaker will use.

- Feeding schedule, brands and quantities, and foods to avoid.
- Medications, doses and how the animal takes them.
- Vet name, phone number, and a sentence about health history.
- Microchip number and the registry that holds it.
- Habits, fears, favorite walks and what calms the animal.
- A line on what you would like if the animal becomes very ill.

Update the microchip registry whenever the caretaker changes. A chip registered to a person who has died makes it harder to prove ownership at a clinic or shelter.

## What goes wrong most often?

- **Naming a caretaker who was never asked.** The surprise is a poor start.
- **No backup.** One refusal and the plan collapses.
- **Forgetting the first two weeks.** The pet is alone before the will is read.
- **Leaving the animal to several people.** Joint ownership of a living creature is a recipe for argument. Name one person.
- **Not updating.** A caretaker who moved or died, or a new pet, leaves the plan stale.
- **Assuming an old will covers a new pet.** Check whether the pet is described by name or by "my dogs" in general.
