Marcus's mother died in March, and the first thing in the mail was a coupon book from her mortgage servicer. The house was in her name alone. He is her only child, he has a job and a place of his own, and he cannot tell whether he now owes $140,000 to a bank he never heard of.

Most families in his position are less worried about the house than about the debt. This page explains who owes what, what the lender can and cannot demand, and how to decide whether to keep, sell or hand back a house with a loan on it. It is part of our [guide to real estate and special assets](/learn/property-and-assets).

## The short version

- The loan stays with the house. It is not erased at death and it is not automatically accelerated.
- Payments should keep going while the estate is sorted out. Missed payments are the fastest way to turn a manageable situation into a foreclosure.
- You inherit the house, not a personal obligation to the bank, unless you were a co-borrower or co-signer.
- You have options: keep the loan, refinance, sell, or walk away from an underwater home.
- Reverse mortgages are the exception and run on short clocks.

## Who is responsible for the mortgage after a death?

The estate is responsible first, and the house is responsible always. A mortgage is a debt of the person who signed the note, plus a lien on the property. When that person dies, the debt becomes a claim against the estate, and the lien stays on the house regardless. The lender is a secured creditor, which puts it ahead of credit card companies and most other claims, as described in [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate).

Heirs are not personally liable merely because they inherit. If you never signed the note, the lender's remedy is limited to the house. If you did sign, because you co-borrowed or co-signed, you owe the debt in your own right, and the lender can pursue you whether or not the house is worth enough.

A co-borrower spouse is in a different position. Spouses who signed together continue to owe, and many lenders will update the account to the survivor's name without a new application.

## Can the lender demand the whole loan at once?

Usually not. Most mortgages contain a due-on-sale clause, which lets the lender demand full payment if the property is transferred. A federal law, the Garn-St Germain Act, carves out transfers that families commonly need. The rule generally protects owner-occupied homes of one to four units when the property passes to a relative at the borrower's death, passes to a spouse or children, or goes into the borrower's own living trust while the borrower still lives there.

There are limits. The protection is aimed at residential property the borrower lived in, so a rental or second home may not qualify. A lender that has not been told of the death may also not know whom to deal with. And protection from acceleration does not mean the loan is free: you still have to keep it current.

Federal servicing rules add a second layer. A person who inherits the home and can show an ownership interest, called a successor in interest, can generally require the servicer to confirm them, give them account information, and consider them for the same help a borrower would get, including a loan modification. Many servicers will let a confirmed heir assume the existing loan and its interest rate without qualifying again. With rates higher than they were a few years ago, an old low-rate loan can be worth keeping.

## What should you do in the first 30 days?

Take these steps in order. None of them requires you to decide what to do with the house yet.

1. **Find the loan.** Look for statements, the coupon book, or a line on the property tax or credit report. If you cannot find it, a title search at the county recorder will show any recorded mortgage or deed of trust.
2. **Call the servicer, not the original lender.** The servicer collects payments. Ask for the successor-in-interest or deceased-borrower department.
3. **Send a death certificate and proof of authority.** That means letters from the court if there is an executor, or proof that you are the heir. See [letters testamentary](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration).
4. **Ask five questions in writing:** the payoff amount, whether the loan has an escrow account, whether assumption is available, what loss-mitigation options exist, and whether it is a reverse mortgage.
5. **Keep paying.** If the estate has cash, use it. If not, an heir who wants the house may advance the payments and keep a record to be reimbursed later.
6. **Insure the house.** The lender will force-place expensive coverage if the owner's policy lapses. Our page on [vacant inherited houses](/learn/property-and-assets/vacant-inherited-house-insurance-repairs-squatters) covers the insurance trap.

## What if the house is worth less than the mortgage?

An underwater house is a problem for the house, not for you personally. Say your father owed $310,000 and the house would sell for $260,000. The $50,000 gap is an unsecured claim against his estate, and if the estate has nothing else, the lender simply takes the loss.

You have a few choices.

| Option | How it works | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Keep and pay | Assume or modify the loan and carry on | You want the home and can afford the payments |
| Short sale | Sell for less than the balance with lender approval | A buyer exists but the price is below the loan |
| Deed in lieu | Hand the deed to the lender to close the debt | No buyer, and the lender agrees to accept it |
| Foreclosure | The lender takes the house | Last resort; check for credit impact to the estate |
| Wait and see | Pay while values recover | Only if you can carry the cost |

Do not guess at the value. Get a broker's opinion or an appraisal before choosing. If the estate also has a lien from a judgment or unpaid taxes, those rank in a set order, which a probate attorney can help sort out.

## What happens to a reverse mortgage?

A reverse mortgage generally becomes due after the last borrower dies, and the clock is short. The lender sends a due-and-payable notice. For the common federally insured type (a home equity conversion mortgage), heirs typically have about 30 days to respond with a plan and then up to six months to sell, refinance or pay, with extensions available in some cases, but the details come from the loan documents and the servicer. These loans are generally non-recourse, which means heirs who want to keep the home can usually pay the lesser of the balance or a percentage of the appraised value, and no one is chased for any shortfall.

An heir who lives in the house is in a tight spot because the deadline does not wait for probate. Ask the servicer for the deadlines and the extension rules in writing the same week you get the notice.

## What if there are several heirs and one wants to stay?

The heir who stays usually needs to either assume the loan, refinance it or buy out the others. Suppose three siblings inherit a house with a $120,000 loan and a market value of $400,000. The sibling who wants the house must come up with the other two shares, $93,333 each after the loan, through a refinance, savings or a payment plan agreed in writing. Our articles on [siblings who inherit a house](/learn/what-if/siblings-inherit-house-and-disagree) and [heirs' property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) explain what happens when they cannot agree.

## How can you plan so your family is not stuck?

A few simple choices make this easier for your heirs.

- **Tell someone where the loan is.** A one-page list of accounts, servicers and loan numbers belongs in your [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction).
- **Put the house in a living trust, or use a transfer-on-death deed.** The successor then acts without waiting for a court. A lender generally cannot accelerate for a transfer into your own revocable trust while you live there. See [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).
- **Leave cash or insurance to carry the payments.** A modest sum available for six months of payments buys time to decide.
- **Name the person who gets the house and the person who pays for it.** Many families leave a house to one child and money to another, and forget that the loan needs to be paid out of someone's share.
- **Do not add a child to the deed to dodge the loan.** It rarely helps and can cost the child a tax break. See [adding a child to your deed](/learn/probate/adding-a-child-to-your-deed).

## How state law changes this

States differ on how foreclosure works, how long an estate has to deal with creditors, and whether a surviving spouse has homestead or family-allowance rights that sit ahead of the lender's unsecured claims. A few states favor court-supervised foreclosure and others allow a trustee's sale. If you are in a state with a particular rule, check the guide for your state, such as [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) or [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), and ask the servicer to confirm anything that sounds unusual.
