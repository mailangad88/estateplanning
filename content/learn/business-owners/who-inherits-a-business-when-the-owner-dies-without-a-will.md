You started the company, you know where everything is, and you have never gotten around to a will. If you die tomorrow, who owns what you built? The honest answer is that the state decides, using a formula written for families in general rather than for your business in particular.

This page explains how that formula treats an LLC, a corporation and a sole proprietorship in Illinois and New Jersey, what the family faces in the first weeks, and which documents can change the result. It belongs to our [business owner planning series](/learn/business-owners).

## Who inherits a business when there is no will?

The business interest goes to your legal heirs in the order the intestacy statute lists. A surviving spouse and descendants come first. If you have neither, parents, siblings and more distant relatives follow. The business is not treated differently from a bank account or a car.

In Illinois, a surviving spouse who is left with descendants takes one-half of the intestate estate and the descendants share the other half. A spouse with no descendants takes everything. In New Jersey, the spouse's share depends in part on whether all of your children are also the spouse's. Our guides to [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) and [in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) give the exact order for each state.

Notice what the statute does not ask. It does not ask who has worked in the business for twenty years, who has the skills, or who you would have picked. A daughter who runs the shop and a son who lives abroad get the same share.

### Example: the landscaping company

Raymond, 61, owned 100 percent of a landscaping LLC in suburban Chicago. He was a widower with two adult children. Dana worked beside him for fifteen years. Marcus is a nurse in Phoenix. Raymond left no will. Under Illinois intestacy the two children each inherit half of the LLC interest. Dana now co-owns her father's company with a brother who has no interest in landscaping and wants cash. Nothing in the statute helps her. She needs her brother to agree to a buyout, and the company needs to keep bidding jobs while they talk.

## What happens to the legal authority over the business first?

Nobody has it until the court acts. A will names an executor. With no will, a relative petitions the probate court to be appointed administrator, receives letters of administration, and only then can sign for the estate. The [letters of administration article](/learn/probate/letters-testamentary-and-letters-of-administration) walks through what the court looks for.

The practical effect depends on how the business is set up.

| Business form | What passes by intestacy | What stalls in the meantime |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Each business asset: equipment, receivables, vehicles, inventory | Bank account in the owner's name, licenses, permits, contracts |
| Single-member LLC | The membership interest | No manager; the LLC bank account may be frozen if the owner was the only signer |
| Multi-member LLC | The deceased member's interest, subject to the operating agreement | Votes and distributions, depending on the agreement |
| Corporation | The shares | Shareholder votes; a sole director or president may leave no one able to sign |
| Partnership | The partner's interest, subject to the partnership agreement | Often dissolves or triggers a buyout clause |

Our article on [what happens to an LLC or corporation when you die](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) goes through the entity side in detail, and the [sole proprietorship article](/learn/business-owners/what-happens-to-a-sole-proprietorship-at-death) covers the owner who never formed an entity at all.

## What can override intestacy for a business?

Several documents decide who gets the business before a will or the statute is ever consulted. This is the part most owners miss.

1. **A buy-sell agreement.** If you and your co-owners signed one, the survivors may have a right or an obligation to buy your share at a set price. The heirs get cash instead of an interest. See [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).
2. **The operating agreement or shareholder agreement.** Many limit who can become a member, or say that an heir receives only a share of profits and not a vote. Our article on whether an [operating agreement overrides your will](/learn/business-owners/does-an-operating-agreement-override-your-will) explains the conflict.
3. **A funded living trust.** If you assigned the membership interest to your trust, it never enters probate and the successor trustee can act at once.
4. **Joint ownership with survivorship.** If you hold a partnership or a piece of real estate jointly with the right of survivorship, it passes to the other owner automatically.
5. **Beneficiary designations.** Life insurance, retirement plans and business-owned policies pay the named person no matter what the statute says.

If none of these exist, intestacy fills the gap. That is the situation this page describes.

## What problems do families run into most?

Three problems repeat. They are all avoidable, and none of them is about the statute itself.

**The business cannot be paid for while it is frozen.** Payroll, rent, sales tax and supplier bills are due on schedule. If the owner was the only signer on the account, the family cannot lawfully draw on it until an administrator has letters. A few businesses manage with a second signer. Most do not.

**Heirs end up as unwilling partners.** Equal fractions mean a spouse, a working child and a non-working child must agree on salary, reinvestment and whether to sell. Siblings who get along at holidays often cannot make those decisions together. When the heirs cannot agree, the administrator, and eventually a judge, may be involved.

**Personal liability follows the paperwork.** Many small business owners signed personal guaranties on a lease, a loan or a line of credit. The creditor can file a claim against the estate whether or not anyone keeps the business running. The article on [business debts and guaranties at death](/learn/business-owners/personal-guaranties-and-business-debts-at-death) lays out which debts survive.

## What should the family do in the first weeks?

If you are the relative left holding this, the order below keeps the damage small.

1. **Find the documents.** Look for the operating agreement, bylaws, buy-sell agreement, trust, insurance policies and the company's formation papers. They may change who has authority.
2. **Check who else can sign.** A spouse, partner or bookkeeper with existing authority on the business bank account can often keep paying essential bills.
3. **Call the lawyer before the court.** A probate attorney can file the petition for administrator and ask the court for any limited powers needed to keep the company operating.
4. **Tell the insurer and the lender.** Business insurance, commercial leases and loans often have notice duties after an owner dies.
5. **Protect the customer relationships.** A short, honest message to key clients keeps revenue coming in while the court works.
6. **Decide on keep, sell or close.** This comes after the heirs know what they own. Rushed sales at a discount are the usual regret.

Our checklist for the [first 30 days after a business owner dies](/learn/business-owners/first-30-days-after-a-business-owner-dies) expands on these.

## How would a will or trust change the result?

A will lets you name an executor who already knows the business and leave the interest to the child who will run it, with cash or insurance for the others. A trust lets you skip probate and name a successor who can act on day one. For a company that has real employees and customers, we usually recommend a trust and a buy-sell or succession clause in the company documents rather than a will alone. A will still needs probate, and the business waits during that time.

The decision between the two depends on your size, your co-owners and your family.
