You built a business, and along the way you bought the building. Now there are two assets in one pile: a company that earns money and a property that holds value. The question for your estate plan is whether they should stay in one entity or be separated.

This page gives a plain answer to "should business real estate be in a separate LLC," shows the tradeoffs with a worked example, and covers what changes if the business or the building is in more than one state. It belongs to our guide on [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## Why do owners separate the building from the business?

Three reasons come up again and again. The first is liability. If a customer is injured or the company is sued, a claim against the operating company can reach the building when the building sits inside it. Put it in its own LLC and the building is a separate target. That protection is imperfect, since a lender or a landlord who signed a personal guaranty can still come after you, but it narrows the exposure.

The second reason is the family. A company needs someone who can run it. A building needs only an owner who collects rent. If you have three children and one runs the shop, you can leave the operating company to that child and spread ownership of the building among all three. We see this structure work well when it is documented and fail when it is handshake-only.

The third is the exit. When you sell the business, most buyers want the operations and a lease, not a building they must finance. Keeping the building lets you convert a business sale into a rent stream.

## What does it look like in numbers?

This is an invented example to show the logic, not a prediction.

Priya, 62, owns a plumbing supply company in Joliet with the building in the company's name. The company is worth about $1.2 million, the building about $1.4 million with a $400,000 mortgage. Her son Devan runs the shop. Her daughter Mira is a nurse in New Jersey and has no interest in the business.

| | Everything in one company | Building in its own LLC |
|---|---|---|
| At Priya's death | Devan and Mira would share ownership of a company one of them runs | Devan gets the operating company; Mira and Devan share the building LLC |
| Rent | None | Company pays market rent to the building LLC, perhaps a monthly figure the family sets in writing |
| Mira's income | Wait for a sale or a dividend | Her share of the rent, after the mortgage |
| Selling the business later | Building goes with it | Building stays; buyer signs a lease |
| Extra costs | None | A second entity, a second tax return, lease drafting |

Devan keeps control of the thing he works in. Mira gets a real asset that pays. Neither has to buy the other out on the day of the funeral.

## How do you move the building into an LLC?

Treat it as a project, not a form.

1. **Read the mortgage.** Look for the clause that lets the lender call the loan when title changes. Ask for written consent before the deed.
2. **Form the LLC** in the state where the building sits, or in your own state and register it there. Write an operating agreement that names a manager and a successor manager.
3. **Call your title insurer** and ask whether the policy continues after the transfer. Many insurers will endorse it for a transfer into a wholly owned LLC.
4. **Check transfer taxes and property tax rules.** Some states and counties exempt transfers to your own entity, and some do not. Do not assume.
5. **Record the deed** and notify the insurance company. Add the LLC as the named insured.
6. **Sign the lease** between the LLC and the operating company.
7. **Put the LLC interest in your plan.** The membership interest is the asset your will or trust now controls. See [does an operating agreement override your will](/learn/business-owners/does-an-operating-agreement-override-your-will) for how those documents interact.

### What are the common mistakes?

Skipping the lease is first. Second is paying no rent, or paying rent far from market, which blurs the separation and makes a liability shield weaker. Third is putting the building in the LLC but leaving the personal loan promise with the old company with no thought to who inherits the liability. Fourth is forgetting that an LLC interest left to three children needs a manager. Without an operating agreement that says who decides, the building is stuck in the same disagreement you were trying to avoid. If the building might also sit in a trust instead, a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is the other common holder, and the choice turns on liability and who will manage it.

## What if the business or the building is in more than one state?

The state where real estate sits controls how it transfers. A building in Indiana owned personally by an Illinois resident usually needs its own court case in Indiana, called [ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate), even if the rest of the estate is handled in Illinois. The same is true for a New Jersey resident with a warehouse in Pennsylvania.

An LLC changes the picture. The owner of the building is now the LLC, and your interest in the LLC is personal property, generally handled where you lived. That means one probate case, or none if the interest is in your living trust. Our article on [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) goes through this in more detail.

If the operating company does business in several states, a few other points apply. It may be registered as a foreign entity in each one and owe state filings that continue after your death. A manager or registered agent in each place should not be someone who will disappear when you do. Licenses are often personal, and a regulated trade may need a qualifying individual in each state.

Tax-wise, Illinois taxes estates over $4 million, and New Jersey has an inheritance tax but no estate tax, so a family with property in both should look at both. Real estate in one state can count toward the other state's threshold calculation. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026.

## Does a separate LLC make estate planning simpler or harder?

Both. It splits one decision into two, and that is usually what a family needs, but it also creates a second set of documents to keep alive. The building LLC needs its own annual report, bank account, insurance and tax return. If those lapse after you are gone, the protection you built can lapse with them.

Our rule of thumb: if the building is a small fraction of your wealth and only one heir is involved, a single entity is fine. If the building is worth more than the business or more than one family member will share it, separate them. See [passing a family business to children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children) for the hand-off side, and [landlord estate planning](/learn/business-owners/landlord-estate-plan-rental-properties-llc-or-trust) if the building has outside tenants.

## What should you ask your lender and insurer first?

Two phone calls prevent most of the unpleasant surprises. Ask the lender whether it will consent in writing to a transfer to your LLC, and whether it will keep you as guarantor or release you if the loan is paid down. Ask the insurance agent to write the building policy in the LLC's name with the operating company as a tenant, and to confirm that liability limits are adequate for a landlord. If a policy names the wrong owner, a claim can be contested at the worst moment.

Also ask your accountant how the rent will be reported. Rent from your own company to your own LLC is real income to the LLC, and the operating company deducts it. The numbers should be consistent with the lease from the first month.
