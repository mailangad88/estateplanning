Most business owners have thought about what happens if they sell. Fewer have thought about the quieter scenario: a stroke at 54, a back injury that keeps you off the job site for a year, or a car accident. The business is still there. The bills are still due. Nobody has the legal right to sign a check.

This page lists what an owner should have in place and why, then gives a short plan for getting it done. It belongs to our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners), and it focuses on the basics that apply whether you are a sole proprietor, a freelancer or an owner of a small company with employees.

## Does a business owner need a will?

Yes, and for a business owner it does more work than for most people. A will names the person who inherits your business interest and the executor who deals with the company while the estate is open. Without one, state law decides, and a court picks who is in charge.

What happens to the business without a will depends on how it is organized. A sole proprietorship has no separate existence, so its assets are yours and go through probate. An LLC or corporation continues, but your ownership interest passes under intestacy, often to a spouse and children together. Those heirs may not agree on what to do. Our articles on [sole proprietorships at death](/learn/business-owners/what-happens-to-a-sole-proprietorship-at-death) and [LLC and corporation shares at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) go through each form.

Think about the details beyond "everything to my spouse."

- Who may run the business while the estate is open?
- Can the executor keep operating, or only sell?
- Does the operating agreement allow your heirs to take your interest?
- Is a trust a better home for the interest than a will? See [putting your business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust).

## Does a business owner need a power of attorney?

Yes, and arguably before the will. A will works only after death. A durable financial power of attorney works when you are alive but cannot act. It lets your agent sign contracts, access accounts, pay payroll and deal with the bank.

Without it, your family may need to ask a court for a guardianship or conservatorship to run your affairs. That takes time, costs money, and puts your finances on a public record. During those weeks or months, no one can pay suppliers. Our guide to [business owner incapacity planning](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) covers the operational side.

Some practical points:

- **Pick a person who can read a profit and loss statement.** Loyalty alone is not enough.
- **Name the business expressly.** Many banks and counterparties want to see powers that refer to business interests, not only a general grant.
- **Consider a separate business agent.** A spouse might handle household finances while a trusted partner or manager handles the company.
- **Decide on timing.** An immediate power starts when you sign, while a springing power starts on incapacity. See our comparison of [springing and immediate powers](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney).
- **Tell the bank.** Many banks will accept a power of attorney more readily if you present it in advance.

## Does a business owner need disability insurance?

For most owners who depend on their own labor, yes. Income, not net worth, is how many owners fund their lives, and a disability can stop it. Social Security disability benefits have strict rules and a long wait, and they are not designed to replace a business income.

There are three common policy types, and they do different things.

| Policy | What it pays | Who it protects | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual disability income | A share of your income while you cannot work | You and your family | Compare the definition of disability and the waiting period |
| Business overhead expense | Rent, utilities, payroll, loan payments | The business | Pays for a limited time, after a waiting period |
| Disability buy-out coverage | Funds a partner's purchase of a disabled owner's share | Partners and the disabled owner | Works with the buy-sell agreement |

The wording matters more than the price. Look for how the policy defines disability: whether it means unable to do your own occupation, or any occupation. Check the waiting period before benefits begin, and whether the benefit is taxable, which often depends on who paid the premiums.

If there are co-owners, the buy-sell agreement should say what happens when one of them is disabled for a long time, including how long the wait is before a buy-out starts. The [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) guide explains the triggers. If you have a person the business cannot replace, [key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance) can cover the company's losses.

## What about life insurance?

Life insurance fits two jobs: funding a buy-out, and replacing your income for your family. It also pays estate costs when most of your wealth is tied up in the company. Which job matters decides who should own the policy and who the beneficiary is. If you are unsure, see our guide on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

## Does a single-member LLC need a succession plan?

It needs one more than most. A multi-owner company has someone else who can sign. A single-member LLC has only you. The LLC does not die with you, but nobody has authority to act for it until someone is appointed. In a bank's eyes, you are the company.

Fix that in three steps.

1. **Name a successor manager in the operating agreement.** The agreement should say who takes over on your death or incapacity.
2. **Say who becomes the member.** Decide whether the interest goes to a spouse, a child or a trust, and make sure the agreement permits a transfer.
3. **Give the manager access.** Add them as a signer on business accounts while you are healthy.

An LLC with no written operating agreement is a common surprise. State default rules apply, and they may not match what you want. Put the ownership on paper.

## What does your business need to give someone to find?

Documents are only half of it. The other half is access. A person with legal authority but no passwords is stuck.

Prepare a single written file that includes:

- bank, payroll, tax and merchant accounts, with names of the people allowed to use them;
- the lease, loan agreements, insurance policies and licenses, with renewal dates.
- customer and vendor lists, and where they are stored.
- software, domain, website and social media logins, in a secure password manager.
- who your accountant, attorney, insurance agent and banker are.
- what to do first, second and third.

Customer data deserves special care. If you hold clients' personal or financial information, your successor needs to know how to protect it, and may have legal duties to do so. The article on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) explains how to store credentials without handing them over today. A [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) is a good container.

## Should you hire a business attorney or an estate planning attorney?

Often both, or one who covers both. A business attorney drafts the operating agreement, shareholder agreement and contracts. An estate planning attorney drafts the will, trust and powers of attorney. The two sets of documents must agree. If your operating agreement says only members can inherit and your will leaves the interest to your son who is not a member, the will may not work. Whoever drafts first should review the other set.

## A twelve-month plan

1. **This month:** sign a power of attorney and a healthcare directive. Add a trusted signer to business accounts.
2. **Next 90 days:** get quotes on disability income and overhead coverage. Gather the access file.
3. **Within six months:** update the operating agreement with a successor manager. Sign or revise your will or trust.
4. **Within twelve months:** review the buy-sell, insurance and plan again. Put a reminder on the calendar for next year.
