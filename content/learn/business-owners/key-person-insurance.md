Some businesses depend on one or two people more than anyone admits. If the founder who holds the client relationships dies, or the engineer who wrote the software leaves a hospital in a wheelchair, revenue drops fast. Lenders get nervous. Customers ask who they should call. And the bills still arrive on the first of the month.

Key person insurance exists for that moment. It gives the company cash while it adjusts. This page explains what it is, how to size it, how it differs from the insurance you use for a buyout, and where it fits in the larger [estate plan for a business owner](/learn/business-owners).

## What is key person insurance?

It is a policy the business buys on the life, and sometimes the health, of a person it relies on. The company is the owner and the beneficiary. It pays the premiums. When the person dies, the company receives the death benefit.

You will also hear it called key man insurance or key employee insurance. It is not a benefit for the employee. The employee's own family gets nothing from it, which is why owners should pair it with personal life insurance. If you want the family covered, read our guide to [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

What the company does with the money is up to it. Typical uses:

- Replace profit lost while customers and staff adjust.
- Recruit and pay a replacement, and cover training time.
- Repay a loan or line of credit that a lender might call.
- Keep payroll going for a few months during a sale or reorganization.
- Reassure vendors and lenders that the business can absorb the loss.

## Who counts as a key person?

Ask a plain question about each person: if they vanished tomorrow, what would it cost us over the next 12 months?

For a small company the owner is often the key person. In a professional practice it may be the rainmaker. In a manufacturer it may be the plant manager who knows why the machine only works at certain settings. In a start-up it may be two founders and a technical lead.

A short test:

1. Does this person bring in a large share of revenue or hold the main client relationships?
2. Do they hold a license, a skill or knowledge that is hard to replace?
3. Would a lender or investor react badly to their loss?
4. Would it take more than six months to hire and train an equal?

A yes to two or more suggests coverage is worth pricing.

## How much coverage do you need?

Avoid round numbers. There are three common ways to estimate, and owners often compare them.

| Method | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Lost profit | Estimate the profit that would disappear for the number of months it takes to recover, then add recruiting costs | Sales-driven firms |
| Replacement cost | Total cost of finding, hiring and training a successor, plus higher pay if the replacement is more expensive | Technical or licensed roles |
| Debt and obligations | Loans and leases you signed for personally, tied to the person that a lender could demand repaid | Companies with bank debt |

Take Lena, 47, who owns a four-person bookkeeping firm that earns about $300,000 a year before her own pay. She estimates it would take 18 months to retain clients and hire a replacement, and the firm owes $80,000 on a line of credit that Lena signed for. She buys coverage in the range of her lost profit plus the debt, and writes her calculation down so she can update it each year. The figure came from arithmetic, not from what felt comfortable.

Update the number when revenue, debt or the role changes. A policy bought for a company of ten employees can be too small for a company of fifty.

## How is it different from buy-sell insurance?

Both use life insurance, and owners often mix them up.

- **Key person insurance** protects the company's operations. The company is the beneficiary and keeps the money.
- **Buy-sell insurance** pays for an owner's share. Under a cross-purchase plan, the other owners get the proceeds and pay them to the estate. Under a redemption plan, the company gets them and pays the estate. See our article on [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).

A company with partners may need both: one to keep the lights on and another to pay the family fairly. If only one policy exists, ask which job it was meant to do. A redemption policy used as the company's cash cushion cannot also be the money that buys out a widow.

Neither replaces personal life insurance, which the owner's family owns and which should be sized to the household's needs. Our [life insurance needs calculator](/tools/life-insurance-needs) is a starting point.

## What do the tax rules look like?

Keep the numbers general and have an accountant confirm them.

- **Premiums.** Generally not deductible when the business is the beneficiary.
- **Death benefit.** Generally received by the company free of federal income tax, but federal law sets conditions for employer-owned policies. They include written notice to the employee, written consent and reporting on the company's return. Skip those steps and part of the benefit can become taxable.
- **Estate tax.** If the insured person is also an owner of the company, the proceeds can increase the value of the company for estate tax purposes. That matters mainly for estates near or above the federal exclusion of $15,000,000 per person in 2026, or in states with lower thresholds. The way a buyout obligation counts against company value is technical, and the Supreme Court addressed one version of it in 2024, so planning around a redemption funded by a company-owned policy deserves current advice. Our article on [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) explains the valuation side.
- **Personal ownership.** Some owners keep the policy in an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) to keep proceeds out of the estate. That is a different tool with a different purpose, used for the family and not for the company's cash.

## How do you set up a policy?

1. **Name the person and the risk.** Death only, or death and disability.
2. **Estimate the amount** using one of the methods above, and keep the worksheet.
3. **Get the person's written consent** before the company applies for the policy. The insured must agree and will need a medical exam in most cases.
4. **Have the company apply and own the policy.** The company is both owner and beneficiary. Record this in the minutes.
5. **Pay from company funds.** Put the premium in the annual budget and keep a note of who is insured.
6. **Review every year.** At renewal, ask if the coverage still matches the risk, and whether the insured has left the company. Handle a departing employee's policy deliberately: cancel it, let it lapse or transfer it under a written agreement.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Naming the wrong beneficiary.** If a spouse or an old partner stays as the beneficiary, the company gets nothing.
- **Keeping a policy on someone who left.** That is a cost without a purpose, and consent rules can still apply.
- **Using the wrong coverage for an incapacity.** Life insurance pays at death. If the owner is ill for two years, you need disability or overhead coverage. Our article on [incapacity planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) covers that gap.
- **Not telling anyone the policy exists.** Your bookkeeper, successor and executor should know who insures whom and where the paperwork is.
- **Never checking the amount.** A policy bought five years ago for a smaller company may not cover this year's payroll.
