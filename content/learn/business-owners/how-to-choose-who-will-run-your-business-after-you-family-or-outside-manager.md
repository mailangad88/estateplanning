Owners often tell us the same thing: "I assume my daughter will take over." Then we ask whether she knows, whether she wants it, and whether the people who work for you would follow her. The room goes quiet. The question of who runs the business after you is a people decision before it is a legal one, and the legal documents should only record what you have already worked out.

This page, part of our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners), helps you pick a successor, deal with a child who says no, and talk to your family without starting a fight. For the full transfer mechanics, see [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).

## Who should run my business after I die?

The person who can keep customers, employees and the bank confident for the next few years. That may be a child, a long-time employee, a partner, or a hired manager. What matters is a clear match between the person and the work.

Start by listing what you personally do. Owners are often surprised how many roles they fill: signing checks, handling the biggest customer, negotiating with the landlord, holding the license, approving every quote. Then list who could take each role. A successor can be one person, or the job can be split.

## How do I choose between a family member and a non-family successor?

Compare candidates against the same scorecard. Here is the one our attorneys use in planning meetings.

| Question | Family member | Key employee | Outside manager |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do customers and staff already trust them? | Sometimes | Usually | No, takes time |
| Will they hold the license or certification the work needs? | Check | Check | Check |
| Do they want to own the company, not just run it? | Varies | Often wants equity | Rarely |
| How is the owner's family paid? | Through shares or wages | Through a buyout over years | Through ownership, with a salaried manager |
| Biggest risk | Resentment among siblings | Cannot afford to buy | Does not stay |

No row decides it alone. A son who loves the work but has never managed people is a different candidate from a daughter with an MBA who lives two time zones away. Be honest on paper.

Two patterns come up often. First, a family business where only one child works in it. That child expects to take over; the siblings expect a share of the value. The usual answer is to give the working child control and balance the others with insurance, other assets, or a note. See [passing a family business to your children, fairly](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children). Second, a business where nobody in the family is interested. There the better choice for the family may be a sale to a key employee, which is covered in [selling your business or passing it to family or employees](/learn/business-owners/selling-your-business-or-passing-it-to-family-or-employees).

## What if my child does not want the family business?

Treat that answer as useful information, not a failure. Ask the child directly and listen. Some say no because they fear the stress, some because they have a different career, and some because they believe a sibling should have it.

If the answer is no, you have three practical paths:

1. **Sell while you can.** You choose the buyer and the price, and you are alive to help the transition.
2. **Let the children own it, hire someone to run it.** Put a manager under contract and set what the children, as owners, may decide.
3. **Leave the business to the interested child and other assets to the rest.** Use life insurance or a promissory note from the child to equalize.

The one path to avoid is saying nothing and leaving the shares to everyone equally in a will. Equal ownership by people who disagree is a standing invitation to deadlock, which is why partnership-style fights show up in estates that were meant to be fair. A will leaving a business "equally to my four children" also forces the executor to find a way to run a company four people do not agree on.

## How do I appoint a successor manager for my LLC or corporation?

Appointing a successor takes three documents, and they need to agree.

1. **The operating agreement or bylaws** should say who becomes manager or officer when you die or become unable to act, and who is the backup.
2. **Your trust or will** should hold the ownership interest. A trust lets the successor trustee vote the interest the day you die, with no court order.
3. **The bank and key vendors** need the successor's name on signature cards, the lease and the license, or the appointment sits unused.

Without these, an executor may need a court appointment before touching your interest. A [living trust that owns the business](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust) avoids that delay. Also check whether your state requires a specific person to hold a license, as professional practices and some trades do.

### Should the manager and the trustee be different people?

Often yes. The trustee must be fair to all beneficiaries and follow the trust terms. The manager makes business decisions that can favor one person's view over another's. If one child is both, the others may feel their interests are unprotected, and they may be right. A common split is a sibling or friend as trustee, a working child as manager, with a clause that allows the trustee to replace the manager for cause. For help picking, read [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).

## Should I involve my children in the planning?

Yes, once you know your own mind. Planning in secret feels kind, but the information gap comes out at the worst moment. Ask the working child to join planning meetings. Tell the non-working children what the plan is, and why, without asking them to approve it.

A script that works: "Here is what I own, here is who I want to run the company, here is how the rest of you will be treated, and here is what happens if I am wrong." Then write it down. A short family meeting with an attorney present lets everyone ask questions while you are still able to answer them.

## How should I tell my family about my succession plan?

Pick a time, a place and an order. Our attorneys recommend this sequence:

1. Tell the chosen successor first, privately, and confirm they want the role.
2. Tell the others the same day, before they hear it elsewhere.
3. Share the numbers: what the business is worth, how the other children are being treated.
4. Put the plan in writing and give each child a copy of the parts that concern them.
5. Revisit it every year or after any major change, such as a divorce, a new partner or a big sale.

If you are remarried, the conversation also has to include your spouse's position. The same issues arise in [preventing inheritance fights in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/preventing-inheritance-fights-in-a-blended-family).

## What does a written legacy plan include?

A legacy plan for a business is just the written version of the above, kept with your estate documents. It should name the successor and backup, describe how ownership passes, state the price and funding for any buyout, and list the three or four people to call first. Add contact details for your accountant, insurance agent and attorney, and note where the entity documents are kept.

Here is an example. Raymond ran an HVAC company in Joliet and told his two sons that the older would manage and both would own half. After Raymond died, the younger son learned the older had never agreed to share decisions. A one-page manager agreement, signed ahead of time, would have settled what each could approve alone and what needed both signatures. A short document like that is cheap next to months of mediation.
