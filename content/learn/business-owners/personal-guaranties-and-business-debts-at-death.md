Most owners say they have limited liability. Then they remember the bank asked for a signature at the bottom of the loan, below the company's, and that the landlord asked for the same thing. Those signatures are the reason business debt becomes an estate problem.

This page walks through what survives the owner's death, how creditors are paid, and the steps that keep a family from inheriting a bill larger than the business. It sits within our [guide to estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## Does an LLC or corporation protect my estate from business debts?

Only up to the line of your signature. A properly run company owes its own debts. If the company cannot pay, a creditor generally cannot go after the owner's house or savings. That protection is the point of forming an LLC or corporation.

The protection ends in a few places. A personal guaranty makes you a co-debtor. Unpaid payroll taxes can reach responsible individuals. And if the company was run as an extension of the owner, with mixed accounts and no records, a court can sometimes disregard the entity. When you die, the debts you backed with a guaranty come into the estate as claims. The company's own debts do not, unless the estate owns a sole proprietorship, where there is no separation at all. See our page on a [sole proprietorship at death](/learn/business-owners/what-happens-to-a-sole-proprietorship-at-death).

## Which business obligations usually follow the owner?

| Obligation | Does it survive death? | Typical issue |
|---|---|---|
| Bank term loan or line of credit with a personal guaranty | Yes, as an estate claim if the company defaults | Loan documents may let the lender call the loan on death |
| SBA loan | Yes, the guaranty remains for owners who signed | Lender may have required life insurance |
| Commercial lease with a personal guaranty | Yes, for the guaranty period or amount | Landlord can sue for rent through the term, less re-letting |
| Equipment or vehicle financing | Yes, and the lender holds the collateral | Estate may surrender the equipment and owe a shortfall |
| Credit cards used for the business | Yes, if the card was in the owner's name | Joint users and authorized users have different exposure |
| Trade credit with vendors | Only if the owner signed a guaranty | A vendor can stop deliveries even without a claim |
| Payroll and sales taxes | Company owes them; certain individuals can be personally liable | Tax authorities have long deadlines |
| Customer contracts and warranties | Company obligations continue | Personal-service contracts may end at death |

## How does an estate pay these claims?

In an orderly way, and in a set order. The executor collects the assets, notifies known creditors, publishes notice where the state requires it, and pays valid claims in the order the law sets: administration costs and funeral expenses first, then taxes and secured debts, then general creditors. Heirs receive what remains. Our pages on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) and the [creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period) explain the process.

Secured lenders have an extra tool. If a loan is secured by equipment, receivables or real estate, the lender can generally repossess or foreclose without waiting for the estate to finish. The estate owes any shortfall afterward.

Two points surprise families.

- **Assets outside probate may still be reachable.** A revocable living trust does not shield assets from the grantor's creditors after death in most states. Our page on [living trusts and creditors](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-protect-assets-from-creditors) goes through the state variation.
- **The business may need to be sold to pay the guaranty.** If the only valuable asset is the company, the executor may have to sell it to satisfy the claim, whether or not the family wanted to keep it.

## What should I do about each type of guaranty?

Start with a list. Gather every loan, lease, credit line and equipment contract. For each, write down who signed, what the balance is, when it ends, and whether it has a death or change-of-control clause. Most owners are surprised by the length of the list.

Then work through the practical options.

1. **Ask for a release.** Once a loan has been paid down or the company has stable cash flow, lenders sometimes agree to drop or cap a guaranty. Landlords may accept a security deposit in place of one.
2. **Cover the guaranty with term life insurance.** Match the death benefit and term to the debt, and name the beneficiary who will pay it, whether the estate, a trust or a surviving owner. An [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) can keep the proceeds out of the estate for tax purposes, though note that proceeds paid to a trust may not be available to the executor for debts unless it is set up for that purpose.
3. **Check for required insurance.** Some loan agreements already require a policy assigned to the lender. Others only require the owner to maintain coverage.
4. **Name a manager who can keep paying.** The person who takes over the day-to-day should have bank access and written authority. A sudden stop in payments often turns a manageable guaranty into a default.
5. **Know the trigger clauses.** Death of a principal can be an event of default. Some lenders waive it if payments are current and a successor is named. Ask while you are alive.
6. **Plan for a sale or wind-down.** If nobody can run the business, a planned sale preserves more value than a forced one. Our page on [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) discusses the options.

## An example of the problem and a fix

Ruth, 63, owns a commercial printing company through an LLC. She signed a personal guaranty on a $620,000 bank loan and a five-year lease with three years left. Her estate plan leaves her house and savings to her two daughters, and neither daughter wants the print shop. When Ruth dies, the company's revenue falls by a third in the first month as customers drift away. The bank calls the loan. The lease continues. Her executor sells the equipment for less than it is owed, the lender claims the shortfall, and the landlord files for unpaid rent. The daughters watch the savings shrink.

Ruth's plan might have looked different. A ten-year term policy sized to the bank guaranty, an agreement with her shop foreman to buy the company at a set formula, a request to the landlord to cap the guaranty, and a letter naming who can sign checks in the first month. None of this makes the debt vanish. It changes who pays and how fast.

## What happens to contracts, bank accounts and customer information?

Contracts signed by the company keep going. Those in the owner's name pass to the estate and may need to be assigned. The business bank account is controlled by whoever is authorized on the signature card, so adding a successor now saves a frozen account later. If the only person with access was the owner, the bank generally requires court letters from the executor, which takes time. Our page on [a deceased person's bank account](/learn/probate/what-happens-to-a-deceased-persons-bank-account) shows how banks handle the personal side.

Passwords, customer data and online accounts need their own plan. A company that cannot reach its accounting software or payment processor in the first week may miss payroll. A written list kept with your attorney, together with a named person who is allowed to use it, solves most of this.

## Can the executor run the business while probate is open?

Often yes, but only as far as the will or a court order allows. An executor is a fiduciary, and running a business is a risk. A will can give the executor express power to continue the business for a period and to hire a manager. Without that language, the executor may need court approval, or may have to sell or close. Talk with the attorney early. Our page on [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) explains the baseline.
