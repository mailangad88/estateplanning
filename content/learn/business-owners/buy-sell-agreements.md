If you co-own a business, you are tied to your partners in a way that resembles marriage, but with fewer legal defaults. When one of you dies, becomes disabled or wants out, someone has to decide who owns that share, what it costs and where the money comes from. A buy-sell agreement answers those questions while everyone is healthy and friendly.

This guide covers the main structures, how pricing and funding work, and the mistakes that cause a well-meant agreement to fail. It is part of our [business owner estate planning guide](/learn/business-owners).

## What does a buy-sell agreement do?

It creates a binding market for an ownership interest that otherwise has none. Shares in a private company are hard to sell, and without an agreement a deceased owner's family may hold a minority stake that pays no dividends and cannot be sold. The remaining owners, meanwhile, may find themselves in business with a spouse or child they never chose.

A buy-sell agreement fixes both problems. It generally covers:

- **Triggers.** The events that start a buyout, such as death, disability, divorce or retirement.
- **Who buys.** Other owners, the company, or a mix.
- **Price and terms.** A number or formula, and whether payment is lump sum or installments.
- **Funding.** Insurance, a note or other source.
- **Restrictions.** Rules that bar transfers to outsiders without consent.

## What are the main types of buy-sell agreements?

There are two core structures and a hybrid.

| Type | Who buys | Strengths | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cross-purchase | The surviving owners buy directly from the departing owner or estate | Buyers receive a stepped-up tax basis in what they buy; insurance proceeds stay outside the company | With many owners, the number of policies grows quickly; buyers' finances matter |
| Entity redemption | The company buys the interest | Simple; one policy per owner | Surviving owners do not receive a basis increase on their existing interests; company creditors can reach proceeds |
| Hybrid or wait-and-see | The company has first option, then owners, with a defined order | Flexible on tax and cash flow | More complex to draft; uncertainty about who buys |

Taxes differ by entity type. For example, partnerships and multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships have their own rules for adjusting basis, and corporations have rules that can affect redemptions. These details are technical, so we describe the variation rather than a single answer. A tax advisor should model your structure before you sign.

## How do you set the price?

The price clause is where most disputes happen. Common methods:

1. **Fixed price.** The owners agree on a figure and update it annually. Simple, but stale if no one updates it.
2. **Formula.** For example, a multiple of average earnings over three years, or book value plus a premium. Predictable, but it can misfire in a very good or very bad year.
3. **Independent appraisal.** A qualified appraiser values the business at the trigger date. Fair, but slow and costly, and appraisers can differ.
4. **Hybrid.** An annual signed valuation sets the price, with an appraisal required if none has been signed within the last 12 to 18 months.

Whatever you choose, state the valuation date, whether minority and lack-of-marketability discounts apply, and what happens if the parties disagree on the appraiser. For estate tax purposes, a price set in an agreement is respected only if it reflects a bona fide business arrangement and is comparable to arm's-length terms, so a price that is far below fair value, especially among family members, can be disregarded by the IRS.

## How is a buy-sell agreement funded?

An agreement without cash behind it is a wish. There are four common sources.

- **Life insurance.** The most common funding for a death buyout. Term insurance is cheaper; permanent insurance builds cash value but costs more. Coverage should be reviewed whenever the valuation changes. See our guide on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) for naming and ownership mistakes.
- **Disability buyout insurance.** Pays out after a long elimination period, often 12 to 24 months, and has strict definitions of disability. Check that the policy definition matches the agreement's definition.
- **Installment note.** The buyer pays over 3 to 10 years, usually with interest and security. Cheaper upfront, but the seller or the family carries credit risk.
- **Company or owner reserves.** Works only when the business is cash rich and the price is modest.

### Example

Priya and Tomas each own half of a design firm valued at $2,000,000. They sign a cross-purchase agreement. Each owns a $1,000,000 term policy on the other, and they update the value each January. When Tomas dies, Priya collects $1,000,000 and, under the agreement, pays it to Tomas's widow Elena for his 50 percent share. Elena receives cash instead of a half-interest in a company she doesn't know how to run, and Priya gets full control. The balance of the price is paid by a five-year note secured by the shares.

## How does a buy-sell agreement interact with your estate plan?

The agreement controls the business interest and your estate plan controls everything else. The interest is usually part of your [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate) unless it has been moved to a trust, and the buy-sell agreement tells your executor to sell it. Your executor must follow the contract, so your will should authorize that sale and say where the proceeds go.

A few coordination points:

- **Beneficiary on the policy.** In a cross-purchase plan the surviving owner is the beneficiary, not your family. Your family gets the purchase price from the buyer.
- **Step-up in basis.** Interests that pass at death generally receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), so the estate typically recognizes little gain when it sells to the agreed buyers at a date-of-death value. This works smoothly only if the price in the agreement is close to the date-of-death value.
- **Spouses.** Many agreements require a spouse's written consent so a divorce or a surviving spouse cannot claim a share outside the agreement.
- **Succession.** The agreement is one piece of [succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning), not all of it.

## What mistakes make buy-sell agreements fail?

- **Never funded.** The insurance lapses or was never purchased, and the buyers cannot afford the price.
- **Never updated.** A formula set in 2012 now produces half of fair value.
- **Mismatched definitions.** The agreement defines disability one way and the policy another, so one pays and the other does not.
- **Wrong owner of the policy.** A policy owned by the wrong party can pull proceeds into the taxable estate.
- **Ignoring family dynamics.** Heirs who expected to inherit the business learn at the funeral that the agreement requires a sale.
- **No dispute resolution.** The agreement lacks a clause for mediation or arbitration when owners disagree about value.
