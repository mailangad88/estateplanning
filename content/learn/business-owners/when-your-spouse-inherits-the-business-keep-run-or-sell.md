Many owners say the same sentence in a first meeting: "It all goes to my wife." It is a natural instinct, and legally it is simple. The harder question comes after. What will she do with a plumbing company, a dental practice or a family restaurant when she has never run it, she is grieving, and employees are asking who is in charge?

This page looks at what a spouse can do with an inherited business, how the choices compare, and how to build the plan that matches your spouse rather than an ideal one. It belongs to our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners).

## Is leaving the business to your spouse a good plan?

It is a good plan when your spouse can use it, and a risky one when the business is an obligation. Tax is rarely the difficulty. Property left outright to a U.S. citizen spouse generally qualifies for the unlimited marital deduction, so the federal estate tax is deferred until the second death. The difficulty is practical.

A business is not a bank account. It needs decisions every week. It depends on relationships and skill that may leave with the owner. It can lose value fast if customers or key employees drift away. A spouse who inherits it holds an asset whose value is fragile, and who may have no cash to carry the family through the slow months.

Consider Ruth, whose husband Anil owned an HVAC company with eleven employees. Anil left everything to her. Ruth is a school administrator and has never held a contractor's license. Three of the technicians leave within a month. The licensed qualifier for the company was Anil. Within six months, the business is worth a fraction of what it was, and Ruth is deciding whether to sell at a discount.

## What can a spouse actually do with the business?

A surviving spouse usually has four realistic choices, and your plan can favor one.

| Choice | What it means | Works best when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keep and run it | Spouse takes over management | Spouse already works in the business | Skills, licensing and stress |
| Keep and hire a manager | Spouse owns, someone else runs | A trusted manager exists | Cost of the manager, oversight |
| Sell to a partner, employee or family | Planned sale, often under a buy-sell agreement | A buyer and a price are agreed in advance | Price disputes if not agreed |
| Sell on the open market | Spouse sells after the death | No other option | Rushed sale, lower price |

The two middle choices are the ones that most owners overlook. A manager with a bonus tied to profit and a clear reporting line can keep a business healthy for years. A buy-sell agreement with a funded price lets the spouse receive cash on the day the owner dies. Our articles on [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) and [key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance) show how both work.

## When does a buy-sell agreement help a spouse?

When you have partners, a buy-sell agreement is often the best protection a spouse can have. It sets the price and the buyer in advance, and life insurance can pay for it. The spouse receives cash and does not become a reluctant partner with the co-owner, and the co-owner keeps control of the company.

Without an agreement, a spouse and a partner can find themselves in a standoff. The partner may not want to work with the spouse. The spouse may not want to sell cheaply. Our article on [what happens when a partner dies without a buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/business-partner-dies-without-a-buy-sell-agreement) explains how this tends to go. If the company is most of the family's wealth, read [when the business is most of your estate](/learn/business-owners/when-the-business-is-most-of-your-estate).

## What if you and your spouse own the business together?

Joint ownership changes the picture because your spouse already owns part of it. The rules depend on the form of the business and how it is titled.

- **A sole proprietorship in one name.** The assets belong to that person and pass through the will or a trust. A spouse's name on a bank account does not change who owns the business.
- **Property held jointly with survivorship.** The surviving spouse takes the whole, outside probate, but the assets may still need to be retitled and licenses changed.
- **An LLC or corporation with both spouses as owners.** The company documents control. The survivor keeps their own share and receives the deceased spouse's share according to the will, trust or agreement.
- **Community property states.** In those states, a spouse generally owns half of what was built during the marriage. Illinois and New Jersey do not use community property, so title and documents tend to decide.

In every case, check the operating agreement or bylaws. Our article on [what happens to LLC interests and corporate shares at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) explains the default rules and how documents override them.

## Should the spouse receive the business outright or in a trust?

A trust can give a spouse the benefit of the business while keeping control with someone qualified to run it. The trust can name a trustee, set the manager's pay, define when a sale should be considered and state what happens when the spouse dies or remarries.

The last point deserves attention. If your spouse inherits the company outright and remarries, the business becomes part of the new marriage. Children from your first marriage may then receive nothing from it. A trust can direct the business to your children after your spouse's death while giving your spouse the income. Our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families) explains the tradeoffs, and the article on [putting a business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust) covers the mechanics.

The cost is flexibility. A tightly drafted trust can frustrate a spouse who wants to sell. Many owners give the trustee, or the spouse, a clear power to sell at a fair price, with an independent appraisal. The value of the business at death is also set for tax purposes, so read [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).

## What happens if there is no plan at all?

If you die without a will, state law decides who inherits your business interest, and a spouse often shares it with your children. A spouse and three adult children can end up co-owners of a company none of them agrees how to run. A court appoints an administrator, and in the meantime nobody may have authority to sign contracts or make payroll.

That is a poor result even in a loving family. A short will, or a trust that names a successor trustee and a manager, avoids most of it. Our article on [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) covers the wider picture.

## How do you build the plan around your spouse?

Use these steps.

1. Ask your spouse, in plain words, what they would want to do with the business if you died next year. Their answer may surprise you.
2. Identify the person who could run it, and what that person would need: a contract, an ownership share, a bonus.
3. Check the operating agreement and any buy-sell agreement. Make them match your wishes.
4. Decide whether to fund a buyout with life insurance. Our [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) is a start.
5. Put the decision in your will or trust: keep, manage, sell, or give the executor the power to choose.
6. Write a brief letter of guidance so your spouse knows your reasons and your advisors.

Then revisit the plan after a major change, such as a new partner, a big contract or a health scare.
