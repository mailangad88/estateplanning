The most common worry we hear from a family business owner is not about the company. It is about the tax bill the children will face when the owner dies. The picture is usually less frightening than the rumor, with a few real exceptions that deserve attention.

This page separates the taxes that apply at death from the taxes that apply to a later sale, and shows where the options lie. It is a companion to our page on [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) and part of our [guide to estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## Is there a tax when my children receive the business?

Receiving an inheritance is not income, so the heirs owe no federal income tax on getting the shares or interest. The possible taxes are on the estate itself, before the heirs receive anything.

| Tax | Who owes it | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Federal estate tax | The estate | Taxable estate above $15,000,000 per person in 2026; top rate 40% |
| State estate tax | The estate | In states that have one, often at a lower threshold than the federal one |
| State inheritance tax | The heir | In a handful of states, depending on the heir's relationship to the owner |
| Income tax on receipt | Nobody | Inheritance is generally not income |
| Capital gains tax | The heir | On a later sale, only on growth above the stepped-up basis |

Our pages on the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) and [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) cover the thresholds. The business is added to the rest of the estate at fair market value, together with the house, retirement accounts, life insurance owned by the decedent and everything else. A $3 million company inside a $4 million estate is nowhere near the line, while a $20 million company inside a $22 million estate is well past it.

## How does the step-up in basis work for a business?

At death, most property takes a new tax basis equal to its value on that date. This is the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis), and for a business that has grown for thirty years it can be the most valuable tax feature in the entire plan.

Consider Gerald, who started a machine shop in 1994 with $40,000 and who dies owning 100 percent of the stock, now worth $2,400,000. His tax basis in the stock was low. His daughter Priya inherits it with a basis of $2,400,000. If she sells for $2,500,000 a year later, her gain is $100,000, not $2,460,000. Had Gerald given her the stock the year before, she would have taken his old basis, and a sale would have shown nearly all of the growth as a gain. That is the central tradeoff in [gifting business interests to children](/learn/business-owners/gifting-business-interests-to-children).

Some points to keep in mind.

- **The executor can choose.** The executor may value the estate six months after death instead of on the date of death, but only if it lowers both the estate's value and the estate tax. It is not a way to pick the higher basis.
- **Valuation matters twice.** A high number raises estate tax but also raises the basis. A low number does the reverse. Be consistent and be honest, since the IRS expects the values to match, and large gaps draw questions.
- **Not every asset steps up.** Income in respect of a decedent, such as unpaid wages or an installment sale note, does not. Retirement accounts do not step up.
- **Community property.** In community property states, both halves of a couple's community property can step up at the first death, which can be a major benefit for a spousal business. Our [community property glossary entry](/glossary/community-property) explains the idea.

## What is different about partnerships and multi-member LLCs?

They have two layers of basis. The heir's outside basis, in the interest itself, steps up to date-of-death value (adjusted for the heir's share of liabilities). The inside basis, in the partnership's own equipment, buildings and goodwill, does not move by itself.

That mismatch matters. The heir may hold an interest with a basis of $900,000 while the company's assets, as the company sees them, are worth less on paper and are still being depreciated by the old schedule. The result is that the heir pays tax on income computed from the old numbers.

A Section 754 election fixes this. The partnership makes it on a timely filed return, and it adjusts the inside basis for that heir only, so the heir gets depreciation and reduced gain based on the stepped-up value. It is a choice the partnership makes, and once made it generally applies to later transfers too, which adds some bookkeeping. If you co-own a business with others, ask in advance whether the agreement requires or prohibits it. An LLC taxed as a partnership follows the same rules. An LLC with one member is usually disregarded, so there is only one layer.

## What if the estate is large and the business is illiquid?

Then Section 6166 may help. This provision of the tax code allows an estate to pay the estate tax attributable to a closely held business over time, if the business interest makes up more than 35 percent of the adjusted gross estate. Broadly, the first payment can be delayed for a number of years, and the rest is paid in annual installments over a period of years, with interest on the balance. Part of the tax qualifies for a lower interest rate.

There are conditions. The business must be an active trade or business, not a passive investment. The interest must be a qualifying one, such as a sole proprietorship, a partnership interest with enough ownership, or stock in a corporation with a limited number of shareholders. Selling a big part of the business can accelerate the deferred tax. And the election must be made on a timely filed estate tax return.

Farms and ranches have an additional valuation rule, covered in our [farm succession page](/learn/business-owners/farm-succession-planning). If the estate lacks cash, our page on [what if the estate cannot pay the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-if-the-estate-cannot-pay-the-estate-tax) lays out the alternatives, including a sale of part of the company or a redemption to pay the tax.

## What happens when the heirs sell the business?

They are taxed on the difference between the price and the stepped-up basis. Inherited property is treated as held for more than a year, so any gain is taxed at the long-term capital gain rates, and the heirs do not need to wait.

Not all of the price is capital gain. For an asset sale, part of the price is allocated to equipment, inventory and receivables, and each category is taxed differently. A buyer who purchases assets instead of stock will want to divide the price in a way that suits the buyer. Heirs should have a tax advisor look at the allocation before they sign, because it can move the tax bill by more than the price negotiation does.

For heirs who intend to keep the business, there is no sale tax at all. They continue to run it, the income is taxed as it is earned, and the stepped-up basis reduces depreciation on the same assets, if the inside basis is adjusted.

## Should an owner sell now, or wait because of the estate tax exemption?

For most owners the answer rests on the estate size, not on the headlines. The federal exemption of $15,000,000 per person in 2026 is set by the 2025 tax law, indexed for inflation after 2026, with no scheduled sunset. A business owner with a $6 million estate is unlikely to owe federal estate tax at all. A sale could turn the business into cash that is still taxable in the estate, and could remove the step-up on the business, since the owner pays capital gains tax in life on the full gain.

That is why selling to avoid estate tax can backfire. The case for an earlier sale is usually about retirement, risk and who will run the company, not about the exemption. Our page on [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) compares the main paths.

## What steps help the heirs most?

1. **Keep a written, dated valuation.** A recent appraisal supports the step-up and the estate tax return.
2. **Have the entity type reviewed.** Sole proprietorship, S corporation, C corporation and partnership each behave differently, and the choice affects the basis and the tax.
3. **Check the state.** State death taxes can apply well below the federal level, so read the guide for your state, such as our [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) or [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) pages, to see how a state page is laid out.
4. **Write liquidity into the plan.** Insurance or a buy-sell agreement gives the estate cash to pay tax and gives the family a clean exit.
5. **File on time.** Elections such as Section 6166 and the S corporation trust elections have deadlines. Our page on [Form 706](/learn/estate-tax/form-706-estate-tax-return) covers when the return is due.
