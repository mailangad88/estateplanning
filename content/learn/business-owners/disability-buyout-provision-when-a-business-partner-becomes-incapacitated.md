Two partners opened a dental lab together twelve years ago. One of them has a stroke at 54. She is in rehab, she may or may not come back, and the lab's bank wants her signature on a renewed line of credit. Her partner cannot sign for her. Her husband has no authority over the company. Payroll is due Friday.

That is the problem a disability buyout provision is built for. This page covers what happens without one, what a good clause says, how to fund it, and how it fits with the incapacity planning described in our [business owner series](/learn/business-owners).

## What happens when a business partner becomes incapacitated?

The business does not end, but the partner's role in it stops working. An incapacitated owner cannot vote, sign, approve a transaction or manage a loan. The remaining partners can often run daily operations, yet anything that needs the absent partner's consent, such as a new lease, an owner distribution or a sale, sits in limbo.

Who can speak for the partner depends on what was signed beforehand.

| Situation | Who can act for the partner | Typical delay |
|---|---|---|
| Durable power of attorney that covers business matters | The named agent | Days, if the bank and company accept it |
| Interest held in a revocable trust | The successor trustee | Days, once incapacity is documented as the trust requires |
| Neither of the above | A court-appointed conservator or guardian of the estate | Weeks or months |

If the partner's spouse is also an owner or manager, the spouse may be able to cover part of the gap. If the spouse is not on any document, the spouse has no more authority over the company than a stranger. Our guide to [business owner incapacity planning](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) explains the documents behind the first two rows.

## What is a disability buyout provision?

It is a clause in a buy-sell agreement, operating agreement or partnership agreement that lets, or requires, the company or the other owners to buy a partner's interest if that partner becomes disabled. It does for disability what a death clause does after a death. If you want the death side first, read [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).

A clause that works answers three questions in writing.

1. **When does disability begin?** The trigger must be something an outsider can verify.
2. **What is the share worth?** The price needs a formula or an appraisal method.
3. **How is it paid?** The terms must be affordable for the buyer.

Leave any of those vague and the clause becomes a lawsuit.

## How should the agreement define disability?

Pick an objective test. Subjective language like "unable to perform duties" invites an argument between the sick partner's family and the healthy partners. Common tests include:

- A written statement from a licensed physician that the partner cannot perform the essential duties of the job, with a second opinion by a doctor chosen by the company if either side asks.
- Continuous absence from work for a stated number of months, often with a shorter period for some exceptions such as a planned surgery.
- Adjudication of incapacity by a court, which is clear but slow and public.
- The same definition used in the partners' disability buyout insurance, so the policy pays when the agreement says the buyout starts.

The last one deserves attention. If the agreement says "six months" and the policy waits twelve, the company is obligated to buy with no money to pay. Align them before you sign.

Include a short waiting period before the buyout begins. Many strokes and surgeries that look permanent in week two look very different in month six. Many owners also choose a gentler first step, such as reduced pay for the absent partner and a salary for a temporary replacement, before moving to a full buyout.

## How is the price set and paid?

Use the same method the agreement uses on death, so the partners are not valuing the company twice on two different theories. The usual choices are an agreed value updated each year, a formula such as a multiple of earnings, or an independent appraisal. Our article on [how a business is valued](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) describes how appraisers think about the number.

### Example: a funded and an unfunded buyout

Example only. Leila and Tom own a two-person engineering firm, 50 percent each, valued at $1,200,000. Each share is $600,000.

- **Unfunded.** Tom is disabled. The firm agrees to pay Leila's side of the purchase over seven years. That is about $7,140 a month for 84 months before interest, from a firm that just lost half its billing capacity. It often does not work.
- **Funded.** The firm carried disability buyout insurance on each partner that pays a lump sum after a twelve-month waiting period. The firm buys Tom's share for $600,000 cash and his family has the money. Leila owns the firm outright.

The point is not the exact numbers. It is that a promise to pay cannot be kept by a company that has just lost a partner.

## Can insurance fund a disability buyout?

Yes, in many cases. A disability buyout policy is a specific product, different from an owner's personal income policy. It is underwritten on each partner's health and occupation, and its premiums, benefit waiting period and definition of disability are fixed in the contract. Business overhead insurance is another product, which pays operating costs while an owner is out.

Questions to ask the agent before you buy:

1. What is the definition of disability, and does it match the buy-sell agreement?
2. How long is the waiting period before benefits begin?
3. Is the benefit a lump sum or a series of payments, and how long do payments run?
4. Who owns the policy and who is the beneficiary?
5. What exclusions apply, and are they a problem for our industry?

Treat the answers as a checklist and keep them in the agreement file. [Key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance) works on a related idea, and your insurance agent and attorney should look at them together.

## Does a power of attorney replace a buyout clause?

No. A durable power of attorney lets someone sign for the partner, which solves the bank problem. It does not decide whether the partner's share is sold, at what price, or to whom. An agent who is also the partner's spouse would face a conflict of interest if the sale price is not already set by the agreement. Read our overview of the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) for the limits.

If your partner has no power of attorney and has already lost capacity, the way forward is usually a petition to the court. Our article on [guardian of the person vs. conservator of the estate](/learn/guardianship/guardian-of-the-person-vs-conservator-of-the-estate) explains what that involves.

## What should partners do this month?

1. Read your current buy-sell or operating agreement and find whether it mentions disability at all.
2. Ask each partner to sign a durable power of attorney that includes business authority.
3. Name a backup signer on the company's bank accounts.
4. Agree on a disability trigger and a pricing method, and put them in writing.
5. Price disability buyout insurance and compare its definition with the agreement.
6. Set a calendar date each year to review the value.

For the full picture of how this fits with transfers at death, see [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning). The [plan readiness assessment](/tools/plan-readiness-assessment) can show where your own personal documents stand.
