Many owners ask the same question once a child joins the business: should I hand over some of it now or leave it all to them at death? Giving during life shifts future growth to the next generation, lets the child feel like an owner, and spreads the work of the transition. It also has costs, especially for taxes and for your own security.

This page covers the gift rules for business interests, the choice between a gift and an inheritance, the discount and family partnership techniques, and the risks. It extends our guide to [passing a family business to your children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children) and sits inside the larger section on [business owner estate planning](/learn/business-owners).

## How does the gift tax apply to a business interest?

A gift of business shares or an LLC interest is valued like any other gift, at fair market value on the day you make it. Three layers of rules apply.

- **Annual exclusion.** In 2026, you can give $19,000 to each recipient without a return. A married couple can combine to give $38,000 per child, which counts only when both spouses agree to split the gift. See our guide to the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).
- **Gift tax return.** A gift above the annual exclusion requires a federal Form 709, even though no tax is paid. The return records how much of your lifetime exemption you used.
- **Lifetime exemption.** The basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, shared between gifts during life and the estate at death. Tax at the top rate of 40 percent applies only to amounts above it.

For most owners that means no gift tax is paid, but the paperwork matters. Gift a 5 percent interest worth $60,000 to your daughter, and you file a return reporting the value. The value therefore has to be supportable, so you will want a written appraisal for anything beyond a trivial amount. Our article on [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) describes the methods.

## Should you gift the business or leave it at death?

The core trade-off is income tax basis. This is the figure used to measure gain when the asset is sold.

| | Gift during life | Inheritance at death |
|---|---|---|
| Child's basis | Your basis carries over | Generally stepped up to date-of-death value |
| Gain if child sells soon | Often large | Often small |
| Future growth | Moves out of your estate | Included in your estate |
| Control | You can keep it using non-voting interests | Passes when you die |
| Your security | Assets are gone | You keep the use of them |

Suppose Hal started a machine shop with $50,000 and it is now worth $3,000,000. If he gifts it all and his son Ben sells for $3,000,000, Ben is taxed on roughly $2,950,000 of gain. If Hal leaves it at death and Ben sells after, Ben's basis is generally close to $3,000,000, so little gain is taxed. For a business that will be kept and run, the carryover basis matters less. For one likely to be sold, it can matter a great deal. Our [step-up in basis guide](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains the mechanics.

The reverse applies when your estate may be taxable. If your total estate exceeds the $15,000,000 exclusion in 2026, or a lower state threshold, then moving growth out of the estate by gift may save more estate tax than it costs in basis. That calculation is individual. Ask your accountant to run both versions with real numbers.

## How do you give value but keep control?

Most family businesses have more than one class of ownership. You can split voting from economic rights, then give away the economic part.

1. **Recapitalize.** Convert your ownership into voting and non-voting shares, or in an LLC into managing and non-managing units. This often needs an amendment to the governing documents and sometimes tax review.
2. **Value the pieces.** Non-voting interests can be worth less than their share of the total, because they carry no control.
3. **Gift the non-voting part over time.** Use the annual exclusion each year, and larger gifts when it makes sense.
4. **Keep management.** You continue as manager or hold the voting shares until you are ready to step back.
5. **Write the rules.** A shareholder or operating agreement should restrict transfers outside the family and set a buyout price on exit.

Controlling the voting shares is also how you handle fairness among children. One child who runs the company can get the voting stock later, while the others receive non-voting interests or other assets. The fairness question is explored in the article on [passing a family business to your children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children).

## What are discounts, and are they safe?

A gift of a minority, non-voting interest in a private company is often valued below its share of the whole, because a buyer would pay less for something with no control and no ready market. Those two discounts, for lack of control and lack of marketability, can reduce the taxable value of a gift and so stretch your annual exclusion further.

They are legitimate when supported, and they are one of the first things the IRS checks. Rules of thumb to keep you out of trouble:

- Use a qualified independent appraiser, not the company's accountant alone.
- Do not gift on the same day the structure is created, with no change in the underlying business.
- Keep the business real. Contracts, payroll, management and genuine operations support the value.
- Accept that a discount in one direction can cost you elsewhere; some interests are better held at full value for basis reasons.

## What is a family limited partnership?

A family limited partnership, or FLP, is a partnership owned by relatives. A common arrangement puts the business or an investment asset such as real estate inside the partnership. The parents hold a small general partner interest, which controls management, and large limited partner interests. The parents then give limited interests to children, or to trusts for them, over time.

An FLP can help in several ways. It centralizes management, gives children an ownership share without voting power, and creates documents that restrict transfers. Gifts of limited interests may qualify for discounts.

It also comes with real risks.

- **IRS challenge.** If you keep the right to the income or control who enjoys the property, the IRS can argue the assets are still in your estate. This is more likely when parents transfer most of their wealth and keep living off it.
- **Mixing funds.** Paying personal bills from the partnership account undermines the structure.
- **Cost.** There are legal and valuation fees, annual filings, and a partnership tax return.
- **State differences.** State law governs the partnership's form and what creditors can reach.

For a family farm or a rental portfolio an FLP may fit. For a small operating business with one owner, an LLC and a simple gifting plan are often enough. Compare the options in our article about [gifting strategies](/learn/estate-tax/gifting-strategies).

## What other ways exist to transfer an interest?

- **Sale to a child.** A sale at fair price, often over time through a note, gives you income and still moves ownership. Tax treatment depends on the structure.
- **Gift to an irrevocable trust.** Children benefit, and a trustee manages the shares. This is a more advanced approach with separate tax rules. See our guide to [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts).
- **Leave it at death.** A will or revocable trust passes the interest and generally the step-up in basis.
- **Retain a stake.** You can give part and keep part for retirement income.

## What risks should you check before giving?

1. **Your own needs.** Do you still have income and savings for retirement without the gifted interest?
2. **Medicaid.** Gifts within the look-back period, which is 60 months in most states, can create a penalty if you later need nursing home coverage. Read about the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).
3. **Your child's circumstances.** A divorce, creditor claim or bankruptcy can reach a gifted interest. A trust gives more protection.
4. **Other children.** A gift to one is not a gift to all, and silence can create resentment.
5. **Lender and contract limits.** Check loans, leases and the operating agreement for transfer restrictions.
6. **Valuation and filing.** Keep the appraisal, the gift documents and the filed return together.
