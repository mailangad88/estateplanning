You may own your company through an LLC or a corporation precisely because it keeps business and personal life apart. At death that separation helps, because the company continues to exist. It also hides a trap: your ownership stake is personal property, and it will be handled by your estate, not by the business.

This guide explains the default rules for the most common entity types, the documents that override them, and the steps that keep control from stalling. It belongs to our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners).

## What happens to an LLC interest when a member dies?

The membership interest passes by will, trust, or intestacy, but what the heir receives depends on the operating agreement and state law. In many states, the default is that an heir becomes a transferee with the right to distributions only, not a full member with a vote. The other members decide whether to admit the heir.

Common agreement provisions fall into three groups:

- **Continuation clauses.** The LLC continues, and the estate holds the interest until the agreement's buyout or transfer procedure runs.
- **Mandatory buyout.** The company or other members must purchase the interest at a formula price. This is often combined with a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).
- **Free transfer to family.** Some agreements allow transfers to a spouse or descendants, sometimes limited to non-voting rights.

If there is no operating agreement, state default rules apply, and they differ meaningfully by state. A multi-member LLC without a written agreement is exactly the kind of company that ends up in court after a death.

## What about a single-member LLC?

A single-member LLC creates a gap, because the one person authorized to act is the one who died. Bank accounts, leases and licenses may all be in the LLC's name, but only you can sign. Your executor must be appointed by a court before acting on behalf of your membership interest, which can take weeks or months.

Fixes include:

1. **Name a successor manager** in the operating agreement, effective on your death or incapacity.
2. **Assign the membership interest to your revocable trust.** The successor trustee can take over immediately, with no court. See [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) for the mechanics of retitling.
3. **Sign a durable power of attorney** that covers business matters for the period before death. For that, see [incapacity planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning).
4. **Give your bank a signature authority resolution** naming a backup signer.

Even with a trust, state law and the LLC agreement should be reviewed to be sure the transfer is allowed. Some lenders also require consent to a change in ownership.

## What happens to corporate shares?

Shares of a corporation pass like other personal property. The new owner holds all the rights that come with those shares, including voting rights, unless a shareholder agreement says otherwise. If the deceased owned a majority, the heirs now control who sits on the board, who serves as an officer and whether to pay dividends.

Key differences from an LLC:

- Officers and directors are separate from shareholders. Your death does not remove a sibling who is president, but their employment may be at will.
- A shareholder agreement may include a right of first refusal or a mandatory buyout.
- A corporation can have different share classes, such as voting and non-voting, which make it easier to split control from economic value.

### S corporation traps

An S corporation can have only certain kinds of shareholders. Individuals who are U.S. citizens or residents generally qualify, and so do estates for a period and certain trusts. A typical revocable trust is allowed while you are alive and for a limited time after your death. After that, a trust holding S stock usually needs to qualify as a qualified subchapter S trust or an electing small business trust, and a timely election is needed. A missed election can terminate the S status, with tax costs for the company and the owners. If you own S corporation stock, tell your attorney before you write or change a trust.

## How do community property and divorce affect ownership?

In the community property states, a spouse generally has a one-half interest in business value built during the marriage, even when the shares are titled in one spouse's name. At death that can mean the surviving spouse already owns half, and only your half passes under your will. In other states, a surviving spouse may have a claim under elective-share rules. Prenuptial and postnuptial agreements can adjust these outcomes. See the [glossary entry on community property](/glossary/community-property) for the basics.

## What does the estate do with the interest?

The sequence usually looks like this:

1. The executor is appointed and obtains legal authority, or the successor trustee takes charge if the interest is in a trust.
2. The estate obtains a date-of-death valuation, which also sets the new tax basis for the heirs.
3. The executor reviews the operating or shareholder agreement for buyout rights, consent requirements and deadlines.
4. If the agreement requires a buyout, the company or co-owners purchase the interest and the estate receives cash.
5. If the heirs keep the interest, the company updates its records, issues new certificates or amends the operating agreement, and files any required notice with the state.

Delays commonly come from missing paperwork. Locate the articles of organization, EIN letter, operating agreement, stock ledger and any loan agreements before anything else.

### Example

Marcus owns 100 percent of an LLC that runs three coffee shops. He dies with no trust and a will naming his wife Jenna as executor. For six weeks, the LLC's bank accounts are frozen while Jenna petitions the court. Payroll is covered by a family loan, and one lender calls a note. If Marcus had placed the LLC interest in his trust, Jenna, as successor trustee, could have taken over the next day.

## What about lenders, leases and licenses?

The company may survive your death, but its contracts may not welcome the change. Bank loans often include a change-of-control clause that lets the lender demand repayment when ownership shifts, and personal guarantees stay with your estate until the lender releases them. Commercial leases may require the landlord's consent to a transfer. Professional or trade licenses, such as a contractor, liquor or medical license, frequently cannot pass to an heir who is not qualified, and the business may have to hire a licensed manager or sell. Ask each lender and landlord what a death or transfer would trigger, and get answers in writing while you can.

## What should you do now?

Do these in order of urgency:

- Read your operating agreement or shareholder agreement for transfer, consent and buyout rules.
- Name a successor manager and a backup signer.
- Decide whether the interest belongs in your trust, and confirm the documents allow that.
- Align your will with the agreement. See [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for how ownership form determines the path.
- If your company is an S corporation, talk to your advisor about the trust provisions before you sign anything.
- Update the plan after any new owner, loan or restructuring.
