You built a company, and it may be the largest asset your family owns. It is also the asset most likely to lose value fast if you are suddenly gone, because much of its worth sits in your relationships, your knowledge and your signature. This article explains what a succession plan contains, how to build one, and where owners most often go wrong.

Succession planning is not only for people about to retire. Death and disability are the two events that arrive without a schedule, so a good plan covers all three exits: an unplanned one, a medical one and a chosen one.

## What does a business succession plan include?

A succession plan answers four questions in writing: who will manage, who will own, what it will cost, and how it will be paid for. Each question has its own documents.

| Question | What it decides | Typical documents |
|---|---|---|
| Who manages day to day? | Authority to sign, hire, pay bills and talk to the bank | Operating agreement or bylaws, power of attorney, board or manager resolutions |
| Who owns it after you? | Who holds your shares or membership interest | Will, [living trust](/glossary/living-trust), beneficiary designations, transfer restrictions |
| What is it worth? | The price any buyer, co-owner or heir pays | Valuation formula or appraisal clause, buy-sell agreement |
| How is it paid for? | Where cash comes from for your family and for the buyer | Life insurance, disability buyout insurance, installment note, company cash reserves |

Management and ownership are different jobs. A daughter who inherits 100 percent of the shares does not automatically know how to run a roofing company, and your best project manager may have no money to buy it. Many successful plans split the two: one person gets authority to run the company while ownership passes to the family under a structure that pays your spouse fairly.

## What happens without a plan?

Without a plan, state default law and the company's own documents decide, and they were usually not written with your family in mind.

- **Sole proprietorship.** The business is not separate from you. Its assets are part of your [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate), and no one can sign for it until a court appoints an executor.
- **Partnership or multi-member LLC.** Your share passes to your heirs, but your partners may have the right, or the obligation, to dissolve the company or buy you out at a price you never agreed to. Your heirs may inherit only economic rights, not a vote.
- **Corporation.** The shares become part of your estate. If you held a majority, your heirs now control the board, whether or not they can run the company.
- **S corporation.** Passing shares to an ineligible holder, such as a non-qualifying trust, can terminate the S election and create tax trouble.

The practical damage often starts within days. Suppliers want payment, the bank freezes an account when it learns the sole signer has died, and key employees begin calling recruiters. Our guide to [what happens to an LLC or corporation when you die](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) walks through those default rules by entity type.

## How do you build a succession plan step by step?

Work in the order below. Each step produces something you can hand to a lawyer or advisor.

1. **Define your goals.** Decide whether you want to keep the business in the family, sell it, or wind it down. Decide how much income your spouse needs from it, and whether you want to stay involved after a handover.
2. **Choose management and ownership successors.** List two names for each role, including a backup. Talk to them. A successor who learns about the plan at your funeral is not a plan.
3. **Get a valuation.** A formal appraisal tells you what the business is worth today and shows how much of your net worth is tied up in it. Re-do it every few years or when the formula in your agreements needs a check.
4. **Pick the transfer mechanism.** Options include a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements), a sale to employees, a gift or sale to family, or a sale to an outside buyer.
5. **Fund it.** Decide whether insurance, a note paid out of future profits, or company cash will provide the money. Underfunded plans fail when the trigger event arrives.
6. **Align your personal documents.** Your will, trust, powers of attorney and beneficiary forms must agree with the business agreements.
7. **Write down the operating manual.** Record where the passwords, vendor contacts, licenses, insurance policies and key contracts are, and who knows the procedures.
8. **Schedule reviews.** Put a recurring date in the calendar and update after any sale, new owner, divorce, birth or death.

## Should you pick a family member, a key employee or an outside buyer?

Each path trades control, price and speed differently.

| Path | Strengths | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Child or relative | Keeps the legacy; flexible terms | Family conflict if siblings are treated unequally; the successor may lack skills |
| Key employee or management team | Knows the business; continuity for staff and customers | Usually cannot pay a lump sum; needs seller financing, so you stay exposed |
| Co-owner | Existing knowledge; a buy-sell agreement can fix price and terms | Co-owner's own finances, health and willingness may change |
| Outside buyer | Often the highest cash price | Loss of control; due diligence; your staff may be let go |

Family handoffs raise their own fairness questions, which we cover in [passing a family business to your children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children). If one child works in the business and another does not, the plan needs to treat both fairly without dividing the company in a way that cripples it.

## How does succession planning connect to your estate plan?

Your estate plan is the container the business ownership travels in. If your shares or membership interest are in your own name when you die, they go through probate, which is slow and public. Many owners transfer the interest into a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) so a successor trustee can step in without a court, as long as the company documents allow the transfer. Always check the operating agreement or shareholder agreement first, because many restrict who can hold an interest.

Three coordination points trip up owners:

- **Conflicting documents.** A will leaves everything equally to three children, while the operating agreement gives the company the right to buy your interest at book value. The agreement usually controls, and your children get far less than you intended.
- **Missing authority.** If you become unable to act, your agent needs power under your [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) to deal with the business, and the company documents must recognize that agent. See [incapacity planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning).
- **Spouse's rights.** In community property states and states with elective-share rules, a spouse may have a claim on the business interest regardless of what your will says. A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement can address this.

## What about taxes?

Taxes shape the structure but should not drive every choice. Federal estate tax applies only to estates above the basic exclusion of $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most owners will not owe it, though a valuable business can push an estate over the line. Interests inherited at death generally receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which can erase built-in capital gain for the heirs. A lifetime gift does not receive that step-up. Some states tax estates or inheritances at much lower thresholds. A tax advisor should model your numbers, because the answer depends on entity type, value and state.

## A short example

Dev, 61, owns 70 percent of a regional HVAC company with a partner, Alicia, who owns 30 percent. Dev has two adult children. His son Raj manages installations; his daughter Meera is a nurse. Without a plan, Dev's shares would pass equally to both children, and Alicia would find herself in business with a nurse who has no voice in operations and a son who is now her boss's heir.

The plan Dev and his attorney build: a buy-sell agreement lets Alicia and Raj buy shares from the estate at an agreed formula, funded by life insurance owned by the company. Meera receives cash from the insurance proceeds and from a note. A durable power of attorney names Meera's husband, an accountant, as backup agent for the business accounts. Everyone gets what Dev wanted, and nobody needs to sell a truck to settle an estate.
