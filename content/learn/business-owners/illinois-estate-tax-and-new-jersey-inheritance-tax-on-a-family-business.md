If you own a company in Illinois or New Jersey, the federal estate tax is probably not your problem. The 2026 federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, and most family businesses sit well under it. The state tax is where owners get surprised, because each state taxes the business in a different way and at a much lower level.

This page shows how Illinois and New Jersey treat a family business at death, runs one example in each state with real numbers, and covers where the cash comes from. It sits inside our [business owner estate planning section](/learn/business-owners) and builds on our general guide to [taxes when a family business passes to heirs](/learn/business-owners/taxes-when-a-family-business-passes-to-heirs) and our two state pages on the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax) and the [New Jersey inheritance tax](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-inheritance-tax).

## How does Illinois estate tax apply to a family business?

Illinois adds up everything you own at death, including the company, and taxes the estate if the total passes $4,000,000. The test uses your gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts. That means the business is counted next to the house, retirement accounts, life insurance you own and the value of a revocable trust.

The $4,000,000 is a gate, not a credit. At or under it, nothing is owed and nothing is filed. Over it, the estate files Illinois Form 700 with the Attorney General, whether or not a federal return is required, and pays the Illinois State Treasurer nine months after death.

Here is an example. Dana, 66, owns a machine shop in Joliet that an appraiser would value at $3,200,000. Add her house at $600,000, a 401(k) of $500,000 and a $300,000 policy she owns on her own life. Her gross estate is $4,600,000, which is $600,000 over the line. Nothing in that list is unusual, and none of it is "wealth" in the sense Dana would use. The tax is figured on a special interrelated formula, so do not guess. The Attorney General publishes a calculator, and its own example shows how steep the first dollars over the threshold are: a $5,000,000 estate located wholly in Illinois owes $285,714.

Two Illinois points matter for owners in particular.

- **Spouses cannot share the exclusion.** Illinois does not allow portability. If Dana's husband Ray leaves everything to her and she dies owning $4,600,000, his unused $4,000,000 is gone. A bypass trust at the first death can fix that, and the business interest is often what funds it.
- **Lifetime gifts are added back.** Handing the shop to a son does not wipe out its value for the Illinois test. It can stop later growth from being counted.

## How does New Jersey inheritance tax apply to a family business?

New Jersey taxes the person who receives the business interest, and the rate depends on how that person is related to you. Spouses, civil union and domestic partners, children, stepchildren, grandchildren and parents are Class A and owe nothing. Siblings and a child's spouse are Class C. Everyone else, including a business partner who is not a relative, a key employee, a niece or a friend, is Class D. New Jersey repealed its estate tax for deaths since January 1, 2018.

| Who receives the interest | Class | Rate on what they receive |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse, child, grandchild | A | None |
| Sibling, daughter-in-law | C | First $25,000 free, then 11% up to 16% |
| Partner, employee, niece, friend | D | 15% on the first $700,000, 16% above |
| Qualified charity | E | None |

The tax is figured on the value of what that one person receives, so splitting an interest among several heirs can lower the bill. That is a point to raise with your attorney, not a trick to try on your own.

### What does the tax look like in dollars?

Rosa, 70, owns a plumbing supply corporation worth $1,500,000 and wants it to go to the person who helps run it. The tax changes sharply with her choice. These figures are an example, applying the Division of Taxation rates.

| If Rosa leaves the whole company to | Class | Inheritance tax |
|---|---|---|
| Her son Paul | A | $0 |
| Her brother Sal | C | $171,250 |
| Her longtime manager Wen | D | $233,000 |

Sal's figure works like this: nothing on the first $25,000, 11% on the next $1,075,000 ($118,250), 13% on the next $300,000 ($39,000) and 14% on the last $100,000 ($14,000). Wen pays 15% on $700,000 ($105,000) plus 16% on $800,000 ($128,000).

Neither Sal nor Wen has $171,250 or $233,000 lying around. They own shares in a company, and the shares do not pay a tax bill.

## How is the business valued?

Both states start from the fair market value on the date of death, not what the company cost or what the tax return shows. The value is argued from the books, comparable sales and the buy-sell agreement if one exists. A minority interest may be worth less than its slice of the whole, as covered in our pages on [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) and [valuation discounts](/learn/estate-tax/valuation-discounts-and-family-limited-partnerships).

Get the appraisal early. In Illinois it supports the number on Form 700. In New Jersey it decides what a Class C or Class D heir owes. A low number invites questions, and a high one costs real money, so a documented valuation protects the family either way.

## Where does the cash come from?

State death tax is paid in cash on a fixed schedule. Illinois is nine months. New Jersey is eight months, and the Division of Taxation says that interest at 10% a year runs on unpaid tax and that there is no extension of time to pay. A company that is worth $1,500,000 but holds $80,000 in the bank cannot meet that.

The usual sources are these.

1. **Life insurance.** In New Jersey, proceeds paid to a named beneficiary are exempt from inheritance tax. In Illinois, a policy you own counts in the estate, so owners often put it in an irrevocable trust. See [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and [key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance).
2. **A funded buy-sell agreement.** The partner or the company buys the share for cash at a set price, and the family receives money. How a particular contract is treated for New Jersey inheritance tax depends on its wording, so have it reviewed before you sign. Our guide to [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) shows the structures.
3. **Other assets.** Liquid accounts, a rental property or a retirement account left to the person who needs to pay.
4. **A sale or partial redemption.** Selling part of the company to cover the bill is the last resort, and often at a poor price because the buyer knows the clock is running.

Your will or trust should also say who bears the tax. Without that sentence, the person who receives the business can end up paying a bill the other children never see.

## What should a business owner do now?

1. **List the heirs by class.** In New Jersey, write each person next to Class A, C or D. Wherever the business goes to someone outside Class A, price the tax.
2. **Add up the Illinois estate.** Count the business, the home, retirement accounts, insurance you own and any revocable trust.
3. **Order a valuation.** One that is current and written is worth far more than an estimate on the back of an envelope.
4. **Match insurance to the bill.** Size a policy to the tax figure, not to a round number.
5. **Update the paper.** Check your operating or shareholder agreement against your will, since the [operating agreement may control who takes your interest](/learn/business-owners/does-an-operating-agreement-override-your-will). Our [LLC ownership at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) guide covers how membership interests pass.
6. **Look at the larger plan.** When the business is most of what you own, read [when the business is most of your estate](/learn/business-owners/when-the-business-is-most-of-your-estate) and [gifting business interests to children](/learn/business-owners/gifting-business-interests-to-children).

## What if you own property in both states?

Illinois figures the tax as if everything were in Illinois, then multiplies it by the share of the estate located in Illinois. A nonresident who owns Illinois property files on the same formula. New Jersey reaches a nonresident's New Jersey real and tangible property, so an owner with a warehouse in Newark and a home in Chicago can end up with filings in both. How an entity that owns the real estate is treated is a question for an attorney who has the documents in hand.
