For many owners the business is not just one asset, it is nearly all of what they own. The house is modest, the retirement account is small, and everything else is in the company. That makes the estate plan unusual. You cannot split the company into slices the way you would split a bank account, and you cannot sell it on a Tuesday to cover a bill.

This page covers the problems that come with a business-heavy estate: treating children fairly when only some of them want the company, keeping enough cash on hand, and what a surviving spouse needs if they inherit a business they never wanted. For the rest of the picture, see our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners).

## Why is a business-heavy estate harder to plan?

Because the value is real on paper and hard to reach. A $2,400,000 company might support a family comfortably, but nobody can pay the funeral director with a stake in it. Meanwhile the estate has deadlines. Taxes, final bills, a mortgage and attorney fees do not wait for a buyer.

Picture Dolores, 68, who owns a commercial laundry. The company is worth about $2,400,000. She has a home worth $350,000, $120,000 in retirement accounts and little else. Her son Marcus has worked there 20 years. Her daughter Yvette is a nurse in another state and has never set foot on the production floor. Dolores's will says, "everything equally to my two children." Yvette and Marcus would each own half of a laundry that only Marcus understands, and Yvette would have no way to turn her half into cash. That is the situation this page is for.

## How do you treat children fairly when only one works in the business?

Fair does not have to mean identical. It means each child leaves feeling that the deal was thought through. We cover the family dynamics in [passing a family business to your children, fairly](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children). Here are the legal tools.

| Tool | How it works | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Other assets | Working child gets the company, others get house, investments | Estates with real non-business assets | Often not enough to match the business |
| Life insurance | A policy, owned by a trust, pays the non-active children | Closing a large gap with cheap dollars | Premiums, health, and the 3-year rule if you transfer an existing policy |
| Buy-out note | Working child owes siblings a payout over years | Businesses with steady cash flow | Child must be able to pay; secure with the shares |
| Nonvoting shares | Siblings hold economic rights without control | Corporations, some LLCs | Need clear distribution rules or siblings feel trapped |
| Preferred return | Siblings receive a set payout before the active child | Keeping value moving to inactive heirs | Needs careful drafting and tax advice |
| Right to sell | Everyone may force a sale after a number of years | Families who might outgrow shared ownership | Can destabilize the company |

Insurance is the tool we see most. Suppose Dolores buys a $1,000,000 policy through a trust for Yvette's benefit. Yvette gets money she can use, Marcus gets the laundry, and the insurance proceeds are structured to stay out of Dolores's estate. Our guide to the [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) shows how that works.

Whatever you choose, decide how the company will be valued and when. An appraisal at death can differ widely from a gut number, and an equalizing plan built on a guess is a lawsuit waiting to happen. See [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).

## Should every child own part of the business?

Usually not. Equal ownership among heirs with different roles turns a business decision into a family vote. The sibling who works there wants to reinvest. The one who doesn't wants distributions. They each have a reasonable view, and neither can move without the other.

Better options: give control to the child who will run it and compensate the others another way, or put the business in a trust with a trustee who can balance interests. Pick the trustee carefully; our piece on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) describes the trade-offs, and a trustee who is also a beneficiary can face conflicts.

## How do you make sure there is enough cash?

Count what the estate will actually have to pay, then compare it to the cash it can reach.

1. **List claims and costs.** Final taxes, income tax on the last return, funeral, medical bills, personal debts, legal fees, and any estate or inheritance tax.
2. **List liquid assets.** Bank accounts, brokerage, insurance that pays to the estate.
3. **Subtract.** The gap is what insurance, a loan, or a sale of other assets must fill.
4. **Add business needs.** Payroll for a few months, line-of-credit renewals, and any buy-out payment that comes due.

For larger estates, federal tax law lets an estate with a closely held business pay estate tax in installments over a number of years, when the business makes up a large share of the estate. The rules are technical, so ask an attorney whether your numbers qualify; our article on [taxes when a family business passes to heirs](/learn/business-owners/taxes-when-a-family-business-passes-to-heirs) summarizes it. In 2026 the exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, so many family businesses never reach that point.

## What if the business is losing money when you die?

Then the executor has hard decisions on a short clock. They can keep operating, cut costs, sell, or close. To do any of that cleanly they need authority in writing.

- **Name a business-savvy executor or co-executor.** An accountant or industry friend can sit beside a family member who handles the paperwork.
- **Give an express power to continue the business.** Without it, some courts treat continuing a risky venture as a breach of the executor's duty.
- **Check personal guaranties.** Business debts backed by your personal guaranty become claims against the estate. See [personal guaranties and business debts at death](/learn/business-owners/personal-guaranties-and-business-debts-at-death).
- **Leave a binder.** Customer lists, bank logins, key vendors, license renewals, employee contacts. Our [letter of instruction guide](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) lists what to include.

## What does a spouse need if they inherit the business?

Options. A spouse who has never run the company may not want it, and your plan should not force them to pick between keeping it and being stuck.

Put these choices in your will or trust, so your spouse can pick one:

- keep the business and hire a manager.
- sell to the co-owner, a key employee or an outside buyer.
- take the proceeds in a lump sum or in installments.
- keep it in a trust and receive income without running it.

Check the company documents too. An operating agreement or shareholder agreement may block outside buyers or give the other owners a right of first refusal at a price they set. If your partner has the right to buy at a formula price, your spouse's only choice may be to sell cheaply. That is why the [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) and your estate documents must agree with each other.

In a second marriage the problem is sharper. Leaving the company outright to a new spouse can end with the business going to their children rather than yours. A trust can give the spouse income while the business is protected for yours. Our article on [preventing inheritance fights in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/preventing-inheritance-fights-in-a-blended-family) takes up that structure.

## Steps to take this year

1. Get an up-to-date value for the business so the numbers are real.
2. Write down who should run it, who should own it, and who should receive its value.
3. Price the gap between those answers, and decide how insurance or a buy-out note will fill it.
4. Update your will or trust to give express powers to continue, manage or sell.
5. Tell your family what you decided, even if you do not share all the numbers. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) gives you a list of the documents that go with each decision.
