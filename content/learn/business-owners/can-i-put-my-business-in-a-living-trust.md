Most owners who set up a living trust for their house or savings then pause at the business. Is it allowed? Will it upset the bank, the partners or the IRS? The short answer is that it usually works, with a few documents to check first and one set of rules for S corporations that you cannot skip.

This page walks through what actually goes into the trust, what the company documents must say, and how to complete the transfer. It is one part of our guide to [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## What does it mean to put a business in a trust?

You do not transfer the business to the trust in the sense of moving offices or renaming it. The business keeps operating under the same name, the same tax number and the same customers. What changes is who owns the ownership interest on paper.

- A **sole proprietorship** has no interest to assign, so you assign its assets: equipment, accounts, the trade name, receivables.
- An **LLC** has membership interests, and the trust becomes the member.
- A **corporation** has shares, and the trust becomes the shareholder.
- A **partnership** has partnership interests, which the partnership agreement may restrict.

In a [revocable living trust](/glossary/revocable-trust) you remain in charge. You are the trustee, and the tax reporting usually stays on your personal return. The trust document says who takes over when you cannot act. That successor trustee can vote the shares, sign for the company and sell or run it, without waiting for a probate court. The general mechanics are in our guide to [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).

## Why would a business owner want to do this?

Three reasons come up repeatedly.

1. **Probate avoidance.** An interest held in your name goes through probate, which can freeze the company for months. A trust-held interest passes by the trust terms. Our article on [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) compares the options.
2. **Incapacity.** If you are hospitalized, your successor trustee can step in. Without a trust, your family might need a court to appoint a conservator.
3. **Privacy and detail.** A will becomes public in probate. A trust does not. It can also give instructions for the business, such as keeping it for a few years, paying a manager, or selling when certain conditions are met.

A trust is a poor fit for some businesses. If your operating agreement bars transfers, or your licenses must be held by individuals, other tools may be better.

## What must you check before you transfer?

Read these before you sign an assignment.

| Document | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Operating agreement (LLC) | Consent requirements, restrictions on transferring interests, whether trusts are permitted transferees |
| Bylaws or shareholder agreement | Right of first refusal, consent of the board or other holders, transfer limits |
| Partnership agreement | Whether a partner can assign an interest without approval |
| Buy-sell agreement | Whether a transfer to a trust triggers a buyout, and who owns the insurance |
| Loan and lease documents | Change-of-control and anti-assignment clauses |
| Professional licensing rules | Whether a non-licensed owner, including a trust, is allowed |

If consent is needed, ask for it in writing and keep it with the company records. A transfer that breaches a restriction can leave you with an invalid assignment or a dispute with your partners at the worst time. Our article on [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) explains why those contracts so often control the outcome.

## What are the special rules for S corporations?

An S corporation can have only certain kinds of shareholders, and a trust is one of them only if it qualifies. If an ineligible trust receives the shares, the S election can end, with real tax cost.

The main eligible trust types are these.

- **A grantor trust.** A revocable living trust you set up is typically a grantor trust while you are alive, so it qualifies. After your death it can continue to qualify for a limited period, generally two years.
- **A qualified subchapter S trust (QSST).** Has one income beneficiary and strict rules on distributions. A beneficiary must elect it.
- **An electing small business trust (ESBT).** Can have multiple beneficiaries, but must make a timely election, and the trust's S corporation income is taxed at a high rate at the trust level.

For the family that matters at death. A revocable trust that holds S corporation shares needs language, and often an election, to move into a QSST or ESBT after you die. Without it, the trust can lose eligibility. Ask your attorney and your accountant to check the trust wording against the current IRS rules before you sign.

Consider Dev, 54, who owns 100 percent of an S corporation that installs solar panels. He puts the shares in his revocable trust and names his sister Anika as successor trustee. His trust says the shares go in equal shares to his two children. The trust has no S corporation provisions. After Dev dies, nobody files the needed election in time and the S status is questioned. The fix costs more than the original drafting.

## How do you actually move the business into the trust?

1. **Get the documents.** Operating agreement, articles, stock ledger, partnership agreement, any buy-sell agreement.
2. **Confirm consent.** Obtain written approval from co-owners, the board or lenders if needed.
3. **Sign an assignment.** An assignment of membership interest, or a stock power and new certificate, in the name of the trustee, such as "Mara Ellis, Trustee of the Mara Ellis Revocable Trust dated March 3, 2026."
4. **Update the company records.** The member list, stock ledger, and any state filing that lists owners.
5. **Notify the right people.** Your bank, insurance agent and, where required, the lender.
6. **Check beneficiary forms and insurance.** A key-person or buy-sell policy may need an owner or beneficiary change. Our guide on [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) shows the general checklist.
7. **Pour-over will.** A will that sends anything you forgot to the trust acts as a safety net.

Skipping the retitling is the most common mistake. A trust that was signed but never funded does not help the business at all, a point we explore in the [unfunded trust scenario](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust).

## What does a trust not do?

Several things surprise owners.

- **It does not reduce federal estate tax.** A revocable trust is included in your estate. The federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and most small businesses sit well under that, but some states tax at lower levels.
- **It does not override the company agreements.** The trust holds the interest subject to every restriction in the documents.
- **It does not provide management skill.** Pick a successor trustee who understands the business, or name a separate business manager and write the authority into the trust. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).
- **It does not stop creditors.** A revocable trust protects no asset from your business and personal creditors.
- **It does not change income tax basis rules.** Assets in a revocable trust at death generally receive a step-up in basis, as they would outside it.

## Does the trust change anything about taxes or filings while you are alive?

For a revocable trust, usually little. You report income as before, and the company keeps its tax number. Check with your accountant on state filings or annual reports that list owners, since some require updating when a trust becomes the holder. Mention the change to your insurance agent too, because some policies name the owner on the declaration page.
