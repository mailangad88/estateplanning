Farms and ranches are different from most family businesses. The same acres are the business, the home and most of the family's net worth. A farmer with a $4 million operation may have $50,000 in the bank. If the next generation has to sell land to pay the bills of the transfer, the plan has failed.

This guide walks through how families pass on a working farm: how to keep the operation going, how to be fair to everyone and which tax rules help. It is part of our [business owner guide](/learn/business-owners), and it pairs with our article on [passing a family business to children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children).

## Why is farm succession harder than other businesses?

Three features of farming make a plan more important.

- **The assets are land.** Land is hard to divide. Four equal shares of 640 acres often make four unworkable pieces.
- **Cash is thin.** Land and equipment are valuable, but income is seasonal. Taxes, debts and payouts to siblings have to be covered from something.
- **The successor may have worked for years at low pay.** A son who farmed beside his father for twenty years on a modest wage has a different claim than a daughter who moved to the city.

Add the national trend toward older farm owners and young people who do not want the work, and an unplanned transfer can end a farm that has been in the family for generations.

## Who gets what: running the farm versus owning it

The cleanest idea is to split operation from ownership. The child who farms controls the operation. The other children share in the value without having a say in daily decisions.

| Piece | Who usually holds it | How it is done |
|---|---|---|
| Day-to-day operation | The farming child | Employment, partnership or a farm LLC with a manager role |
| Machinery and livestock | The farming child, often by sale or gift over time | Bill of sale, gifting schedule or buy-sell terms |
| Farmland | Parents, a trust, or a land-holding LLC | Lease to the operator, with shares or a trust for all children |
| Cash value for off-farm heirs | The other children | Life insurance, other investments, buyout note |

The lease between the land owner and the operator is the hinge. A written, market-rate lease protects the farming child from being displaced and gives the non-farming children income from their share. It also keeps everyone from arguing about who is treating whom fairly.

Our article on [heirs property](/learn/property-and-assets/heirs-property) describes what can happen when a family leaves land to several people with no plan. Co-owners can force a sale, and farms are among the properties most often lost this way.

## What tax rules help farms?

Several, though each has conditions. This is general education, and a tax professional should confirm how it applies to you.

**The federal exclusion.** The basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and a married couple can shelter $30 million with portability. Many farms fall under it, but not all. With land prices as high as they are in some regions, a large operation can cross the line. Our [federal estate tax guide](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) explains the rules.

**State taxes.** Some states have an estate tax or an inheritance tax with much lower thresholds. A farm in one of those states may face a tax bill even when no federal tax is due. See our overview of [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

**Special-use valuation.** For a qualifying estate, federal law lets the executor value farmland by its use as a farm, not by what a developer might pay. The reduction is capped. To qualify, a large share of the estate must be farm property, the farm must have been in qualifying use and the owner or family must have materially participated in the operation for a set period before death. If the heir stops farming within a period after the death, some of the benefit can be recaptured.

**Extended payment.** When a closely held farm business makes up a large share of the estate, the executor can often elect to pay estate tax in installments over several years, with interest, instead of at once. It protects against a forced sale.

**Step-up in basis.** Land held at death generally gets a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which can wipe out years of untaxed appreciation for the heir. Land given away during life keeps the old basis. This is the main reason many farm families do not rush to gift the land, even when they transfer the operation. Our article on [gifting business interests to children](/learn/business-owners/gifting-business-interests-to-children) explains the trade-off.

**Conservation easements.** Placing a permanent restriction on development can lower the land's value and, in some cases, provide estate tax relief. The restriction is permanent, so it should be a deliberate choice and not a last-minute tax move.

## How do you plan the handover? A sample timeline

Management usually passes before title does. The children need years to learn the books, the lenders and the landlords.

1. **Ten years or more ahead.** Hold a family meeting. Find out who wants to farm, who does not and what each expects. Write down the goals.
2. **Five to ten years ahead.** Form an entity if it fits, sign a lease with the farming child and begin shifting equipment or livestock in stages. Get a current appraisal.
3. **Three to five years ahead.** Draft the will or trust. Name a successor manager and a trustee who understands the operation. Review insurance and farm loans.
4. **At retirement.** Move management responsibility, keep a role or a salary if you want one and confirm the lease terms.
5. **Every few years.** Revisit the plan. Land values, children's lives and tax law all change.

Consider Nadia and Tom, both 68, with 900 acres and three children. Their son Eli farms with them. Their daughters are a nurse and a teacher in other states. They put the land into an LLC, lease it to a separate farm operating company that Eli will own, and give each daughter an equal share of the land LLC with no vote on the operation. Eli gets an option to buy the daughters out over time, and life insurance covers the gap if Tom dies first. They wrote a letter to the children explaining the choice. There was still some grumbling at the table, but nobody was surprised.

## What about the farmer's own care in later years?

A farmer who needs long-term care can see the farm used up fast. Planning for that is part of succession. A durable power of attorney that names someone who understands the operation, and a [business incapacity plan](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning), keep things running if you cannot. Giving land away to protect it from nursing-home costs triggers the look-back rules, so read our guide to the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) before making large transfers.

## What mistakes do farm families make?

- **Waiting to talk.** The conversation is usually harder than the paperwork.
- **Treating equal as fair.** Equal acres can be a recipe for disagreement. Equal value is usually better.
- **Ignoring the in-laws.** A child's spouse who is not part of the plan can complicate a divorce or a death. Consider a trust for each child's share.
- **Leaving land to a child with a spouse who may later divorce.** Use trusts or entity rules to keep the land in the bloodline.
- **Forgetting the debts.** Operating loans and equipment notes follow the farm, and the heirs should know what they owe.
- **No buy-sell terms.** If the siblings share ownership, a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) sets how a sibling can leave.
