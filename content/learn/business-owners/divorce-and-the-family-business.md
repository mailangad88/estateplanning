A business is usually the largest thing a couple builds, and the hardest thing to cut in half. When a marriage ends, a court has to put a price on the company and decide who keeps it, and the owner's succession plan is often left pointing at a person who is about to be an ex-spouse.

This page covers two related situations: an owner whose spouse is not in the business, and spouses who own the business together. It also explains what to change in your own plan. For how ownership works when an owner dies, see our guide to [business owner planning](/learn/business-owners).

## Is a business marital property in a divorce?

Often, at least in part. Courts in every state divide property accumulated during a marriage in a way they call fair. In a community property state that means a basic presumption of a half-and-half split of what was earned during the marriage. In most other states the rule is equitable division, which looks at fairness and need rather than a strict half.

For a business, courts usually ask three things:

1. **When did it start?** A company formed before the wedding starts with a separate-property base.
2. **What grew it?** The increase in value from either spouse's work, or from joint money, is often shared even when only one name is on the paperwork.
3. **How is title held?** A name on the shares matters less than where the money and effort came from.

Priya, 44, founded a landscaping company at 30 and married at 36. The company was worth $300,000 on the wedding day and $1.4 million at filing. Her spouse never worked there. In many states, the $300,000 would be hers, and the growth might be shared in part, depending on how much came from her labor, outside market growth or marital funds. The rules vary, which is why a lawyer's early read on your state matters.

## How is the business valued?

A court usually appoints or accepts a business appraiser. That person looks at earnings, assets, debts, goodwill and whether the value depends on the owner personally. A business that would fall apart without you is often worth less to a buyer than a business with managers and contracts in place. Our page on [how a business is valued](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) describes the methods in plain terms. A divorce valuation and an estate tax valuation are separate exercises, but they use similar tools.

A word of caution. A valuation can lower or raise a number depending on which discounts apply, such as for a minority stake or lack of marketability. States differ on whether those discounts are allowed in a divorce. Do not assume that the number you used for planning will be accepted.

## What are the usual outcomes?

| Outcome | How it works | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| Owner keeps the business | Spouse receives other assets, such as the house or retirement accounts, worth the same | When other assets are enough |
| Buyout over time | Owner pays the spouse's share in installments, often with interest | When cash is tight but the company is healthy |
| Sale of the business | Company is sold and proceeds are divided | When neither spouse can run it or buy the other out |
| Both keep ownership | Spouses stay co-owners under a written agreement | Rare; works only if they can still work together |

The business rarely continues in joint ownership with an ex-spouse because the operating agreement and basic management make that hard. Judges usually prefer to avoid it.

## What if you and your spouse own the business together?

Two people who are married, co-own a company and then divorce face two breakups at once. The first is the marriage, which a family court handles. The second is the business, where the operating agreement, shareholder agreement or partnership agreement controls how an owner can leave.

Marcus and Tanya, both 50, own a bakery as 50-50 members of an LLC. They file for divorce. If their operating agreement has a divorce clause, it likely says what happens: one member buys the other out at a stated formula, or the departing member's rights shrink to a share of profit. If it has no such clause, they may be arguing in court over value, control and who runs the shop in the meantime. A pending divorce does not change either spouse's duties to the company.

If this is you, check your agreement now. If you have no written operating agreement, see our page on [LLC ownership at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) for what to put in one, and a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) for how to set a price in advance.

## How can you protect the business before a divorce?

- **A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement** that names the business as separate property and sets how growth is treated. This works best when both sides have separate lawyers and full disclosure.
- **A buy-sell or operating agreement** restricting transfers to non-members and setting a buyout formula if an owner divorces.
- **Clean books and clear separation** of personal and business money. Mixing funds invites the argument that the spouse contributed to the company.
- **Fair pay for the owner** and, where a spouse works there, a written job and salary. It helps show what the business produces and what is owed.
- **Insurance on the owner** so the company can afford a buyout if one comes.

Moving the company to a family member, a trust or a new entity when divorce is likely rarely helps, and a court can undo it. See our page on [estate planning during separation or a pending divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-during-separation-or-a-pending-divorce) for what is restricted while a case is open.

## What should you update in your succession plan?

A divorce quietly breaks the plan around the business. Your spouse may be named as your agent, successor manager, trustee, executor or beneficiary of your company shares and your key-person policy. Check each of these:

1. **Power of attorney.** Replace a spouse as agent if you no longer want them to act for the company.
2. **Operating agreement or shareholder agreement.** Look for the clause naming a successor manager or a spouse's rights.
3. **Will and trust.** Some states cancel provisions for an ex-spouse automatically on divorce, and some do not. Confirm, then sign new documents.
4. **Beneficiary forms.** Forms for life insurance, buy-sell policies and retirement accounts follow the form, not the divorce decree, unless state law says otherwise.
5. **Bank signature cards.** Remove an ex-spouse from business accounts.
6. **Buy-sell terms.** If the spouse was named as a buyer or seller, redo the agreement.

Our page on [estate planning after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) has the full checklist, and [what if you die during a divorce](/learn/what-if/death-during-divorce) explains why timing matters.

## How state law changes this

Community property or equitable division, the treatment of separate-property growth, whether valuation discounts are allowed and whether divorce cancels will provisions all vary by state. Our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides cover the local estate rules, but a business divorce nearly always needs a local family law attorney in addition to estate planning counsel.
