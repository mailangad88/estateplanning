Passing a family business to the next generation is harder than passing a house or an investment account. A house can be sold and split. A business has to be run by someone, and it cannot be split into four equal parts without risking the thing that gave it value. Add a few siblings with different interests and different spouses, and a sound plan becomes the difference between a legacy and a lawsuit.

This guide walks through the choices that matter most: who gets control, how to be fair to everyone else, and how to move the business while managing taxes. It is part of our [business owner guide](/learn/business-owners).

## Is equal the same as fair?

No. Equal means each child gets the same share. Fair means each child receives what you think is right given what each contributed, needs and wants. The two can match, but often don't.

Consider Rosa, 66, who owns a plumbing supply company worth $3 million. Her son Carlos has worked there for 22 years. Her daughter Elena is a teacher. Her youngest son Mateo lives overseas and has never worked there.

- An **equal** plan gives each one-third of the shares. Carlos now answers to two siblings with no operating knowledge, and any sale needs their vote.
- A **fair** plan might give Carlos the voting shares, give Elena and Mateo non-voting shares or an equal share of cash and investments, and require Carlos to buy their shares over ten years if they want to be bought out.

There is no single right answer. What matters is that you choose deliberately and tell the children why.

## What are your options for transferring the business?

| Option | How it works | Main tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|
| Leave it at death | Interest passes by will or trust | Full step-up in basis; but no time for the successor to learn, and probate unless in a trust |
| Gift during life | Transfer interests now, often in stages | Removes future growth from your estate; recipient takes your basis, with no step-up |
| Sell to a child | Child pays over time, often by note | You receive income; needs a supportable price; the child carries debt |
| Sell to an outside buyer and divide proceeds | Cash is easy to split equally | Business leaves the family |
| Family trust | Interests held by a trustee for children | Control and protection, but needs a capable trustee |

The right option depends on how much your family needs the business income, your age, estate tax exposure and whether the child is ready.

## How do you give control to one child and still be fair to others?

Separate control from value. Four tools do most of the work.

1. **Voting and non-voting interests.** Convert the business into two classes. The active child gets the voting class. Others get non-voting interests that still participate in profits and in a sale. This usually requires an amendment to the operating agreement or articles, which an attorney should draft.
2. **Life insurance equalization.** You buy a policy for the benefit of the children outside the business. The proceeds, which can be held in a trust, give them value equal to what the successor receives in the company. See our article on [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) for how to set up ownership properly.
3. **Other assets.** Direct the house, investments or real estate to the non-working children. Real estate used by the company can be held separately and leased to it, giving non-working children rent income.
4. **Buyout rights.** Give the non-working children the right to require the company or the successor to purchase their interests over time at a fair formula price, under a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).

A trust can hold the shares and set rules, which is especially useful when children are young or when a child has creditor or divorce risk. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).

## What are the tax issues?

The main ones are gift tax, estate tax and income tax basis.

- **Gift tax.** In 2026 you can give up to $19,000 per recipient per year without filing a gift tax return, under the [annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion). Larger gifts use up part of your lifetime exclusion, which is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. A return is still needed for gifts above the annual amount, even when no tax is due.
- **Estate tax.** Most family businesses fall below the federal threshold, but a larger one can exceed it. Some states tax estates at far lower thresholds. Federal law has special relief for closely held businesses, including the option to pay estate tax in installments over a period of years when a business makes up a large share of the estate. These provisions have conditions, so ask a tax advisor early.
- **Basis.** If your child inherits the interest, it generally gets a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) to date-of-death value, which can eliminate gain accumulated during your life. If you gift it, your child takes your original basis and may pay capital gains tax on a later sale. For a business you expect the child to keep for decades, this may matter less than the estate tax benefit of removing growth. For one you expect to sell, it matters a lot.
- **Valuation.** Interests in a closely held business without voting control or ready markets are often valued at a discount for gift and estate purposes. The IRS scrutinizes these, so documented, independent appraisals are essential.

## How do you prepare the child who will take over?

Succession fails more often from lack of preparation than from poor paperwork. Practical steps:

- Give the successor real authority and accountability years before the transition, including a seat in the bank and vendor meetings.
- Pay a market salary, documented in writing, so no one believes the business was a hidden inheritance.
- Hire an outside advisor or board member who can speak when siblings cannot.
- Write down the operating manual: key accounts, pricing logic, vendor terms and licenses.
- Set a transition date for stepping back, and keep to it.

## When should you talk to your children?

Early, and with each of them. Surprises create the conflicts that documents cannot fix. Explain what you decided, why, and what each child gets, even if the answers are uncomfortable. Our guide on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) covers how to open the conversation, and whether to do it in a family meeting or one on one.

## What if a child doesn't want the business?

Then do not force it. A reluctant successor leads to a sale under bad conditions later. Consider a key employee as manager, with the family retaining ownership and a trust or independent board overseeing it. Or plan a sale on your own terms, and divide the proceeds among all the children. The [succession planning overview](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) explains how to compare these paths.
