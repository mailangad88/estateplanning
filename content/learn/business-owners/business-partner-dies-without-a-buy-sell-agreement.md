You wake up to a call from your partner's wife. He died overnight. You own the company together, you have no buy-sell agreement, and by lunchtime you have realized that nobody knows who owns what from this morning on. The bank wants someone to sign. The payroll runs on Friday.

This page is for that person, and also for the owner who is reading this while their partner is healthy and wants to know what a missing agreement actually means. We take it in the order that matters: the first month, the question of who now owns the share, the options for buying it, and the paperwork to put in place afterward. For the wider picture see our [guide to estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## What are the first things to do in the first 30 days?

Keep the company operating, funded and legally able to act. Everything else can wait a few weeks.

1. **Gather the documents.** Operating agreement, bylaws, partnership agreement, shareholder agreement, loan agreements, the lease, the insurance policies. Read every one for transfer restrictions, death clauses and signing authority.
2. **Confirm who can sign.** In an LLC, check who the managers are. In a corporation, the officers continue. In a general partnership, the surviving partner generally has authority to wind up affairs, and may have more. If the deceased signed everything, ask your bank what resolution it needs.
3. **Look for life insurance.** Ask the family and the company's insurance agent. A policy on your partner may exist that nobody linked to the business.
4. **Notify key parties carefully.** The bank, the main lender, the landlord, the insurer and your biggest customers should hear from you before they hear from rumor. Some loans have clauses triggered by death.
5. **Make a list of immediate cash commitments.** Payroll, taxes, vendors. If your partner's signature was needed on the account, ask the bank how the estate's authorized representative fits in.
6. **Speak to an attorney before you speak to the family about price.** Early statements about value tend to stick.

Our [after-a-death checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) covers the personal side for the family, and you may need it for your own household too.

## Who owns your partner's share now?

Their estate does, until the share is distributed. With no agreement, the share does not vanish and it does not pass to you. It becomes part of the deceased partner's estate and passes under their will or, if there is none, under [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession). In most cases that means a spouse or children.

Until a court names an executor, nobody may have legal authority to act for the estate. That gap matters. If nobody can vote the shares or approve a decision on behalf of the deceased partner's interest, a company with a two-person vote can deadlock. Our page on [what happens when there is no executor](/learn/probate/what-happens-if-there-is-no-executor) explains how that gap is filled.

The default rules depend on the entity.

| Entity | Default result when an owner dies, absent a written agreement | What the heirs typically get |
|---|---|---|
| General partnership | In many states the partnership dissolves or the partner is dissociated; the estate is owed the share's value | A claim for value, not necessarily a seat at the table |
| LLC | State statute governs; often the heirs receive economic rights only | Distributions; a vote only if members admit them |
| Corporation | Shares pass as personal property | Full ownership of the stock, including votes |
| Sole owner plus a minority holder | The company continues; the estate holds the shares | Whatever the share percentage controls |

Your state's rules vary in details, and some states changed their partnership and LLC statutes in recent years, so confirm with an attorney in your state. Our page on [LLC ownership at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) goes through the LLC side in more depth.

## What if I do not want to work with my partner's spouse or children?

You have three honest choices: buy them out, make a working arrangement, or sell the company.

A buyout is usually best for both sides. The family wants cash, not a role in a business they do not understand. You want control. The sticking points are price and payment. Without an agreement, you will need to agree on a value, probably with an independent appraisal, and decide how to pay. Insurance may cover some of it. Otherwise the family may accept installments over several years, secured by the shares, if the business can carry them.

Take Joel and Nina, who co-own a landscaping company worth about $1,400,000. Joel dies. His widow Carmen is a nurse with no interest in running crews. Nina offers $600,000 for his 45 percent, in installments over four years with interest and security in the shares. Carmen's advisor suggests an appraisal first. The appraisal comes in at $1,550,000 for the business, and they settle at a price on that number with a down payment drawn from the company's line of credit. It is not the speed Nina wanted, but nobody is angry.

A working arrangement can also work. The spouse stays as a passive owner with a fixed distribution, a board seat or consulting rights, while you run the business. Put it in writing, including how decisions get made and what happens if they want to sell later.

If neither works, a sale of the whole company may follow. The estate cannot be forced to sell, but a minority owner in a small company gets little cash back from a stalemate.

## What if the heirs want to sell to someone I do not know?

Check what restrictions exist on transfer. In a corporation with no shareholder agreement, the heirs can generally sell their shares to anybody, though few buyers want a minority stake in a private company, and the discount is steep. In an LLC or partnership, the operating or partnership agreement usually controls who becomes a member, and a buyer may get only the right to distributions.

If you have no document at all, state default law applies. Some states give a partner the right to block a new partner. Most states do not give you a right to buy first, which is why a right of first refusal is worth negotiating now, even if the other owner is gone and you are dealing with the estate.

## What if my partner is alive but not well?

Do not wait. Incapacity creates the same gap as death, with less closure. A partner in a long illness cannot sign. An agent under a [durable power of attorney](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney) may be able to, if the power of attorney covers business interests and the documents allow it. Our page on [business owner incapacity planning](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) covers what to put in place, and it applies to you too.

## What should the two of you sign while both are healthy?

A short buy-sell agreement, even a simple one, changes everything. A workable first draft needs only a few clauses.

- Triggers: death, disability, divorce, retirement.
- Who buys: you or the company.
- Price: an agreed number reviewed each year, or a formula with an appraisal if the number is stale.
- Payment: insurance proceeds, then a note for the rest.
- A restriction on transfers to anyone outside the group without a chance to buy first.
- A spouse's signature that acknowledges the agreement.

Our [buy-sell agreement overview](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) explains each clause, and [cross-purchase versus redemption](/learn/business-owners/cross-purchase-vs-entity-redemption) helps you pick a structure. Each partner should also read their own will against the agreement. The will can only leave what the documents allow.
