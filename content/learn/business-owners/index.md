If you own a business, your estate plan has a harder job than most. A salaried employee has a house, a retirement account and some savings, all of which pass cleanly through a will or beneficiary forms. You also have an enterprise that needs managers, customers, lenders, employees and a person with the legal authority to sign.

This guide explains how the pieces fit: the succession plan, the buy-sell agreement, entity ownership at death, family transfers and incapacity. Each section links to a detailed article. If you read only one thing, read the section on coordination, because that is where plans most often break.

## Why is the business the hardest asset to plan for?

A business is illiquid, hard to value, dependent on people and subject to private contracts. Four features make it different from other property.

- **It is not easy to divide.** You can split a brokerage account three ways. You cannot split a restaurant three ways and expect it to run.
- **It is hard to price.** No market quote exists. Value depends on earnings, customers, contracts and the owner's role, and different advisors reach different numbers.
- **It depends on you.** In many small companies, your relationships and knowledge are a large part of the value, and they do not transfer at death.
- **It is governed by its own documents.** An operating agreement, shareholder agreement or partnership agreement can limit who can inherit, force a sale or override your will.

Add to that the family dimension: spouses with rights in the business, children with different interests, and partners who did not agree to share a company with your relatives.

## What are the three exits you must plan for?

Every owner leaves in one of three ways. A complete plan covers all of them.

| Exit | When it happens | What the plan must supply |
|---|---|---|
| Death | Any time, often without warning | Authority for a successor, cash for the family, a buyer or a transfer path |
| Disability | Any time, from weeks to permanently | A power of attorney, a successor manager, income protection, and a rule for when a disability becomes a buyout |
| Chosen exit | On your timetable | A valuation, a buyer or successor, financing, tax planning, and time to transition |

Owners who plan only for the chosen exit are exposed on the other two. Owners who plan only for death leave their business exposed to disability, which is more common during working years. Our article on [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) shows how to assemble the four questions every plan must answer: who manages, who owns, what is it worth, and how is it paid for.

## How do buy-sell agreements fit in?

A buy-sell agreement is the most important single document for owners of a business with partners. It says what happens to a share when an owner dies, becomes disabled, divorces or leaves. It fixes the buyer, sets the price method, and usually relies on insurance to provide the cash.

Without one, a deceased partner's family may hold a minority stake with no market, and the surviving partners may be forced into business with people they did not choose. With one, the family receives cash and the survivors keep control. The details of structures, pricing and funding are in [buy-sell agreements: what they are and how they are funded](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).

Three questions to ask about any existing agreement:

1. Is the price current, or based on a formula that no longer fits?
2. Is there actual money behind it, such as active insurance in the right amount?
3. Does it match my will, trust and spouse's rights?

## What happens to LLC and corporation ownership when you die?

Your ownership interest becomes part of your estate. The company continues, but control depends on the documents and the entity type. In many states, the heir of an LLC member receives only the right to distributions until the other members admit them, while the heir of a corporate shareholder receives full voting rights in proportion to shares. A single-member LLC can be left with nobody authorized to act, and S corporations restrict who can hold shares.

Moving the interest into a revocable trust can avoid probate and allow a successor trustee to act immediately, but the company documents must permit it. Details, including the S corporation trust issues and community property effects, are in [what happens to your LLC or corporation shares when you die](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death).

## How do you pass a business to children fairly?

Pass control to the child who will run the company and value to the others. Tools include voting and non-voting shares, life insurance, other assets, buyout rights and trusts. The tax picture differs for gifts during life and transfers at death, because lifetime gifts carry over your basis while inherited interests generally receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis).

The harder part is human. Siblings may disagree about salary, distributions, growth and when to sell. Clear roles, written rules and an honest conversation matter as much as the legal structure. See [passing a family business to your children, fairly](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children).

## What if you cannot work?

A stroke, an accident or an early diagnosis can freeze a company as fast as death can, and without the finality of a will. The central tools are a durable power of attorney with business powers, a successor manager named in the company documents, banking authority, disability insurance, and a written emergency binder.

If you do nothing, a family may need a court-supervised conservatorship before anyone can sign, which is slow and public. The full checklist is in [incapacity planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning).

## How should your documents work together?

This is the section that matters most. Business owners typically have two sets of documents, written at different times by different people, and they rarely agree.

### The coordination checklist

- **Operating or shareholder agreement.** Who may hold an interest, who has consent rights, what buyout terms exist.
- **Buy-sell agreement.** Price, buyer, funding, definitions of death and disability.
- **Will or revocable trust.** Who receives the interest or the sale proceeds, who serves as executor or trustee, and whether they can deal with the business.
- **Power of attorney.** Whether the agent has business powers and whether the company recognizes the agent.
- **Beneficiary designations and insurance.** Whether policies are owned and payable the way the buy-sell requires. The [beneficiary designation guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations) explains why these forms override wills.
- **Spousal documents.** Consents, prenuptial or postnuptial agreements and community property considerations.
- **Personal guarantees and loans.** Whether lenders can call a note on a change of control.

When documents conflict, the contract usually beats the will for the business interest, and the beneficiary form beats the will for the insurance. That is how a plan that looks fine on paper ends up distributing the opposite of what you intended.

### Example

Nadia owns 60 percent of a landscaping company and has two children. Her will leaves her entire estate equally to both. Her operating agreement lets the company buy a deceased member's interest at book value. Her life insurance, which was meant to fund a buyout, names her ex-husband because she never changed the form. When Nadia dies, the company buys the interest at a low price and the insurance goes to the wrong person. The children inherit far less than Nadia thought they would.

## What are the tax issues in estate planning for owners?

Taxes affect the structure, but they shouldn't be the first decision.

- **Federal estate tax.** Applies above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, with portability for married couples who can shelter up to $30 million together. The rate is 40 percent on the excess. See our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).
- **State estate and inheritance taxes.** A number of states have thresholds well below the federal level. A business can push a moderate estate over the line.
- **Income tax basis.** Interests that pass at death generally get a stepped-up basis, while gifted interests keep the donor's basis.
- **Gift tax.** The annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient in 2026. Larger gifts use lifetime exclusion and require a return.
- **Closely held business relief.** Federal law offers installment payment of estate tax in some cases when a closely held business makes up a large part of the estate. The qualifying rules are technical.
- **Valuation.** The IRS can challenge discounts, so documented appraisals are important.

Your accountant should model these items with actual numbers.

## How does a trust help a business owner?

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts) can hold your ownership interest so that a successor trustee, not a court, takes charge when you die or become incapacitated. It keeps the transfer private, avoids probate for that asset, and can specify how the business is managed for beneficiaries who are young or inexperienced. It does not by itself reduce estate tax, and it does not override the company's documents. Where estate tax is a concern, an irrevocable trust may be used to move growth out of the estate, with trade-offs in control and basis.

If you own S corporation stock, trust language matters. Ask your attorney to check it before you sign.

## What does a practical timeline look like?

1. **This month.** Locate your entity documents and policies. Name a backup signer at your bank. Sign a durable power of attorney if you don't have one.
2. **Next three months.** Get an independent valuation. Review or create a buy-sell agreement. Meet with an attorney, accountant and insurance agent together.
3. **Within the year.** Fund any gaps in insurance. Transfer the business interest to a trust if appropriate. Write the emergency binder. Talk to your successor and family.
4. **Every two or three years.** Review everything and update values.

## What does it cost to get this wrong?

The losses are rarely dramatic at first. They show up as small decisions made under pressure: a distressed sale to the first buyer, a key employee who leaves, a lender who calls a loan, a family that stops speaking. Owners who plan usually spend a modest amount on legal fees, appraisals and insurance premiums. Families that do not plan often spend more on probate fees, court-supervised conservatorships, tax advice and litigation, and then accept a lower price for the business.

A useful test is to imagine that you are unreachable for ninety days, starting tomorrow. Who pays payroll on the first of the month? Who talks to the bank? Who signs the renewal on the lease? Who decides whether to take the large order that arrived on Tuesday? If the answer to any of those is "nobody" or "my spouse, who has never seen the books," then the plan has a gap.

## What do owners with partners need to think about differently?

Partners complicate every question. Your partner has an interest in who inherits your share, how it is priced and whether your spouse gets a seat at the table. You have the same interest in theirs. Three practical points follow.

- **Decide together.** A buy-sell agreement works only if everyone signs it while healthy and every owner is comfortable with the price method.
- **Insure each other.** If life or disability insurance funds the buyout, confirm who owns each policy and who pays the premiums, and check that the policies are still in force every year.
- **Include spouses.** Ask spouses to read and, where state law allows, consent to the agreement, so that a surviving spouse cannot later claim an interest outside it.

Owners who work alone have the opposite problem: no one else knows the business. For them, the written emergency binder and a named backup are the most valuable steps.
