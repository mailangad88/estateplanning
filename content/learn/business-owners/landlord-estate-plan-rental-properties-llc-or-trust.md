Rental property is the one asset that behaves like a business and looks like real estate. It has customers, a bank account and employees of a sort, and it can hurt someone. When the owner dies, all of that keeps going whether or not anyone is ready for it. Rent is due on the first. A pipe bursts in unit 2. The mortgage payment does not pause.

This page covers what happens to rentals at a landlord's death, how a living trust and an LLC each help, and where they do not. It is part of our guide to [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners). If you want the tax side for heirs, see our article on [taxes on inherited rental property](/learn/property-and-assets/inherited-rental-property-taxes).

## What happens to a rental property when the landlord dies?

The property passes the way any real estate passes: under the will, by the trust, or by your state's intestacy rules. Until the right person has legal authority, though, nobody can sign a new lease, hire a roofer or collect rent in the owner's name.

Here is how the common setups behave on the first day.

| How the rental is titled | Who can act | Probate needed? |
|---|---|---|
| Your name alone, will names an executor | Executor, after the court appoints them | Yes, in the state where the property sits |
| Your name alone, no will | Administrator chosen by the court | Yes |
| Revocable living trust | Successor trustee, with the trust and a death certificate | No |
| LLC owned by you personally | Executor steps into your ownership, then follows the operating agreement | Yes, for the LLC interest |
| LLC owned by your trust | Successor trustee votes the interest and any named manager continues | No |

Leases survive. A tenant with a signed lease keeps the right to stay for the term, and the estate or trust becomes the landlord. Month-to-month tenants usually stay too, though the new owner can change terms with proper notice under state law.

## Who holds the deposits and pays the bills?

This is where families get hurt, because the details live in the owner's head. Before anything else, someone needs a list.

1. **Tenants and lease dates.** Names, rent amounts, due dates, lease end dates.
2. **Security deposits.** The amount held per unit and the account where it sits.
3. **Money going out.** Mortgage, property tax, insurance, utilities, landscaping, and the property manager if there is one.
4. **Who does repairs.** The plumber and the electrician who answer the phone.
5. **Where the logins are.** Rent portal, bank, insurance, the county tax site.

Take Walter, 66, who owns four small rentals in his own name. He dies in March. His daughter Ines is named executor, but the court takes seven weeks to issue letters. During that time rent keeps arriving by Zelle into Walter's personal account, which the bank has frozen. The mortgage on two units goes unpaid for a month. A tenant in the third unit mails a deposit for the following year to a dead man. Ines eventually sorts it out, but it costs her about two months of late fees, a lapsed insurance policy she has to reinstate, and a tenant who leaves.

Had Walter's four buildings been in a living trust, his successor trustee could have walked into the bank the following week.

## Can a living trust handle rental properties?

Yes, and for most landlords it is the first tool to reach for. A [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) owns the property during your life while you stay in control as trustee. When you die, the [successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) takes over management without a court. Rent keeps flowing and the leases carry on.

The benefit grows if you own property in more than one state. Real estate outside your home state normally needs its own court case, called [ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate), with a local attorney and local fees. A trust that holds each deed removes that step, as our guide on [owning property in another state through a trust](/learn/trusts/living-trusts-and-moving-or-owning-property-in-another-state) explains.

A few practical points:

- **The deed has to be changed.** Writing a trust is not enough. Each rental needs a new deed into the trust, a step covered in [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
- **Check the loan.** Federal law protects a transfer of your home into your own living trust from a due-on-sale demand. That protection is tied to residential property you occupy, so it does not clearly cover a rental. Ask the lender, or have your attorney do so, before you record.
- **Update the insurance.** The landlord policy should name the trust as an insured owner, or the claim you need later may be argued.
- **Keep the books clean.** A revocable trust uses your Social Security number during your life, so the tax picture does not change while you are alive.

## What does an LLC do for a rental property?

An LLC addresses a different problem. It tries to separate the property from your other assets, so a lawsuit over one building cannot reach your savings or your home. It does not, on its own, change what happens at your death.

If you own a rental through an LLC, you own the membership interest, and that interest is what passes at your death. If it is in your name, it goes through probate like anything else. This is the situation described in our article on [what happens to LLC interests at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death). The fix is to assign the membership interest to your living trust, so the trust owns the LLC and the LLC owns the building.

The structure many landlords end up with:

1. The **LLC** holds title to the building and signs the leases.
2. Your **living trust** owns the LLC membership interest.
3. A **successor manager** is named in the operating agreement.
4. A **will** pours any stray assets into the trust.

Some points on the other side of the ledger. An LLC will not protect you from your own negligence, and a court may disregard it if you pay personal bills from the LLC account. Some states charge annual LLC fees. Lenders often write loans that bar transfers to a new borrower, so putting a mortgaged building into an LLC can set off a due-on-sale clause. And insurance still matters more than the entity. A well-written umbrella policy often protects a small landlord better than an extra company does.

## What about taxes when heirs inherit rentals?

Most heirs receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which resets the tax cost of the property to its value at the date of death. That means depreciation you claimed over the years is generally not taxed to your heirs when they sell shortly after. For a landlord who has depreciated a building for 25 years, this is a large reason not to give the properties away during life, because a lifetime gift keeps your old basis. Our article on [giving your house to a child](/learn/property-and-assets/giving-your-house-to-your-child-taxes-and-trade-offs) shows how that trade works.

An LLC with one owner is usually ignored for income tax, so the step-up generally works the same way. Two-owner LLCs and partnerships have their own rules and are worth running past a tax preparer. The 2026 federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000 per person, so most landlords will not owe federal estate tax, though some states tax estates at lower levels.

## How do you plan for a rental portfolio?

Not every landlord needs the same plan. A rough guide:

- **One or two small rentals, modest equity:** a living trust, good insurance and a short instruction sheet.
- **Several properties or properties in other states:** a living trust plus one or more LLCs, with a named successor manager.
- **Heirs who do not get along or do not want to be landlords:** a trust that tells the trustee to sell, or hire a property manager, rather than pass the work to the children.
- **Properties with large debts:** check each loan before you retitle and talk to a tax adviser.

Whatever you choose, tell your successor now. Ask whether they want the job, and pay for a property manager if they do not. A reluctant child managing a tenant dispute in the first month is how families lose both the property and the relationship. Our guide to [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) shows which of your holdings would go through probate.

## What should you do this month?

1. Make the tenant, deposit and bill list described above.
2. Pull the deed for each property and note how it is titled.
3. Ask each lender what they require before a transfer into a trust or LLC.
4. Name a successor trustee and, if you have an LLC, a successor manager.
5. Review your insurance, including liability limits, with the trust as an insured.
