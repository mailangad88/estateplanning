If you and your partners have decided to sign a buy-sell agreement, the next argument is usually about structure. Does each owner buy the others' shares, or does the company do it? The accountant says one thing, the insurance agent another, and everyone is a little afraid of choosing wrong.

This page is about that choice and its tax consequences. For the basics of what a buy-sell contract covers, see our [overview of buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements). Here we compare the two structures head to head and flag the traps. It is part of our [guide to estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## What is the difference in plain terms?

In a cross-purchase agreement, the owners are the buyers. Each owner agrees to buy a deceased co-owner's shares, and each usually owns insurance on the others. In an entity redemption agreement, the company is the buyer. It owns the insurance on each owner and pays the estate when an owner dies.

Take three equal owners of a roofing company, Hector, Dana and Luis. In a cross-purchase plan, Dana and Luis each hold a policy on Hector, Hector and Luis each hold one on Dana, and so on. That is six policies. In a redemption plan, the company holds three policies, one per owner, and when Hector dies it pays his widow and cancels his shares. Dana and Luis now own half each, without having written a personal check.

## How do the two structures compare?

| Question | Cross-purchase | Entity redemption |
|---|---|---|
| Who buys the shares | The surviving owners | The company |
| Who owns the insurance | Each owner, on the others | The company, on each owner |
| Number of policies | Owners x (owners minus 1) | One per owner |
| Survivors' tax basis | Rises by the price they pay | Generally unchanged |
| Reachable by company creditors | Proceeds sit outside the company | Policy value and proceeds are company assets |
| Effect on company value for estate tax | None from the policy | Can raise value (see Connelly below) |
| Works well with | Two or three owners of similar age | Four or more owners, uneven ownership |
| Unequal ages or health | Costly: younger owners pay for older owners' coverage | Company absorbs it |

## Which structure gives the owners a better tax result?

Cross-purchase usually wins on basis. When a survivor buys shares from the estate, the purchase price becomes that owner's tax basis in those shares. Years later, when the survivor sells the whole company, the gain is measured from a higher starting point. In a redemption, the shares simply disappear, and the survivors' basis in what they already own does not increase.

On the estate's side, the picture is the same in both structures. A deceased owner's shares generally receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) to date-of-death value, so when the estate sells them at that value to the owners or the company, there is little or no gain to tax. That is why the price in the agreement should stay close to real value.

Entity type changes the analysis. Partnerships and LLCs taxed as partnerships have their own rules, including an election that can adjust the company's inside basis after a partner dies, which can narrow the gap between the two structures. C corporations and S corporations have separate rules on redemptions, and an S corporation that redeems may have to watch its accounts. Treat anything you read online, including this page, as the starting point for a modeling session with your tax advisor.

## What is the Connelly problem with company-owned insurance?

In 2024 the Supreme Court decided Connelly v. United States, a case about two brothers who owned a building-supply company with a redemption agreement. The company held life insurance to fund the buyout. When one brother died, the Court held that the insurance proceeds counted toward the company's value for estate tax, and that the company's duty to redeem the shares did not reduce that value.

The practical result: with a redemption plan, the company's value, and so the deceased owner's shares, can be pushed up by the very policy meant to buy them. For an estate far below the federal exemption of $15,000,000 per person in 2026, this may never produce a tax bill. For an estate near or above that line, or in a state with a lower threshold, it can be costly. A cross-purchase plan avoids the issue because the policies belong to the owners, not the company. A hybrid or a trust-owned arrangement can also address it.

If your agreement was written before 2024 and uses company-owned insurance, ask your attorney whether it should be revisited. Our page on [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) explains how the numbers get built.

## Is the insurance premium deductible, and are the proceeds taxable?

The premium is generally not deductible. Federal law denies a deduction when the payer is directly or indirectly the beneficiary of the policy, which is exactly the buy-sell setup. The proceeds, in return, are generally received free of income tax.

That trade holds only if the policy stays in the right hands. The transfer-for-value rule can make part of the proceeds taxable when a policy was sold to someone for money or other value. There are exceptions, including transfers to the insured, to a partner of the insured, to a partnership in which the insured is a partner, and to a corporation in which the insured is a shareholder or officer. Moving an old policy between owners to simplify a cross-purchase plan is the classic place to stumble.

Insurance owned inside an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust) is a third approach for owners with large estates, since the death benefit then stays out of the insured's own taxable estate. That adds a trust, and the trust needs its own rules to pay premiums and to buy the shares.

## How do you decide?

Walk through these questions, in order.

1. **How many owners are there?** With two or three, cross-purchase is manageable. With five, the policy count (twenty) becomes silly.
2. **Are the owners close in age and health?** If one owner is fifty-eight and another thirty-four, the younger one will pay for most of the older one's coverage under cross-purchase, and may resent it.
3. **Is the estate near a tax threshold?** If so, avoid company-owned insurance unless an attorney has tested it.
4. **Do owners have enough personal cash flow to carry premiums?** If not, a redemption plan spreads the cost across the company.
5. **What is the entity?** A partnership-taxed LLC and an S corporation do not behave alike. Ask for a side-by-side projection for your entity.
6. **Who should hold the policies for safety?** Some groups use a separate trustee or an escrow agent who holds all policies in a cross-purchase plan, which avoids needing one policy per pair of owners and keeps the policies alive if a person leaves.

Two other options are worth knowing about. A wait-and-see or flexible agreement lets the company buy first and the owners buy what the company does not, so the decision is made when the facts are known. And in a company with a mix of family and non-family owners, the family may prefer a [succession approach](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) that does not rely on a forced sale at all.

## What mistakes show up most often?

- **No one checked the policies.** The agreement says each owner owns a policy on the others, but the one on Dana lapsed two years ago.
- **Face amounts never moved.** The business tripled in value and the coverage did not.
- **The trigger list is thin.** Death is covered, but disability and divorce are not, which is when partners actually fall out.
- **Conflicting wills.** An owner dies with a will that tries to leave shares to a child, in conflict with the agreement. The agreement controls the shares, but the dispute costs money.
- **The spouse never signed.** In community property states especially, a spouse who did not consent may argue they own half of the shares.
