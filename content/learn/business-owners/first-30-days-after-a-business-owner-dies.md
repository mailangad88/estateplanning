The owner died on a Tuesday. On Wednesday the bakery needs flour, the crew expects to be paid on Friday, and a client is asking whether the Monday delivery is still on. Nobody has read the will yet. The owner kept the passwords in their head.

If you are the spouse, the manager or the partner who has been left holding the keys, this page puts the first month in order. It assumes a small or mid-sized company. The planning that prevents this scramble is in our [guide for business owners](/learn/business-owners) and in [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).

## Who actually has authority?

Before you do anything with the company's money, work out what kind of business it is. The answer decides who can sign.

| Structure | Who can act in the first days | What stops people |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Nobody has formal authority until an executor is appointed | The business is the owner's personal property |
| Single-member LLC | A named successor manager, if the operating agreement has one | Without one, authority waits for the executor or estate |
| Multi-member LLC | Remaining members, under the operating agreement | Transfer limits and voting rules |
| Corporation | Other officers and directors, under the bylaws | A sole director or officer who has died |
| Partnership | Surviving partners, under the partnership agreement | Dissolution rules if the agreement is silent |
| Business in a living trust | The successor trustee | Needs the trust document and a death certificate |

Read the paper the owner left, in this order: the operating agreement, shareholder agreement or bylaws, the buy-sell agreement, the trust, the will, then the power of attorney (which ends at death and no longer helps). Our page on [documents every business owner needs](/learn/business-owners/documents-every-business-owner-needs) lists what should exist. For a sole proprietor, [what happens to a sole proprietorship at death](/learn/business-owners/what-happens-to-a-sole-proprietorship-at-death) explains why the business effectively pauses.

## The first 72 hours

1. **Locate the documents.** Ask the attorney, accountant and bank which of them holds the originals.
2. **Choose one person to speak for the company.** Mixed messages to staff, customers and lenders cost trust.
3. **Tell the key people calmly.** Employees, top clients and the largest suppliers should hear from a person they know, before they hear from gossip.
4. **Secure the premises, keys and cash.** Change locks and alarm codes only if the executor or a trusted manager authorizes it, and keep a record.
5. **Call the insurance agent.** Confirm that general liability, property, workers' compensation and any key-person policy stay in force, and whether a death benefit is payable. Our page on [key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance) explains how those policies work.
6. **Call the owner's attorney.** If the company has a buy-sell agreement, some of its deadlines start running at death.

## Bank accounts, payroll and bills

This is where trouble starts. A bank notified of a death will often freeze accounts titled in the dead person's name. A company account is different, because the company is its own legal person, but if the owner was the only authorized signer the bank may still stop activity until it sees proof of authority.

What helps:

- **Another signer already on the card.** This is the easiest fix and is why succession planners add one early.
- **A certified death certificate and the governing documents.** A bank usually wants the operating agreement or bylaws, a resolution naming the new signer, and ID.
- **Court letters.** For an account in the owner's own name, expect to need letters from the probate court. Our page on [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties) explains how the executor obtains them.

Keep payroll first on the list. Wages earned are debts, and payroll taxes carry strict deposit and filing dates. If the account is frozen, ask the bank about a temporary arrangement and ask the payroll service about their process.

Do not pay personal bills out of the business, or business bills out of the owner's personal accounts, even for a good reason. The executor has to account for both, and mixed funds raise questions with the court, the tax office and the heirs. The company's existing contracts keep running, and bills on those should continue to be paid. A sole proprietorship's debts, and debts the owner backed with a personal signature, are a different matter; see [personal guaranties and business debts at death](/learn/business-owners/personal-guaranties-and-business-debts-at-death).

## Taxes, licenses and filings

Several items have dates that do not wait:

- **Payroll and sales tax deposits.** Keep them current.
- **Licenses and permits.** Many are personal to the owner and are not transferable. A professional or liquor license may need a temporary arrangement with the licensing body.
- **Business tax ID.** An LLC or corporation keeps its own. A sole proprietorship's number may need to be updated to the estate. The IRS and your accountant can guide this.
- **Annual state filings.** A company that misses its annual report can lose good standing.
- **Final personal tax return.** The executor files it; see [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns).

Ask the accountant for a list of the next ninety days of due dates and put it on a calendar before anything else.

## Customers, vendors and digital access

The business is only as stable as the trust of the people who pay it. A short written note to customers helps: who is in charge now, that service continues and who to call. Do not promise things you cannot control, such as long-term pricing or a particular new owner.

Digital access often turns into the real bottleneck. The owner may have been the only one with the payment processor login, domain registrar, accounting software or customer database. Providers generally require proof that you have authority before they reset anything. Our page on [domain names and websites after death](/learn/digital-assets/domain-names-and-websites-after-death) covers the registrar side. A written list kept with the attorney, naming who may use it, would have saved weeks.

## When does the executor take over?

If the business was a sole proprietorship, or if the owner's shares are part of the probate estate, the executor holds the legal authority. An executor is a fiduciary, which means personal responsibility if the business loses value through carelessness. A will can give the executor power to continue the business for a set time and to hire a manager. Without that power the executor may need court approval to keep operating, or may have to sell or close.

For a company with other owners, the executor typically steps into the estate's role as an owner and follows the operating agreement. That is often a buyout. The details are in [LLC ownership at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death).

## What if the named successor says no?

A named successor may decline, or may not be able to run the company. Then the fallback list matters: a second named person, a co-owner, a long-time manager, an outside manager under a short agreement, or a sale. Many families decide in the first month that selling is wiser than a rushed takeover. Do not make that decision under pressure. A short stabilizing period, followed by a decision with an attorney and accountant, usually produces a better outcome than an immediate sale.

## What to do after the first month

By day thirty, you should know who has authority, the bank is working, payroll is steady, insurance is confirmed and the due dates are on a calendar. The next phase is the longer one: a valuation, a decision to keep, sell or close, and a conversation with the heirs. Our page on [selling a business or passing it to family](/learn/business-owners/selling-your-business-or-passing-it-to-family-or-employees) is a good next read, and [what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist) covers the personal side.
