If you write, design, code, photograph, consult or drive for yourself, you probably think of estate planning as something for people with an office and a pension. It is the other way around. An employee leaves a manager, a payroll department and a team who can finish the project. You leave a laptop and a folder of contracts.

This page is for people who work for themselves and want to know what actually happens to the income, the clients and the creative work. It belongs to our guide to [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners), and it pairs with the article on [what happens to a sole proprietorship at death](/learn/business-owners/what-happens-to-a-sole-proprietorship-at-death), which covers the legal structure in more depth.

## What does a freelancer own that needs a plan?

More than most people count. A rough inventory:

- **Money owed to you.** Open invoices, retainers, royalty statements, platform balances.
- **Work you created.** Copyrights in writing, photographs, music, designs, code you kept the rights to. Possibly patents.
- **Business tools.** Equipment, software licenses, your domain name and website, social accounts.
- **Client relationships.** Not property in the strict sense, but a contact list has value to a buyer or successor.
- **Personal protection.** A retirement plan such as a solo 401(k) or SEP IRA, and disability or life insurance.

Each of these passes differently. The retirement account goes to whoever is on the beneficiary form. Equipment and invoices go through the [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate) unless they sit in a trust. Rights in your creative work pass by will or intestacy like any property.

## Who inherits copyrights, patents and royalties?

Your heirs do. Copyright is personal property under federal law, and it can be left in a will like a car or a painting. For a work you created as an individual, copyright generally runs for your life plus 70 years. A songwriter's royalties, a photographer's licensing fees or a novelist's backlist can keep paying your family long after your last invoice.

Patents are also transferable, but they carry maintenance fees that someone has to pay on schedule. Miss a payment and the patent can lapse. If you hold one, put the due dates in the instruction sheet you leave.

Here is how it commonly works in practice.

| Item | Who receives it | What the heir usually needs |
|---|---|---|
| Copyright in your own work | Whoever your will or trust names, otherwise heirs by law | Letters from the court, or the trust, to show authority |
| Royalty payments from a publisher or label | The estate, then the heirs | Death certificate, proof of authority, a W-9 for the payee |
| Patent | Same, with fees still due | Patent number, maintenance schedule, attorney of record |
| Work-for-hire you created | The client who commissioned it | Nothing; it never belonged to you |
| Licensed (not sold) work | Your estate, as the licensor | The license terms and renewal dates |

The row about work-for-hire catches people out. If you signed an agreement saying the client owns what you produce, there is nothing to inherit. Look at your contracts before you assume you have an asset.

Consider Priya, 44, a freelance illustrator with six children's books in print. She has a husband but no will. She dies, and the publisher is willing to keep paying royalties, but it wants a court-appointed representative to sign. Her husband spends four months in probate to get that. If Priya had put her rights in a living trust, her husband could have shown the trust and a death certificate and the next statement would have gone to the trust account. The money would have been the same. The delay would not.

## What happens to unpaid invoices and client work?

Unpaid invoices are an asset of the estate. A client who owes you $8,000 still owes it; the check just needs to be written to the estate and deposited in an account the executor opens. Your executor cannot chase what they do not know about. So keep one list: client, project, amount, date sent.

Tax treatment is a trap here. Income you had earned but not been paid at death is generally treated as income in respect of a decedent. It is taxed to whoever receives it, and the heir does not get the usual step-up in basis on it. This is a point to raise with your tax preparer.

Active contracts split into two groups. Agreements built on your personal skill, such as writing a book chapter or designing a logo, usually end at death, and the client may owe you only for work done. Agreements about money and rights, such as payment terms, confidentiality and licensing, usually continue. Many clients will also expect your files back, and may hold confidential material you are obliged to protect, so your executor needs to know which folders are theirs.

## What if you become sick and cannot work?

Dying is the clearest risk, but not the most common. A freelancer who has a stroke at 52 faces months of invoices that nobody sends and clients who drift away. A durable power of attorney lets someone you trust access your business accounts, send invoices and deal with your clients. Without one, your family may need a court-ordered guardianship or conservatorship just to get into your business checking account. Our guide to [incapacity planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) walks through that, and the page on a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) explains the document itself.

Also look at disability insurance. Employer coverage vanished when you went solo. An individual policy replaces part of your income during illness, and it is easier to buy when you are healthy.

## How do you set up a freelancer's plan?

1. **Write the inventory.** Contracts, accounts, platforms, software licenses, who owes you and who you owe.
2. **Check ownership of your work.** For each major piece, note whether you own the copyright, license it or assigned it.
3. **Name beneficiaries.** On your solo 401(k) or SEP IRA, your life insurance and any bank account that allows [payable-on-death](/glossary/payable-on-death) designations.
4. **Sign a will.** Name an executor who is comfortable with spreadsheets and contracts, and say who gets your creative rights.
5. **Think about a living trust.** It keeps royalties, invoices and equipment out of probate. Moving rights into a trust requires a written assignment for each category of work.
6. **Sign a power of attorney and healthcare documents.** Both, not one.
7. **Tell one person where everything lives.** Include the password manager's emergency contact, as covered in our article on [domain names and websites after death](/learn/digital-assets/domain-names-and-websites-after-death).

Platforms add their own wrinkles. Our article on [online shops and channels after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-business-etsy-shop-or-youtube-channel-after-death) covers how those accounts are treated.

## Does a freelancer need a lawyer for this?

A simple freelancer with no creative rights and little debt might manage with a will, a power of attorney and clean beneficiary forms. Once royalties, patents, large receivables or a spouse who does not work in the business are involved, drafting the rights language matters. A vague sentence like "I leave my creative work to my sister" can leave open whether she gets the copyrights, the physical files, or both.

Also consider whether you need to talk to a business attorney about contract terms and an estate planning attorney about how those assets pass. They solve different problems, and each should know what the other has done.
