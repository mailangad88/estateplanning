A dentist, a lawyer, a veterinarian or an accountant owns something that looks like any other company, with a lease, staff, equipment and a bank account. The part that sets it apart is the license. The law treats the person with the license as the one who can practice, and most states treat the practice as something only licensed people may own or control.

That rule changes what the family can inherit. This page explains how, and how to arrange things so the family receives real value instead of an unsellable shell. It is part of our [estate planning guide for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## Why can't the family just take over a professional practice?

Because the family does not hold the license. Most states require that the shareholders of a professional corporation, or the members of a professional LLC, be licensed in the profession the entity practices. The goal is to keep non-professionals from controlling professional judgment, such as a lender or a spouse telling a doctor how to treat patients.

When the owner dies, the share is part of the estate, but the estate may not be allowed to hold it for long. Typical statutes say that the estate or heirs of a deceased shareholder may hold the shares for a short period, then must transfer them to a licensed person or have the corporation redeem them. The period varies by state, and a few professions have their own rules. Your state's professional corporation act, and the licensing board, set the answer.

The result is that the value of the practice, not the practice itself, passes to the heirs. A buyer pays, the estate receives the price, and the estate plan distributes it.

## How do the different professions compare?

| Practice | Who can own | Special concerns at death |
|---|---|---|
| Medical or dental | Licensed physicians or dentists; some states permit other licensed health professionals in limited roles | Patient records retention, notices to patients, prescribing registrations, payer contracts |
| Law | Lawyers only in most states; nonlawyer ownership is barred in nearly all | Client files, trust account, conflicts review, appointed successor attorney |
| Accounting | Licensed CPAs in most states; some allow minority non-CPA owners | Client records, peer review, tax filing deadlines in progress |
| Veterinary | Licensed veterinarians in most states, with variations | Controlled drugs, records, boarding or retail components |
| Engineering and architecture | Licensed professionals, often with a requirement that a licensed person be in charge | Seal and signature authority, active projects, liability insurance |

These are general patterns, and states differ. Some are strict, some allow limited nonprofessional ownership, and some treat related businesses differently. Check your state and your profession.

## What happens to a medical practice at the owner's death?

Patient care is the first concern and the ownership is the second. The practice must tell patients that the doctor has died, say where they can get care, and keep records available for the period state law requires, which is often several years and longer for children. A named records custodian, usually another physician or a records-storage company under contract, should be in place before it is needed.

Take Dr. Alan Ruiz, 61, who owns a solo family medicine practice through a professional corporation. He has never thought about what comes next. When he dies, his wife Teresa becomes the executor and the sole heir to the shares. She cannot practice and cannot own the corporation for long. Staff are unpaid within two weeks, the insurance carriers freeze billing under his provider number, and patients with prescriptions hear nothing. Teresa spends three months finding a buyer, who then negotiates against a practice with no doctor.

Compare Dr. Ruiz with a colleague who signs an agreement with a younger physician, Dr. Okafor, naming her as the person who may buy the practice at a formula price and covering the practice during the transition. Insurance funds half the price. The family receives cash, and patients keep a doctor.

## What happens to a law practice?

Lawyers have the most developed rules, because client money and confidences are at stake. In many states, when a solo lawyer dies without a plan, a court or the bar can appoint another lawyer to protect client interests, notify clients, return files and handle the trust account. Without a plan, the estate pays for the process and clients face delay.

A solo lawyer can avoid this by naming a successor attorney in advance, in writing, with access to files, the client list, the trust account and calendar deadlines. Ethics rules in most states also permit the sale of a law practice to another lawyer, with notice to clients and respect for their right to choose their own lawyer. The sale price belongs to the estate. Only a lawyer can own the practice, so the widow of a lawyer cannot keep the firm.

## What should a plan for a licensed owner include?

Think of it as a continuing-care plan for the practice and a cash plan for the family.

1. **A succession agreement with a licensed colleague.** A buy-sell agreement in which the buyer is a licensed person, a partner or an associate, with a price and funding. Our [buy-sell agreement guide](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) sets out the clauses.
2. **Insurance behind the price.** Life insurance for the death buyout and disability insurance for an owner who cannot work. See [business owner incapacity planning](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) for the disability side.
3. **A designated person to protect clients or patients.** A covering physician, a successor attorney, a CPA colleague. Written authority is necessary.
4. **A durable power of attorney that fits the license.** An agent can handle money and property, but cannot practice or exercise professional judgment. Use the document for the business side, and name a licensed colleague for the professional side. See our [durable power of attorney page](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
5. **A records plan.** Where records are stored, who holds the keys, how long they are kept, and who can release them. Electronic systems need passwords stored safely, which is a [digital assets](/learn/digital-assets) question as much as a professional one.
6. **Tail coverage for malpractice claims.** Policies written on a claims-made basis stop covering at death or retirement unless an extended reporting endorsement is bought. Ask the carrier about death-and-disability tail provisions.
7. **A will or trust that expects a sale.** Authorize the executor to sell to the designated buyer, and to employ a temporary manager. Whether a trust can hold the shares depends on the licensing rules, as our page on [putting a business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust) notes.

## What about the building, the equipment and the goodwill?

Some pieces are not subject to licensing rules and can be owned by the family. Many practitioners own the building through a separate LLC that leases to the practice. Equipment and real estate can be inherited or sold on the ordinary terms. Family members can also own a management company that provides non-clinical services such as billing, staffing and leasing, at fair market fees. Some states restrict these arrangements, so have an attorney check them.

Goodwill is the tricky piece. In a solo practice, goodwill depends on the owner's own patients or clients. After death, that value leaks away quickly. A transition period with a buyer or associate already in place preserves it.

## What does the family need to do right away?

Do not try to run the practice. Call the licensing board or bar if you are unsure of the notice rules, contact the named successor, secure the records, pause ads and scheduling for services that cannot be delivered, and keep the payroll covered. Then talk with an attorney about the sale. The longer a practice stands idle, the less it is worth.
