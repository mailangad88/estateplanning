If you own a company in Illinois, the federal estate tax is probably not your problem. The 2026 federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, and most family businesses sit well under it. The Illinois estate tax is where owners get surprised, because it starts at $4,000,000 and counts the business at full value.

This page shows how Illinois treats a family business at death, runs an example with real numbers, and covers where the cash comes from. It sits inside our [business owner estate planning section](/learn/business-owners) and builds on our general guide to [taxes when a family business passes to heirs](/learn/business-owners/taxes-when-a-family-business-passes-to-heirs) and our state page on the [Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax).

## How does Illinois estate tax apply to a family business?

Illinois adds up everything you own at death, including the company, and taxes the estate if the total passes $4,000,000. The test uses your gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts. That means the business is counted next to the house, retirement accounts, life insurance you own and the value of a revocable trust.

The $4,000,000 is a gate, not a credit. At or under it, nothing is owed and nothing is filed. Over it, the estate files Illinois Form 700 with the Attorney General, whether or not a federal return is required, and pays the Illinois State Treasurer nine months after death.

Here is an example. Dana, 66, owns a machine shop in Joliet that an appraiser would value at $3,200,000. Add her house at $600,000, a 401(k) of $500,000 and a $300,000 policy she owns on her own life. Her gross estate is $4,600,000, which is $600,000 over the line. Nothing in that list is unusual, and none of it is "wealth" in the sense Dana would use. The tax is figured on a special interrelated formula, so do not guess. The Attorney General publishes a calculator, and its own example shows how steep the first dollars over the threshold are: a $5,000,000 estate located wholly in Illinois owes $285,714.

Two Illinois points matter for owners in particular.

- **Spouses cannot share the exclusion.** Illinois does not allow portability. If Dana's husband Ray leaves everything to her and she dies owning $4,600,000, his unused $4,000,000 is gone. A bypass trust at the first death can fix that, and the business interest is often what funds it. Our page on [credit shelter trusts and the Illinois QTIP election](/learn/illinois/illinois-qtip-election-and-credit-shelter-trusts) shows how.
- **Lifetime gifts are added back.** Handing the shop to a son does not wipe out its value for the Illinois test. It can stop later growth from being counted.

## Does it matter who inherits the business?

For the amount of Illinois tax, mostly not. Illinois has no inheritance tax. It taxes the estate as a whole, so the bill is the same whether the shop goes to a son, a brother, a longtime manager or a friend. Who receives the company changes the family's problems, not the tax rate.

Two kinds of heirs do change the number, because the law lets the estate deduct what they receive.

| Who receives the interest | Effect on the Illinois estate tax |
|---|---|
| Child, grandchild, sibling, partner, employee or friend | Counted in full. The estate pays the tax. |
| Spouse, in a qualifying way | Marital deduction. The tax is delayed, and the value is counted in the spouse's estate later. |
| Qualified charity | Deductible. It lowers the taxable estate. |

The marital deduction is a delay, not a cure. If Dana leaves the shop outright to Ray and he dies owning it, his estate stands alone against one $4,000,000 exclusion. A gift to charity is a real reduction, and our page on [charitable gifts and the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/charitable-gifts-and-the-illinois-estate-tax) runs the numbers. Even when deductions bring the tax to zero, a return may still be due if the gross estate is over $4 million.

### What does the tax look like in dollars?

Go back to Dana and her $4,600,000 estate. For roughly the first $1.3 million over the line, the Illinois tax works out to about two sevenths of the overage, as our [Illinois estate tax worked examples](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax-worked-examples) explain. These figures are our own arithmetic, so use the Attorney General's calculator for a real estate.

| If Dana leaves the shop to | Illinois estate tax at her death |
|---|---|
| Her son Eli, with everything else split among her children | About $171,400 |
| Her longtime manager Wen | About $171,400 |
| Her husband Ray, outright and qualifying for the marital deduction | $0 now, but counted in Ray's estate later |

The figure for Eli and Wen is $600,000 times two sevenths. It is the same for both because Illinois looks at the estate, not at the heir.

Now think about where $171,400 comes from. The estate owns a machine shop, a house and a 401(k). The shop's bank account may hold enough to run payroll, not to pay a tax bill. If the will does not say otherwise, the person who receives the shop may end up carrying a share of the tax out of an asset that does not pay it.

## How is the business valued?

Illinois starts from the fair market value on the date of death, not what the company cost or what the tax return shows. The value is argued from the books, comparable sales and the buy-sell agreement if one exists. A minority interest may be worth less than its slice of the whole, as covered in our pages on [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) and [valuation discounts](/learn/estate-tax/valuation-discounts-and-family-limited-partnerships).

Get the appraisal early. It supports the number on Form 700, and appraisals of a business often arrive late. Near the $4,000,000 line, the value decides whether a return is due at all. A low number invites questions, and a high one costs real money, so a documented valuation protects the family either way.

## Where does the cash come from?

State death tax is paid in cash on a fixed schedule. Illinois is nine months. An extension gives more time to finish the return, but the extension form says it does not waive interest, and the Form 700 we reviewed lists interest at 10% a year from the nine-month date. A company that is worth $3,200,000 but holds $80,000 in the bank cannot meet that. Our page on [Illinois estate tax late filing, interest and penalties](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax-late-filing-interest-and-penalties) shows what a missed date costs.

The usual sources are these.

1. **Life insurance.** A policy you own counts in the estate, so owners often put it in an irrevocable trust. A transfer of an existing policy has to happen more than three years before death to keep the proceeds out. See [life insurance and the Illinois estate tax](/learn/illinois/life-insurance-and-the-illinois-estate-tax), [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) and [key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance).
2. **A funded buy-sell agreement.** The partner or the company buys the share for cash at a set price, and the family receives money. The price in the agreement can also support the value on the return, so have it reviewed before you sign. Our guide to [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) shows the structures.
3. **Other assets.** Liquid accounts, a rental property or a retirement account left to the person who needs to pay.
4. **A sale or partial redemption.** Selling part of the company to cover the bill is the last resort, and often at a poor price because the buyer knows the clock is running.

Your will or trust should also say who bears the tax. Without that sentence, the person who receives the business can end up paying a bill the other children never see.

## What should a business owner do now?

1. **Add up the Illinois estate.** Count the business, the home, retirement accounts, insurance you own and any revocable trust.
2. **Compare the total with $4,000,000.** If you are near or over, estimate the tax with the [estate tax estimator](/tools/estate-tax-estimator) or the Attorney General's calculator.
3. **Order a valuation.** One that is current and written is worth far more than an estimate on the back of an envelope.
4. **Match insurance to the bill.** Size a policy to the tax figure, not to a round number.
5. **Update the paper.** Check your operating or shareholder agreement against your will, since the [operating agreement may control who takes your interest](/learn/business-owners/does-an-operating-agreement-override-your-will). Our [LLC ownership at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) guide covers how membership interests pass.
6. **Look at the larger plan.** When the business is most of what you own, read [when the business is most of your estate](/learn/business-owners/when-the-business-is-most-of-your-estate) and [gifting business interests to children](/learn/business-owners/gifting-business-interests-to-children).

## What if you own property in another state?

Illinois figures the tax as if everything were in Illinois, then multiplies it by the share of the estate located in Illinois. A nonresident who owns Illinois real estate or tangible property files on the same formula. So an Illinois owner with a warehouse in Indiana still counts the warehouse when deciding whether a return is due, and the tax is then spread by where the property sits. Your other state may have its own rules. How an entity that owns the real estate is treated is a question for an attorney who has the documents in hand, and our page on [Illinois estate tax for nonresidents](/learn/illinois/illinois-estate-tax-for-nonresidents) covers the details.
