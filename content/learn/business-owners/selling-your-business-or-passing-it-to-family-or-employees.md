Most owners reach a point where they ask the same question: do I sell this thing, or hand it to someone I know? The honest answer is that it depends on three variables, and owners tend to weigh them in the wrong order. Money gets discussed first, feelings second, and the person who might actually run the business last.

This page compares the five exits side by side, explains where the tax bill differs, and shows how a key employee can buy in over time. It sits within our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners). For the broader plan around a transfer, see [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).

## What are your five options?

| Exit | Who takes over | How you get paid | Control you keep | Typical tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Family | A child or relative | Gift, installment note or inheritance | High, if you choose | Price is often lower, family tension is possible |
| Partner or co-owner | The other owner | Buy-sell terms, often insurance funded | Set by the agreement | Price may be formula-based, not a market bid |
| Key employee | One or several managers | Staged purchase, note, earn-out | Moderate | Slow, and they may need financing |
| Employee stock plan (ESOP) | All eligible employees through a trust | Lump sum or installments from plan financing | Low after closing | Complex and regulated, but keeps the company independent |
| Outside buyer or competitor | A new owner | Cash, plus perhaps a note or earn-out | Low | Highest price potential, least say over legacy |

Pick the row that matches your goals, then test it against your health, your timeline and your heirs.

## Should you sell during life or leave the business at death?

Start with taxes, because they surprise owners. If you sell during life, you pay capital gains tax on the difference between the sale price and your tax basis. For a founder with a low basis, that can be a large number.

If you hold the business until death, heirs generally receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) to the value at death. A child who sells right afterward may owe little or no gain. That is a real advantage of leaving it in the estate. If you gift the business during life, the child takes your old basis, with no step-up. Our guide on [gifting business interests](/learn/business-owners/gifting-business-interests-to-children) covers that trade.

Estate tax is a separate matter. For 2026 the federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, so many family businesses never owe it. Some states tax at lower thresholds. Our [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) is a place to look.

None of this means you should wait to die. If you need the cash, can't find a successor, or the business only works with you in it, selling earlier may be right even at a higher tax cost. Waiting also has risk: the value can fall if you are ill, and your heirs may sell in a hurry at a discount.

## What does selling to family involve?

You choose a successor, set a price or a gift structure, and decide how siblings who do not join the business are treated. The details are in our article on [passing a family business to your children, fairly](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children).

The common mistake is to assume the oldest or the most willing child can run the company. Ask whether they have worked in it, whether employees and customers respect them, and whether the numbers support a salary for them plus income for you. If the answer is unclear, a trial period as general manager costs little and tells you much.

## How do you sell to a partner or co-owner?

The tool is a buy-sell agreement. It states the price formula, the triggering events, and how the purchase is funded. If both partners signed one years ago, the exit may already be set: when one dies, retires or becomes disabled, the other buys. Our guide on [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) shows the types, and [cross-purchase vs entity redemption](/learn/business-owners/cross-purchase-vs-entity-redemption) explains who owns the insurance.

Without one, the partner and your heirs are strangers negotiating with a stopped clock. That scenario is covered in [what happens when a business partner dies without a buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/business-partner-dies-without-a-buy-sell-agreement).

## How can a key employee buy in over time?

The employee probably can't write a check for the whole company. So the usual structure is gradual. These are the common building blocks.

1. **A staged share purchase.** The employee buys a slice now, another later at a price fixed by formula or appraisal, and funds each step from salary, bonus or a bank loan.
2. **A seller note.** You finance part of the price and the employee repays from profits. Secure it with the shares and add a right to take them back on default.
3. **An option.** The employee holds the right to buy at a set price within a set window. If the business grows, they gain. If it doesn't, they walk away.
4. **Phantom equity.** The employee gets cash bonuses tied to the value of the company, without owning shares. It lets you test commitment first.
5. **Vesting.** Shares or rights earned over several years, with forfeiture if the employee leaves early.

A few cautions. Shares granted for services are often taxable income to the employee when they vest, so get a tax advisor involved before handing them out. The agreement should say what happens if the employee dies, becomes disabled or quits, and who values the shares. And if you die mid-way through the plan, your executor must know the deal exists. Put the documents where they can be found, and mention them in the [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction).

Consider Tomasz, 64, who owns an HVAC company with eleven employees. He offers his operations manager 10 percent now, another 10 percent each year for four years at an appraised price, with a note from Tomasz funding half. If Tomasz dies in year two, the agreement obliges his estate to complete the sales. It is slower than a lump sum but it keeps the crew intact.

## ESOP or an outside buyer?

An employee stock ownership plan sells to a trust that holds shares for employees. It can offer tax advantages in the right setup, keeps the company independent and rewards staff. It also takes a valuation, a trustee, a plan document and compliance work, so it suits companies with enough size and steady profit. See our summary of [ESOP basics](/learn/business-owners/esop-basics-for-business-owners).

Selling to a competitor is faster and often brings the strongest cash offer, especially if the buyer values your customer list or location. In exchange you accept less say over the name, the staff and the culture. Expect a non-compete clause, due diligence that reaches your books, and an earn-out if some of the price depends on future results. If you are leaving employees behind, ask for retention terms in the contract.

## How should you decide?

Work through these questions in order.

1. **How much cash do I need from the business to retire?** If the number is high, family and employee deals may not carry it.
2. **Does anyone want and deserve the job?** Do not fill the role out of guilt.
3. **Does the business run without me?** If not, a sale price will be discounted or a sale may not close.
4. **What happens if I die before this closes?** Draft the agreement so it survives you.
5. **How is my estate otherwise arranged?** A will or trust should say what happens if the sale is incomplete.
