Owners ask about cost early, and the honest answer is a range that depends on what the plan has to do. A plan for a sole proprietor who wants a daughter to take over is one project. A company with two partners, a bank loan and a lease is another. This page explains what drives the price, who bills for what, and how long the process really takes, so you can compare quotes on the same terms.

If you are still deciding whether you need a plan, read our guide to [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) first. Everything here sits inside our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners).

## How much does business succession planning cost?

There is no single price, and any number quoted without knowing your company is a guess. Attorneys generally charge either a flat fee for a defined package or an hourly rate for open-ended work, and the choice usually tracks how predictable the project is. A will and trust for one owner is predictable. A negotiation among partners is not.

What moves the price up or down:

| Factor | Effect on cost |
|---|---|
| Number of owners | More owners means more agreements to draft and more people to hear |
| Entity type | A sole proprietorship, LLC and S corporation each need different documents |
| Transfer method | A gift, a sale to a child or a buyout by partners need different steps |
| Funding | Life insurance or installment notes add drafting and review |
| Family agreement | Disputes add meetings and revisions |
| Taxes and valuation | A business near the estate tax threshold needs more analysis |
| Real estate | Land held outside the company may need its own entity or lease |

Ask any firm what the fee covers: the review of existing documents, a buy-sell agreement, new operating agreements, trust documents, powers of attorney, and the meeting where you sign. If something is outside the quote, ask now.

## What does the legal fee not include?

The legal fee rarely covers everything a plan needs. Four other costs show up:

1. **Valuation.** A business appraiser charges separately, and the price rises with the size and complexity of the company. A rough estimate can come from your accountant at lower cost, and a full written appraisal is for a gift, a sale or an estate tax filing. See [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).
2. **Insurance.** A life insurance policy that funds a buyout has premiums, paid by the company or the owners. [Key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance) works the same way.
3. **Accounting.** Your CPA reviews the tax effect of a sale, a gift or an entity change, and usually prepares any gift tax return.
4. **Filing and transfer costs.** Recording a deed, changing a lease or amending a state registration can have small fees.

The sum of these is the real budget. A firm that quotes only its own fee has not answered your question.

## How long does it take?

<!-- visual: timeline id=making-a-plan -->

Most plans fall into three stages. The timing below is typical, not a promise.

1. **Fact-finding and decisions (a few weeks to several months).** You gather the entity documents, tax returns, loan agreements and leases, and decide who will run and own the business. This is the slowest stage because it is about people.
2. **Valuation and structure (about one to three months).** An appraiser values the company if needed, and your advisers choose between a gift, a sale, a trust or a buy-sell agreement.
3. **Drafting and signing (a few weeks).** The attorney drafts, you review, partners comment, and everyone signs. Funding the plan, such as moving the interest into a trust and putting life insurance in place, follows.

A sole owner with a clear successor and a small company can finish in weeks. A partnership, a family dispute or a search for an outside buyer can run a year.

## What do two real-looking budgets look like?

These are illustrations of scope, not price quotes.

Ruth, 58, owns a sole proprietorship that does bookkeeping for local clients. She wants her niece to take it over and wants her house and accounts to go to her son. Her plan is a will, a durable power of attorney, a business continuity memo and a beneficiary review. Scope: small. Timeline: several weeks.

Carlos and Dev, both 49, each own 50% of a Chicago-area logistics S corporation with a warehouse lease, an equipment loan and personal guaranties. They need a buy-sell agreement funded with life insurance, a valuation formula, new shareholder documents, individual estate plans and a plan for the guaranties. Scope: large. Timeline: six to nine months, mostly waiting on the appraisal, insurance underwriting and partner negotiation.

## Who should be in the room?

Cost climbs when people join late. Invite your accountant to the first or second meeting, because the tax answer shapes the structure. If a spouse will inherit or co-own the business, bring them too, since a plan the spouse finds out about at signing tends to be rewritten. Children who will run the business should hear the outline before documents are final; the conversation can be short, but a child who learns the plan from a lawyer's letter often objects, and objections mean redrafting. Partners need their own attorney when their interests differ from yours, which is common in a buyout. One firm drafting for both sides of a negotiation is a conflict our attorneys will tell you about.

## Can you cut cost without cutting protection?

Yes, in ways that do not cut corners:

- Bring organized documents to the first meeting. Time spent hunting for a three-year-old operating agreement is billable.
- Decide the successor question before drafting. A change of mind in month five is expensive.
- Start with the pieces that protect the family if you die this year: a will or trust, a power of attorney and a buy-sell agreement. Add gifting and tax strategies later.
- Ask whether a document can serve two purposes, like a trust that holds both the business interest and personal assets.

What not to cut: the buy-sell agreement's funding, and the documents that let someone run the company if you are incapacitated. Our article on [documents every business owner needs](/learn/business-owners/documents-every-business-owner-needs) lists them.

## What does it cost to skip the plan?

Compare the planning budget with the alternatives. A business with no plan can go through probate, with its own costs (see [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs)). Customers leave, a lender may call a loan, key employees take other jobs, and a surviving partner may be forced to deal with an heir who wants cash. Our article on [the first 30 days after a business owner dies](/learn/business-owners/first-30-days-after-a-business-owner-dies) shows what that looks like, and [when a partner dies without a buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/business-partner-dies-without-a-buy-sell-agreement) covers the partnership version.

For the personal side of the plan, see [how much a living trust costs](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost), since many owners hold the business interest in a trust.
