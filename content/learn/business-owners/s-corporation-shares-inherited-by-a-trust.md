A client who owns an S corporation usually thinks of the trust as the finish line. The shares go in, the successor trustee is named, the children are the beneficiaries. Job done. The part that gets skipped is what the tax code does after the owner dies, when the trust stops being a mere extension of the owner.

This page covers that moment: which trusts can hold S corporation stock, the two elections that keep the company's status alive, and how to draft a trust so nobody has to scramble. If you want the broader question of putting a company in a trust, read our page on [putting a business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust). The wider planning context sits in our [business owner estate planning guide](/learn/business-owners).

## Why does an S corporation care who owns the shares?

An S corporation is taxed through its shareholders rather than at the company level, but only if it keeps meeting the rules. One rule is that every shareholder must be an eligible type. Individuals who are U.S. citizens or residents qualify. Estates qualify. A short list of trusts qualify. A partnership, a corporation or a non-qualifying trust generally does not.

If shares land in the wrong hands, even for one day, the S election can end as of that day. The company is then taxed as a regular C corporation, with consequences for the owners, and getting S status back is not easy. That is why this seemingly narrow rule gets so much attention from estate planners.

## What trusts can hold S corporation stock?

| Trust type | Who it fits | Key conditions | Tax effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grantor trust (your revocable trust during life) | The owner and a trust they control | Treated as owned by one individual who is eligible | Owner reports the S income personally |
| Same trust after death | The former revocable trust | Qualifies for a limited period, generally two years from the date of death | Trust reports income, then must move to another category |
| Testamentary trust receiving stock under a will | Trust created by the will | Also gets a limited period, generally two years from the transfer | Same as above |
| QSST | A trust for one beneficiary | One income beneficiary at a time; all income paid out; beneficiary elects | Beneficiary is taxed on the S income |
| ESBT | A trust for several beneficiaries or one that accumulates income | Beneficiaries are limited to eligible persons or charities; trustee elects | Trust is taxed, generally at the top income tax rate on S income |
| Estate of the owner | The executor, during administration | Must be a real estate in administration, not held open for years | Estate reports income |

The two-year window is a safety net, not a plan. It gives the trustee time to decide what the trust will look like long term.

## How does a QSST work, and when does it fit?

A QSST is built for one beneficiary. The trust document must require that all the income goes to that person, that there is only one income beneficiary at a time, and that the principal can go only to that person while the trust lasts. The beneficiary, not the trustee, makes the election in writing. Once made, that beneficiary is treated as owning the S stock for income tax purposes.

Suppose Margaret owns an S corporation that makes custom cabinets. Her trust leaves the shares in a separate trust for her son Wesley, with all income paid to him for life. This is a natural QSST. Wesley pays tax on the S income at his own rates, whether the company distributes cash or not, so the trust should say that the company will distribute enough for taxes.

The weakness is rigidity. A QSST cannot easily accumulate income, divide it among children, or hold back funds if the beneficiary is struggling. If you need that flexibility, the other option is better.

## How does an ESBT work, and when does it fit?

An ESBT can have many beneficiaries and can accumulate income. The trustee makes the election. In return for the flexibility, the S corporation income is taxed inside the trust, generally at the highest income tax rate, regardless of how much is paid out. That can be expensive, but it is sometimes worth it for a spendthrift beneficiary or a trust for several siblings.

The ESBT has its own technical conditions. Interests in the trust must not have been bought, with some exceptions, and the beneficiaries must be eligible. A nonresident alien can be a problem in certain structures. If you have a child living abroad, mention that early.

## What is the deadline for these elections?

The election is generally due within two months and sixteen days after the shares are transferred to the trust. In a death situation, that clock can start when the transfer occurs, which may be at the owner's death or when the estate distributes the stock to the trust, depending on how the documents are set up.

That is a short window in the middle of a funeral, a grief reaction and often a probate. Here is the order of events we like to see.

1. **Identify the date the shares move.** The trustee and the attorney agree on the trigger date and mark the deadline on a calendar.
2. **Decide QSST or ESBT.** Tax advisor, trustee and beneficiary decide, with the family's tax picture in view.
3. **Prepare the election statement.** It must include certain information about the trust, the beneficiary or trustee, and the corporation.
4. **File with the IRS service center for the corporation, and send a copy to the company.** Keep proof of delivery.
5. **Note the election in the minute book and on the stock ledger.**
6. **Make sure the new trust has its own taxpayer ID.** Our page on the [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election) covers the basics.

## What goes wrong in practice?

- **The trust has no S language.** It splits the shares among three children in one pooled trust, which cannot be a QSST and has no ESBT mechanics. This is the failure we see most.
- **Nobody is responsible for the election.** The trustee assumes the accountant will do it, and the accountant assumes the attorney will.
- **Shares pass to the wrong trust.** A bypass trust for a surviving spouse, with the children as remainder beneficiaries, may not qualify as written.
- **A shareholder agreement forbids trusts.** The company documents may require consent or bar all transfers except to individuals. Review the [shareholder or operating documents](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) before drafting.
- **Charitable beneficiaries are added casually.** Certain charitable interests are allowed in an ESBT, but not in a QSST.

## How do you draft the trust to avoid these problems?

Ask your attorney for four things in writing.

- A clause giving the trustee power to make or consent to a QSST or ESBT election, and a duty to file.
- A direction that any trust created to hold S stock is divided so that each part can qualify, for example one separate trust per child.
- Permission for the trustee to sell the S stock to an eligible buyer, or to convert the company to another form, if qualification becomes impossible.
- A distribution instruction so there is cash to pay tax on pass-through income. S income taxed to a beneficiary who gets no distribution is a classic family quarrel.

Pair that with a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) that gives the other shareholders or the company a right to buy shares from a trust that cannot qualify. The buyout is a safety valve for the whole company, because one ineligible holder can hurt the other owners' tax position as well.

## Does S corporation status affect the estate tax or the step-up?

No, not directly. Shares owned at death generally receive a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) and are valued for [estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax) the same way, whether or not the S election survives. Where S status does matter is the annual tax bill afterwards. Lose it, and the company is taxed on its profits, then the owners are taxed again on dividends. Most heirs would pay a good deal to avoid that.
