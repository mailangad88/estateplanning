Most owners plan for the day they sell or die. The more likely threat to a business is quieter: a stroke, a car accident, a cancer diagnosis or early dementia that leaves you alive but unable to sign checks. The business needs you on Monday morning, and no one knows the passwords.

This article lays out how to build continuity for a period when you are unable to work, whether for a few weeks or permanently. It is one of several guides in our [business owner estate planning series](/learn/business-owners).

## What happens to a business when its owner cannot act?

The answer depends on how the business is organized, but the problem is the same: someone needs legal authority, and only you have it.

| Business type | Immediate problem | Fallback if no plan |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | No legal separation between you and the business; bank accounts and contracts are in your name | A court-appointed conservator or guardian |
| Single-member LLC | You are the only member and manager | Court-appointed conservator may take control of the membership interest |
| Multi-member LLC or partnership | Partners can run it but may lack authority over your share or your personal guarantees | Partners continue; your interest and any guarantees remain exposed |
| Corporation | Board and officers continue, but if you are the sole director or president, no one can sign | Remaining directors fill the gap if bylaws allow; otherwise, shareholders may need a meeting |

If a court has to step in, the process is public, slow and costly. A [conservatorship](/glossary/conservatorship) (called guardianship of the estate in some states) can take weeks or months to establish, and the conservator may be a stranger and may be subject to court reporting and approval of major decisions. Our article on [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) explains why signing documents early avoids that.

## Which documents keep a business running?

Five documents do most of the work.

1. **Durable power of attorney with business powers.** Your [agent](/glossary/agent) can sign contracts, run bank accounts, file tax returns and handle business debts for you as owner. The document must be "durable," meaning it survives incapacity. Read more in our [durable financial power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney). Some states require specific language to authorize gifts, trust funding or business transactions, so use an attorney.
2. **Successor manager or officer designation.** In an LLC operating agreement or corporate bylaws, name who becomes manager, president or authorized signer if you cannot serve, and how incapacity is determined.
3. **Revocable living trust holding the business interest.** If your membership interest or shares are in your trust, the successor trustee can act immediately, with no court. See [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).
4. **Banking authority.** Banks have their own forms. Ask each bank what it requires to add a backup signer or accept your agent's authority, and complete the paperwork now.
5. **Healthcare documents.** A healthcare power of attorney and HIPAA release make sure the person who runs your business is informed about your condition, and that your medical wishes are known. See our guide to planning for dementia for a longer view of capacity issues.

## Should the power of attorney be immediate or springing?

For a business, an immediate power usually works better. A [springing power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) takes effect only after a doctor, or two, certify that you can't manage your affairs. In a crisis that creates a gap of days or weeks, and banks may resist accepting it. Many owners sign an immediate durable power, entrust it to their lawyer or keep the original in a safe place, and give the agent permission to use it only on defined events. That arrangement depends on trust, so choose carefully.

If you prefer a springing power, define the trigger in plain terms, name who decides, and test the document with your bank in advance.

## How do you name the right person?

Your business agent does not need to be your spouse or your oldest child. Ask:

- Does this person understand the business, or can they quickly rely on those who do?
- Are they comfortable handling money and signing for a company?
- Will they be accessible, not overseas or overwhelmed by their own job?
- Do they get along with your partners and key staff?
- Would a conflict of interest arise, such as an heir who might benefit from decisions they make?

You can name co-agents or a separate agent for business decisions. For high-stakes companies, some owners add an independent advisor, such as a CPA or attorney, as a check, and require annual accounting to the family. See our guidance on [choosing a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent).

## What operational preparation helps?

Legal documents give authority. They do not tell your agent where anything is. Prepare a short emergency binder or secure digital folder, updated twice a year, with:

- Names and numbers for your attorney, accountant, banker, insurance agent and key customers and vendors
- Bank accounts, credit lines, loan agreements and personal guarantees
- Where the entity documents, EIN letter, licenses, leases and insurance policies are kept
- Payroll provider, tax calendar and upcoming deadlines
- Password manager access and a plan for two-factor authentication (see our article on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access))
- A list of recurring payments and tasks only you do
- A one-page "first 72 hours" instruction sheet for your successor

Cross-train at least one person on every task that only you perform, from invoicing to customer onboarding.

## What insurance fills the gap?

Insurance does not replace planning, but it buys time.

- **Personal disability income insurance** replaces part of your pay. Check how the policy defines disability: "own occupation" coverage is far more protective for owners than "any occupation."
- **Business overhead expense insurance** reimburses fixed costs such as rent and salaries while you recover.
- **Key person insurance** pays the business if you are the person who generates most of its revenue.
- **Disability buyout insurance** funds a buyout of your interest after a long disability, working with a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).

Elimination periods vary, so match them to how long your business can operate on its own reserves.

### Example

Hannah, 54, owns a small architecture firm as a single-member LLC. She has a stroke and is hospitalized for three months. Because she signed a durable power of attorney naming her office manager, and her operating agreement names that manager as acting manager if Hannah is unable to serve, the firm keeps paying staff and finishing projects. Her husband, as healthcare agent, speaks with the doctors. No court is involved. Her former neighbor, Paul, a sole proprietor with no documents, had the same stroke last year, and his family spent four months obtaining a conservatorship while customers left.

## What if the incapacity is gradual?

Dementia and degenerative illnesses give a window, but a closing one. A person must have legal capacity to sign a power of attorney or trust, so act before diagnosis if you can. If you have already received a diagnosis, ask an attorney promptly, because capacity is judged at the moment of signing, not by the diagnosis alone.
