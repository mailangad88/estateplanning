If you run a business as a sole proprietor, you may think of it as a separate thing: it has a name, a bank account, customers and maybe employees. Legally it is not separate from you at all. When you die, the business does not carry on under its own name. What is left is a pile of assets and obligations that belong to your estate.

That matters for the family you leave behind. This page explains what stops on the day you die, who can act, how the assets pass, and what you can set up now so the people closest to you are not locked out. It sits inside our guide to [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## Why does a sole proprietorship end at death?

A sole proprietorship is just you, doing business under your own name or a trade name. There is no company that owns the equipment or signs the lease. You own the van, the tools, the receivables and the client list personally, and you owe the debts personally.

Because there is no separate legal entity, there is nothing to continue. Contrast that with a corporation or LLC, which keeps existing after an owner dies and only changes hands, as described in our article on [what happens to your LLC or corporation shares when you die](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death). A sole proprietorship has no shares to inherit. Your heirs inherit the individual pieces.

## Who is in charge right after the death?

Nobody, for a short while, and then your executor. The person named in your will must usually be appointed by the probate court before acting. That appointment produces [letters testamentary](/glossary/letters-testamentary), the paper a bank or a landlord wants to see. If you have no will, the court appoints an administrator under your state's rules.

In many places the court can issue something quicker, often called special or temporary letters, when a business would otherwise spoil or lose value. It costs money and requires a petition, so it should be a fallback and not the plan.

Compare how the common setups work on day one.

| Your setup | Who can act on the business account | Likely delay |
|---|---|---|
| Account in your name only, will names executor | Executor, after court appointment | Weeks to months |
| Account with a payable-on-death beneficiary | The named beneficiary, with a death certificate | Days to a couple of weeks |
| Business assets in your revocable trust | Successor trustee, with the trust and a death certificate | Days to a couple of weeks |
| Joint account with a spouse | Surviving spouse | Days, but ownership rules still apply |

None of these removes the need to decide what the business should do next.

## What happens to the assets, debts and contracts?

Assets first. The tools, inventory, vehicles, bank balances, accounts receivable and any real estate titled in your name are part of your [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate), unless they are held in a trust or carry a beneficiary designation. They pass under your will, or under your state's intestacy rules if there is no will. Our guide to [probate and non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) lists which belongs where.

Debts come next. Because you were personally liable, trade creditors, suppliers, the landlord and the taxing agencies can make claims against the estate. Your executor pays valid claims before heirs receive anything, in an order set by state law. A loan you personally backed with your signature stays in force.

Contracts vary. Some end at death, especially those built on your personal skill. Others, such as a commercial lease or an equipment financing agreement, continue and become the estate's problem. A few allow the other side to terminate or demand payment when the owner dies. Reading these before they matter is part of a good plan.

Licenses and permits deserve their own check:

- **Professional and trade licenses** are personal to you. They do not pass to a spouse or child. A buyer or successor must qualify on their own.
- **Sales tax and business registrations** usually need to be closed or reissued in a new name.
- **A trade name** you registered, such as a "doing business as" filing, may lapse or be reusable by someone else if nobody renews it.
- **Customer lists and goodwill** have value, but only if someone buys or takes them over.

## What can your executor actually do with the business?

Your executor has three realistic options.

1. **Keep it running for a short time.** Often to finish jobs under contract, collect receivables and keep staff paid. State law and your will determine how long and how far this authority goes. A will that expressly allows the executor to continue the business helps a great deal.
2. **Sell it.** The assets, inventory, client list and any name rights can be sold as a package. A buyer will usually want to see clean books and a reasonable transition. A sale in a hurry rarely brings full value.
3. **Wind it down.** Collect what is owed, sell equipment, close accounts, file final tax returns and pay creditors.

Which one makes sense depends on how much of the value was you. A plumber whose customers called him by name has little to sell. An owner of a small shop with a lease, inventory and a staff manager has a lot more.

Consider Carla, 61, who runs a catering business as a sole proprietor. Her will names her daughter Mia as executor, but Carla dies on a Friday with a wedding booked for Saturday week. Mia cannot touch the business account until the court appoints her. She asks the judge for limited authority to honor the booked events, which is granted ten days later. The wedding goes ahead, but two vendors had already cancelled. Had Carla's account carried a payable-on-death designation to Mia, and a short note listing contacts, the delay would have been shorter.

## How do you plan for a sole proprietorship?

A sole proprietorship is the easiest form of business to plan around once you know it will not plan itself. A workable checklist:

1. **Make a list.** Accounts, vendors, customers, licenses, passwords, loans, leases, insurance, and where the records are kept.
2. **Name someone and tell them.** Your executor, a backup, and a person who knows the business, who may be the same person or not.
3. **Write the will with the business in mind.** Authorize your executor to continue, sell or close the business, and say who gets the proceeds.
4. **Consider a revocable trust.** Business assets assigned to a [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) pass to your successor trustee without probate. Our article on [putting a business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust) covers how it works.
5. **Sign a durable power of attorney.** If you are ill and not dead, your agent needs authority to deal with the business. See [incapacity planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning).
6. **Line up a buyer or successor.** A handshake agreement with a colleague who will buy your client list or take over contracts has real value, in writing.
7. **Review insurance.** Liability, equipment and life insurance that can cover debts and cash needs.

## How does state law change this?

States differ on how quickly an executor can be appointed, whether small estates qualify for a simplified process, how long creditors have to file claims, and whether community property gives your spouse an automatic half interest in the business. In a community property state, a surviving spouse may already own half of the business assets, which changes who needs to be paid or consulted. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/texas) shows how one state handles it, and an attorney in your state can tell you the rest. If the whole estate is modest, a small estate process may be available, though business assets with licenses or debts often complicate that.

## Is a sole proprietorship a good fit for estate planning?

It is simple while you are alive and a little clumsy once you are not. The same traits that make it cheap to run, no separate filings and no separate owner, mean there is nothing for your heirs to step into. Owners of a business with growing value or real liability often move to an LLC, then hold the interest in a trust. That is a business decision with tax consequences, so talk to your accountant before changing anything.

Whatever form you use, a short written plan beats a perfect one that never gets written.
