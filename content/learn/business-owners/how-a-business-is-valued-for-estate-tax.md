When a business owner dies, someone has to put a number on the business. The executor needs it for the estate tax return, if one is due. The heirs need it to set their income tax basis. A partner or the company needs it to carry out a buy-sell agreement. The trouble is that no market quote exists. A closely held company sells rarely, and the people best placed to value it are often the ones with a stake in the answer.

This page explains the standard the law uses, the methods appraisers rely on, how discounts work, and what you can do now to reduce arguments later. It belongs to our wider guide on [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## What standard does the law use?

The test is fair market value: the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither forced to act and both having reasonable knowledge of the facts. It is measured on the date of death. The IRS publishes long-standing guidance for closely held companies that lists the factors an appraiser should weigh, such as the nature of the business, its financial condition, earning capacity, dividend history, goodwill, sales of similar companies and the economic outlook.

Three consequences follow.

- **Not what you paid.** Cost matters only as evidence.
- **Not what the family wants.** An emotional attachment adds nothing to the number.
- **Not what the owner could do.** The question is what the business is worth to a hypothetical buyer, and a business that depends entirely on the owner's personal skill is worth less after the owner is gone.

## Do you need to value the business if there is no estate tax?

Probably, yes. The federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and married couples can shelter up to $30 million with portability. Most owners fall under that line, so no federal tax is due. The valuation still does three jobs.

1. It sets the [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). Heirs who later sell are taxed on gain above the date-of-death value, so a defensible number can save real money.
2. It guides division among heirs. If three children inherit and one wants out, you need a price.
3. It may trigger state taxes. A number of states tax estates below the federal level, as described in our guide to [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

Our overview of the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) explains who has to file a return.

## What methods do appraisers use?

Appraisers work from three approaches and usually reconcile them.

| Approach | What it measures | Best for | Weak spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income | Expected future earnings, converted to today's value using a risk-based rate | Profitable operating businesses | Sensitive to the forecast and the rate chosen |
| Asset | Fair value of assets minus liabilities | Real estate holding companies, investment entities, struggling businesses | Understates goodwill in service firms |
| Market | Prices at which comparable companies or interests sold | Businesses with active deal markets | Few true comparables for small firms |

Rules of thumb, such as a multiple of revenue common in an industry, show up in practice but are not a substitute. An appraiser may use one to cross-check.

Consider Walt, who owned a heating and cooling company with a steady maintenance base and a fleet of trucks. His appraiser started with income, adjusted for the owner's above-market pay, then checked the asset value of vehicles and inventory as a floor. The trucks alone were worth less than the income approach produced, which suggested real goodwill. The appraiser then reduced goodwill to reflect Walt's role in the sales process. His family could point to that reasoning if the IRS asked.

## What are valuation discounts?

Two discounts appear most often.

- **Minority interest discount.** A buyer of a non-controlling stake cannot set pay, direct strategy or force a sale, so pays less per share than for control.
- **Lack of marketability discount.** A private company interest cannot be sold on an exchange. Time and cost to find a buyer lowers the price.

Together they can reduce the value of a partial interest meaningfully. Whether they apply depends on the facts: the size of the stake, the governing documents, the legal form and whether the interest is truly stranded. A discount that sounds reasonable at 10 percent may be questioned at 40.

An additional point surprises families. A controlling interest can carry a premium rather than a discount. If you own 100 percent and divide it among three children before death, discounts may apply to each piece, but the IRS looks carefully at transactions that appear designed only to produce a discount.

## Can the executor choose a different date?

Sometimes. The alternate valuation date lets the executor value the whole estate six months after death, but only when this reduces both the gross estate and the estate tax. Assets sold or distributed in the meantime are valued at that date. For a business that lost customers after the owner died, the choice can matter. It does not apply when no estate tax return is required, so many small estates never use it.

## Do special relief rules exist for family businesses?

Federal law has some, with technical tests. In general:

- **Installment payment.** When a closely held business is a large share of the estate, the executor may be allowed to pay the estate tax over a period of years instead of in one sum, with interest.
- **Special use valuation.** Real property used in a family farm or business can in some cases be valued by its current use instead of its highest and best use, subject to qualifying conditions and a recapture period.

Qualification depends on percentages, family participation and holding periods, so confirm eligibility with an attorney and accountant before assuming relief.

## How does a buy-sell price interact with the appraisal?

A price in a buy-sell agreement can fix the value for estate tax, but only when the agreement is a bona fide business arrangement, not a device to pass property to family for less than full value, and the terms are comparable to those of an arm's-length deal. A price that was set decades ago and never updated invites a challenge, and a formula that no longer reflects the company can produce a number that no one believes.

The Supreme Court's 2024 decision in a case about a company-owned life insurance policy and a stock redemption means the structure of the funding also matters. In that case, the insurance proceeds counted in the company's value and the obligation to redeem the shares did not reduce it. Companies whose agreements rely on redemption funded by their own policies should have the setup reviewed. Our article on [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) describes the structures, and the one on [key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance) explains how company-owned policies work.

## What can you do now to make valuation easier?

1. **Keep clean financials.** Three to five years of tax returns, statements and a list of owner perks and one-time items.
2. **Get a baseline appraisal.** Some owners commission one every few years, especially with a buy-sell agreement.
3. **Document the owner's role.** If the business depends on you, say so, and begin shifting relationships to others.
4. **Hold the key papers in one place.** Leases, contracts, licenses, loan agreements and entity documents.
5. **Tell your executor who the appraiser is.** Choose a person with a business valuation credential and experience in your industry.
6. **Revisit the buy-sell price** with each appraisal.
