You built a company over twenty years. Your children have other careers. A competitor has made an offer, but you know what would happen to the shop floor the month after closing. Some owners in your spot hear about an ESOP and wonder if it is a way to hand the business to the people who made it work.

An ESOP is one of the few exit routes that is both a sale and a benefit for staff. It is not simple, though. This article explains how it works, what you would receive and where it fits next to the other choices in our [business owner guide](/learn/business-owners).

## What is an ESOP?

An employee stock ownership plan is a retirement plan, governed by federal pension law, that invests mainly in the stock of the company that sponsors it. A trust holds the shares. Each eligible employee gets an account within the plan, and the shares are allocated to accounts over time, commonly based on pay.

Three things set it apart from other ways to sell:

- **Employees pay nothing.** The company funds the plan, usually by repaying a loan the plan took to buy your shares.
- **The plan is run by a trustee.** The trustee is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary) for the employees. They must act in the employees' interest, and not the owner's.
- **A price floor and ceiling.** The plan cannot pay more than fair market value, and it needs an independent appraisal to prove it.

Because the plan is a retirement benefit, employees do not "own" shares outright while working. They receive the value of vested shares when they leave or retire, and the company repurchases them.

## How does an ESOP sale work, step by step?

A typical transaction takes many months and a team of advisers. The outline is similar in most deals.

1. **Feasibility study.** An adviser looks at profits, payroll and the owner's goals to see whether the company can support the debt.
2. **Valuation.** An independent appraiser sets a fair market value. This number drives everything.
3. **Structure and financing.** The company sets up the plan and trust, then a lender, and often the owner through a seller note, finance the purchase.
4. **Trustee review.** The trustee, who must be independent of the owner in practice, negotiates the price and terms for the employees.
5. **Closing.** The trust buys the shares. The owner receives cash, a note or both.
6. **Years of repayment.** The company makes tax-deductible contributions that repay the loan. Shares are released to employee accounts as it is paid.

A first-time seller should expect legal, valuation and administration fees that run well into five or six figures for a small company. Those costs recur, since the plan needs an annual valuation. Whether the cost is worth it depends on the size of the sale.

## What are the tax advantages?

They can be significant, and the details turn on how the company is taxed. This is general education, not tax advice, so confirm with a tax adviser.

| Company type | Main advantage | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| C corporation | The seller may defer tax on the gain by reinvesting in qualified securities, if the plan owns enough of the company after the sale | Strict holding period and reinvestment rules apply |
| S corporation | Income that belongs to the plan's share is not taxed at the corporate level | Rules on who may receive allocations, and anti-abuse tests |
| Both | The company's loan repayments to fund the plan may be deductible | Annual limits on contributions per employee |

For a C corporation seller, the deferral is often the headline. A seller who meets the conditions can postpone tax on the gain as long as the replacement securities are held. At death those securities generally receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which can make the deferred gain disappear for heirs. That is a feature to raise with your tax adviser, not a promise.

Do not choose an ESOP for the tax benefits alone. The transaction costs and the commitment to employees are real.

## Where does an ESOP fit with estate planning?

It affects your estate in three ways.

**It turns an illiquid asset into cash or a note.** A family business is hard to divide among heirs. After an ESOP sale, your estate holds money, notes and perhaps some retained shares, which are much simpler to split. Our article on [passing a family business to your children](/learn/business-owners/passing-a-family-business-to-children) discusses the problem the ESOP solves.

**It changes the estate tax picture.** A sale converts the business into cash that is valued at face amount. The federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so many owners owe no federal estate tax, though some states tax at lower levels. If your business is large, review it alongside [how a business is valued for estate tax](/learn/business-owners/how-a-business-is-valued-for-estate-tax).

**It leaves loose ends if you die mid-transaction.** If you die after signing but before closing, or after a partial sale, your executor inherits an unfinished process. That is a good reason to name a [capable executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and write a clear succession role for the person who will lead the company.

## ESOP, buy-sell agreement or sale to a competitor?

There is no best answer, only a better fit. This is how the three compare.

| Question | ESOP | Buy-sell among owners | Sale to outside buyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who buys | Employee trust | Co-owners or the company | Competitor or investor |
| Price | Fair market value by appraisal | Formula or agreed value | Negotiated, can include premium |
| Culture continues | Usually | Yes | Often changes |
| Cost and complexity | High | Moderate | Moderate to high |
| Owner liquidity | Often over years | At a trigger, such as death | Typically at closing |

An ESOP is a retirement-benefit transaction, not just a sale. If you only need a plan for what happens if a co-owner dies, a [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) is simpler. If your priority is a clean exit with the highest price, a sale to an outside buyer may beat both.

## Common mistakes

Owners get into trouble in a few predictable ways.

- **Assuming the company can carry the debt.** The loan is repaid from cash flow. If profits dip, a leveraged ESOP can squeeze the company.
- **Skipping the repurchase plan.** When employees retire, the company must buy back their shares. Without a funded plan, that obligation can grow into a crisis.
- **Treating the trustee as a rubber stamp.** A trustee who is not independent exposes everyone to legal claims.
- **Not explaining it to staff.** An ESOP that nobody understands feels like a trick and does little for morale.
- **Forgetting the rest of the estate.** The sale does not fix your will, your power of attorney or your beneficiary forms.

A short example shows the shape. Walter, 66, owns a 60-person machine shop. His daughter is a physician and does not want it. A competitor offered a price he liked, but would move the work out of state. Walter sells 40 percent of the company to an ESOP now and the rest in a second step over five years. The company uses its profits to repay the loan. He stays as chairman, and the plant manager, Denise, becomes CEO. Walter also updates his will and trust so the sale proceeds go to his daughter and a charity, and names a successor to carry out any unfinished deal if he dies.

## What should you do first?

Start with the plan, not the structure. Ask what you want: top price, a clean exit date, a legacy for the staff, an income stream for retirement. Then compare routes in light of those goals. Our guide to [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) lays out the main choices and the questions to ask yourself.

If an ESOP still looks right, build a team: an ESOP-experienced lawyer, an independent appraiser, a tax adviser and a lender. Keep your estate planning attorney in the room so the sale and your personal plan fit together.
