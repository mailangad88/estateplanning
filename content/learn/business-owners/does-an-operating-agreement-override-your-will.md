Maria owns 60 percent of a landscaping company in Naperville. Her will, signed five years ago, leaves "all my business interests" to her two sons. The operating agreement she signed when the company was formed says that on a member's death, the company has 90 days to buy that member's interest at a formula price. Which document wins?

For the business interest, the agreement does. A will decides who receives what you own, but it cannot widen what you own. The interest was always subject to the company's rules, and your heirs take it with those rules attached. This page, part of our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners), explains how the two documents fit together, what happens when there is no agreement, and how to line them up before a death forces the question.

## Why does the operating agreement usually win?

An operating agreement is a contract among the members, and the LLC itself is usually a party to it. When you die, your estate steps into your position, but it takes the position as it was, including every restriction you accepted. Think of it like a lease. Your will can leave your apartment lease to your nephew, but the landlord's clause about assignments still binds him.

Three kinds of clauses do most of the work:

- **Transfer restrictions.** Many agreements say no member may transfer an interest without consent of the others. Death is a transfer, and a clause that says "any transfer, voluntary or involuntary" catches it.
- **Admission of new members.** Even if the heir may hold the interest, becoming a voting member is a separate question that often needs a unanimous or majority vote.
- **Buyout on death.** The company or the surviving members may have the right, or the duty, to purchase the interest from the estate. See [how buy-sell agreements work](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) for the pricing and funding side.

Under the default rules of the Illinois and New Jersey LLC statutes, a deceased member's estate generally holds the economic rights, meaning a share of distributions, while management rights stay with the remaining members unless the agreement says otherwise. Check the exact language in your own agreement, because the statutes allow the members to change most of these defaults.

## What does a shareholder agreement do that a will cannot?

A corporation works the same way, with different paperwork. The shareholder agreement, sometimes called a stock restriction agreement, is a contract signed by the shareholders. It can restrict who may own shares, give the corporation a right of first refusal, or require a sale at death.

The bylaws and the stock certificates themselves may also carry restrictions. Look for a legend on the back of the certificate that refers to an agreement. A will that leaves "my shares" to a nephew does not erase that legend.

S corporations add a separate layer. Only certain kinds of owners are allowed, and a trust or heir that does not qualify can end the S election. That is a tax rule, not a contract rule, so your will cannot fix it either. If you have no will at all, the interest passes under [state intestacy rules](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) and the same restrictions apply. The [S corporation shares and trusts guide](/learn/business-owners/s-corporation-shares-inherited-by-a-trust) covers the elections.

## A worked example: Maria's company

This is an example with invented numbers. Maria's 60 percent interest is valued at $900,000 by the formula in the agreement.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| Maria dies | Her sons are named in her will, but nothing transfers to them yet. The executor is appointed by the court. |
| Day 1 to 30 | The company's other member, Dev, reads the agreement. He has 90 days to exercise the buyout. |
| Day 60 | Dev's company, funded by a $600,000 life insurance policy on Maria, pays that amount and signs a note for the remaining $300,000. |
| Result | Maria's sons receive $900,000 in cash and a note, not a 60 percent interest in a landscaping company. |

The sons may have wanted the business. The will said so. The agreement said otherwise, and the agreement was the one Maria signed with a counterparty. If she had wanted the sons to take over, she needed to change the agreement while Dev would still negotiate.

## What happens if the LLC has no operating agreement?

If there is no written agreement, the state's default LLC rules apply, and they are not designed for your family's wishes. Typical defaults:

1. The estate holds the economic interest only.
2. The remaining members control the company and may decide whether to admit the heirs.
3. Valuation and payout are not set, so the estate and the company have to agree or litigate.
4. A single-member LLC may be left with no one authorized to sign until a court names a representative.

Missing agreements also create a proof problem. Banks, lenders and the Secretary of State may ask for documents that show who owns what. If you are in this position, a short written agreement signed by all members is much cheaper now than a contested estate later. For how to document ownership when the paperwork is thin, start with your formation filing, the tax returns (the K-1s show each owner's share) and any loan documents that list owners.

## How do a trust and a will interact with the agreement?

Putting the interest into a revocable trust moves it out of your probate estate, which means your will no longer governs it. The trust document then tells the successor trustee what to do. Two cautions from what our attorneys see:

- Check that the operating agreement allows assignment to a trust. Some require consent, and an unauthorized assignment can trigger a default or a transfer restriction.
- Make sure the trust and the agreement name the same successor manager. A trust that says "my son runs the company" and an agreement that says "the remaining members manage" produce a standoff.

You can read more in [putting a business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust). For the probate side, [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains why titling beats the will.

## How do you line up your will, trust and company documents?

Use this checklist at your next review:

1. Pull the operating agreement or shareholder agreement and all amendments.
2. Underline every clause about death, disability, transfer and buyout.
3. Compare each one with the instructions in your will and trust.
4. Decide whether you want the family to keep the interest or be paid for it. Both are legitimate. Muddle is not.
5. If the agreement needs to change, get the other owners to sign an amendment now.
6. If the will needs to change, say expressly that the interest is subject to the company agreement.
7. Confirm the buyout, if any, is funded, with insurance or a payment schedule the company can really afford.
8. Name a successor manager and a backup signer for bank accounts.

A common mistake is relying on a will written before the company existed or before a new partner joined. The will then says nothing about the business, so the interest falls into the residue and is split among everyone the will names, which may include people who cannot or should not be involved.

## What about out-of-state and multi-owner situations?

The LLC is governed by the law of the state where it was formed, usually named in the agreement. Your will is governed by the law of your home state. A New Jersey resident who owns an Illinois LLC may need ancillary probate in Illinois unless the interest sits in a trust. If that sounds like you, look at [property in two states](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) and ask whether a trust is the cleaner route.

For the default rules when there is no agreement, see [what happens to your LLC or corporation shares when you die](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death).
