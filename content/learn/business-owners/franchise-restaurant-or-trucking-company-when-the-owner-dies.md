Some businesses are harder to pass on than others, and the reason is usually a piece of paper from someone else. A franchise sits under the franchisor's contract. A restaurant that serves alcohol needs a license that belongs to a person or entity. A trucking company runs on authority and insurance filings with federal and state agencies. Your heirs may inherit the building, the ovens and the trucks, and still not be allowed to open the doors.

This page explains what usually happens in each of those three industries, what the clock looks like, and what you can do while you are healthy. It is part of our guide to [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners), and it builds on our general article on [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).

## Why do licensed and franchised businesses pass differently?

Ordinary business property, such as equipment, inventory and cash, passes through your will or trust. Permissions are different. A franchise is a contract with a company that has the right to choose its owners. A license is a government permission granted to a named holder after a background check. Operating authority is a registration tied to a legal entity.

None of those can be left to someone by name in a will. The best you can do is arrange things so the person you want can apply, and so there is time for the application to be approved.

Compare how the three industries typically work.

| Business | What passes by will or trust | What usually does not | Who has to approve |
|---|---|---|---|
| Franchise | Ownership interest, equipment, lease rights | The right to operate the brand | The franchisor |
| Restaurant with alcohol | Building, kitchen, inventory, lease | The liquor license, health permit | State alcohol agency, local health department |
| Trucking company | Trucks, trailers, contracts, entity interest | Personal driver credentials, operating authority held personally | Federal and state transportation agencies, insurers |

The specifics vary by state and by agreement, so treat this as a map of questions to ask, not a set of answers.

## What happens to a franchise when the owner dies?

The franchise agreement controls. Start by finding it, then look for four clauses.

1. **Transfer.** Most agreements require the franchisor's written consent before ownership changes. Some give the franchisor a right of first refusal to buy the location.
2. **Death or disability.** Many agreements allow a set period after an owner's death to appoint a qualified manager, and another to transfer to an approved buyer. Missing the deadline can allow termination.
3. **Training and qualification.** A new owner or manager typically has to complete the franchisor's program.
4. **Fees and royalties.** The percentage owed to the franchisor continues while the unit operates.

Take Denise, 57, who owns two quick-service sandwich franchises and has a son, Marcus, who manages one of them. She dies without telling the franchisor her plan. Her agreement allows 180 days to transfer to an approved person and requires someone trained to be on site. Marcus qualifies as manager, but he is not the owner on paper, and her executor has not yet been appointed. The franchisor sends a notice about the transfer clause. Marcus keeps both stores open, the executor files a transfer application after eight weeks, and the franchisor approves Marcus for one unit and agrees to a sale of the other. It worked because someone on the ground was already qualified and the 180 days were long enough.

If you own a franchise, the most useful thing you can do is call the franchisor now. Ask what succession approval looks like, put the answer in writing and keep a copy with your will. Some franchisors will pre-approve a named family member.

## What happens to a restaurant when the owner dies?

The lease, the equipment and the staff continue. The permissions may not.

- **Liquor license.** Rules vary by state. In many places a death triggers a short grace period, and the executor or a manager can apply for a temporary permit so the restaurant can keep serving while a sale or transfer is processed. Without one, selling alcohol could be unlawful.
- **Health and food permits.** Often issued to a named operator at a specific address. A new owner usually needs a new inspection.
- **The lease.** Many restaurant leases carry a personal guaranty. The landlord can look to the estate for rent if the business closes, as covered in our article on [personal guaranties at death](/learn/business-owners/personal-guaranties-and-business-debts-at-death).
- **Payroll and tips.** Wages continue to be owed, and tip reporting does not stop. Late payroll tax deposits are the fastest way to turn a family business problem into a personal one.
- **Perishable stock.** Food spoils and suppliers want to be paid.

A restaurant is worth little without its owner's energy. Buyers look at books, location and a team that can run it without you. If the chef-owner is the brand, a sale may bring far less than the equipment is worth.

## What happens to a trucking company when the owner dies?

Start with how the company is owned. A trucking company run through an LLC or corporation continues to exist, and the person who inherits the entity interest controls it. An owner-operator who held authority in his own name has a bigger problem, because the registrations and insurance are tied to him.

What to look at first:

1. **Insurance.** A policy that names the owner as driver or as the insured may stop covering a truck once he dies. A truck on the road without coverage is an immediate risk.
2. **Operating authority and registrations.** Federal and state filings generally follow the legal entity. An heir may need to file new ones, and that takes time.
3. **Equipment loans and leases.** A lender may require payment or consent when the owner dies. Check for balloon payments and personal guaranties.
4. **Drivers and contracts.** A shipper's contract may be with the company or with the owner personally. Brokers and carriers often have the right to end it.
5. **Driver credentials.** A commercial driver's license is personal. Your heirs cannot use it, so you need other qualified drivers.

Because truck insurance and compliance are so specific, owners often use an entity with a named successor manager and a written plan for who calls the insurer first.

## Who can act in the first week?

Not the family, unless the paperwork says so. The business bank account, the lease and the license may all be in your name or in the name of an entity you own. Until a court appoints an [executor](/glossary/executor), or a [successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) takes over, nobody has clear authority to sign. A court can issue limited authority to keep a business open. It takes a petition and a few weeks.

A plan with a living trust and an entity avoids most of that wait. Holding the business in an LLC that your trust owns means the trustee can vote your interest and a named manager can sign checks the next day. Our article on [LLC interests at death](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) walks through the ownership chain.

## How should an owner of one of these businesses plan?

- **Train a second person to run it.** A general manager with the right credentials is worth more than any document.
- **Put the permissions in a binder.** Franchise agreement, licenses, insurance policies, lender contacts, agency numbers.
- **Ask for approvals in advance.** Franchisors, insurers and some lenders will tell you what they require.
- **Fund the transition.** Life insurance owned by a buy-sell agreement or by the company gives the family cash while the business is transferred. See [key person insurance](/learn/business-owners/key-person-insurance).
- **Decide who the business is for.** If no child wants it, planning for a sale beats leaving a hard choice to your executor.
- **Keep the creditor timeline in view.** Claims against an estate have a limited window, as explained in our article on the [probate creditor claims period](/learn/probate/probate-creditor-claims-period).
