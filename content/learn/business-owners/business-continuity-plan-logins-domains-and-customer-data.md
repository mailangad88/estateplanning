Most owners picture the legal side of their death: who gets the company, what the will says. Fewer picture the Monday after. An employee asks who approves payroll. A customer emails about an overdue invoice. The website's renewal notice goes to an email account nobody can open. Meanwhile, the family is in shock.

A business continuity plan is the document for that Monday. This page explains what it contains, how it fits with your legal documents, and how to build one in a few weekends. Our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners) shows how it connects with succession, buy-sell agreements and taxes.

## What does a business continuity plan do that a will does not?

A will says who inherits. A continuity plan says what to do tomorrow. The two work together, and neither does the other's job.

Consider Elena, who runs a three-person bookkeeping firm as a single-member LLC. Her will leaves the company to her husband. That is fine. But the firm's bank account is in her name only, the client portal uses her personal email, the firm's domain is registered under a login only she knows, and the next quarterly tax deadline for six clients falls in eight days. Her husband can inherit the company. He cannot yet touch anything in it.

The plan she needs is operational. It has to answer who has legal authority, who has practical access, and what must happen this week. Our article on [the documents every business owner needs](/learn/business-owners/documents-every-business-owner-needs) covers the legal paperwork, and [business owner incapacity planning](/learn/business-owners/business-owner-incapacity-planning) shows how to prepare for a disability, which is more common than death.

## What should be in the plan?

Keep it short enough to read in an hour. These are the sections that matter.

1. **People.** The first call, the second call, the attorney, the accountant, the insurance agent, the banker and the key employee. Include mobile numbers.
2. **Authority.** Who may sign checks, approve payroll, speak to the bank and sign contracts. Attach the power of attorney, the operating agreement, any trust and the resolution naming a successor manager.
3. **Money.** Bank accounts, credit lines, merchant processors, payroll provider and how much cash the business needs per month.
4. **Calendar.** Tax deadlines, license renewals, lease dates, insurance renewals, loan payments and annual filings.
5. **Customers and vendors.** The top ten of each, what they expect and who owns the relationship.
6. **Systems.** Accounting software, email, cloud storage, domain registrar, website host, and any software that holds customer data.
7. **Where the documents live.** Formation papers, EIN letter, leases, titles, insurance policies and licenses.
8. **Instructions.** What should be kept going, what should be paused, and when to call an advisor about selling or winding down.

A table helps the person who reads it under stress.

| Item | Where it lives | Who can access it today | Who should be able to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business bank account | Main branch | Owner only | Successor manager, with bank signature card |
| Domain registrar | Personal email login | Owner only | Company account with a second admin |
| Accounting software | Cloud login | Owner and bookkeeper | Bookkeeper and successor |
| Customer list | CRM | Owner and one employee | Two named employees |
| Insurance policies | Filing cabinet | Owner | Spouse and agent |

## Who owns the logins and the domain?

Whoever is named as the registered owner, and that is often one person using a personal email. This is one of the most common failure points in small businesses. The business may belong to the estate, but the domain, the website and the email remain locked by a password in the owner's head.

The fix is straightforward. Register domains in the company's name, not yours. Use a company email for the registrar login. Add a second administrator. Store the credentials in a password manager that lists a successor, and keep the recovery codes and two-factor devices in a safe place. Our articles on [domain names and websites after death](/learn/digital-assets/domain-names-and-websites-after-death) and [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) explain how providers handle requests and what a successor needs to show. If your income comes from a shop or channel online, see [online business, Etsy shops and YouTube channels after death](/learn/digital-assets/online-business-etsy-shop-or-youtube-channel-after-death).

## What about customer, patient and employee data?

Privacy duties continue when the owner does not. A bookkeeper holds tax data. A dental office holds health records. A shop holds customer cards. An owner's death does not make those obligations go away, and a hurried successor can create a breach by forwarding files or sharing a password.

Write down where the data lives, who may open it and what the legal rules are for your business. Health and financial businesses often have formal rules about records and notice. Ask your business attorney what applies to you. The plan should say who is authorized to access the data in an emergency, and it should list the vendors that hold it, such as the cloud provider and the payroll company. Under most state laws, an executor or trustee can act with proper authority, but a provider may still ask for documents.

## How does the plan connect to your legal documents?

The plan only works if someone has the legal right to use it. That is where the legal documents come in.

- **Power of attorney.** If you are disabled, an agent needs written authority to sign for you. Name the business expressly and consider a separate business agent. See our overview of [powers of attorney for business and other special assets](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-and-retirement-accounts-safe-deposit-boxes-vehicles-and-businesses).
- **Operating agreement or bylaws.** These should name a successor manager and say how the company continues. They also decide whether heirs become members or only receive distributions.
- **Living trust.** If the business interest sits in a trust, a successor trustee can act without waiting for a court. Our article on [putting your business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust) covers the tradeoffs.
- **Buy-sell agreement.** If you have partners, the agreement says who buys your share and how it is paid for. See [buy-sell agreements](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements).

If the plan and the papers disagree, the papers usually win, so check them against each other once a year.

## How do you build the plan in four weekends?

Break the job into small pieces.

1. Weekend one: list the people, the accounts and the deadlines.
2. Weekend two: move logins into a password manager, add a second administrator to the key systems and register domains to the company.
3. Weekend three: write the one-page instructions for the first week and sit down with the person who will use them.
4. Weekend four: check that the legal documents give that person actual authority, and fix any gap with an attorney.

Then run a test. Ask your chosen successor to find the payroll login and the next tax deadline using only the plan. Whatever they cannot find is what you fix.
