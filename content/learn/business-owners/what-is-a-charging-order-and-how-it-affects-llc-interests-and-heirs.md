If you own part of an LLC, you have probably heard that a "charging order" protects it from creditors. That is partly true, and the part that is not true is where families get hurt. A charging order is a court-created collection tool, and it limits what a creditor can do to your LLC interest. It is not a force field.

This page explains what a charging order does, when it protects, when it fails, and why it matters again once the interest passes to a spouse or child. It belongs to our [business owner planning guide](/learn/business-owners), and it assumes you have a basic LLC.

## What is a charging order in plain terms?

Suppose Gary owns 40% of an LLC that holds a small apartment building, and a driver wins a $200,000 judgment against Gary after a car accident unrelated to the business. The driver wants Gary's LLC interest. A judge could issue a charging order that tells the LLC: whenever you pay Gary his share, pay it to the driver instead.

That is all. The driver does not become a member. The driver cannot vote, cannot sell the building and cannot demand a payout. If the manager decides to reinvest profits for the year, the driver waits. Most state LLC laws were written this way so that one member's personal debt does not dismantle the business for the others.

## Does Illinois or New Jersey treat charging orders differently?

Both states' LLC statutes generally make a charging order the remedy for a creditor of an individual member, which means the creditor cannot simply seize the interest and sell it. Details differ on points that matter, such as whether a court may order foreclosure of the interest in some cases and how a single-member LLC is treated, and courts have shifted over time. Treat a statement like "my LLC is judgment proof" with caution, and ask an attorney how a court in your county has applied the statute.

## Can the manager just refuse to pay distributions?

For the length of the charging order, a manager who controls distributions can decide not to make them, and many do. But a court can see through a pattern. If the LLC suddenly pays the other members salaries, "loans" or bonuses while withholding the distribution that would reach the creditor, a judge may treat that as bad faith. Managers should also remember that members can owe tax on their share of LLC income even when no cash is paid, so withholding all distributions can leave the other members with a tax bill they cannot cover. The practical answer is usually a negotiated settlement, not a standoff.

## When does a charging order protect, and when does it not?

| Situation | Does the charging order help? |
|---|---|
| Member's personal creditor (judgment from a car accident) | Usually yes, as the main remedy |
| Member in a multi-member LLC | Strongest protection, because other members are harmed by any sale |
| Single-member LLC | Weaker; courts in some states allow more than a charging order |
| Divorce | No; family courts divide or offset the value |
| IRS or state tax debt | No; tax liens and levies have their own powers |
| Creditor of the LLC itself | Not relevant; the company's assets are exposed |
| Member who signed a personal guaranty on a loan | No; the guaranty makes the debt personal |
| Fraudulent transfer into the LLC | No; a court can undo transfers made to dodge a known creditor |

That last point is the one we stress most. Moving assets into an LLC after you are sued, or when you know a claim is coming, does not protect them and can create a second problem. See [personal guaranties and business debts at death](/learn/business-owners/personal-guaranties-and-business-debts-at-death) for how guaranties work.

## What happens to a charging order when the member dies?

The member's rights pass to heirs under the will, a trust or the operating agreement. Our guide to [what happens to your LLC when you die](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death) covers who becomes a member, and the charging order issue comes in at the next step.

Once the interest is held by your daughter, her personal creditors, her bad car accident and her divorce all become risks to the family business that your death did not create but your plan can address. Consider Lena, 61, who owns 100% of a landscaping LLC. She leaves it outright to her son Mark, 34. Five years later Mark is sued over a collision and also separates from his wife. The charging order limits what a collection creditor takes, but his spouse's divorce claim reaches the value of the interest, and a single-member LLC may not enjoy the protection Lena assumed.

## How can heirs be protected better?

An operating agreement is the first layer, because it can restrict who may hold an interest, require consent to transfers and set a buyout price. Our article on the [buy-sell agreement](/learn/business-owners/buy-sell-agreements) covers one version of this.

A second layer is how the heir holds the interest. A trust with a trustee, rather than outright ownership, keeps the interest out of the heir's own name, and a spendthrift clause blocks a beneficiary's creditors from reaching it. [Keeping an inheritance safe from a child's divorce, addiction or bad spouse](/learn/trusts/keeping-an-inheritance-safe-from-a-childs-divorce-addiction-or-bad-spouse) explains the trust version, and [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) describes the clause. Many families combine both: the LLC holds the business, and a trust holds the interest.

A family LLC or limited partnership that holds investments or real estate can also support gifting and valuation discounts; those are tax strategies, and the details are in [valuation discounts and family limited partnerships](/learn/estate-tax/valuation-discounts-and-family-limited-partnerships).

## Checklist: questions to ask about your own LLC

1. Is it single-member or multi-member? Charging order protection changes.
2. Who can manage, and can the manager decide when distributions are made?
3. Does the operating agreement restrict transfers and say what happens at death?
4. Do any lenders hold personal guaranties from you?
5. Do you hold the interest directly, or through a living trust? Our article on [putting a business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust) explains the tradeoffs.
6. Does your heir have a spouse, or debts, that would reach the interest?
7. Have you kept the LLC separate, with its own bank account and records? A court that sees personal and business money mixed may ignore the LLC.

## Where do creditors of a deceased owner come into this?

They are a different group. Creditors of the estate, who must file claims during probate, are paid from estate assets, and an LLC interest the person owned at death is one of them. Their claim is not a charging order case, and the personal liability of a guarantor continues after death. Our article on [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) lays out the order in which debts are paid.
