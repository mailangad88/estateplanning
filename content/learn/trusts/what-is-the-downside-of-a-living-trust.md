Plenty of articles about living trusts read like brochures. This one is the other side of the ledger. If you are weighing a trust, you should know exactly how they disappoint people, because most of the bad outcomes are predictable and avoidable.

We will go through six drawbacks, who each one hurts most, and what to do about it. For the benefits and the situations where a trust earns its cost, our guide to [whether you need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) is the companion piece, and the [trust overview](/learn/trusts) shows where this tool sits among the others.

## The short version

- A living trust costs more than a will, and the fee is only part of the cost.
- It only works for assets that are actually in it, so retitling is part of the job.
- It does not save income tax, shrink an estate tax bill or stop your own creditors.
- It needs attention when you buy property, remarry, move or change your mind.
- It does not replace a will, a financial power of attorney or a healthcare directive.
- Handled well, none of these is a reason to avoid a trust you need. Ignored, any of them can undo it.

## Drawback 1: Is the cost really higher?

Yes. A trust-based plan includes a trust agreement, a pour-over will, powers of attorney and healthcare documents, and the attorney's time to fit them to your family. That is more drafting than a will package. You can see typical ranges in [how much a living trust costs](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost).

The less obvious cost is your own time. You will gather deeds and account statements, sign new forms at banks, and answer questions about where things are. Add the fee to that effort and ask a plain question: what does this trust save my family? If your state has quick, cheap probate and your only big assets pass by beneficiary form, the answer may be "not much."

## Drawback 2: What happens if the trust is never funded?

This is the one that actually hurts families. A trust controls only what is titled in its name, or what names it as beneficiary. Sign the papers, put them in a drawer, and leave the house in your own name, and the house goes through probate as if no trust existed. The pour-over will then sends it to the trust, but only after the court process the trust was meant to avoid.

Consider Dolores, 71, who paid for a trust in 2019. She meant to deed her house over but the lawyer's office and the county recorder both seemed like a hassle, so she waited. When she died, her son found a handsome binder and a deed still in her name alone. He paid for a probate he thought he had been spared. Our page on [what happens with an unfunded trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) walks through that outcome.

The fix is a funding checklist and a person responsible for finishing it. [How to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) lists the steps, and many firms will handle the deed for you if you ask at signing.

## Drawback 3: Does a trust need maintenance?

It does, and this is where good trusts decay. Life changes and the trust does not notice.

| Event | What can go wrong | What fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| You buy a second property | It stays in your name and goes to probate | Deed it to the trust at closing |
| You open a new brokerage account | It is outside the trust | Title it in the trust's name |
| A named trustee dies or moves away | The backup list is stale | Amend the trust |
| You remarry or divorce | The document still reflects your old family | Review with an attorney |
| A child has a baby or a disability | Shares and protections no longer fit | Amend the trust |
| You move to a new state | State rules on trusts or property differ | Have the plan reviewed |

Plan a review every few years, and after any event in that table. Our checklist of [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) is a good prompt.

## Drawback 4: Why does a revocable trust save no taxes?

Because the law treats the assets as yours. You can take them back, so while you are alive the trust is ignored for income tax, and at death the assets count in your taxable estate. Many people buy a trust expecting a tax benefit and are disappointed.

The federal estate tax only applies above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families owe nothing at that level anyway. State death taxes are a separate matter and can start much lower; the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) shows whether yours applies. For what a trust can and cannot do on tax, read [does a trust avoid taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes).

The same logic explains the creditor point. Assets you can reclaim are assets your creditors can reach, which is covered in [whether a living trust protects assets from creditors](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-protect-assets-from-creditors).

## Drawback 5: Will banks and other institutions give me trouble?

Sometimes, in small ways. A bank may ask for a certification of trust, which is a short summary of the trust that does not reveal its terms. A brokerage may have its own form. An insurance company may want the trustee named on the policy. Most of this is routine, and a good attorney will prepare a certification for you.

The place friction shows up is later, when a successor trustee is dealing with a branch employee who has never seen a trust. Keep copies of the signed pages and the certification where the successor can find them.

## Drawback 6: Does a trust make me feel finished when I am not?

This one is subtle. People walk away from signing day thinking the plan is done. It is not. Retirement accounts and life insurance pass by beneficiary form, not by trust, unless the trust is named. The trust does nothing about guardians for minor children, which are named in a will, so you still need the [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) that comes in a good package. And a trust does not give anyone authority over assets outside it, which is why a durable power of attorney remains on the list.

Our overview of [common estate planning mistakes](/learn/basics/common-estate-planning-mistakes) covers more of these traps.

## When are the downsides worth it?

A fair test is to total the trouble and compare it with the specific problem you are solving.

1. Write down the problem: slow probate, property in two states, a young beneficiary, a blended family, privacy.
2. Price the alternative: a will, a transfer on death deed, joint ownership, or beneficiary forms.
3. Add up the trust's costs: the fee, the retitling and the upkeep.
4. Ask whether you will realistically keep it current.
5. Decide, and put the funding task on a calendar with a date.

If the answer to step 4 is no, a simpler plan you will maintain beats a better plan you will not. Our [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) can help frame step 1.

## How state law changes this

Probate cost and speed are the main reason the trade-off swings from state to state. Where probate involves percentage-based fees or months of supervised court work, the downsides of a trust shrink next to the cost of doing nothing. Where small estates can be settled by affidavit, a trust is harder to justify. Start with your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois) or [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) if you live there, since the numbers differ.
