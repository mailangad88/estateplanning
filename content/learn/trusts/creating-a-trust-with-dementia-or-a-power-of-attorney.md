A mother is forgetting names. Her children are worried about the house, about the bills, about what happens if she falls. Someone says, "We should put everything in a trust." The next question is the hard one: is it too late? Can she sign? Can her daughter, who holds the power of attorney, do it for her?

The honest answer is that it depends on the person's capacity on the day of signing and on what the power of attorney actually says. This page explains both, and what to do when the door looks closed. It belongs with our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts), and it pairs with the page on a [parent with dementia and no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

## Can a person with dementia sign a trust?

Yes, if they have legal capacity when they sign. Dementia is a spectrum, and capacity is not the same as a diagnosis. Many people in early or middle stages understand their finances well enough to make decisions, and they have a legal right to do so. The test is applied to a specific moment and a specific decision.

For a trust, the usual questions are whether the person:

1. Knows what kinds of property they own, in general terms.
2. Knows who their closest family members are and how they relate to them.
3. Understands what the trust document does, such as who gets property and who will manage it.
4. Can make and express a choice without being pushed.

The standard for a trust is generally close to that for a will, and somewhat lower than for a complicated contract. Our [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) glossary entry gives the concept. Because dementia fluctuates, many people do better in the morning than in the evening, and attorneys schedule signings to match.

## How does an attorney protect a signing like this?

When a client has a diagnosis, a careful attorney will:

- Meet the client alone, without the relative who is paying or pushing.
- Ask open questions, not leading ones, and write down the answers.
- Request a letter from the treating physician close to the signing date, stating the client can make financial decisions.
- Consider a video recording, with the client's consent.
- Use independent witnesses, not family members who would benefit.

None of this makes a trust immune to a challenge. It builds the record. If a relative later argues the person was [unduly influenced](/glossary/undue-influence), that record is what a judge reads. See [whether a living trust can be contested](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested).

## Can a power of attorney create a trust?

Not unless the document says so. A [power of attorney](/glossary/power-of-attorney) is a grant of specific powers, and courts interpret it narrowly. Creating a trust, changing beneficiaries and making gifts are three powers that many forms leave out or limit. In some states a statute requires explicit authority for each one. Our page on [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) lists the usual limits.

Even where the power exists, the agent is a fiduciary. They must act for the person's benefit and follow what the person would have wanted, as shown in earlier estate plans or statements. A trust that rewrites who inherits, or that benefits the agent, is a lawsuit waiting to happen.

## Can an agent fund a trust the person already signed?

This is the easier case. Suppose Walter signed a revocable trust at 74, but never retitled the house or accounts. At 79 he has moderate dementia. His son Ravi, his agent, wants to finish the job. Many states let an agent transfer property into an existing revocable trust if the power of attorney allows it or the trust says the agent may. Because the trust already carries Walter's wishes, the agent is carrying out his plan, not making a new one.

If the power of attorney is silent, Ravi can still bring the issue to an attorney, who will check state law, and possibly ask a court to confirm. Compare this with the [steps to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## What are the options when the person cannot sign?

| Situation | Options |
|---|---|
| Has capacity, even on good days | Sign a trust or amend an existing one, with safeguards |
| Lacks capacity, has a power of attorney with trust powers | Agent may fund an existing trust or create one in line with known wishes |
| Lacks capacity, power of attorney is silent on trusts | Agent can manage assets under the power; for a trust, seek a court order |
| Lacks capacity, no power of attorney | Guardianship or conservatorship; see [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) |
| Family wants to help with their own money | Create a third-party trust for the person's benefit |
| Medicaid is the concern | Special rules apply; see below |

A court can authorize a guardian or conservator to create a trust for a person who cannot, generally with a judge's approval and a requirement that it serves the person's interests. This costs more and takes longer than signing at the kitchen table, but it carries more authority.

## How do family dynamics change the picture?

These signings are rarely quiet. One child lives nearby and does the driving, another lives across the country and hears about it later. Whatever the law allows, a plan that looks like it favors the caregiver child will draw suspicion. Two habits help. First, talk to the siblings before the signing, even if the conversation is uncomfortable; the page on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) offers a way in. Second, let the parent's own words drive the plan. A short letter in the parent's handwriting, written when they were clearer, explaining why they want the house to go one way, carries real weight later.

Keep a calm record of what you did and why. If you are the agent, save receipts and notes. If you are the child who was left out, ask for the documents politely and early. Most disputes soften when people see the reasoning.

## What if the goal is Medicaid planning?

Families often reach for a trust because a nursing home is on the horizon. A revocable trust does nothing for Medicaid eligibility. An irrevocable trust can, but transfers are subject to a five-year look-back in most states, and a person who needs care soon may not benefit. Transfers by an agent, in particular, get close scrutiny. The rules are detailed, so see [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) before moving anything.

## What should you do now?

If a parent still has capacity, act soon. A visit to an attorney can cover a trust or a simpler will, a durable financial power of attorney, a healthcare directive and a HIPAA release in a single appointment. See [dementia and advance directives](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) for the healthcare side. If capacity is borderline, ask the doctor and attorney to coordinate. If it is gone, get a copy of whatever documents exist and bring them to an attorney before any retitling.
