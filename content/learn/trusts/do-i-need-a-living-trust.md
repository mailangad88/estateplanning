"Do I need a trust?" is one of the most common questions people ask about estate planning, and the honest answer is that some people do and many do not. A living trust is a useful tool in specific situations. It is an expensive extra in others.

This article walks through the situations where a trust usually helps, the ones where a will is enough, the real downsides, and a short self-check. It builds on [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) and the side-by-side comparison in [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust).

## What is the quick answer?

You are more likely to benefit from a trust if several of the first list describe you, and more likely to be fine with a will if the second list does.

**Signs a trust fits:**

- You own a house or other real estate, especially more than one parcel.
- You own property in another state.
- You live in a state where probate is slow, expensive or heavily court-supervised.
- You worry about becoming unable to manage your own affairs and want a smooth handoff.
- You want your plan to stay private rather than become a court record.
- You have a blended family, a child with special needs, or a beneficiary who is young or poor with money.
- You own a small business or rental properties.

**Signs a will is probably enough:**

- Your assets are modest and most of them have named beneficiaries (retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death bank accounts).
- You rent, or your only large asset is a retirement account.
- Your state has a simple small-estate process that fits your situation.
- Your heirs are adults who get along and can manage money.

## Which situations make a living trust worth considering?

### You own real estate

Real property is the clearest reason. A house in your own name goes through probate unless something else moves it. A trust that owns the house avoids that court process, and the successor trustee can sell or transfer it without waiting for court authority. Our article on [putting your house in a trust](/blog/should-i-put-my-house-in-a-trust) weighs this in more detail, and [how to transfer a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) covers the steps.

### You own property in more than one state

Probate is handled in each state where you own real property. Marcus, 62, lives in Ohio and owns a lake cabin in Michigan. Without a trust, his family may need an [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) in Michigan on top of the Ohio case. A deed putting the cabin into his trust, recorded in the county where it sits, can avoid that second proceeding. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

### Probate is slow or costly where you live

Probate time and cost differ sharply by state. Some states charge percentage fees on the estate's value; others use simple affidavits for small estates. Read [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) and [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take) for your state's pattern, because this single factor can decide the question.

### You want incapacity planning

A will does nothing while you are alive. If a stroke or dementia leaves you unable to manage money, a trust lets your successor trustee step in without a court-supervised conservatorship. A financial power of attorney can do part of this, and every plan should have one, but banks sometimes resist them, and a trust gives a cleaner structure for assets inside it. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).

### You want privacy

A will becomes a public court record once probate opens, and the inventory of assets and heirs can be public too. A trust usually stays private. Not everyone cares, but people with unusual family arrangements or large gifts to charity often do.

### You have a blended family

When there is a second spouse and children from a first marriage, a trust can provide for the spouse for life and protect what is left for the children. A will alone can do part of this, but a trust gives a trustee ongoing authority to carry out the plan. See [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage) and [stepchildren and inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance).

### A beneficiary cannot manage money directly

A minor, a young adult, a person with an addiction or spending problem, or a person with a disability may do better with a trustee managing distributions. A child with a disability who receives means-tested benefits needs a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) so an inheritance does not cost them benefits. See also [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).

### You own a business

A trust can hold ownership interests, and the successor trustee can keep the business running without a court-appointed representative. Operating agreements and buy-sell terms need to be coordinated with the trust.

## Who may not need a living trust?

| Situation | Trust helps? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Single renter, retirement account and savings | Rarely | Beneficiary designations and a will cover most of it |
| Young family with term life insurance | Often a will is enough at first | Insurance passes by beneficiary form; a trust may matter for minor children |
| Married homeowners in a slow-probate state | Often yes | The house is the main probate asset |
| Married homeowners in a quick small-estate state | Maybe | Compare cost; a transfer on death deed may be an alternative |
| Rental property owner | Usually yes | Multiple deeds, and management continuity |
| Owner of property in two states | Usually yes | Avoids a second probate |
| Blended family | Often yes | Lets you control who gets what, and when |
| Beneficiary on public benefits | Yes, but a special needs trust, not just any trust | Protects eligibility |
| Small business owner | Often yes | Continuity and privacy |
| Retiree with mostly IRAs and a paid-off condo | Depends | Retirement accounts stay out of the trust anyway |

If you are in one of the "maybe" rows, a transfer on death deed or joint ownership might do part of the job, but each has risks. Read [transfer on death deed vs. trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust) and [joint ownership vs. trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust) before choosing.

## What are the downsides of a living trust?

Be clear-eyed about these before you decide.

- **Cost.** A trust costs more than a will to prepare. See [how much a living trust costs](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost).
- **Funding work.** The trust only controls what you put in it. Deeds, accounts and other assets must be retitled. Skipping this step is the most common failure; see [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
- **No tax savings.** A revocable trust is ignored for income tax and is included in your taxable estate. The federal estate tax only applies above $15,000,000 per person in 2026 anyway. See [does a trust avoid taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes).
- **No creditor shield.** Because you can revoke the trust, your creditors can generally reach its assets during your life.
- **Upkeep.** New property, new accounts and life changes should be reflected. A trust that is two houses behind is half a plan.
- **Paperwork with institutions.** Some lenders, insurers and brokerages want a certification of trust and extra forms.

## What are the common misconceptions?

**"Trusts are only for the wealthy."** The relevant question is the type of asset and your family, not wealth. A modest estate with a house and a vulnerable beneficiary can justify a trust.

**"A trust avoids all taxes."** It does not. It is mainly a probate and incapacity tool.

**"A trust replaces a will."** It does not. A trust-based plan includes a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) to catch anything left outside the trust.

**"Set it and forget it."** A trust needs to be reviewed after major life events. See [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## How much money do you need?

There is no minimum. Consider two examples. Carla, 70, has a $250,000 home in a state with expensive percentage-based probate fees and a son who lives in another city. A trust might save time, money and stress. Raj, 45, has a $500,000 401(k), a term life policy and a rented apartment, with his spouse named on everything. A trust would add cost and give him little, since the accounts already pass by beneficiary form. Asset type decides far more than asset size.

## A ten-question self-check

Answer yes or no. Three or more "yes" answers suggest that a conversation about a trust is worth having.

1. Do I own real estate in my own name?
2. Do I own property in more than one state?
3. Would probate in my state take more than a year or cost thousands of dollars?
4. Is anyone I would leave money to a minor or unable to manage it?
5. Do I have children from more than one relationship?
6. Does a beneficiary receive means-tested public benefits?
7. Do I own part of a business?
8. Would I like my estate matters kept out of public court records?
9. Would I want someone to take over my finances smoothly if I became ill?
10. Am I willing to retitle my property and keep the trust updated?

Question 10 matters as much as the others. A trust you will not maintain is not a good fit.
