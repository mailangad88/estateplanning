Naming a trustee feels like one decision. It is not. In practice it is a chain of decisions: who serves first, who serves with them, who takes over if they quit, and how anyone gets replaced when things go sideways. Families run into trouble at the links nobody wrote down.

This page covers those links. If you are still picking the person, start with [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee). If you are the one setting up a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), use this page to decide how much backup the document needs.

## Can you name co-trustees?

Yes, and sometimes it is the right call. Two siblings who trust each other, or a family member alongside a bank, can share the job. The drawback is speed and friction. If the document says nothing, co-trustees must generally act together, and many banks will want both signatures on every check.

Four choices to make in the document:

1. **Unanimous or majority.** With two, you cannot have a majority, so one disagreement freezes the trust. With three, a majority can break the tie.
2. **Who can act alone.** You can let either trustee sign for ordinary matters, like paying a utility bill, while requiring both for selling real estate.
3. **Who holds the money.** A common arrangement is for a bank to hold the investments and the family member to make distribution decisions.
4. **What happens if one leaves.** Say clearly whether the remaining trustee continues alone or the vacancy is filled.

A realistic example. Carol, a widow, names her son Anil and her daughter Beth as co-trustees with majority rule, and she names a family friend as a third co-trustee for tie-breaking only. That is more machinery than most trusts need, but Carol knew her children disagreed about money. A simpler answer would be one trustee, with Beth required to send Anil a yearly summary.

## Can one child be trustee over the others?

It is the most common setup, and families handle it better than lawyers expect. The risk is not dishonesty but perception. A sibling who controls the checkbook can look like a boss to the others, even when she is doing everything right.

The plan below lowers the temperature:

- Require an annual written report to every beneficiary.
- Say plainly what the trustee may pay herself, if anything. Our page on [trustee compensation](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation) explains typical arrangements.
- Let the other siblings replace the trustee by majority vote, or let a neutral person do it.
- Name a professional as backup or as a co-trustee for large or complicated assets.

None of this signals distrust. It tells everyone the rules before there is a conflict.

## How does a trustee resign?

A trustee who no longer wants the job can resign, but must do it in a way that does not leave the trust without leadership. The usual steps are:

1. Read the resignation clause in the trust document.
2. Give written notice to the people named there. Often that is the grantor, the next trustee in line and the adult beneficiaries.
3. Wait out any notice period, commonly 30 to 60 days, or get the written waiver of those who are entitled to notice.
4. Hand over assets and records to the successor and obtain a signed receipt.
5. Provide a final accounting if the beneficiaries or the document ask for one.

If the trust names no successor and the beneficiaries cannot agree, a court can appoint one. That takes months and costs money from the trust. A well-drafted document with two or three backups avoids it. Our page on [what to do when a trustee dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-a-trustee-dies) addresses the related case.

A resigning trustee still answers for what happened while in office. Quitting is not a way to erase an earlier mistake.

## What happens if the trustee is incapacitated?

For many families this is the more likely event. A trustee in her 80s can develop dementia, and a trust that just says "if she is unable to serve" leaves everyone guessing.

Good drafting sets a test that does not need a judge. The usual version says a trustee is incapacitated upon written statements from two licensed physicians, or when a court finds so. When that happens, the successor takes over immediately, using a certification of trust that includes the doctors' letters.

Without such a clause, relatives may need a court proceeding to prove incapacity, which exposes the family's private information and freezes accounts in the meantime. This is also why you should look at what a [power of attorney can and cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do). A power of attorney does not give an agent authority over trust assets. Only the trustee and successor trustee have that.

What if you are the grantor and trustee of your own trust? The same logic holds. Our page on [being your own trustee](/learn/trusts/can-i-be-my-own-trustee) explains why your successor needs a clear signal for when to step in.

## How do you replace a trustee who is not working out?

The route depends on whether the grantor is alive and the trust is still revocable.

| Situation | Who can replace the trustee |
|---|---|
| Grantor alive, trust revocable | The grantor, by amendment |
| Grantor incapacitated | Whoever the document authorizes, otherwise a court |
| Grantor dead, document gives a removal power | The person or group named, often beneficiaries by majority |
| Grantor dead, document silent | A court, on petition of a beneficiary or co-trustee |

Courts remove trustees for serious problems: self-dealing, ignoring beneficiaries, failing to invest or account, or being unable to act. Conflict alone is not enough. Our detailed page on [how to remove a trustee](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-remove-a-trustee) walks through the grounds and process.

Think of the [trusts section](/learn/trusts) as the map for the rest of this planning.

## Should the trust name a bank or trust company?

Maybe as a backup, rarely as the only choice for a modest estate. Corporate trustees charge a percentage of assets, often with a minimum annual fee, so they tend to make sense when the trust is large, lasts a long time, or the family cannot stay neutral. Ask any institution for its fee schedule in writing before you name it, and compare that against what a family trustee would reasonably do for free or for modest pay. Some families split the work: the bank invests, the daughter decides who gets what and when, and the document says so in plain words so the bank's staff do not have to guess.

## How state law changes this

States set default rules for how co-trustees vote, how resignation notice is given, what counts as incapacity if the document is silent, and which court hears removal petitions. Most of these defaults apply only when the trust does not address the point. That is the strongest argument for a document that does. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/new-york) has local details, and the trust is only as strong as its backup plan.
