Plenty of people leave the attorney's office with a binder and wonder whether they are supposed to take it to the courthouse. They are not. The signing was the last formal step. A living trust is a private agreement and, in most places, it never meets a judge unless something goes wrong.

That is the main answer, but the details matter: who has to watch you sign, whether a notary is optional, what a bank will ask for, and where the original should live so your family can find it. This page covers each. For how the trust works day to day, see our [revocable living trust guide](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), and for the whole subject, our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## The short version

- Signing: sign in front of a notary at minimum. Add two witnesses if your state or your attorney asks for them.
- Filing: the trust itself is generally not filed. The deed that moves real estate into it is recorded.
- Privacy: a trust stays private unless a dispute pulls it into court.
- Proof: use a certification of trust with banks and title companies.
- Storage: keep the original safe, and tell your successor trustee where it is.

## Do trusts need to be witnessed?

Sometimes. The rule is set by state law and it is not uniform.

A will almost always needs two witnesses. A trust is a different kind of document, and in many states a revocable trust can be signed by the creator alone, with no witnesses required, as long as the creator signs voluntarily and has capacity. Other states treat a revocable trust's dispositive provisions as close enough to a will that they require the same formalities. Florida is a well-known example, and its practitioners use two witnesses and a notary on a trust.

Our approach is to ignore the minimum and use the safe version. Two adult witnesses who are not beneficiaries and a notary cost nothing extra when the signing happens at an attorney's office, and they make a later challenge harder. If someone ever asks whether you understood what you signed, a witness who can say yes is valuable. The challenge grounds are discussed in [can a living trust be contested](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested).

## Does a trust need to be notarized?

Rarely required by law, nearly always done. The notary matters because other institutions rely on it.

- **County recorders** will not record a deed without a notarized acknowledgment.
- **Banks and brokerages** usually ask for a notarized certification of trust or the signature page.
- **Title companies** want proof the trustee has authority, which a notarized certification gives.

A trust that is signed without a notary is not necessarily invalid, but it may be hard to use. Remote online notarization is allowed in many states, and some trusts can be signed that way. See [signing estate planning documents electronically](/learn/basics/sign-estate-planning-documents-electronically) if you are considering it.

## Does a trust have to be filed with a court or registered?

In most states, no. You sign it, you keep it, and that is all. A small number of states offer optional registration of a trust with a court, mostly useful for trusts that will last a long time. Nothing is required while the creator is alive.

After the creator dies, some states require the trustee to give notice to beneficiaries and heirs, and in some a trustee may choose to file a notice with the probate court to start a short deadline for creditor claims. Those steps are about administration, not about creating the trust. See [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) for what comes after.

The one filing everyone does is on the real estate. A deed to the trustee is recorded in the county where the land sits, which makes it a public record. That does not reveal who the beneficiaries are, only that the trustee holds title.

## Is a trust really private?

More than a will, yes. A will is filed with the probate court after death and anyone can read it. A trust normally never leaves your files. Banks see a certification, not the document. Heirs see it when they have a legal right to. The reasons and exceptions are in [what trust beneficiaries can see and when](/learn/trusts/what-trust-beneficiaries-can-see-and-when).

Do not overstate this. A pour-over will that accompanies the trust is public if it goes through probate, and a trust pulled into a dispute can end up in a court file. For most families, the practical difference is that the trust's terms, such as who gets what and how much, are not published.

## What is a certification of trust?

A certification of trust, sometimes called a trust certificate or abstract, is a one- or two-page summary that proves the trust exists, names the trustee and states the trustee's powers. It leaves out the beneficiaries and the dollar amounts.

Banks and title companies accept it in most states because state law requires them to. If a bank insists on reading the full document, ask to speak with the manager or send a letter from your attorney citing your state's trust code. Keep several signed originals, since each institution will want to keep one.

## What is an assignment of personal property?

It is a one-page transfer of your everyday possessions to the trust. Furniture, art, clothing and jewelry have no title document to retitle, so the assignment states that all your tangible items now belong to the trust, including those you buy later. Without it, those items could be left behind in probate. It is standard at the signing. For more on this, see [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## Can a trust be set up after someone dies?

Not by the person who died. A living trust has to be created while its creator is alive and has capacity. Three things sometimes get mistaken for that:

1. **A testamentary trust** is written into a will and begins at death. It goes through probate, and [what a testamentary trust is](/learn/trusts/testamentary-trusts) explains the trade-offs.
2. **A court or family agreement** can set up a trust from the estate for a minor or an incapacitated heir.
3. **An heir can place their own inheritance into a trust** they create, which is a new trust, not a continuation.

If someone you love died without a trust, there is rarely a quick fix. The usual route is probate. Our guide to [what happens when someone dies without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains how it unfolds.

## Where should you keep your trust documents?

Keep the original signed document somewhere safe that a second person can reach. That second part is the one that fails.

| Place | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Fireproof home safe | Quick access | Does anyone else know the combination? |
| Your attorney's vault | Safekeeping, a record of the original | You must tell the family the firm holds it |
| Bank safe deposit box | Security | Access after death may need a court order in some states |
| Digital copy | Backup for the trustee | A scan is not the signed original |
| With the successor trustee | Immediate access | Only if you trust them with it now |

Whatever you choose, tell the people who need to know. Put the location in a letter your successor trustee keeps, and list the trust, the pour-over will, the deeds and the account statements in one place. The page on [organizing your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents) offers a simple system, and [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) covers the companion document.

## Who should have a copy?

Your successor trustee should have a full copy now, not at your death. Your attorney should hold one. Consider giving one to your spouse or the person who would sort out your affairs, as well. Adult children who are beneficiaries generally do not need one yet, though some families prefer to share it early to avoid surprises. That choice is yours.
