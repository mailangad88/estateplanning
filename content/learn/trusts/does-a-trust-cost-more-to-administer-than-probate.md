People who set up a trust often hear that it will save their family money. Then they hear the opposite from a neighbor: "My mother had a trust and my brother still spent a year and a pile of fees settling it." Both stories are true, and the difference lies in what gets done and who does it.

This page lays the two processes side by side, so you can see where costs come from, which ones a trust removes, and which ones it adds.

## The short version

- Probate and trust administration do the same work. The trust avoids court supervision, not the work itself.
- A trust moves cost forward, to the time you create it. Probate pushes cost to the end, onto your family.
- Costs rise with disputes, complicated assets and sloppy records, whichever route you take.
- If the trust holds nothing, you pay twice: once to create it and again for probate.
- No single national number answers this. Fees depend on your state, the size of the estate and who is hired.

## What does each process involve?

After a death, someone has to do the same chores. They locate documents, secure property, collect mail, notify banks, list assets, pay valid debts, file the final income tax return and any estate or trust returns, and then distribute what remains.

In probate, a court oversees those steps. The executor files a petition, receives court authority (letters), gives notice to heirs and creditors, files an inventory in many states, may report on the accounting, and asks the court to close. The [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works) guide describes this path.

In a trust, the successor trustee does the chores without a judge. The trustee follows the document, informs beneficiaries, obtains a tax ID for the trust, and distributes. The [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) page walks through each step.

## How do the costs compare line by line?

| Cost item | Probate | Trust administration |
|---|---|---|
| Court filing fees | Yes, set by the court | Usually none unless a petition is filed |
| Attorney | Often hired, sometimes a statutory or percentage fee in some states | Often hired for guidance, usually hourly or flat |
| Executor or trustee compensation | Allowed, set by statute or "reasonable" | Allowed, set by the document or "reasonable" |
| Publication or notice to creditors | Often required | Sometimes, depending on the state |
| Bond | Sometimes required of the executor | Rarely, if the document waives it |
| Appraisals | Needed for real estate and valuable items | Same |
| Tax returns | Final personal return, maybe an estate return | Final personal return, a trust return, perhaps more |
| Time to finish | Months to over a year | Often shorter, often several months |
| Cost to set up | None before death | Cost of drafting and funding the trust |

Notice how many rows match. The savings from a trust come mostly from the first four lines and from time. They do not come from appraisals or tax returns, which both routes need.

## Where does a trust save money?

**Real estate.** A house is the asset that most often turns a small probate into an expensive one. A house in a funded trust can be sold or transferred by the trustee without court approval. The [selling a house held in a trust after death](/learn/after-a-death/selling-a-house-held-in-a-trust-after-death) page shows how that works.

**Property in more than one state.** Land in a second state can trigger a separate probate there, with its own filings and local counsel. A funded trust can avoid that. See [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

**States with costly probate.** Some states charge fees by a percentage of the estate, and some have slow courts. Two readers in different states may see very different savings. For local figures, look at [Illinois probate costs](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-costs) and [probate costs in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/probate-costs-in-new-jersey).

**Time.** Faster distribution means shorter periods of upkeep on a vacant house, fewer months of insurance, and less of the legal clock running.

## Where does a trust add or hold on to cost?

- **Setup.** Drafting and funding take professional time. The [how much does a living trust cost](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost) page covers typical ranges.
- **The trust tax return.** After death, the trust is its own taxpayer, which means an EIN, a return, and decisions about tax elections. The [trust EIN and 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election) page explains the choices.
- **Trustee fees.** A bank or trust company charges for its work. A family member can be paid or can waive the fee. The [trustee compensation](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation) page explains what is reasonable.
- **Funding gaps.** Every asset left out of the trust is a probate case waiting to happen, as the [how to tell if your trust is funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded) page shows.
- **Long-term trusts.** If the trust continues for children or grandchildren, the cost continues too, year after year.

## Two examples

**Case one.** Amy, 58, owns a house worth well above the typical small-estate limit, a few bank accounts and an IRA. Her husband is gone and her three children get along. She sets up a trust, funds it, and names her son as successor trustee. When she dies, her son settles the trust with a short attorney consultation and an accountant for the returns. There is no probate for the house or accounts. The expenses are real but modest.

**Case two.** Tom, 66, rents and has a checking account, a brokerage account with a transfer-on-death designation and a life insurance policy with named beneficiaries. He has no real estate. Almost everything passes by form. A trust would add cost without saving much, since there is little to probate. A will, and up-to-date beneficiary forms, do the job. Compare these with the [probate vs non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) page.

The lesson is that the same trust can be wise for one person and wasteful for another. The [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) page can help you decide.

## What questions should you ask about fees?

Before you hire someone to set up a trust, or before a trustee hires help, ask:

1. Is the fee flat or hourly, and what does it include?
2. Does it cover funding the trust, including the deed?
3. What happens if the trustee later needs advice? Is there a fee for that?
4. What will a corporate trustee charge, and how is it calculated?
5. Who prepares the trust tax return, and at what cost?
6. How do you handle a dispute, and is it billed separately?

Written answers are better than verbal ones.

## How can you keep administration costs down?

- **Fund the trust fully.** This is the largest single lever.
- **Keep clear records.** A list of accounts, a deed folder and contact numbers for advisors save hours of searching.
- **Choose a capable trustee, and a backup.** A well-organized trustee needs less professional help. The [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) page explains what to look for.
- **Write plain instructions.** Vague trust terms cause questions, and questions cost money.
- **Talk to the family.** Surprises lead to disputes, and disputes are the most expensive item on any list.
- **Estimate probate for your own state.** Use the [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) to see a rough figure for comparison.
