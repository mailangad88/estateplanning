Families usually ask this question in one of two moments. Either you are making a trust and worry that a brother or stepchild will fight it, or you are a beneficiary reading a trust that does not look like what you were told, and you wonder if it can be undone.

The honest answer is the same in both cases. A trust can be challenged, and sometimes the challenge wins, but the grounds are narrow and the person making the claim carries the burden of proof. This article covers who can sue, what they must show, how the clock works and how to protect a plan from the start. For the wider picture of how trusts work, see our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## The short version

- Only interested persons can bring a contest, and only on defined grounds.
- Courts presume a signed trust is valid. The challenger must prove otherwise.
- Time limits are short, and often begin when the trustee sends a formal notice.
- A trust that was made carefully, with independent advice and good records, is hard to overturn.
- If you are a beneficiary who thinks something is wrong, ask a lawyer about deadlines this week, not next quarter.

## What are the legal grounds to challenge a trust?

Courts do not rewrite a trust because a relative thinks it is unfair. A challenge must fit one of a few categories.

| Ground | What the challenger must show | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Lack of capacity | The person did not understand their property, their family or what the trust did when they signed | Medical records, diagnoses, witness accounts, the signing lawyer's notes |
| Undue influence | Someone replaced the person's wishes with their own through pressure or control | A new helper with access, isolation from family, a sudden change in the plan |
| Fraud | The person was tricked about what they signed, or about a key fact | Misstatements, forged explanations, documents swapped at signing |
| Forgery or forged amendment | The signature is not the signer's | Handwriting analysis, notary logs, who had the document |
| Improper execution | A signing rule required by state law was not followed | Missing signature, no notary where required, witness issues |
| Revoked or replaced | A later document or act superseded this one | The later trust, amendment or deed |

The first two do most of the work in practice. Capacity is a lower bar than people think, because the law asks whether the person understood what they were doing at that time, not whether they were healthy in general. Someone with early dementia can still sign a valid document on a clear day. Undue influence is more about behavior: who arranged the appointment, who was in the room, and who benefited. Our glossary entries on [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) and [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity) lay out the legal terms.

## Who can contest a trust?

Someone has to be harmed by the trust to ask a court to undo it. That usually means:

- Children or other heirs who would inherit if there were no trust.
- Beneficiaries of an earlier will or trust that the new document replaced.
- A spouse who claims a statutory share, in states that give one.
- Occasionally a creditor with a legal claim against the person who made the trust.

Consider Rosa, 79, who changes her trust four months before she dies. Her two sons, who were each to receive half, now find that her longtime caregiver gets the house. Both sons have standing. The caregiver will be asked how the change came about, who drove Rosa to the lawyer and who knew about it. Because she benefits, a court will look hard at her role.

## How long do you have to contest a trust?

Not long. Many states set a short deadline that runs from the date the trustee sends a formal notice to the heirs and beneficiaries, and the period is measured in months in a number of states. Missing it can end the right to sue even if the claim was strong. Other states give a longer window, and some tie it to different events.

The practical point is that the notice matters. When a trustee mails the letter announcing that the trust exists, read it all the way through and write down the date. The trustee's duty to give that notice is covered in our guide to [trustee duties to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account). If you are not sure whether a deadline has begun to run, an attorney in your state can tell you quickly.

## Is a trust harder to challenge than a will?

Often, in practice, though not because the law is softer. A will goes into [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate), where heirs are notified by the court and the document is filed publicly. A trust is private, and the trustee, not a court, handles notices. A person who does not know a trust exists cannot challenge it. That is a fact about visibility, not legal protection.

Once a challenge is filed, the same questions get asked: capacity, influence, fraud. And the same kinds of evidence decide it. Our article on [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) walks through the court side of those fights, and the [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) guide explains how a trust differs structurally.

## How do you make a trust hard to contest?

This is where you have real power, and it takes less effort than most people expect.

1. **Meet with a lawyer alone.** Do not bring the child who benefits most to the signing meeting. A lawyer who sees you alone and records that you understood the plan is your best witness.
2. **Keep the paperwork clean.** Follow your state's signing rules exactly. Use a notary even where it is not strictly required.
3. **Document capacity when there is any doubt.** If you are older or ill, ask your physician to note your condition around the signing date. Do this before the signing, not after.
4. **Explain unequal plans.** A short letter saying why one child receives more can head off a claim of confusion. Our guide on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) covers how.
5. **Avoid surprise.** Many contests grow from shock. A fifteen-minute conversation can prevent a lawsuit.
6. **Consider a no-contest clause.** It may help in some states and has limits. Read [no-contest clauses](/learn/wills/no-contest-clause-in-a-will) before deciding.
7. **Update properly.** Change the trust by a formal amendment, not by handwritten notes.

## What should you do if you want to challenge a trust?

If you are a beneficiary or heir and something seems off, the early steps matter.

1. Ask the trustee in writing for a copy of the trust and any amendments, and for the date of the notice.
2. Mark every deadline you can find, then confirm them with an attorney.
3. Collect what you already have: medical information, emails, dates of changes and who was involved.
4. Do not confront the trustee or other beneficiaries with accusations. Statements made in anger are often used later.
5. Talk with a lawyer about cost against likely recovery, including whether mediation would help.

## How state law changes this

States differ on the deadline to sue, who counts as interested, how a no-contest clause is applied and whether a trustee can use trust money to defend the document. Many have adopted a version of the Uniform Trust Code, but local changes are common.
