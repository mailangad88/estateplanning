Two questions come up in almost every trust meeting. First: does my son have a right to read this? Second: should I tell my children what I have done before I die? The first has a legal answer that depends on one fact, whether the trust can still be changed. The second is a family judgment, and a reasonable parent can go either way.

Below you will find who is entitled to see what and when, what stays private, and a practical way to decide what to tell your children. For the bigger picture, see our section on [trusts](/learn/trusts).

## Who can see a revocable trust while the grantor is alive?

Only the grantor, the trustee and anyone the grantor chooses. A beneficiary named in a revocable trust holds what lawyers call an expectancy. It is a hope, not a right, because the grantor can sign an amendment tomorrow that removes them. Courts and statutes in most states say the trustee owes duties to the grantor during that time, not to the people who will inherit.

Take Helen, 72, who made a trust naming her three children as equal beneficiaries. Her son Marcus asks for a copy. Helen does not have to give him one, and neither does the trustee, who is also Helen. If Helen loses capacity and her daughter becomes successor trustee, the daughter owes her duties to Helen, still not to Marcus. That changes when Helen dies.

A few states apply special rules where the grantor is incapacitated or where the trustee is someone other than the grantor, so check your state if this matters in a live dispute.

## When do beneficiaries get to see the trust?

Once the grantor dies, or the trust otherwise becomes irrevocable, the trustee takes on duties toward the beneficiaries. In most states that includes:

1. Telling beneficiaries the trust exists, often within a set number of days after the trustee accepts the job.
2. Giving each of them a copy of the trust terms or a summary of the parts that affect them.
3. Providing periodic reports on what is held, what came in and what was paid out.
4. Responding within a reasonable time to a request for information.

Our detailed pages on [the trustee's duty to inform and account](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-duty-to-inform-and-account) and [what happens to a living trust when you die](/learn/trusts/what-happens-to-a-living-trust-when-you-die) walk through the timeline.

Some trusts try to limit these rights, for example by saying "no accounting is required." Courts in many states will not enforce a clause that strips every beneficiary of the ability to learn anything. A beneficiary who suspects a problem can petition the court.

## Is a trust really private?

A trust is private from the general public. It is not filed with any court at creation, and nothing about it appears in an index. A will, by contrast, becomes a public court record when it goes through probate.

Privacy has edges, though.

| Situation | Does it become public? |
|---|---|
| Signing and amending the trust | No |
| Recording a deed into the trust | The deed is public, the terms are not |
| Trust administration after death | No court filing in most cases |
| A beneficiary sues the trustee | Terms may be filed in the case |
| Someone contests the trust | Terms are usually part of the court file |
| Trust tax returns | Filed with the IRS, not public |

If privacy matters to your family, make sure the rest of the plan matches. A [pour-over will](/glossary/pour-over-will) that goes to probate puts the will on file, though the trust itself stays out of sight. Our article [on whether trust administration is public](/learn/after-a-death/is-trust-administration-public) covers the edge cases.

## What can an irrevocable trust beneficiary see?

More, and sooner. An irrevocable trust cannot be rewritten by the grantor at will, so the people who benefit have a stake from day one. Most states let them request the terms and reports while the grantor is alive. If a family member created an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) for you, ask the trustee in writing. If you are refused, an attorney can explain what your state allows.

## Should you tell your children about your trust?

There is no legal duty, and many parents wait. A better question is what happens if you say nothing.

Silence tends to work when the plan is simple and fair: everything equally to your kids, a trusted sibling as trustee. It tends to backfire when something looks unequal. A child who learns about a smaller share, a different trustee, or a house left to one sibling after the funeral often assumes manipulation. Our guide to [preventing inheritance fights in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/preventing-inheritance-fights-in-a-blended-family) shows how a short conversation heads that off.

A middle path works for many families:

- Tell the successor trustee the trust exists, where the original sits, and what the job involves.
- Tell all children that a plan exists and who is in charge, without sharing amounts.
- Explain any unequal choice yourself, in a letter or conversation, while you can still answer questions.
- Keep a list of accounts and professionals somewhere the trustee can find it.

Reasons to hold back are real too. A child with an addiction, a spendthrift habit or a volatile marriage may be better off not knowing exact amounts for years. A parent with a second spouse may want to avoid conflict while both are alive. Those are fair concerns. They are a reason to talk about the plan with your attorney first, not a reason to avoid planning.

## Can a beneficiary challenge a trust?

Yes, but only on legal grounds, such as incapacity, undue influence, fraud, or improper signing. Disappointment is not a legal ground. Contests are usually harder to win against a trust than a will because trusts are typically signed years earlier with an attorney present. For the details, see [can a living trust be contested](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested).

## What if you are a beneficiary and the trustee will not share anything?

Start quietly and in writing. Most silence comes from a trustee who is overwhelmed, not one who is hiding something. A short letter often does the job.

1. Confirm that the grantor has died or the trust is irrevocable. If the grantor is alive and the trust is revocable, you probably have no claim yet.
2. Write to the trustee asking for a copy of the trust terms, a list of assets, and the date they accepted the role. Keep a copy and send it by a method that gives you a receipt.
3. Wait a reasonable time. Thirty days is common, though your state may set its own number.
4. If nothing arrives, ask an attorney to send a formal demand.
5. If the demand is ignored, a court petition can compel an accounting, and in serious cases remove the trustee. Our page on [how to remove a trustee](/learn/after-a-death/how-to-remove-a-trustee) explains that route.

Some beneficiaries are tempted to call banks directly. That rarely works, since financial institutions will speak only to the trustee. Patience and a paper trail do more for you than anger.

## How state law changes this

States differ on how soon a trustee must notify beneficiaries, whether a beneficiary may waive notice, whether the full document or only a summary must be shared, and how much a trust can limit disclosure. A handful follow the Uniform Trust Code closely, and others have their own approach. Our [state estate planning guides](/estate-planning/texas) are a place to begin, and an attorney in your state can tell you the current deadlines.
