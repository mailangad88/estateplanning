Your mother's memory is slipping, or your father has had his first fall, and you have started to wonder whether a trust would keep her house safe from a nursing home. The question is a good one, and the answers are less encouraging than the sales pitches suggest.

This page explains who can create a trust for a parent, what each kind does for Medicaid, how benefits like SSI and SSDI react, and the steps to take in order. It builds on our guides to [the Medicaid asset protection trust](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust) and [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan). For trust basics, see our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## The short version

- Your parent signs. You cannot form a trust over their property by yourself.
- A revocable trust is good for management and probate, not for Medicaid.
- An irrevocable trust can protect assets, but it takes planning five years ahead in most states, and your parent gives up control.
- Never retitle a parent's assets to yourself as a shortcut.
- If time is short, talk to an attorney about options other than a trust.

## Who can legally set up a trust for a parent?

The person whose property is being placed in the trust, called the grantor, has to create it. That requires the capacity to understand the document: what they own, who their family is, and what the trust will do. The test is lower than people assume, and a parent with early memory loss can often still sign on a good day, with an attorney who documents the conversation. Our article on [creating a trust with dementia or a power of attorney](/learn/trusts/creating-a-trust-with-dementia-or-a-power-of-attorney) goes through the line in detail.

You, the adult child, may help by finding the attorney, going to meetings, gathering account statements and driving. Where the attorney is cautious is when the child does all the talking. The firm will often meet the parent alone for part of the appointment, to confirm the wishes are theirs. Do not take offense. That protects the document from a later challenge by a sibling.

If your parent has a durable power of attorney naming you, the document may or may not allow you to create or fund a trust. Many standard forms do not. The same dementia article covers how an agent's authority is read.

## What can a trust do for a parent who is aging?

Different trusts do different jobs. Here is how the common ones compare on the points families care about.

| Trust type | Helps with | Protects from Medicaid? | Parent keeps control? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revocable living trust | Managing money if capacity is lost, avoiding probate | No, assets are still countable | Yes |
| Standby trust | Same, but funded only when needed | No | Yes, until funded |
| Irrevocable Medicaid trust | Shielding a home or savings after the look-back | Yes, if set up early and drafted correctly | No, income often only |
| Income-only or Miller trust | Qualifying when income exceeds a state cap | Not a protection tool | Limited |
| Special needs trust | A disabled child or grandchild's benefits | Separate rules | Not applicable |

The revocable trust is the one most families begin with, and for good reason. If your father has a stroke, a successor trustee can pay his bills the next day without a conservatorship. It also lets the house pass to the children without probate. It simply does nothing to keep the house from being counted when he applies for nursing home Medicaid. The page on [protecting a home from nursing home costs](/learn/elder-care/protecting-your-home-from-nursing-home-costs) lists the tools that do.

## Can I put my parent's money in a trust for Medicaid?

Only if the trust is created by your parent, is irrevocable, is drafted for Medicaid, and is funded at least 60 months before they apply in most states. The details:

1. **No transfers to you.** If you take money out of your mother's account and place it in your own, or in a trust for your benefit, Medicaid treats it as a gift. A gift inside the look-back window produces a penalty period when she needs care.
2. **No quick fixes.** A trust signed the week before an application is likely to be counted, because assets given away within the look-back period are penalized.
3. **Control matters.** If your parent can take the principal back, or acts as sole trustee with full power, the trust is probably countable.
4. **An independent trustee** is usual, often a child or a professional, with defined powers. Your parent may keep the right to income and the right to live in the house.

You can estimate when a transfer would clear the window with our [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date), and the rules are explained in [the Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period).

Walter, 74, owns a home worth $320,000 and has $140,000 in savings. His daughter Fran is a nurse and sees what is coming. She urges him to visit an attorney now. He signs an irrevocable trust with Fran as trustee, keeps the right to live in the house and to receive the income, and funds it. If he enters a nursing home after five years, the house is generally not countable. If he enters in year three, a penalty period applies to the value of what was transferred.

## Does a trust affect SSI, SSDI or other benefits?

The answer depends on the benefit.

- **SSDI** and regular Social Security retirement benefits are not means tested. A trust does not change them.
- **SSI** is means tested. Assets in a revocable trust count as your parent's resources. Money in an irrevocable trust they cannot reach is not counted, but there are timing rules for transfers.
- **Medicaid** counts countable assets and reviews gifts, as above.
- **Veterans' pension with Aid and Attendance** has its own net worth and look-back rules. See [VA Aid and Attendance](/learn/elder-care/va-aid-and-attendance).

If your parent receives benefits now, ask before moving anything. A well-meant transfer can interrupt coverage. The details on income and the Miller trust are in [income-cap trusts](/learn/elder-care/income-cap-trust-miller-trust).

## What if my parent already needs care now?

A trust may not be the right tool, and an attorney can show what is. Spouses can transfer assets between themselves without penalty. Some assets, such as one home with a spouse living in it, are exempt. A Medicaid-compliant annuity, a caregiver agreement, or spending down on exempt items can all help. The overview is in [Medicaid crisis planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-crisis-planning), and [the Medicaid spend-down](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-spend-down) page explains the rules.

## What are the steps if you want to start?

1. **Check capacity and willingness.** Have a calm conversation. A parent who feels pressured will not sign, and should not.
2. **Collect the facts.** Account statements, deed, insurance, existing documents, and a list of debts and income.
3. **Make sure a durable power of attorney and healthcare directive are in place.** They matter more than the trust if your parent cannot decide. See the [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
4. **Meet an estate planning attorney** together, with time alone for your parent.
5. **Decide the trust type** based on the goal: management, probate, or Medicaid.
6. **Fund it.** Retitle the house and accounts, as described in [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
7. **Mark the date.** If Medicaid is the goal, note when each transfer happened.

## How state law changes this

Medicaid rules are set by each state within federal limits, so asset limits, home equity caps, and how a state treats a trust vary. The look-back is 60 months in most states, though California's approach differs. A few states have partial protections for a home. Your state's estate planning guide, linked from [our state pages](/estate-planning), is a starting point, and an attorney in your state should confirm the details.
