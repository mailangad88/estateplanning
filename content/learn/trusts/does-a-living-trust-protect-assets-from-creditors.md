People often hear that a trust "protects" their assets and assume that means from lawsuits and creditors. With a revocable living trust, that is not what it does. The confusion causes real harm, because someone who thinks they are protected may skip steps that actually would help.

This page separates the three questions hiding inside the headline: your creditors during your life, your creditors after your death, and your heirs' creditors after they inherit. The answers differ. For background on the document itself, see the [revocable living trust guide](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), and for the whole subject, our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## The short version

- During your life, a revocable trust gives no protection from your own creditors or lawsuits.
- After your death, valid debts and taxes still come first, whether or not you had a trust.
- An inheritance can be shielded for your heirs through a spendthrift trust.
- Moving property to avoid a debt you already know about can be reversed by a court.
- Real asset protection is a planning project with trade-offs, not a feature of a standard trust.

## Why does a revocable trust fail to shield assets?

The reason is control. In a revocable trust you name yourself trustee and keep the right to amend it, cancel it and take assets out. The law's view is simple: if you can reach it, so can your creditors. Courts look at what you can do with property, not whose name is on the title.

Consider Tom, 58, who owns a small landscaping company and puts his house and savings into a living trust. A customer injured on a job wins a judgment larger than his insurance. The judgment creditor can ask a court to reach the assets in the trust, the same as if they were in Tom's own name. The trust was a fine probate tool, but it was never a shield.

That is not a flaw in the trust. It is the price of flexibility. Our guide on [whether a trust avoids taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes) describes the same trade: what you can take back is treated as yours.

## What happens to creditors after you die?

Debts do not disappear at death. They are paid out of what you left, in an order set by state law. Probate is one route for that, which is why people sometimes believe avoiding probate also avoids debts. It does not.

When a revocable trust becomes irrevocable at your death, many states allow creditors to reach it if the probate estate is not enough to pay the valid claims. The successor trustee must take this seriously. Distributing everything to the family and then hearing from a creditor can leave the trustee answerable for what is owed. Our [trust administration guide](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) lists the steps, and [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains the order creditors are paid in.

Some states offer a way to shorten the period during which late claims may appear. Others rely on general limits. It varies enough that a trustee should ask locally before closing the books.

A few debts get special handling. Medicaid can recover long-term care costs from some assets after death, which is covered in [Medicaid estate recovery](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-estate-recovery). Mortgages stay attached to the property. Federal taxes come ahead of many other claims.

## What tools do protect assets?

Trusts are only one choice, and often not the best.

| Tool | What it can protect | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Liability insurance, umbrella policy | Against accident and injury claims | Policy limits and exclusions |
| LLC for rental or business property | Personal assets from business liabilities | Must be run as a separate entity; not total protection |
| State homestead and retirement exemptions | Part of a home or retirement savings | Caps and rules vary widely |
| Tenancy by the entirety (some states) | A couple's home from one spouse's debts | Not available everywhere; ends with divorce or death |
| Irrevocable trust funded long before a claim | Assets you gave up control of | You lose control; look-back and fraud rules apply |
| Spendthrift trust for an heir | An inheritance from the heir's creditors | Protects the heir, not you |

## Can I protect my assets by moving them into an irrevocable trust?

Sometimes, if you do it early and mean it. An [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) works because you give up ownership and control, so there is nothing for your creditors to reach. The conditions are strict.

1. The transfer has to be real. If you keep the right to take the assets back, the protection fails.
2. Timing matters. A transfer made after a lawsuit is filed, or when you already know a claim is coming, can be undone as a fraudulent transfer.
3. Solvency matters. You cannot make yourself unable to pay your debts.
4. State law shapes the result. Some states allow trusts that you create for yourself and also benefit from, and others treat them as reachable by your creditors.
5. There are costs: you lose control, may trigger gift tax filings and may change the tax basis your heirs receive.

The Medicaid version of this idea has its own five-year clock; see [Medicaid asset protection trusts](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-asset-protection-trust). It is for long-term care costs, not for general liability.

## What about creditors of the people who inherit from you?

This is where a trust genuinely shines. Money left outright to a child is theirs the moment they receive it, which means a divorcing spouse, a lawsuit or a bad business partner may reach it. Money left in a discretionary trust with a [spendthrift clause](/glossary/spendthrift-clause) is generally out of reach until the trustee decides to distribute it. Our guide to [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) explains the design, and [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) covers other ways to structure the gift.

Priya, 66, leaves her son Anand a lifetime trust with an independent trustee instead of a lump sum. Anand is a physician with malpractice exposure and a shaky marriage. The inheritance is still in the trust if he is sued, because he never held it. That is real protection, and it can be built at no extra complexity for a trust you are already making.

## How state law changes this

Creditor rules are among the most state-specific parts of trust law. States differ on homestead exemptions, on whether and how a self-settled trust is respected, on how long creditors have to file claims against an estate and on whether a spendthrift clause has exceptions for child support or taxes. Read your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois) for local detail, and do not assume a rule from a neighboring state applies.
