If you create a trust, the person you name as trustee decides how it works in real life. They interpret your words, manage the money, deal with the people you love and often the taxes. The trust document can be flawless, but a poor trustee choice can undo much of it.

This guide explains what the job involves, the strengths and weaknesses of each type of trustee, and a practical process for deciding. For how trustees fit into the larger picture, see our [trust overview](/learn/trusts).

## What does a trustee actually do?

A [trustee](/glossary/trustee) holds legal title to trust property and manages it for the beneficiaries. Specific responsibilities include:

- Locating, securing and valuing trust assets
- Investing prudently, which usually means diversified and in line with the trust terms
- Keeping accurate records and accounts
- Paying bills, expenses and taxes, and filing trust tax returns
- Making distributions as the trust directs, and using discretion when it allows it
- Keeping beneficiaries informed, as state law requires
- Treating beneficiaries fairly and avoiding conflicts of interest

These duties are legally enforceable. A trustee is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary) and can be personally liable for losses caused by mistakes or self-dealing. See [fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty) for more.

During your life, you are usually your own trustee of a [revocable trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust). The real choice is who becomes the [successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) when you die or cannot act, and who serves for trusts that last for many years, such as [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).

## Should the trustee be a family member, friend or professional?

| Option | Strengths | Weaknesses | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spouse or adult child | Knows you and the family, low cost, shares your values | May lack skills, can feel conflicted, may face family pressure | Often none, or reasonable fees |
| Friend or trusted advisor | May be neutral and capable | May decline, may age out, can strain friendships | Often modest |
| Bank or trust company | Continuity, expertise, impartial, regulated | Fees, less personal, may require minimum balances | Percentage of assets, often with minimums |
| Individual professional (attorney, CPA) | Skills, flexibility | Succession problem when they retire | Hourly or percentage |
| Co-trustees | Combine strengths, checks and balances | Slower decisions, possible disagreement | Varies |

### When a family member works well

A family trustee is a good fit when the trust is simple, the family gets along and the person is organized. Elena, 41, a school administrator, is a sensible choice to administer her mother's trust that divides a house and two accounts among three siblings, provided she is willing to hire a CPA and attorney as needed.

### When a professional is better

Consider a professional when the trust is large, lasts for decades, holds a business or real estate, involves family tension, or has a beneficiary who may press for distributions. A neutral trustee can say no without wrecking Thanksgiving. Professional trustees also provide continuity if the family trustee dies or becomes incapacitated.

### When to combine them

A common solution is co-trustees: a family member who knows the beneficiaries and a corporate trustee who handles investments and tax filings. The trust can say who has final say on what, for example the family member decides on distributions and the corporate trustee handles investment management.

## What qualities should you look for?

1. **Integrity.** The trustee will have access to money and the power to make decisions that affect others.
2. **Organization.** Records, deadlines and tax filings are central to the job.
3. **Financial sense.** They need not be an investment expert, but should be comfortable seeking advice.
4. **Neutrality and backbone.** The ability to disappoint a beneficiary without being cruel.
5. **Availability and location.** Being close to the property helps, and so does being willing to commit time.
6. **Age and health.** Pick someone likely to outlast the trust, and name backups.
7. **Willingness.** Ask them. Many people say yes out of loyalty and later regret it.

Be honest about red flags: financial trouble, a history of conflict with the beneficiaries, a spouse who would be influencing decisions, or someone who would also be a beneficiary with a conflicting interest.

## How should you handle a trustee who is also a beneficiary?

It is common for a surviving spouse or a child to be both trustee and beneficiary. The law allows it, but there is a built-in conflict. Reduce risk by limiting discretionary distributions to the trustee to an ascertainable standard like health, education, maintenance and support (a phrase with tax significance), and by requiring an independent co-trustee for decisions that affect the trustee personally. This also matters for tax reasons, so ask an attorney about the right wording.

## How do you name successor trustees and co-trustees?

Name at least two successors in order. Think about whether the successors must act together or can act alone, and whether a majority of co-trustees can decide. Deadlock between two siblings is a real risk, so many trusts name a tiebreaker, require a professional co-trustee or give the grantor's chosen advisor power to resolve disputes.

Also name how a trustee can be replaced, either by a trust protector (an independent person with power to remove and replace trustees) or by a vote of the beneficiaries. Otherwise the only route may be a court petition; see [whether an executor or trustee can be removed](/learn/after-a-death/can-an-executor-be-removed).

## What about trustee pay?

State law usually allows a trustee reasonable compensation unless the trust says otherwise. Professional trustees follow a fee schedule, often a percentage of assets under management that declines as the balance rises, with minimums. Family trustees commonly waive fees, but you can state a rate in the trust. Compensation is taxable income to the trustee. Also expect costs for tax return preparation and, in some cases, legal advice.

## How is a trustee different from an executor or guardian?

An [executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) is appointed through a will and handles the probate estate. A trustee operates under a trust, usually privately. A guardian raises children and is chosen in a will. Some parents choose the same person for guardian and trustee; others deliberately split the roles so one person raises the kids and another manages the money. See [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) for how to think about that decision.

## How can you set the trustee up for success?

- **Write a letter of wishes** explaining how you want discretion used, especially for beneficiaries with special circumstances.
- **Authorize the trustee to hire advisors** such as a CPA, investment manager and attorney, and to pay them from the trust.
- **Give clear distribution standards** rather than vague phrases.
- **Waive or limit bond requirements**, which can be costly.
- **Share the plan** with the trustee while you are alive: where documents are, which accounts hold what, and who your advisors are.
- **Review the choice** every few years, as people's lives change.

State rules on trustee duties, notice to beneficiaries and compensation vary. The [state guides](/estate-planning/new-york) (New York, for example) summarize local rules, and our attorneys can explain what applies to your trust.
