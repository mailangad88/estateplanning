Search for "do I need a trust" and you will find answers that range from "everyone does" to "never bother." Both come from people with something to sell or a point to make. The honest answer is that a trust solves particular problems, and you either have those problems or you do not.

This page runs through the situations our clients bring most often, sorted by stage of life and family shape, and ends with how five large states change the picture. For the general decision, see [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) and [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust). The pillar for everything on trusts is our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## The short version

- Start with the problem, not the product. List what you want to happen if you die or lose capacity, then see whether a will already does it.
- The strongest reasons for a trust are minor or vulnerable heirs, a blended family, property in more than one state, and a wish to avoid a conservatorship.
- The weakest reason is "my estate is big." Under the 2026 federal exemption of $15,000,000, estate tax is not the issue for most families.
- A trust that is not funded does little. Budget the time to retitle your assets.

## A quick map of who tends to need what

| Your situation | Will, forms and powers of attorney enough? | A trust usually worth a look when |
|---|---|---|
| Single, 30s, no kids, no real estate | Yes | You own a home in a slow-probate state |
| Married with minor children | Often not alone | Children could inherit before they can manage money |
| Married with grown children, one house | Often yes | The house is in a high-probate-cost state or you want incapacity planning |
| Second marriage with children from before | Rarely | Almost always, to protect both families |
| Child with a disability | No | Almost always, a special needs trust |
| Retired with a house and investments | Sometimes | You want a plan for incapacity and a smooth handoff |
| Estate near or above the exemption | No | Tax planning trusts may be needed |

## Do I need a trust in my 30s?

Probably not yet, with two exceptions. If you have young children, the question is not a trust for you but what happens when a child inherits. A will can name a guardian, but a court-appointed person generally manages a minor's inheritance until age 18 and then hands it over. Many parents prefer a trust that holds the money into the child's twenties. Our page on [what happens when a minor inherits without a trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust) shows the problem.

The second exception is a home. If you bought a house at 34 in a state where probate is slow, a living trust can pass it on without court. For most 30-somethings with a bank account, a 401(k) and a car, beneficiary designations and a will do the job. See [estate planning documents at 30, 40 and 50](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-documents-at-30-40-and-50).

## Do I need a trust if I am married with kids?

If the children are minors, yes, a trust is worth considering more often than not. Two parents who die together, or a surviving parent who remarries, can leave children with a large sum controlled by a court or a spouse. A trust lets you decide the age or ages at which they receive money, and who manages it in between. It also keeps the trustee and the guardian separate if you want that, as explained in [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee).

Take Priya and Jonah, both 38, with two children under 8, a house and about $600,000 in retirement accounts and life insurance. A will names a guardian, but without a trust the life insurance proceeds would go to the children outright at 18. A trust for the children, with Priya's sister as trustee, holds the money until 25 and pays for school and health in the meantime. The rest of their plan is basic.

## Do I need a trust if I am single with no kids?

Not always. The central question is who steps in if you cannot manage your own money. A durable power of attorney covers much of that. A trust adds a successor trustee who can act with no court involved and no bank argument over a form, which some single people value precisely because there is no spouse to turn to. If you plan to leave money to nieces and nephews or a charity, a trust can pay it over time rather than at once. See [estate planning for single people](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people).

## Do I need a trust for a second marriage?

Usually yes. Without one, everything you leave to your spouse becomes theirs to leave to whomever they choose, including a new spouse of their own. A trust can give your spouse income or the use of the house for life and then pass the rest to your children. The options are covered in [joint vs. separate trusts for remarriage](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage).

## Do I need a trust in my 50s or in retirement?

This is where trusts earn their keep most often. The house is paid down, accounts have grown, adult children may be married, and the chance of a health event has become real. A funded trust gives a successor trustee authority over the assets the moment you cannot manage them, which can prevent a court-supervised conservatorship. [Estate planning for retirees](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-retirees) has a longer discussion, including the role of Medicaid planning, which usually requires an irrevocable trust and an early start.

## Do I need a trust if my estate is under the estate tax exemption?

You might, but not for tax reasons. At $15,000,000 per person in 2026, the federal exemption leaves nearly every family with no federal tax to plan around. The reasons that remain are the practical ones: probate avoidance, privacy, incapacity planning and control over how heirs receive money. If your estate is large enough that tax is a live issue, see [the federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

## How does your state change the answer?

Probate cost and speed, state estate taxes and homestead rules differ enough that the same person might choose differently in two places.

- **California:** Probate fees for attorneys and personal representatives are set by statute and based on the gross value of the estate, not the equity, so a home with a large mortgage still triggers a large fee. That is why trusts are popular there. Read the [California guide](/estate-planning/california).
- **Texas:** No state estate or inheritance tax, and independent administration keeps many estates simple. A trust is a choice, not a default. Read the [Texas guide](/estate-planning/texas).
- **Florida:** Probate is a court process with its own attorney rules, and homestead restrictions can limit who may inherit the house. Many residents use a trust, but the drafting must follow Florida's homestead law. See the [Florida guide](/estate-planning/florida).
- **New York:** Probate runs through Surrogate's Court, and the state has its own estate tax with a threshold well below the federal figure, so a trust may be part of tax planning for larger estates. See the [New York guide](/estate-planning/new-york).
- **Pennsylvania:** The state taxes inheritances by relationship, including most transfers to children and siblings, and a living trust does not avoid that tax. A trust is more about privacy and incapacity here. See the [Pennsylvania guide](/estate-planning/pennsylvania).

Our [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) gives a rough sense of what probate may cost in your situation.

## What should you do next?

1. Write down the three outcomes you most want: who gets what, who manages it, and who decides if you cannot.
2. Test each against a will plus beneficiary forms. Anything left unsolved points to a trust.
3. Price both routes. Compare the cost with [what a living trust costs](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost).
4. If you go with a trust, schedule the retitling before you leave the attorney's office.
