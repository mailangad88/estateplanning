Some families do not want a full living trust but still do not want a ten-year-old to receive a check for $300,000 at eighteen. A will can solve part of that problem. It can create a trust that begins at death and holds the money until the children are older. That is a testamentary trust, and it is more common than most people realize, especially in simple wills drafted for young parents.

This page explains how one works from signing to final payout, where it falls short, and how to decide between it and a living trust. Our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) has the wider picture.

## What is a testamentary trust?

A [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust) is a trust set up by instructions inside your will. The word "testamentary" just means "by will." While you are alive, nothing exists. You can change the language any time you update the will. At your death the executor takes the will to court, the court admits it, the estate is administered, and then the assets that the will directs into the trust are delivered to the trustee you chose.

Nothing about the trust's rules is different from other trusts. It can have a spendthrift clause, pay for health and education, hold money until a child reaches thirty, or provide a lifetime income for a surviving spouse. The difference is only where it lives and when it starts.

## How does a testamentary trust work, step by step?

1. **You sign a will with trust language.** The will names the trustee, the beneficiaries, what the trustee can pay for, and when the trust ends.
2. **You die and the executor files the will** with the probate court. See [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) for the full process.
3. **The court appoints the executor,** who pays debts and taxes and inventories property.
4. **Assets are distributed to the trustee** according to the will, not to the beneficiaries.
5. **The trustee manages and pays out** under the terms. In some states the trustee files periodic accountings with the court. In others, supervision is light or optional.
6. **The trust ends** at the date or event the will sets, and the remainder goes to the named beneficiaries.

## When does a testamentary trust make sense?

The best fit is a family whose assets are modest, whose main goal is protecting children, and who does not own much that needs to avoid probate or be managed during incapacity.

Take Nathan and Carla, both 34, with a two-year-old and a baby on the way. They rent, have term life insurance and a retirement account, and little else. A will naming each other as primary beneficiaries, with a testamentary trust for the children if both die, gives them real protection at low cost. A living trust would add cost and funding work for an estate that is still small.

It also works for a specific bequest, such as a trust inside an otherwise outright will for a nephew who is not ready for money. It is easy to attach a trust to a single gift.

## When is a living trust the better choice?

| Situation | Testamentary trust | Living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Starts working | After death and probate | When signed and funded |
| Helps if you become incapacitated | No | Yes, successor trustee steps in |
| Avoids probate on assets it holds | No | Yes |
| Privacy | Will becomes public in probate | Trust stays private |
| Court oversight afterward | Common in many states | Usually none |
| Cost to set up | Included in a will | Higher, plus funding time |
| Out-of-state real estate | Likely requires ancillary probate | Can avoid it if deeded in |

If you own a house, real estate in more than one state, or want a plan that works during a long illness, the living trust column wins. See [will vs. trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) for a fuller comparison. A pour-over will often accompanies a living trust, so the two are rarely rivals; read about the [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will).

## What are the downsides?

Cost shows up later, not now. Because a testamentary trust is born in probate, your family pays probate costs before the trust ever holds a dollar. Probate delays distributions, which can be a problem when a surviving parent needs money for a mortgage. Our [probate cost page](/learn/probate/probate-costs) explains typical fees.

Court supervision is the other piece. In some states a court reviews the trustee's accounts every year or two. That protects beneficiaries, but it adds filing fees and paperwork for a trustee who is often a grieving sibling.

The incapacity gap matters most for older clients. If a stroke leaves you unable to manage money, a testamentary trust does nothing, because it does not exist. Only a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) or a living trust covers that.

## How are testamentary trusts taxed?

Once funded, the trust is a separate taxpayer. It needs its own tax ID number and files a trust income tax return each year. Trust income tax brackets reach the top rate at a much lower income than an individual's, so trustees often distribute income to beneficiaries, who pay at their own rates. How that interacts with other tax rules deserves an accountant's attention. Our page on [whether a trust avoids taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes) covers the big picture, including that the federal estate tax exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, which most families will never reach.

## What should the will say?

- Who the trustee is, and a backup. Read [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) before you name a sibling.
- What the trustee may pay for, such as health, education, housing and support.
- When each child receives money. Staged payments at 25, 30 and 35 are common, though some families choose a lifetime trust with discretion.
- What happens if a child dies before the trust ends.
- Whether the trustee can sell a house, hire help, or pay a guardian for the child's care.

A trust inside the will does not replace the guardian choice. If your children are minors, you still need to name someone to raise them; see [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) and [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Can you have more than one testamentary trust in the same will?

Yes, and it is common. A will might create one trust for a surviving spouse, with the remainder going into separate trusts for each child. Each trust is its own bucket with its own trustee and rules. Keep the structure readable. A will that creates five trusts with five different payout ages invites mistakes, and the executor has to explain it all to a probate judge. If your plan needs that much architecture, a living trust is usually easier to administer.

## Common mistakes

Parents sometimes assume that naming a trust in the will also controls life insurance and retirement accounts. It does not. Those pass by beneficiary form. If the form names a minor child, the money goes through a court-appointed guardian of the estate instead of your trust. Name the trustee on the form, or name the trust, if your will creates one that exists at death; see [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

A second mistake is leaving a will unsigned or with the wrong witnesses, which can wipe out the trust too. Our page on [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) covers the signing rules.

If the trust is mainly for young children, [a testamentary trust for minor children](/learn/guardianship/testamentary-trust-for-minor-children) walks through ages, trustees and how it works alongside a guardian.
