People hear that a trust "saves taxes" or "creates a whole new tax return" and both statements get repeated as if they were always true. Neither is. How a trust is taxed turns on one question: can the person who made it still take the property back?

This page walks through the income tax side, the tax ID question, what happens to the cost basis of assets in a trust, and when the IRS can reach trust property. If you want the broader myth-busting version, read [does a living trust save taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes). For the basics of trusts themselves, start at our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## The short version

- Revocable trust, creator alive: no separate return, no separate rate. Your 1040 reports everything.
- Irrevocable trust: usually a separate taxpayer with an EIN, a Form 1041 and steep rates on income it keeps.
- Income paid out to beneficiaries is generally taxed at their rates, not the trust's.
- Death is the usual moment a revocable trust gets its own tax identity.
- A trust changes who pays and when. It rarely reduces what is owed, unless it is built for a specific tax purpose.

## How is a revocable living trust taxed while you are alive?

It is not taxed separately at all. The IRS calls a trust like this a grantor trust, which just means the person who made it is treated as the owner of everything inside. Interest on the trust's savings account, dividends from its brokerage account and the gain from selling its rental house all land on the creator's personal return, the same as before the trust existed.

In practice, the bank statement may show "Dana Whitfield, Trustee of the Whitfield Living Trust," but the tax form 1099 still carries Dana's Social Security number. No second return is filed. Many people never need an EIN for the trust at all while they are alive and serving as trustee. Our article on [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) covers the day-to-day mechanics.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the sale of a home held in the trust still qualifies for the home sale exclusion if you meet the ordinary ownership and use tests, because the trust is invisible for this purpose. Second, a living trust does not lower your income tax or property tax. If a salesperson says otherwise, that is a red flag.

## How is an irrevocable trust taxed?

An irrevocable trust is usually a separate taxpayer. The trustee gets an EIN, keeps its own books and files Form 1041 each year when the trust has taxable income or gross income of $600 or more. The trust then pays tax on whatever income it keeps, and it passes the rest out to beneficiaries, who report it on their own returns from a Schedule K-1 the trustee sends.

Some irrevocable trusts are deliberately built so the creator still pays the income tax, even though the assets are outside their estate. These are called grantor trusts for tax purposes, and the idea is that the creator paying the tax lets the trust grow without being reduced by it. It is a planning tool for larger estates. Our page on [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) explains what the creator gives up in exchange.

## What are the trust tax rates?

Trusts use the same federal rate schedule as individuals, but the brackets are compressed. A trust hits the top 37 percent bracket at roughly $15,000 to $16,000 of taxable income, and the IRS adjusts that figure each year. A single filer needs several hundred thousand dollars to get there. The 3.8 percent net investment income tax can also begin at that same low level, and long-term capital gains reach the 20 percent rate far sooner than they do for most people.

| Where the income sits | Who pays | Rate pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Revocable trust, creator alive | Creator, on Form 1040 | Normal individual rates |
| Irrevocable trust keeps the income | Trust, on Form 1041 | Top rate starts near $15,000 to $16,000 |
| Irrevocable trust pays income out | Beneficiary, via Schedule K-1 | The beneficiary's own bracket |
| Trust created to be a grantor trust | Creator, even though the assets left | Normal individual rates |

That table explains a common trustee decision. If a trust holds $200,000 and earns $9,000 of interest in a year, the trustee may have little reason to move money. If the same trust earns $60,000 and the beneficiary is a college student in a low bracket, paying the income out can cut the total tax bill noticeably. State income taxes add another layer and vary widely, so ask your attorney or tax preparer about your state.

## Does a trust need an EIN, and when?

A trust needs its own EIN, or employer identification number, whenever it becomes a separate taxpayer. For most families that happens in three situations:

1. **The creator dies.** The revocable trust becomes irrevocable and cannot keep using the creator's Social Security number. The successor trustee applies.
2. **An irrevocable trust is signed.** It needs a number before it can open a bank account.
3. **A revocable trust has a business or special account** that the bank or the IRS wants under a separate number.

The application is free, takes a few minutes online on the IRS website, and returns the number immediately. Do not pay a service for it. Our guide to the [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election) shows the steps and a timing option for shifting income to beneficiaries.

## Do assets in a trust get a step-up in basis?

It depends on what kind of trust and when the asset went in. [Step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) means the tax cost of an asset resets to its value on the date of death, which wipes out the built-in gain for the heirs.

- **Revocable trust:** yes. The assets are still treated as yours when you die, so heirs get the step-up. A house bought for $90,000 and worth $510,000 at death is generally treated as costing $510,000 to the child who sells it.
- **Irrevocable trust funded during your life:** usually no. A gift keeps your original basis, and the trust inherits it. Many families who gave away a low-basis house through an irrevocable trust discover this at sale.
- **Irrevocable trust that is still included in your taxable estate:** it can get a step-up, because estate inclusion is what triggers it. Some trusts are drafted with this in mind.

That last distinction is why a Medicaid or asset protection trust needs careful drafting. The trade-off between control, protection and basis is one of the first things an attorney should walk you through. The full rule is in our article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## Can the IRS take assets from a trust?

Yes, in some situations, and it depends on who owes the tax.

If you personally owe back taxes, the IRS can reach property in your revocable trust. You can take it back at any time, so for collection purposes it is still yours. If you put property into an irrevocable trust and nothing is left for you, it is harder to reach for your own debts, but a transfer made while you owed tax can be challenged as a fraudulent conveyance. And if the trust itself fails to pay its Form 1041 tax, the trust property is what answers for it, and a trustee who distributed everything first can be personally responsible.

For general protection questions, see [does a living trust protect assets from creditors](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-protect-assets-from-creditors).

## What about the estate tax and the gift tax?

A trust is not itself an estate tax solution. The federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families never owe federal estate tax with or without a trust. Assets in a revocable trust count toward your estate. Assets in a properly built irrevocable trust do not, but gifts into it may use some of your exclusion and need a gift tax return. Our [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) page covers the numbers. Special rules apply to trusts for a disabled beneficiary, covered in [how a special needs trust is taxed](/learn/special-needs/how-a-special-needs-trust-is-taxed).

## A quick example

Marcus, 71, has a revocable trust holding his house, a brokerage account and two CDs. For years his CPA files one return. When Marcus dies, his daughter Ellen, as successor trustee, gets an EIN, retitles the accounts, and the trust begins filing a Form 1041. The brokerage account gets a new basis as of Marcus's date of death. Ellen sells some stock, pays the income out to herself and her brother within the first year, and sends them each a K-1. Because the income left the trust, most of it is taxed at their rates, not the trust's.

Nothing about that was complicated, but it was easier because the paperwork was set up in advance.
