Trust names sound like a menu from a restaurant you cannot afford. Credit shelter. Dynasty. Crummey. Blind. A friend recommends one, a financial website describes another, and you leave wondering if you have the right kind.

The honest answer is that most of these names describe a specific job a trust can do. You do not choose a name, you choose a goal, and the attorney writes the trust that meets it. This page translates each label into plain terms so you can tell whether it applies to you. For the basic types, see our [overview of trusts](/learn/trusts).

## The names at a glance

| Name | What it does | Usually relevant to |
|---|---|---|
| Family trust | Everyday label for a living trust held by a family | Almost any household |
| Discretionary trust | Trustee decides who gets what and when | Parents protecting a vulnerable heir |
| Credit shelter (bypass) trust | Uses a spouse's estate tax exemption at the first death | Couples near or above the exemption |
| Generation-skipping trust | Benefits grandchildren, skipping a generation | Larger estates, generous grandparents |
| Dynasty trust | Keeps assets in trust for many generations | Wealthy families, long-term control |
| Crummey trust | Lets gifts to a trust count for the annual exclusion | Gifting life insurance premiums |
| Charitable lead trust | Pays a charity first, then family | Philanthropic families with taxable estates |
| Blind trust | Manager invests without the owner's knowledge | Public officials, some executives |

## What is a family trust?

It is not a separate kind of trust at all. Most of the time, "family trust" means a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) a family uses to hold the house and accounts. People also use it for a trust that continues for a surviving spouse or children after death. When a website or an advisor uses the phrase, ask what the document does at your death and at your spouse's death.

## What is a discretionary trust?

A discretionary trust gives the trustee judgment over payouts instead of fixed amounts. The document usually sets a standard, such as health, education, maintenance and support, and the trustee decides what fits.

That discretion is the protection. A beneficiary with an addiction, a creditor problem or a shaky marriage has no right to demand a payment, so there is nothing for a lawyer, creditor or ex-spouse to grab. It also asks a lot of the trustee, who has to say no sometimes. Our guide to [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) explains the related clause, and [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) shows how parents use it.

## What is a credit shelter trust?

Also called a bypass or family trust, it is created at the first spouse's death. It holds up to that spouse's federal estate tax exemption, currently $15 million per person in 2026, so the assets are not counted in the survivor's estate later. The survivor can often receive income and sometimes principal, while the children get what is left.

Before portability existed, couples needed this trust to avoid wasting the first spouse's exemption. Portability now lets a surviving spouse claim the unused portion by filing a return, so most couples under the exemption skip it. A credit shelter trust still earns its place when the couple wants to lock in who inherits, in a second marriage for instance, or to cap exposure to a state estate tax with a much lower threshold. Our page on [bypass trusts versus portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability) works through when it still makes sense.

## What are generation-skipping and dynasty trusts?

A generation-skipping trust benefits grandchildren or more remote descendants. Federal law taxes certain transfers that skip a generation, at the top 40% rate, with an exemption that currently equals the estate tax exemption. Planning is about using that exemption on purpose.

A dynasty trust is the long-lasting version. Assets stay in the trust, protected from each generation's creditors and divorces, while descendants get distributions rather than ownership. Some states allow trusts to last for centuries or forever, and that is why you hear of Nevada, South Dakota and Delaware trusts. Most families will never need this. If you leave $900,000 and a house, a dynasty trust adds cost without adding much.

## What is a Crummey trust?

A Crummey trust is an irrevocable trust where each beneficiary has a short window, often 30 days, to withdraw gifts you add. That brief right turns the gift into a present interest, which lets it count against the [annual gift tax exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion), currently $19,000 per recipient in 2026. Beneficiaries nearly always let the window pass and the money stays in the trust.

The most common use is paying premiums on a life insurance policy held in an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust). Each year the grantor sends a gift letter to the beneficiaries, a detail that is easy to forget and costly to skip.

## What is a charitable lead trust?

It is the mirror of a [charitable remainder trust](/learn/estate-tax/charitable-remainder-trust). The charity receives payments for a term of years, then what remains goes to your family. It can lower gift or estate tax on the family's share. It fits donors with taxable estates and a real intention to give, not a household looking to save a few dollars.

## What is a blind trust?

A blind trust lets a trustee manage investments without telling the owner what is held, so the owner cannot be influenced by or accused of acting on a conflict. Elected officials and some senior executives use it. For ordinary families, a blind trust adds a layer of mystery you do not need.

A quick example of the cost of mismatch: Gil and Mei, both 61, were told they needed a dynasty trust. Their combined estate is a house, retirement accounts and about $1.1 million in savings. They are nowhere near the exemption, and their real concern is a son who spends freely. A discretionary trust for his share solves that at a fraction of the price.

## How long can a trust last?

How long depends on state law and the document. An older rule against perpetuities limits many trusts to a life in being plus 21 years, which in practice is about a century. Several states have loosened or abolished the limit for some trusts. Most family trusts end sooner, when the youngest beneficiary reaches the age you chose or when the assets are gone.

## Which one do you actually need?

Work through this short order of questions.

1. **Do you want your home and accounts to skip probate?** A living trust, which many people call a family trust, does that.
2. **Is your combined estate well under $15 million per person?** If so, skip the tax-driven trusts and focus on control.
3. **Does a beneficiary need protection or guidance?** Look at a discretionary or spendthrift design.
4. **Are you married with a large estate or a second marriage?** Ask about a credit shelter or marital trust.
5. **Do you plan to hold life insurance outside your estate?** Ask about an irrevocable life insurance trust with Crummey powers.
6. **Do you want to benefit grandchildren and beyond?** Ask about generation-skipping provisions.
