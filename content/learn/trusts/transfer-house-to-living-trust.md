You signed a living trust and the attorney, or the website, told you to "fund" it. For most people the house is the biggest piece of that job. Moving it into the trust is not complicated, but it is easy to do halfway, and a half-finished transfer is one of the most common reasons trust plans fail.

This article explains how the transfer works, step by step, and what to check with your lender, insurer and county before you record anything. It is part of our [trusts series](/learn/trusts), and it goes deeper on one step from [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## Why does the deed matter?

A house is owned by whoever is named on the recorded deed. The trust agreement is a contract, and a list of assets inside it does not change what the county records show. If the deed still names you personally when you die, the house is a probate asset, even if your trust says it belongs to the trust. Your family may then need a court proceeding for the very asset you meant to protect.

Take Elena, 71. She signed a living trust three years ago and listed her home on a schedule attached to it, but never recorded a new deed. When she dies, her successor trustee cannot sell the house without a probate court first appointing someone, because the county records show Elena as the owner. A short document, signed and recorded, would have prevented that. For background on what probate involves, see [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate).

## What are the steps to transfer a house into a living trust?

1. **Confirm the trust is signed.** The trust should be fully signed, dated and notarized, and its exact name should be settled, such as "The Elena Torres Revocable Living Trust dated March 4, 2026." You will use that name on the deed.
2. **Get the current deed.** Pull your existing deed from the county recorder's website or your closing file. You need the exact legal description, which is the long paragraph describing the lot, and the parcel number. Do not retype the street address as a substitute.
3. **Choose the deed type.** Common forms are a warranty deed, a grant deed and a quitclaim deed. They differ in the promises the signer makes about title. Many attorneys prefer a deed that keeps title insurance protection intact, and the right form depends on your state. Our attorneys can tell you which fits. A bare quitclaim deed is often used, but it is not always the safest choice.
4. **Prepare the deed.** The deed names you as the grantor and you, as trustee, as the grantee, in the form "Elena Torres, Trustee of the Elena Torres Revocable Living Trust dated March 4, 2026." It must include the legal description exactly.
5. **Sign before a notary.** Most states require a notary acknowledgment for recording, and some require witnesses.
6. **Record the deed.** File the deed with the county recorder or register of deeds where the property sits. Pay the recording fee, which varies by county.
7. **Complete tax forms.** Some states and counties require a transfer-tax exemption form or a change-of-ownership report even when no tax is due. Others require nothing. Check before recording.
8. **Notify your insurer.** Update the homeowners policy so the trustee is covered. See below.
9. **Keep the recorded deed with your trust documents.** Tell your successor trustee where both are.

## What happens to the mortgage?

Most people can transfer a mortgaged home into their own living trust without the lender calling the loan. Many mortgages contain a "due on sale" clause that lets the lender demand full payment if the property is transferred. A federal law, the Garn-St Germain Act (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)(8)), prohibits lenders from enforcing that clause on a transfer of a one-to-four-unit residential property into an inter vivos (living) trust where the borrower remains a beneficiary and occupies the home. The law is summarized on [Cornell's Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1701j-3).

That protection has limits. It applies to certain residences, not to every type of property, and not to a trust with a different beneficiary. Read your loan documents, and send the lender a short written notice with a copy of the trust certification. If you are refinancing, tell the lender up front that title will be held in a trust, since requirements differ among lenders.

## Does title insurance still apply?

Often it does, but confirm. Whether an owner's title policy continues after a transfer to your own trust depends on the policy's wording and the insurer's practice. Some policies expressly continue coverage for transfers to a living trust, and some insurers offer an endorsement. Ask your title company or your attorney, and keep the answer in writing. A bad deed type, such as a quitclaim, may raise questions with certain insurers, which is one reason deed type matters.

## What about property tax and transfer tax?

In many states, a transfer to your own revocable trust is excluded from property tax reassessment and from transfer tax, because you are still the beneficial owner. This is state and county law, not federal, and it varies. Some places require a form to claim the exclusion. California, for example, has its own rules about reassessment on later transfers to children, which can matter when the trust eventually passes the home on.

Check your state's rules in our [state estate planning guides](/estate-planning/california) if you live in California, or ask the county assessor in any state before you record. Also check with the county that your homestead exemption, senior discount, veterans' exemption or other reduction continues after the deed. In most places it does for a revocable trust where you are the occupant, but a missed form can cost you the discount for a year.

## What about homeowners insurance?

Call the agent before or right after recording. Most insurers will add the trustee as an additional insured or name the trust on the policy without changing the premium. If the insurer is never told, a claim could be disputed because the named owner on the policy no longer matches the owner of record. Keep the declaration page showing the update.

## What if you own a second home, a rental or an out-of-state house?

Each parcel needs its own deed, recorded in the county where it sits. A trust avoids ancillary probate in another state only if the deed there is recorded. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) and [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate). Rental property raises extra items: leases, security deposits, landlord insurance and any LLC that already holds title. If the property is held through an LLC, the trust owns the LLC interest instead of the house.

## What happens when you sell or refinance a house held in a trust?

You can sell as trustee. The buyer's title company will ask for a certification of trust, which proves the trust exists and that the trustee has authority without revealing the full document. Because you are the trustee, the sale feels much like any other. If you refinance, some lenders ask you to take the house out of the trust for closing and put it back after, though many do not. Ask early. See the [funding your trust guide](/guides/funding-your-trust) for related steps.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Wrong legal description.** Retyping from memory can leave out a lot or an easement.
- **Signing but not recording.** An unrecorded deed may not protect you.
- **Trust name mismatch.** The name on the deed should match the trust exactly.
- **Skipping the insurer and lender.** This creates avoidable disputes.
- **Transferring when the home is jointly owned without a plan.** If a spouse or sibling is also on title, both need to sign and the form of ownership may change. See [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks).
- **Transferring too soon without considering benefits planning.** Putting a home into a revocable trust does not remove it from Medicaid counting rules. Different trusts do different things.

## Should you do this yourself or hire an attorney?

You can legally prepare and record a deed. Many people do. The risk is in what you cannot see: a defect in the legal description, a deed form that affects title insurance, or a state tax form that no one told you about. When a house is paid off, in a single county and owned by one person, the risk is smaller. When there is a mortgage, a co-owner, an LLC or more than one property, professional help is worth considering. The [cost of a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost) article explains how funding help is typically priced, and [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) can help you decide whether to proceed at all.
