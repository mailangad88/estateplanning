You retire to Arizona. Your daughter takes a job in Georgia and becomes the trustee you named years ago. Your mother leaves you a cabin in Maine. Each of these facts is ordinary. Each one raises the same worry: does the trust we paid for still do its job?

Most of the time it does. This article explains what carries over, what to update, how to handle land in a second state, and where the fancy-state trusts fit. For general background, see our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## What happens to a living trust when you move to another state?

The trust stays valid. A revocable living trust is a contract-like document created under the law of the state where you signed it, and a new address does not undo that. Courts and banks in your new state will generally honor it.

What happens is that the law around the trust shifts. Your new state's rules apply to your home, your bank accounts, your will and your [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney). A few things are worth checking.

| Item | Why it can matter after a move |
|---|---|
| Governing law clause | The trust names a state's law. Staying with the old state is often fine, but may not fit how you live now. |
| Pour-over will | Must meet the new state's signing rules or be re-signed. |
| Powers of attorney and health care documents | Often valid, yet banks and hospitals in the new state may balk at old forms. |
| Marital property rules | Moving between community property and common law states changes ownership. |
| State death or inheritance tax | A new state may tax estates at a much lower threshold than the federal one. |
| Homestead and creditor rules | Protections for a home in a trust differ widely. |

A common rule of thumb among practitioners is to review within a year, or sooner if you moved between very different states. Our guide to [common estate planning mistakes](/learn/basics/common-estate-planning-mistakes) includes this one for good reason.

### An example

Dennis and Priya signed their trust in Ohio and retired to Florida. Their trust still controls what happens to their savings. But their Ohio will uses outdated witness language, and the new house was bought in Dennis's name alone after the move. Nobody deeded it to the trust, so it is stuck going through Florida probate. The trust was fine. The follow-through failed.

## What should you do after you move?

1. Keep your trust and note the date you moved.
2. Check that every new account and any new house were retitled to the trust.
3. Sign new durable powers of attorney and health care documents on forms your new state uses, even if the old ones remain valid.
4. Update the addresses and phone numbers of your trustees and agents.
5. Ask an attorney licensed where you now live to review the trust, particularly if you moved between a community property state and a common law state.
6. Look at your [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) for the death tax, homestead and probate rules that apply.

## How does a trust handle real estate in another state?

A deed is governed by the law of the state where the land sits. If you own a lake house in Michigan and live in Texas, probate of that lake house happens in Michigan, under Michigan law, unless the house is out of your probate estate. This is called [ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate), and it means a second lawyer, a second filing, and a second set of fees.

A revocable trust fixes this when the deed is recorded in the trust's name. The Michigan house then passes under the trust and a Michigan probate case never opens. You need a deed that complies with Michigan's formalities, which is why an attorney licensed there should prepare or review it. Our guide to [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) compares trusts with transfer-on-death deeds and other tools, and the page on [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) covers the mechanics.

## Can the trustee live in another state?

Yes. The law does not require a trustee to live near the grantor, the property or the beneficiaries. A few practical points do come up.

- **Local paperwork.** Selling a house or collecting from a local bank is slower when the trustee is a long flight away.
- **State income tax.** Some states tax a trust based on where the trustee lives, others based on where the grantor or beneficiaries live. Rules vary, so ask before choosing.
- **Backups.** Name a successor who lives closer, or a professional trustee, in case the out-of-state child cannot manage on short notice. Our page on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) helps with that decision.

## Do you need a Delaware, Nevada or South Dakota trust?

Almost certainly not for an ordinary revocable living trust. Those states have trust statutes that let some irrevocable trusts last for generations, shield assets from certain creditors, or keep trust income free of state income tax. The features are real. They are built for wealthy families who already have a plan and a reason to pay for administration in another state.

Three cautions for everyone else:

1. A revocable trust gives no creditor protection anywhere, so a Nevada label on it does not change that.
2. Your home state may still tax trust income, apply its own homestead rules, or refuse to enforce the other state's rules against local creditors.
3. You pay for it, with a trustee in that state, filings, and an attorney who knows two systems.

If your estate is large enough that this is worth discussing, ask your attorney what the plan is meant to accomplish first. Often a plain domestic trust accomplishes it. For federal estate tax planning, see the current [$15 million exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

## What about married couples who move?

Couples moving between a community property state and a common law state may find that the way they own assets changes character, or that a spouse's inheritance rights shift. A joint trust drafted for one regime may split property in a way that does not fit the other. Our page on [joint living trusts for married couples](/learn/trusts/joint-living-trust-for-married-couples) explains the basics. Show your attorney exactly where you lived when you bought each asset. If a move could also mean probate in two places, our [probate section](/learn/probate) explains what that process looks like.

## What if you do nothing after the move?

Usually nothing dramatic happens right away, which is the trap. The trust sits in a drawer. Years later a bank in the new state asks for a certification that cites the wrong statute, or a hospital will not accept a ten-year-old health care form. Worse, the new house never gets deeded in and the family learns about it at the funeral.

The fix costs far less before a crisis than after one. Most reviews amount to a few updated documents and one new deed, not a rewrite. The trust you already paid for does most of the work. You are making sure the paperwork around it matches where you live now.

## How state law changes this

The core variations are signing formalities, how a trust is certified to third parties, notice rules on death, creditor claims, how long a trust may last, and state-level estate or inheritance tax. Your own state's guide, for example [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), is the right place for the specifics.
