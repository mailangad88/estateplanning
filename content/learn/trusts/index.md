A trust sounds like something for the wealthy, yet trusts are used by families of many sizes: a couple protecting their home, a parent leaving money for young children, an adult child making sure a sibling with a disability is cared for. The word is intimidating, but the idea is not. A trust is a set of written instructions for managing property, with a person you pick responsible for following them.

This guide explains the basic parts of a trust, the main types, how a trust compares with a will, what funding means, who should serve as trustee and what trusts cannot do. Each section points to a deeper article in our trusts series, and to related guides on [wills](/learn/wills), [probate](/learn/probate) and [estate and gift tax](/learn/estate-tax).

## What is a trust and who is involved?

A [trust](/glossary/trust) is a legal arrangement in which one person or institution holds property for the benefit of others. Three roles appear in nearly every trust:

| Role | Who they are | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| Grantor (also settlor or trustor) | The person who creates and funds the trust | Sets the rules, transfers property in |
| Trustee | An individual or company | Holds legal title, manages property, follows the rules |
| Beneficiary | The people or organizations who benefit | Receive income, principal or use of property |

The same person can fill more than one role. In a typical living trust, you are the grantor, trustee and beneficiary while you are alive. A [successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) takes over when you die or cannot act, and your chosen heirs become the beneficiaries.

A trust is a contract-like document, but it is not filed with a court the way a will is. That is why trusts are private and why they can operate without court supervision. The trustee owes the beneficiaries [fiduciary duties](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), meaning legal duties of loyalty and care, and can be held accountable if those duties are breached.

## What are the main types of trusts?

Trusts are classified in two main ways: whether you can change them, and when they begin.

### Revocable and irrevocable

A [revocable trust](/glossary/revocable-trust) can be amended or canceled by the grantor at any time. Because you can take the property back, the law treats it as yours for taxes and creditors. An [irrevocable trust](/glossary/irrevocable-trust) cannot be changed by the grantor, with limited exceptions. The property is generally treated as no longer yours, which is why irrevocable trusts can offer tax, benefits or protection advantages. Every trust that starts as revocable becomes irrevocable when the grantor dies.

### Living and testamentary

A [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) is created during your lifetime. A [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust) is created by your will and starts at death, after probate. Living trusts avoid probate for assets they own, while testamentary trusts do not.

### By purpose

| Trust type | Typical purpose |
|---|---|
| Revocable living trust | Avoid probate, plan for incapacity, control distribution |
| Marital or QTIP trust | Provide for a spouse and protect remainder for children; see [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts) |
| Children's trust | Hold money until children are mature; see [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) |
| Spendthrift trust | Shield an heir's inheritance from creditors; see [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) |
| Special needs trust | Support a person with a disability without losing benefits; see [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) |
| Life insurance trust | Keep insurance proceeds out of a taxable estate |
| Charitable trust | Combine charitable giving with tax or income goals |
| Pet trust | Provide for animals; see [pet trusts](/learn/property-and-assets/pet-trusts) |
| Medicaid asset protection trust | Plan for long-term care costs; see [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) |

## How does a revocable living trust work?

For most families, the revocable living trust is the workhorse. You sign a trust agreement, name yourself trustee and move your assets into the trust. Nothing changes day to day. If you become incapacitated, your successor trustee can step in without a court case. When you die, the successor trustee distributes the property according to your instructions, often without probate.

The benefits are practical: avoiding probate, planning for incapacity, keeping affairs private and controlling timing. The limits matter too: no income or estate tax savings, no protection from your own creditors, and some work to keep the trust funded. Our detailed article, [revocable living trusts: how they work, pros and cons](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), covers each of these along with a setup checklist.

## Will or trust: which fits you?

A will is a simpler tool that takes effect at death and goes through probate. A trust costs more and requires funding but can avoid probate and cover incapacity. The two work together, because a trust-based plan includes a [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) that names guardians and catches stray assets.

Quick rules of thumb:

- **A will-based plan may be enough** when your estate is modest, your assets mostly pass by beneficiary designation, and your state has a simple probate process.
- **A trust is worth a close look** when you own real estate (especially in more than one state), want incapacity planning, have young or vulnerable beneficiaries or want privacy.

Our article [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) offers a side-by-side table and a decision checklist. If you are asking whether a trust is worth it for you, read [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust), and for pricing see [how much a living trust costs](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost). For background on wills, see [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do), and for the probate process see [what is probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate).

## Why does funding a trust matter?

A trust only controls property that it owns. If you sign a trust but leave your house and accounts in your own name, those assets are not governed by the trust, and your family may still face probate. [Funding the trust](/glossary/funding) means changing title on property so the trust is the owner: recording a new deed for real estate, retitling bank and brokerage accounts, assigning personal property and updating beneficiary designations where appropriate.

Some assets should not be retitled. Retirement accounts such as IRAs and 401(k)s generally stay in your name with beneficiaries designated, because moving them is treated as a taxable withdrawal. Our article on [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) has a step-by-step process and table of which assets to retitle, and the guide to [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains why some assets bypass court regardless of a trust.

## When does an irrevocable trust make sense?

An irrevocable trust is a deliberate trade: you give up control and flexibility, and in return you get a result a revocable trust cannot provide. Common reasons include reducing estate tax for large estates, owning life insurance outside your estate, planning for Medicaid long-term care benefits, supporting a person with a disability and protecting heirs.

With the 2026 federal estate tax exemption at $15 million per person (and $30 million for a married couple using portability), most families do not need an irrevocable trust for federal estate tax. Some states have lower thresholds, so that can change the analysis. Irrevocable trusts also create gift tax filings, compressed trust income tax brackets, and often the loss of a step-up in basis on gifted assets. Read [irrevocable trusts: when giving up control makes sense](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) for the types, tradeoffs and a checklist.

## Who should serve as trustee?

Trustee choice shapes how your trust works in real life. A family member knows your values and costs less. A bank or professional trustee brings expertise, impartiality and continuity but charges fees. Co-trustees can combine strengths, with the risk of disagreement. Qualities to look for include integrity, organization, financial sense, the ability to be fair and to say no, and willingness to serve.

Name at least one backup, and give the trustee tools, including a letter of wishes and a way to replace them if things go badly. See [how to choose a trustee: family member, friend or professional](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee). The same thinking applies to choosing an [executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor), but the roles differ: an executor settles a probate estate, while a trustee manages a trust, sometimes for decades. After a death, the trustee's practical tasks are laid out in our guide to [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).

## How do trusts help children and heirs?

Trusts shine when you want to control how and when someone inherits. Without a trust, a minor who inherits may need a court-supervised account and then receives everything at 18 or 21. With a trust you can:

- Delay distributions until set ages, such as 25, 30 and 35
- Authorize payment of education, medical and living costs in the meantime
- Keep a single "pot" for young children and split it into shares later
- Name a trustee who is different from the child's guardian

See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) for structures and pitfalls, and [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) for how to divide the roles.

For adult heirs who face lawsuits, divorce, debt or addiction, a trust with a [spendthrift clause](/glossary/spendthrift-clause) can keep an inheritance protected until the trustee decides to distribute it. [Spendthrift and asset protection trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) explains the strength of that protection, the exceptions such as child support and federal tax claims, and why asset protection trusts for your own money are far harder to build.

## Does a trust save taxes?

Usually not. A revocable living trust is ignored for income tax and estate tax. Your income is reported on your own return and the trust assets are counted in your estate. The benefit you do get, the [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) at death, comes from owning assets until you die, whether or not they are in a trust.

Some irrevocable trusts are designed to reduce estate tax, but they are complex and mostly relevant for estates near or above the exemption. Our article [does a living trust save taxes? Common myths about trusts](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes) goes through the common misunderstandings, the federal numbers for 2026 and where trusts can create tax problems, such as retained income taxed at compressed rates. For the full tax picture, see the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) and [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## What are the most common trust mistakes?

1. **Never funding the trust.** The most common failure, and the easiest to prevent.
2. **Forgetting beneficiary designations.** Retirement accounts and life insurance follow the form on file, not the trust, unless the form names it.
3. **Naming a trustee without asking.** Someone who is unwilling or unprepared causes delays and conflict.
4. **Leaving out a pour-over will.** You still need a will to name guardians and catch forgotten assets.
5. **Using a trust for the wrong reason.** A trust is not a tax shelter and does not protect you from your own creditors if revocable.
6. **Not updating after life changes.** Divorce, marriage, births, deaths, moves and new property should prompt a review; see [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).
7. **Relying on an out-of-state or generic template.** Trust law, signing rules and property transfers vary by state.

## Where do state laws differ?

Trusts are governed mainly by state law. Many states have adopted a version of the Uniform Trust Code, but details vary, including how much notice trustees must give beneficiaries, how long a trust can last, whether a decanting statute exists, how creditor claims are handled after death, and whether self-settled asset protection trusts are recognized. Probate costs and procedures also differ, which affects how much a trust helps. If you own property in more than one state, review [owning property in more than one state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) and [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

Look at the state guides for specifics, such as [California](/estate-planning/california), [Texas](/estate-planning/texas) or [New York](/estate-planning/new-york), or find your own state in our directory.

## A simple way to decide

1. **List what you own and how it is titled.** Home, accounts, retirement plans, life insurance, business interests.
2. **Identify the problems you want to solve.** Probate, incapacity, young heirs, privacy, benefits eligibility, taxes, family conflict.
3. **Match tools to problems.** Probate and incapacity often point to a revocable trust. Vulnerable heirs point to a spendthrift or special needs trust. A very large estate points to tax planning.
4. **Pick people.** Trustee, successor, guardian, agent under power of attorney.
5. **Commit to funding and maintaining it.** Plan to retitle assets and review the plan every few years.

If a trust is not needed, that is a perfectly good outcome. Many families do well with a will, a power of attorney, a healthcare directive and clean beneficiary designations.
