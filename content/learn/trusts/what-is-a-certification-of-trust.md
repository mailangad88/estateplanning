You sit down at the bank with your new living trust. The banker says she needs to see "the trust." You hand her a forty-page document that spells out who gets the house, how much goes to each child, and what happens if your son divorces. You did not mean to share all of that with a branch employee.

A certification of trust solves the problem. This page explains what it is, what goes in it, who signs, and what to do if an institution still pushes back. It is part of our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts), and it supports the retitling steps in [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## What does a certification of trust say?

It states the facts an institution needs and nothing more. A typical certification includes:

| Item | Why the institution wants it |
| --- | --- |
| Trust name and date | To match the trust to the account or deed |
| That the trust exists and has not been revoked | Confirms it is still in force |
| Names of the current trustees | Shows who may sign |
| The trustee's powers | Shows whether the trustee can buy, sell, borrow or open accounts |
| Whether the trust is revocable | Affects tax reporting and who controls it |
| How title should be held | For example, "Maria Santos, Trustee of the Santos Family Trust dated March 3, 2026" |
| The trust's tax identification number, or the grantor's Social Security number | For account reporting |
| Whether one trustee can act alone | Avoids refusals at the teller window |
| Signature and, usually, a notary acknowledgment | Proves it is genuine |

What stays out is the heart of the plan: the beneficiaries, the shares, the ages and conditions for distributions, and any private instructions. A certification lets the bank deal with the trustee while keeping the family's choices private. The privacy rules for the full trust are in our article on [what trust beneficiaries can see and when](/learn/trusts/what-trust-beneficiaries-can-see-and-when).

## Does the law require banks to accept it?

Both states have a statute that says a business can rely on a proper certification, and that protects the business if the facts turn out to be wrong, as long as it did not know better. New Jersey enacted the Uniform Trust Code, and Illinois has its own Trust Code with a similar provision. The details, such as what the certification must contain and how it must be signed, differ from state to state, so the form should be written to fit the state where the trust is administered.

The statutes also include a second protection for you. A person who asks for the whole trust document after receiving a proper certification can be liable for damages if the court finds the demand was not made in good faith. Most banks know this, but internal policies lag. In practice, the message "our state trust code lets you rely on this" works better when it comes from your attorney's letter than from your own memory of a statute.

## How do you get one?

1. **Ask your attorney to prepare it with the trust.** Most firms draft one at signing, and it costs little extra.
2. **Check the institution's own form.** Some banks and brokerages prefer their own certificate. Ask what they need before you visit.
3. **Sign and notarize.** Do it with the notary your attorney's office provides, or at your bank.
4. **Make several copies.** You will use one for each account, one for the title company and one for the county recorder if a deed needs it.
5. **Keep the original with the trust.** Store the documents as described in [where to keep your trust documents](/learn/trusts/trust-signing-notary-witnesses-and-where-to-keep-the-documents).

## When do you need a certification?

You need one every time someone else holds an asset and asks who controls it. Common examples follow.


- Opening or retitling a **bank account**.
- Moving a **brokerage account** or buying securities in the trust's name.
- Recording a **deed** for a house, sometimes alongside a notarized memorandum.
- Selling a house held in the trust, when the title company asks for proof of authority.
- Naming the trust as owner or beneficiary of an **insurance policy**.
- Dealing with a **lender** during a refinance, which our article on [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) mentions.

Notice the pattern. Each time, the other party wants proof that you can sign, not a map of your family tree.

## What if the bank still says no?

Start with a call, not an argument. Ask the branch manager what specific item is missing, and ask whether the bank's legal or trust-department staff can review the certification. The reasons banks reject a certification are usually ordinary: no notary, the wrong trustee named, the trust name differs from the account title, or the trust number is missing.

Suppose Daniel, a trustee, brought a certification to a credit union and the teller wanted the full trust. Daniel asked for the operations manager, who found the bank's template required a line about revocability that his certification lacked. His attorney added it, the credit union accepted the amended version, and the accounts were retitled in a week. No one saw the trust.

If an institution digs in, the next steps are a letter from your attorney citing the state statute, a request for the bank's written policy, and, as a last resort, moving the account to an institution that accepts the certification. Our page on [how to tell if your trust is actually funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded) lists what to retitle first.

## What changes after the trust creator dies?

The certification is used even more at that stage. The successor trustee gives each institution a new certification, a death certificate and the trust's new tax number. The certification names the successor as the one with authority, and says the original trustee has died. A successor trustee should expect every institution to want it, and should order several originals. The first steps are in our guide on [what a successor trustee must do](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).

Updating matters in life too. If you replace a trustee, resign, or amend the trust's powers, the old certification is out of date. Give the new one to every institution that has the old one in its file.

## Is a certification the same as a deed or a trust amendment?

No. It proves authority; it does not move property or change terms. A house still needs a recorded deed, as explained in the funding guide, and a change to who inherits still needs an amendment signed under the trust's rules. People sometimes assume that signing a certification at the bank funded their trust. It did not. The account must also be retitled in the trust's name, and the certification is only the paperwork that lets the bank do that.

For a couple with a joint trust, the certification also settles a practical point: whether either spouse can sign alone. Write that into the document at the start, so a branch employee does not have to guess.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the wrong name.** The title on the account must match the trust name and date.
- **Leaving out the powers.** If the form does not mention the power to sell real estate, a title company may balk.
- **Skipping the notary.** An unsigned or unnotarized certification gets sent back.
- **Not updating it.** A certification that names a trustee who has resigned is worse than none.
- **Handing over the full trust by default.** Once a bank has the whole document, you cannot take it back.
