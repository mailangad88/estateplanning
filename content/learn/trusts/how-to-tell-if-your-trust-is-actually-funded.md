Walter, 67, signed his living trust three years ago. He has the thick binder on a shelf and tells his daughters the house is "all taken care of." He has never opened it since. His bank statements still say "Walter J. Novak." So do the deed and the brokerage account. If Walter died tomorrow, nearly everything would pass through probate.

That is the most common failure in trust planning, and it is easy to fix. This page gives you a way to check the trust you already have, one asset at a time, and tells you what to do when something is not where it should be.

## The short version

- Funding means changing the title or the beneficiary so the trust, not you personally, is the owner or the recipient.
- You can test funding by reading the titles on your actual documents.
- Real estate and most bank and brokerage accounts get retitled. Retirement accounts and insurance use beneficiary forms.
- A schedule of assets attached to the trust helps, but it does not do the work of retitling.
- Fix gaps now, because after death the successor trustee cannot retitle what you still own personally.

## What does "funded" actually mean?

A [funded trust](/glossary/funding) holds the assets it was designed to hold. A trust is a legal arrangement where the trustee owns property for the benefit of the people you named. If you never transfer the property to the trustee, there is nothing for the trustee to manage. The document describes a plan for assets that are not part of it.

The [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) page explains the process from the start. This page is the audit that comes after: a way of checking your work.

## How do you check each kind of asset?

Take one afternoon, a stack of recent statements and a pen. For each asset, find the exact name on the title or account and compare it to the right answer in the table.

| Asset | Where to look | What funded looks like | What unfunded looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Home | Recorded deed from the county | Owner is "Your Name, Trustee of the Your Name Living Trust dated ___" | Deed shows only your name, or you and a spouse as joint owners |
| Checking and savings | Statement header and signature card | Account titled in the trustee name | Account in your personal name with no trust title |
| Brokerage account | Statement or online registration page | Registration shows trustee and trust date | Individual or joint registration |
| CDs | Bank certificate | Titled to the trust, or has a payable-on-death to the trust | Individual with no payable-on-death |
| Vehicles | Title certificate | Often left out on purpose | Individual name |
| 401(k), IRA | Beneficiary form | Owner stays you, beneficiary form reviewed | Form blank, outdated or names an ex |
| Life insurance | Policy and beneficiary form | Beneficiary is the trust, or a person on purpose | Form blank or estate named |
| Business interest | Operating agreement or share register | Assignment of interest to the trust | Still in your name |
| Valuables and furniture | Assignment of personal property | General assignment signed | No assignment |

If a column on your document reads "Walter J. Novak" and nothing more, that asset is not in the trust. If it reads "Walter J. Novak, Trustee of the Walter J. Novak Revocable Trust dated March 4, 2023," it is.

## What if the answer is mixed?

Most real files are. A couple might find that the house deed was done correctly, the checking account was left alone, an old brokerage account at a firm they stopped using is still individual, and a life insurance form names a deceased parent. That is normal, and it is fixable.

Sort what you find into three piles.

1. **Right already.** Photograph or copy the paper that proves it and put it in the trust binder.
2. **Needs retitling.** Houses, bank and brokerage accounts, and business interests. These need new deeds, forms or assignments.
3. **Needs a beneficiary decision.** Retirement accounts and insurance. This is a choice about who should receive the money, not a mechanical change. The [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) page covers the common choices.

## How do you retitle each asset?

Step by step, in the order that tends to go smoothly.

1. **Gather the trust certificate.** Many institutions accept a short certification of trust, which proves the trust exists and names the trustee without exposing the private terms. Your attorney can prepare one.
2. **Start with the account closest to home.** Bank accounts are usually quick. Bring identification, the certification, and your trust's name and date.
3. **Move brokerage accounts with the firm's own forms.** They often have a "change of registration" or "account re-titling" form. Ask whether the cost basis information carries over. It normally does.
4. **Record a new deed for real estate.** The deed must be drafted correctly for your state, signed, notarized and recorded with the county. Read [transferring a house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) before you sign, because mortgage, insurance and title issues can arise.
5. **Review the beneficiary forms.** Change them if they are wrong. Do not retitle retirement accounts.
6. **Ask for written confirmation.** Keep the new statement or letter showing the changed title.
7. **Update the schedule.** If your trust has a list of assets, add the new items with dates.

Illinois and New Jersey residents will find state-specific points in the [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) and [New Jersey living trusts](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) pages, including how recording and transfer taxes work for real estate.

## Which assets should you leave out on purpose?

Funding does not mean everything. A few assets are better kept out or handled by another route.

- **Retirement accounts.** A transfer during life can trigger income tax. The beneficiary form is the tool.
- **Cars.** Many people leave them in their own name, because insurance is simpler and the state may have a small-vehicle process on death.
- **Health savings accounts.** These have their own beneficiary rules.
- **Accounts you use daily.** Some people keep a small personal checking account and fund the rest, so a single bad day with a bank does not freeze their spending money.

Whatever you leave out, make sure the pour-over will, discussed in the [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) page, is current and the beneficiary forms agree with your plan.

## What if you cannot tell?

If a title is unclear, ask the source. A bank officer can tell you how an account is titled. A title company or the county recorder can provide the deed. A brokerage can email the registration page. For a full picture, the [what assets go in a living trust](/learn/trusts/what-assets-go-in-a-living-trust) page lists what belongs and what does not.

An attorney can also run a funding review, which means collecting the current titles and comparing them to the trust. That review is a modest project, and it often finds the one account that would have caused a probate case.

## How often should you check?

Once a year works for most people, and again after any of these events: buying or selling a home, a refinance, opening a new account, an inheritance, a marriage or divorce, a business sale, or a move to a new state. Banks merge, and when they do accounts can be re-registered without warning. The [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) can help you remember.
