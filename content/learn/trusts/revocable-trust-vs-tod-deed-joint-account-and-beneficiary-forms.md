There are at least four ways to pass an asset to someone without going through probate: a revocable living trust, a transfer on death deed, a joint account or title, and a beneficiary designation. Friends and relatives tend to say one is "all you need" and another is "a mistake." Both can be right, depending on what you own.

This comparison looks at what each does, what it cannot do, and where people get hurt by choosing the wrong one. If you want the background on trusts themselves, begin with our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts).

## How do the four options compare?

| | Living trust | TOD deed | Joint ownership | Beneficiary form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Covers | Almost any asset retitled into it | One parcel of real estate | One account or one property | One account, policy or plan |
| Works if you become incapacitated | Yes, successor trustee steps in | No | Partly, co-owner can act | No |
| Control during life | Full, if revocable | Full, until death | Shared, often fully | Full |
| Conditions or staged payouts | Yes | No | No | No |
| Handles a minor or disabled heir | Yes | Poorly | Poorly | Poorly |
| Court involvement at death | None for funded assets | None, but a recorded affidavit may be needed | None | None |
| Cost to set up | Highest | Low | Very low | Free |
| Availability | Every state | Many states, not all | Every state | Every state |

No column wins every row. A trust costs more and covers more. The other three are narrow tools that do one thing quickly.

## What does a joint account really do?

A joint account or joint title makes the other person a current owner, not a future one. That has consequences that surprise people.

- The co-owner can withdraw the money today. If your daughter is on your checking account, the bank treats her as an owner.
- Her creditors, an ex-spouse or a lawsuit can reach the account.
- If you put a child on the deed to your house, you may have made a gift, and you hand that child your tax basis on that share instead of the stepped-up basis they would have received at your death. The outcome is often not what the owner meant, and our guide to [ways to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) lists the trade-offs of each method.
- On your death, the account goes to the surviving owner regardless of what your will or trust says. A parent who adds one child "for convenience" can unintentionally leave that child with everything.

For spouses, joint ownership is often fine and common, since spouses usually want each other to inherit. The trouble is the second death. Whoever survives owns everything outright, with no plan for what comes next.

## When does a TOD deed work well?

A transfer on death deed, called a beneficiary deed or TOD deed in states that allow it, names who gets a specific parcel when you die. You keep full ownership, can sell or mortgage the property, and can change your mind. At your death the heir records an affidavit and a death certificate and takes title, with no probate. See our guide to the [transfer on death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) for the state-specific rules.

It fits when:

- You own one house and one person is the obvious heir.
- The heir is a competent adult who will outlive you.
- Your main goal is to skip probate on the house, cheaply.

It struggles when the heir might die first without a backup named, when there are several heirs who may disagree, when the heir is a minor, or when you might need someone to manage the house if you become incapacitated. A deed does not help with incapacity at all. If you cannot make decisions about the property, your agent under a [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) may or may not have authority over it, depending on the document.

## When do beneficiary forms work?

Retirement accounts, life insurance and annuities pass by beneficiary designation and not by will or trust unless you name one of those as beneficiary. A payable-on-death designation on a bank account does the same. These are free and fast and the right choice for most such accounts.

They are weak when the beneficiary is a child, someone with a disability or someone with money problems, because the money arrives in one lump. They also fail when nobody updates them. A divorce does not always cancel an ex-spouse's designation under every plan, a point we cover in [ex-spouse still named as beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary). The usual fix is to name a trust as the beneficiary of the account, covered in [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

## When does a living trust earn its cost?

A revocable living trust becomes the better choice when one or more of these is true:

1. **You want protection if you cannot manage your affairs.** The successor trustee can pay your bills and sell the house without a court-supervised guardianship.
2. **You own property in more than one state.** One trust can avoid separate ancillary probate cases.
3. **Your heirs need rules.** Minors, a child with a disability, a spendthrift, or a blended family that needs protection for both sides.
4. **You have several assets and several heirs.** The trust coordinates them in a single place.
5. **You want privacy.** A trust is not filed with the court the way a will is.

The costs are the drafting fee and the work of retitling accounts and deeds, which is where plans fail. An unfunded trust is just paper; the [unfunded trust scenario](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) shows what that looks like. Our article on [whether you need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) offers a longer checklist.

## Can you use more than one?

Yes, and most good plans do. A common combination looks like this:

- The house and non-retirement investments are in the trust.
- Retirement accounts name the spouse first and the trust, or the children, as backup.
- Life insurance names the trust as contingent beneficiary if heirs are young.
- A pour-over will catches anything missed.
- A power of attorney covers the accounts that cannot go into the trust.

The danger is overlap. If a TOD deed on your house names your son and your trust leaves the house to both children, the deed wins and your daughter gets nothing from it. Review all titles together, and recheck after any marriage, divorce, death or move. Our [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) walks through that check account by account.

## An example

Denise, 71, widowed, owns a house worth $420,000, a brokerage account, an IRA and a checking account. Her two children are Tom, who lives nearby and handles her paperwork, and Alicia, who lives across the country.

Her friend suggests putting Tom on everything as joint owner. Denise's attorney points out what that would do: Tom would own the house outright at her death, Alicia would receive nothing from it, and a creditor of Tom's could reach the accounts today. Denise instead puts the house and brokerage account in a living trust, with Tom as successor trustee and equal shares for both children. The IRA names both children, and the checking account is payable on death to the trust. When Denise has a stroke at 76, Tom steps in as trustee the next week and pays her care bills without going to court.

## What mistakes should you watch for?

- Adding a child to a deed "just in case" without thinking about gift tax and basis.
- Naming a minor as beneficiary and leaving no custodian or trust.
- Forgetting a contingent beneficiary on any form.
- Leaving a will that says one thing and forms that say another. The forms win.
- Treating a TOD deed as a complete plan when incapacity is the likelier problem.
