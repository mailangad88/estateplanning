Most people sign a trust and then put it in a drawer. Years later a child divorces, a trustee moves away, or a house is sold, and the question comes up: can I change this thing, and how? The short answer for a revocable trust is yes. The longer answer is that the document you signed decides how, and doing it sloppily can create a bigger problem than the one you were fixing.

This guide walks through the three ways to change a trust, how to cancel one completely, and the situations where the door has already closed. For background on how these trusts work in general, start with our [guide to trusts](/learn/trusts) and the page on [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).

## Can you change a living trust after you sign it?

You can change a [revocable trust](/glossary/revocable-trust) as often as you like, as long as you are alive and have the mental capacity to sign. That flexibility is the whole point. The trust is yours, you are usually the trustee, and nobody else gets a say.

Two limits apply. First, the document itself may set a method for amendments, such as "in a writing signed by the grantor and delivered to the trustee." If the trust names a method, state law in most places treats that method as the one to use. Second, a change only matters for assets the trust owns. Rewriting who receives the house means little if the house is still titled in your own name; see [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## What is the difference between an amendment and a restatement?

An amendment changes one or two provisions and leaves the rest alone. A restatement replaces the whole document with a new version while keeping the original trust's name and date, so existing deeds and account titles stay valid. A full revocation ends the trust and returns assets to you.

| Method | Best for | What happens to titles | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amendment | Swapping a trustee, changing one beneficiary, fixing a name | Nothing changes | Conflicts with older sections; stacking many amendments |
| Restatement | Several changes, new family situation, moved states | Nothing changes, trust keeps its name and date | Must be signed with the same care as the original |
| Revocation | Ending the trust for good | Everything must be retitled back | Forgotten accounts left in the trust's name |
| New trust | A different structure or a different state's law | Everything must be retitled to the new trust | Cost and paperwork; usually only when a restatement cannot do it |

Think of amendments as sticky notes and a restatement as a clean copy. After the third or fourth sticky note, a clean copy is easier on everyone who has to read it later. Our attorneys generally suggest a restatement once amendments start touching each other.

## How do you amend a trust, step by step?

1. **Read the amendment clause in your trust.** It tells you whether a signature alone is enough or whether you need a notary or witnesses.
2. **Decide exactly what changes.** Write down the old language and the new language side by side.
3. **Draft the amendment so it names the trust.** Use the trust's full title and date, for example "the Okafor Family Trust dated April 12, 2022," and state which section it replaces.
4. **Sign it the way the trust requires,** and get it notarized even if the trust does not demand it. Banks and title companies like notarized documents.
5. **Give the trustee a copy.** If you are the trustee, give a copy to your successor trustee and keep the original with the trust.
6. **Check your funding.** If you named a new beneficiary on a life insurance policy or retirement account to line up with the change, update those forms too.

Handwritten edits on the trust document are a trap. Crossing out a name and writing in another one may or may not count as a valid amendment, and it invites a lawsuit. Sending an email that says "change the trust so Ben gets the cabin" is no better in most states.

## How do you revoke a trust?

You revoke a trust by following the revocation method in the document, which is usually a signed written statement. Then comes the part people forget, which is moving the property back.

1. Sign a written revocation, notarized.
2. Record new deeds for real estate, changing title from the trustee back to you personally.
3. Retitle bank, brokerage and other accounts.
4. Reassign business interests and personal property.
5. Update insurance and notify your lender.
6. Replace the plan. If you revoke the trust and have no will, your assets go through your state's default rules; see [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

Revoking is rarely the right move by itself. If your goal is to avoid the cost of a trust, ask whether the trust is still doing a job for you, using our page on [the downsides of a living trust](/learn/trusts/what-is-the-downside-of-a-living-trust). If your goal is different beneficiaries, an amendment is quicker and keeps your funding intact.

## An example

Priya, 63, signed a trust in 2019 leaving everything equally to her two sons, Dev and Arun. In 2025 Arun was diagnosed with a condition that makes him eligible for disability benefits. Priya's first thought was to rewrite everything. Her attorney instead recommended an amendment for Arun's share, sending it to a supplemental needs trust, plus a short change naming Dev's wife as the backup trustee. The trust already owned the house and her brokerage account, so no retitling was needed. Total time was about two weeks, mostly waiting on a notary appointment and a bank's review of the revised certificate of trust.

## When can you not change a trust?

A revocable trust turns into an irrevocable one in three situations, and in each the ordinary amendment route closes.

- **You die.** The terms freeze. The successor trustee follows them; see [when a living trust becomes irrevocable](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-living-trust-becomes-irrevocable).
- **You lose capacity.** Someone who cannot understand what they are signing cannot amend a trust. Some trusts let an agent under a power of attorney act, but only if the trust says so. Most [powers of attorney cannot rewrite a trust](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) without explicit language.
- **You gave up the power.** An [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) is designed to be hard to undo. Changes usually require the consent of the beneficiaries, a court order, or a power the document reserves to a trustee or trust protector.

Capacity is worth a note. The standard for signing a trust amendment is usually the same as for a will: you understand what you own, who your family is, and what the document does. Waiting until a diagnosis is advanced makes any change easier to attack later. If a relative could object, see [whether a living trust can be contested](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested).

## What mistakes cause the most trouble?

- **Changing a will and forgetting the trust,** or the reverse. The two documents should be read together; our page on [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils) covers the will side.
- **Signing amendments but losing track of which is current.** Keep a dated list inside the trust binder.
- **Naming a new trustee without asking them.** The person you pick may say no, and a declining trustee delays everything.
- **Moving to a new state and leaving the trust alone.** Most trusts remain valid, but terms and tax references may need updating.
- **Making a change in a rush at a hospital bed.** Courts look hard at last-minute documents.

When life changes, such as a marriage, divorce, birth or a large sale, take the trust out and read it. The [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder) can nudge you every year.
