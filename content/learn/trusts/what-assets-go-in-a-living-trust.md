You have signed a trust, or you are about to, and now comes a very practical question: what exactly goes into it? The answer is not "everything." Some assets belong in the trust, some must stay out, and a handful sit in a gray area where the right choice depends on the state or the family.

This guide sorts your assets one at a time. It is the companion to [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust), which covers the paperwork step by step; here the focus is the decision itself. Our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) has the overall picture.

## How do you decide what goes into a trust?

Ask three questions about each asset.

1. **Is there a title or registration I can change?** A deed, an account title, a stock certificate or an assignment. If not, the asset cannot be held by the trust directly.
2. **Would it go through probate if I died today?** If it already passes by a beneficiary form or by survivorship, the trust may add nothing.
3. **Would retitling cost me a tax benefit or protection?** Retirement accounts and some joint titles do.

Assets that pass these questions are what attorneys mean by "funding" the trust. The ones that do not still need a plan, usually a beneficiary designation. The [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) is a quick way to see which of your accounts would go through court.

## Which assets usually go in?

- **Your house and other real estate.** A new deed moves it; see [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).
- **Checking, savings and money market accounts** held in your name alone.
- **Brokerage and mutual fund accounts** that are not retirement accounts.
- **Business interests,** such as LLC membership or closely held stock, subject to operating agreement rules. See [putting a business in a living trust](/learn/business-owners/can-i-put-my-business-in-a-living-trust).
- **Valuable personal property** with a general assignment, from art to jewelry.
- **Rental property.** The trust holds title; ask about liability and insurance first.
- **Cryptocurrency and digital property** you can assign, covered below.

## Which assets stay out?

| Asset | Why it stays out | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| IRA, 401(k), 403(b) | Retitling is treated as a taxable withdrawal | Name beneficiaries; see [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) |
| HSA | Same tax issue | Name a beneficiary |
| Social Security and pension benefits | Paid to you personally, cannot be assigned | Nothing to retitle; deposit funds into a trust account if you wish |
| Annuities | Titling can trigger tax or lose features | Name beneficiary |
| 529 plan | The account owner is fixed | Name a successor owner |
| Life insurance | Usually passes faster by beneficiary form | Name the person, or the trust where there is a reason |
| Vehicles | Often easy to transfer without probate | See below |

Moving a retirement account into a trust is a mistake with a tax bill attached. Naming the trust as beneficiary can be correct, but the wording must be careful. Read [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) before touching the form.

## What about jointly owned property?

Jointly owned property is where most people get tripped up, because the deed already says who inherits.

If you and your spouse own a house jointly, it can usually go into a joint trust without a problem. If you own a house with a brother, an unmarried partner or a friend, the situation is different. With [joint tenancy](/glossary/joint-tenancy), the surviving owner inherits automatically. If you deed your half to your trust, you may break the survivorship right, and your half then passes under the trust's terms. That could be what you want, or it could be a family surprise. Joint bank accounts work the same way: the co-owner usually owns the money on your death, no matter what your trust says. Our page on [joint tenancy risks](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) goes into those traps.

## What about your car?

Most attorneys leave ordinary cars out of the trust. States commonly allow a vehicle to be transferred with a short form and the death certificate, or through a small-estate process, and dealers and lenders often make retitling a hassle. Insurance can also complicate it. A classic or high-value vehicle is the exception: it makes sense to include it. See [transferring a car, boat or RV after death](/learn/property-and-assets/transfer-a-car-boat-or-rv-after-death) for how the process runs.

## Can you put Social Security or a pension in a trust?

You cannot put the right to receive benefits in a trust. Social Security pays the person entitled to it, and federal law bars assigning those rights. The same goes for most pensions. What you can do is deposit the money you receive into a bank account titled in your trust's name. Some people do this to keep their funds in one place if a successor trustee must step in. A pension with a survivor option works through the plan's own beneficiary form.

## What about digital assets and out-of-state property?

Digital assets split into two groups. Things with a clear owner and value, like cryptocurrency, domain names and an online store, can be assigned to the trust. Accounts governed by terms of service, such as email, photo storage and social media, usually cannot be owned by a trust. The workaround is giving your trustee and your executor clear authority and a password plan. See [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).

Out-of-state real estate is the best argument for a trust. Without one, your family may face probate in your home state and a second, ancillary proceeding where the property sits; see [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate). A deed to the trust in that state's form ends the problem.

## What about assets you buy or inherit later?

A trust is not a one-time project. The house you buy in five years, the account you open at a new bank and the inheritance you receive from a parent all arrive in your name unless you take a step. Get in the habit of saying "in the name of the trust" when you open anything new. Banks and brokerages will ask for a certificate of trust, which is a short summary of the trust's existence and trustee powers, so you do not have to hand over the whole document. Keep a few copies signed and ready.

## Where do people go wrong?

- **Retitling a retirement account** because a salesperson said everything should be in the trust.
- **Putting a mortgaged house in and forgetting to tell the insurer,** which can leave a claim disputed.
- **Leaving a small account behind.** A forgotten $12,000 savings account can force a full probate, which the trust was meant to avoid. Small-estate shortcuts may help, but they vary by state.
- **Assuming a beneficiary form was updated** after a divorce or a death.
- **Treating the list as permanent.** Review it every few years, or when you move, marry, retire or sell something big. The [beneficiary audit tool](/tools/beneficiary-audit) helps with the forms.

## An example

Tom, 67, is widowed. He has a house, a lake cabin in a neighboring state, a checking account, $400,000 in an IRA, a brokerage account, a 2015 pickup truck and a life insurance policy. His list looks like this: both properties into the trust by deed; checking and brokerage retitled; IRA beneficiary form names his two daughters; the truck stays out; life insurance names the daughters directly. A pour-over will catches anything he forgets. After the deeds were recorded, he kept a one-page list of what the trust owns in the binder.
