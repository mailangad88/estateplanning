If you are pricing a living trust, you have probably seen numbers that range from under $100 to well over $10,000. Both ends are real. The gap comes from what is being sold: a blank form, a guided online questionnaire, or a plan drawn up and signed with an attorney who then helps you move your property into it.

This article gives typical national ranges, explains what moves a quote up or down, and lists the costs that rarely appear in the headline price. The ranges are general market figures, not a quote and not a price list. Fees vary by state, by county and by provider, so use these numbers to judge whether a quote is in a reasonable neighborhood, not to predict your own.

## What does a living trust usually cost?

A revocable living trust prepared by an attorney commonly costs somewhere between $1,500 and $5,000 for an ordinary individual or couple, with plans for larger or more complicated families costing more. Online services and template packages sit at the low end, often a few hundred dollars or less. Attorneys who bill by the hour will land wherever the hours take them, and hourly rates for estate planning commonly run from roughly $250 to $500 or more depending on the market.

Treat these as typical ranges only. Large metro areas tend to cost more than small towns. A single person with one house and a few accounts costs less to plan than a couple with rental property in two states and children from earlier marriages.

| Option | Typical upfront cost | What it usually covers | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blank template or book | Little to nothing | A form you fill in yourself | Wrong state formalities, no funding guidance, no advice |
| Online service | Roughly low hundreds of dollars | Guided questionnaire and generated documents | Does not check your family facts or your deeds and account titles |
| Attorney, flat fee | Often about $1,500 to $5,000 for ordinary plans | Tailored trust, pour-over will, powers of attorney, healthcare directives; sometimes funding help | Confirm what is excluded, such as deed preparation or recording |
| Attorney, hourly | Varies with hours; often similar to or above flat fees for complex plans | Whatever is billed | Cost is harder to predict without an estimate |
| Will only | Much lower than a trust | A will, sometimes with powers of attorney | Goes through probate; no incapacity management through a trust |

## What is usually included in the price?

A complete attorney package for a trust-based plan typically includes the trust itself and a set of companion documents. The exact list varies, so ask for it in writing.

- **The revocable trust agreement.** The core document that names you as trustee, your successor trustee and your beneficiaries.
- **A pour-over will.** A backup will that sweeps forgotten assets into the trust. See [pour-over wills](/learn/wills/pour-over-will).
- **A durable financial power of attorney.** So someone can manage assets that are not in the trust if you cannot. See [durable financial powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).
- **Healthcare directives.** A [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and living will.
- **Guardian nominations** for minor children, if relevant.
- **A signing meeting** with notary and witnesses as your state requires.

What is often priced separately: preparing and recording deeds, assignment documents for business interests, separate trusts for a child with special needs, and any tax planning beyond the basics.

## What makes a living trust cost more?

Complexity, not the size of the estate, drives most of the price. The same attorney may quote very different numbers for two clients with identical net worth. Common factors:

1. **Couples versus single people.** A couple using one joint trust usually costs somewhat more than an individual, but less than two separate plans. Separate trusts for each spouse, often used in blended families, cost more.
2. **Number and location of properties.** Each parcel needs a deed. Property in a second state may need a local attorney's input because deed formalities and recording rules differ. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).
3. **Family structure.** Children from earlier relationships, a stepchild you want to protect or exclude, or a child with a history of money trouble all call for more careful drafting. See [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage).
4. **Trusts within the trust.** Staged distributions for young beneficiaries, a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) for a disabled child, or a spendthrift provision add drafting time.
5. **Business interests.** An LLC, a partnership interest or a family company usually requires reviewing operating agreements and may involve a separate assignment.
6. **Tax planning.** Estates near a federal or state estate tax threshold need additional analysis. The federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families are well below it, but some states tax at lower levels.
7. **Funding help.** A provider who retitles accounts, drafts deeds and coordinates with banks and brokerages charges more than one who hands you instructions.

## What costs do people forget?

The drafting fee is the visible part. The following arrive later, and a good quote will tell you about them in advance.

- **Recording fees.** A county recorder usually charges a fee per deed, commonly in the range of tens of dollars, sometimes more, and a few places add per-page charges or surcharges. A transfer into your own revocable trust is often exempt from transfer taxes, but the rules vary by state and county. See [how to transfer a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).
- **Your own time.** Funding means calls to banks, brokerages and insurers, and each has its own forms. Plan on several hours spread over weeks.
- **Updates.** Life changes such as divorce, a death, a move or a new property mean amendments. Some firms include a review period, and others charge for changes.
- **Professional trustee fees.** If you name a bank or trust company as successor trustee, it will charge an ongoing fee, commonly a percentage of assets under management, which the trust document should address.
- **Costs at death.** Trust administration is a real process, with notices, accountings and asset transfers. See [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).

### What happens if the trust is not funded?

An unfunded trust is the most expensive mistake in this area. Suppose Dennis, 66, pays a low price for an online trust, signs it, and never changes the deed to his house. When he dies, the house is still in his name, so it goes through probate anyway, and his family has paid for a trust that did not do its main job. The reverse also happens: a trust prepared by an attorney who never helps with the deed can fail the same way. That is why the question to ask any provider is not just "what does the trust cost" but "who retitles my house and accounts, and is that in the price?" The mechanics are in [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## Is a living trust cheaper than probate?

Sometimes, but not always. Probate costs vary enormously by state. Some states use statutory fee schedules that rise with the size of the estate, so a home worth $600,000 can produce attorney and executor fees in the tens of thousands. Other states have simple, low-cost processes, and a small estate may cost only a few thousand dollars. Our article on [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) explains the components.

A fair break-even comparison looks at four things:

- The probate cost in your state for an estate like yours.
- How many properties you own, because each out-of-state property can trigger a separate [ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate).
- How long your family could wait. Probate time is a cost too; see [how long probate takes](/learn/probate/how-long-does-probate-take).
- The value of privacy and incapacity planning, which a trust offers and a will does not.

If you live in a state with a streamlined probate process and your assets mostly pass by beneficiary designation, the trust may not pay for itself in dollars. Other tools such as [transfer on death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed) can sometimes do part of the job at lower cost. See [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) for the full set of options.

## How do you compare quotes?

Ask each provider the same questions and write down the answers.

1. Is the price flat or hourly, and is it in writing?
2. Which documents are included? Pour-over will, powers of attorney, healthcare directives?
3. Does the price cover deeds and recording, or only the trust document?
4. Who will meet with me, and who will answer questions after signing?
5. What happens if my family situation changes in the next two years?
6. Is a review of my beneficiary designations included?

For a longer list, see [how to choose an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney). For the cost of the whole plan, not just the trust, see [how much does estate planning cost](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost).

## Does a living trust have ongoing tax or filing costs?

Not usually while you are alive. A revocable trust is generally treated as a "grantor trust" for income tax purposes, which means you report its income on your own return and the trust typically does not file a separate one. The IRS discusses grantor trust reporting in the instructions for Form 1041 at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1041). After death the trust becomes irrevocable and may need its own tax identification number and returns, which belongs to the administration stage rather than the creation price.
