Choosing between a will and a living trust is one of the first decisions in an estate plan, and most explanations make it sound like a contest. In practice the answer depends on four things: what you own, where you live, who is depending on you, and how much privacy and control you want.

This article walks through the differences, shows how the choice plays out for a few typical families, and gives you a way to decide. For background on each tool, see our guides to [wills](/learn/wills) and [trusts](/learn/trusts).

## What is the main difference between a will and a trust?

A will is a set of instructions that a court reviews after you die. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is a holding arrangement that starts working as soon as it is funded and continues after your death without a court case.

| Question | Will | Living trust |
|---|---|---|
| When does it take effect? | At death | When signed and funded |
| Does it go through [probate](/glossary/probate)? | Yes | Not for assets in the trust |
| Does it cover incapacity? | No | Yes, a successor trustee can step in |
| Is it public? | Yes, once filed with the court | No |
| Can it name a guardian for minor children? | Yes | No |
| Can it control timing of inheritance? | Yes, through a testamentary trust | Yes, directly |
| Upfront cost | Lower | Higher |
| Ongoing work | Minimal | Must retitle new assets |
| Protects from your creditors? | No | No (if revocable) |

Notice that the two are not rivals. The things a trust cannot do, like naming guardians, a will can, and the things a will cannot do, like covering your incapacity, a trust can.

## When is a will enough?

A will, combined with beneficiary designations and a power of attorney, can be a sound plan when:

- Your assets are modest or mostly pass by beneficiary designation (retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death bank accounts).
- You own no real estate, or your state allows a simple [transfer-on-death deed](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).
- Your state's probate process is quick and inexpensive, or a [small estate procedure](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit) would apply.
- Your wishes are straightforward, such as everything to a spouse, then to children equally.

Consider Daniel and Priya, both 34, with a toddler, a rented apartment, term life insurance and 401(k)s. Each names the other as beneficiary, and their kids as backups. A will naming each other as executor and a guardian for their daughter, plus powers of attorney and healthcare directives, covers what matters. A trust adds cost without solving a problem they have yet. Our guide to [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) lays out this starter set.

## When does a trust make more sense?

A trust earns its cost when one or more of the following applies.

### You own real estate

A house is usually the largest asset that must go through probate if it is titled in your name alone. If the property is in a different state from where you live, your family may face two probate cases, which a trust can avoid. See [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

### You want a plan for incapacity

If you develop dementia or have a stroke, a funded trust lets a successor trustee manage trust property immediately. A power of attorney does similar work for assets outside the trust, but some banks and title companies treat a trust more smoothly. Read more in our [durable power of attorney guide](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney).

### You want to control how heirs receive money

Leaving $300,000 outright to a 19-year-old is rarely what a parent has in mind. A trust can hold the money until set ages or pay for specific purposes. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children). A trust can also protect an heir who is in an unstable marriage, has creditors, or has a disability; see [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) and [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).

### Your family is blended

When you want a second spouse housed and supported for life while your children from a first marriage eventually inherit, a trust enforces that structure. A will can try, but a trust is generally more reliable and private. Learn more in our guide to [blended families](/learn/blended-families).

### You value privacy

Anyone can look up a probated will. If you do not want your children, charities or business interests visible to the public, a trust keeps the details private.

## What about a will and a trust together?

Most trust-based plans use both. The trust holds your house and accounts. A [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) says that anything left outside the trust at death goes into it. If a forgotten asset exceeds your state's small estate limit it will still go through probate, but it ends up governed by the trust's terms.

A typical package also includes a financial power of attorney and a healthcare directive. Those documents cover decisions the trust does not, like medical care.

Your will is also the document where you name guardians. A trust cannot appoint someone to raise your children, so see [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) before you sign.

## How do state rules change the answer?

Probate varies a great deal. Some states have streamlined procedures and low fees; others are slower, with court-set percentages or lengthy creditor periods. A few states, including some with a version of the Uniform Probate Code, make informal probate fairly inexpensive. Where probate is easy, the argument for a trust is weaker, and where it is costly, stronger. Check your [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) (Florida is one example) or ask an attorney how local probate really works. Our overview of [probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) shows what drives them.

## A decision checklist

Answer yes or no:

1. Do I own real estate (especially more than one property or property in another state)?
2. Would my family be harmed if no one could legally manage my money during an illness?
3. Do I have a beneficiary who should not receive money outright?
4. Is my family blended, or are there relatives likely to dispute things?
5. Is privacy important to me?
6. Do I expect my assets to pass mostly through probate rather than by beneficiary designation?

If you answered yes to two or more, a trust deserves a serious look. If you answered no to nearly all, a will-based plan is probably sufficient for now. Revisit the question after major events such as a home purchase, a new child, an inheritance, or a move; see [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## What does each approach cost?

A will-based plan typically costs less than a trust-based plan, because the trust document is longer and funding takes time. Compare that with probate costs your family may face later, which depend on your state and estate size. Our guide to [estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost) explains flat fees, hourly rates and DIY risks. If you do choose a trust, expect to spend time [funding it](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust), because an unfunded trust does not do its job. For typical price ranges, see [how much a living trust costs](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost) and [how much a will costs](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost); to test your own situation, try [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust).
