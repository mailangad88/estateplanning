Trusts get described as permanent, as though once signed, the arrangement runs on its own indefinitely. The truth is more varied. A trust is a tool with a lifespan, and the right lifespan depends on what you want it to do.

If you are writing a trust, you will be asked when it should end. If you are a beneficiary, you may wonder how long you must wait. This page covers the usual timelines, the law that puts an outer limit on them, and the ways a trust can close early.

## The short version

- Most trusts built to avoid probate finish their work within one to two years after death.
- Trusts that hold money for children usually run until the child reaches certain ages or for life.
- State law sets an outer boundary through the rule against perpetuities, and the boundary varies a great deal.
- A trust ends by its own terms, by distribution of everything, by agreement and court approval, or because it became too small to keep.
- Longer terms mean more yearly cost and more reliance on future trustees.

## How long does a typical living trust last?

Think of a living trust in two phases. During your life you are usually the trustee, and the trust may exist for decades without anything visible happening. After your death, the trustee pays final bills, handles taxes and distributes what is left.

For a simple trust where everything goes outright to adult children, the second phase is short. A successor trustee might need six months to a year to settle accounts, sell a house, file the final returns and distribute. The [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration) page lists the steps, and the [how long a trustee has to distribute](/learn/after-a-death/how-long-does-a-trustee-have-to-distribute-assets) page covers timing and the point where beneficiaries can press for answers.

Some trusts hold on to money for a purpose. Imagine Carol, 72, who leaves her estate in trust for her son Jim, 41, and his two teenagers. Her trust says: pay Jim's health and education needs now, give him half at 50 and the rest at 60, and keep each grandchild's share in trust until age 30. That plan could last more than 20 years after Carol's death. For ideas on staging, see [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).

## What does the rule against perpetuities do?

The rule against perpetuities stops property from being tied up forever by a person who has died. In its classic English form, a future interest must become certain within the lifetime of someone alive when the trust began, plus 21 years. That is a "life in being plus 21 years" test, and it is why trusts for grandchildren and great-grandchildren sometimes include a savings clause naming a period after which the trust must end.

States have taken different paths:

| Approach | What it means | Effect on a trust |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional rule | Life in being plus 21 years | Trust must vest or end within that window |
| Wait-and-see with a fixed period | Often about 90 years, measured from creation | Longer reach, easier to draft |
| Opt-out or abolition for trusts | The rule does not apply if the trust says so | A trust can last for generations, subject to other law |
| Charitable trusts | Often treated differently | May last far longer |

If you hear that your state has no limit, that is a feature of its trust law, and not every use of that is wise. A dynasty trust with no end date may need a series of trustees across a century, and the family's needs change over time. The [trust names explained](/learn/trusts/trust-names-explained-dynasty-credit-shelter-family-and-more) page describes dynasty trusts and how they relate to federal rules like the [generation-skipping transfer tax](/glossary/generation-skipping-transfer-tax).

A trust can also be governed by the law of a different state than the one where you live, if the connection is real. Your attorney should tell you which state's perpetuities rule applies to the document you sign.

## What are the ways a trust ends?

Most trusts end in one of these ways.

1. **By its own terms.** The trust says it ends on a date, at a beneficiary's age, or at a death, and the trustee distributes the property.
2. **By complete distribution.** Once the trustee has paid out everything, there is nothing left to hold, and the trustee files a final accounting and closes the trust.
3. **When the purpose is accomplished or becomes impossible.** A trust for a child's college costs has done its job once the degrees are paid for.
4. **By revocation, if the trust is revocable.** The grantor can cancel it during life. The [how to change or revoke a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-change-or-revoke-a-living-trust) page covers the steps.
5. **By agreement of beneficiaries and a court.** If everyone with an interest agrees, a court can often approve ending the trust. Courts are more careful if a material purpose, such as a spendthrift protection, would be defeated.
6. **Because it is too small.** Many states allow a trustee to terminate a trust whose value no longer justifies the cost of running it, subject to a dollar limit that varies by state.
7. **By merger.** If one person ends up as the sole trustee and sole beneficiary, the trust may merge and end.

## Can a trust be changed or ended if circumstances change?

An irrevocable trust is hard to change, but not always frozen. Several tools exist, and which ones you have depends on how the trust was drafted and what your state allows.

- **A trust protector.** Some trusts name a person with power to adjust administrative terms or move the trust to a new state. See the [trust protector and decanting](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-trust-protector-and-decanting) page.
- **Decanting.** The trustee pours the assets into a new trust with updated terms, where state law allows it.
- **A court order.** A judge may modify a trust when unexpected events make the old terms impractical or harmful.
- **Nonjudicial agreements.** In some states, the trustee and beneficiaries can sign an agreement that adjusts non-essential terms without going to court.

None of these is a free pass. The more a change cuts against the grantor's stated goals, the more likely a court is to say no. If you are the grantor, build flexibility in at the start. The [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) page explains why that matters.

## What should you consider before choosing a long term?

Longer is not always safer. Ask these questions before you tell your attorney "keep it in trust until she is 40."

- **Is there a real reason?** A beneficiary with a history of debt, a disability, a second marriage or a business with creditor risk gives a reason. A general nervousness about money may not.
- **Who will serve?** A trust that lasts 25 years will likely outlive its first trustee. Name successors, and think about a professional backup.
- **What will it cost?** Each year brings a tax return, record keeping and sometimes fees. The [trustee compensation](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation) page explains how fees work.
- **How will the beneficiary feel?** A 45-year-old who must ask a sibling for money may resent the arrangement. Pair control with a sensible release schedule.
- **What happens at the end?** Include a final backup beneficiary so the trust does not stall if everyone named has died.

A flexible approach helps. Many trusts give the trustee discretion to distribute earlier for health, education, a home purchase or a business, and to end the trust if it becomes uneconomical. The [trust distribution standards](/learn/trusts/trust-distribution-standards-hems-and-discretion) page shows how those standards read.

## What does this look like in Illinois and New Jersey?

Both states follow the general pattern: a trust runs under its terms and the governing law's outer time limit. Questions about how long your trust may last, and whether your state limits the term, belong with the drafting attorney. The [Illinois living trusts](/learn/illinois/illinois-living-trusts) and [New Jersey living trusts](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey) pages cover points that are particular to each state, including how trustees give notice and account.
