When someone you love dies and left a living trust, there is usually a short stretch of confusion about who is in charge and what happens next. The trust does not vanish, and it does not need a judge to start working. It changes character, and a person named in the document takes over.

This page follows a living trust through the first year from the family's point of view. The trustee's detailed duties are in our guide to [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration). For how the document works while its maker is alive, see the [revocable living trust guide](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) and our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## The short version

- The trust becomes irrevocable at death. No one can freely change it afterward.
- The successor trustee takes over, with a death certificate and the trust document.
- The trustee collects assets, pays bills and taxes, and keeps records.
- Beneficiaries are told, and usually can ask for the terms that affect them.
- Distribution happens when the debts and tax issues are under control, not before.

## What changes the moment the person dies?

Two things. The trust stops being revocable, and the successor trustee gains power the grantor used to hold. A revocable trust could be rewritten at will. After death, nobody can reopen the terms except through a court or, in some states, a narrow statutory process. The beneficiaries named in the document are what they are.

The trust also gets its own identity for tax purposes. While the grantor lived, it usually used the grantor's Social Security number. Now the trustee needs an employer identification number from the IRS, and there is a timing choice for the trust's tax year, explained in our article on the [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election).

## What happens in the first few weeks?

Think of this as the stabilizing stage. Nothing is distributed yet.

1. **Death certificates.** The trustee orders certified copies, since banks, insurers and the county will each want one.
2. **Find the documents.** The signed trust and every amendment, a pour-over will, and a list of assets if there is one.
3. **Secure property.** Change locks if needed, confirm homeowner's insurance covers a vacant house, and forward mail.
4. **Read the trust.** The trustee should know who is a beneficiary, who is not, and what limits apply before telling anyone anything.
5. **Notify.** Many states require the trustee to send formal notice to beneficiaries and heirs within a set number of weeks or months after learning of the death, and the notice often starts a clock for challenges.
6. **Get the tax ID and open a trust account.** Money collected goes into a new account in the trust's name.

For the broader checklist covering the household side, including funeral, utilities and benefits, use [what to do when someone dies](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

## Do you still need probate?

Not for what the trust owns. This is the main reason people create one. A house deeded to the trust passes under the trust's terms without court supervision, the successor trustee can sell it, and the buyer's title company will rely on a certification of trust.

Probate may still be needed for anything left outside the trust and not set up to pass by beneficiary form. Marcus, 74, signed a trust and deeded his house, but his brokerage account stayed in his own name. His daughter, as trustee, handles the house easily, then has to open a probate case for the brokerage account so the pour-over will can move it into the trust. The extra step is why [funding the trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) matters, and why [an unfunded trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) disappoints families.

Retirement accounts and life insurance are different again. They pass by beneficiary designation to whoever is named, whether or not a trust exists. Review [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) if the trust is on a form.

## What does the trustee do over the next few months?

The work is mostly paperwork, and it follows a rhythm.

| Stage | Typical activity |
|---|---|
| Months 1-2 | Notices sent, tax ID obtained, assets located and valued as of the date of death |
| Months 2-6 | Bills and final expenses paid, house maintained or listed, claims period runs |
| Months 4-9 | Final income tax return for the person, and the trust's first return, are prepared |
| Months 6-12 | Distributions made, often in stages, with a reserve held back for taxes and costs |
| After distribution | Final accounting, receipts from beneficiaries, trust closed |

A trust that continues, such as one for a surviving spouse or for a child until age 30, does not close after the first year. The trustee keeps managing and reporting. See [how long a trustee has to distribute assets](/learn/after-a-death/how-long-does-a-trustee-have-to-distribute-assets) for what slows things down.

Assets are generally valued as of the date of death, and most receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), which can erase capital gains on appreciation during the person's life. That helps whoever later sells inherited property, and it is one more reason the trustee should get date-of-death values on the house and accounts.

## What can beneficiaries expect and ask for?

Beneficiaries are owed honest information. A reasonable list of expectations:

- A notice telling you the trust exists, who the trustee is and how to contact them.
- A copy of the trust terms that affect you, in many states.
- Updates, and often an annual accounting while the trust is open.
- Equal fairness among beneficiaries as the document states, without favoritism.
- Payment, after the debts, taxes and costs have been paid.

You are not owed the trustee's immediate decisions, and you cannot demand an early distribution if the trust is slow because of a legitimate reason. If the trustee goes silent or acts badly, [what to do when a trustee does nothing](/learn/after-a-death/when-a-trustee-does-nothing) covers your options.

## What happens to debts and taxes?

The person's valid debts and taxes come first. A trust does not hide anything from creditors, and the trustee has to make provision for them before distribution. Last-year income tax, any state or federal estate tax, and final medical bills are common examples. Federal estate tax is only an issue above $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most families will not face it, though a few states have their own death taxes at lower levels. Trustees who pay beneficiaries too fast can be personally responsible for the shortfall, which is why a good one holds a reserve.

## How state law changes this

Notice deadlines, the length of a creditor claims period, what a trustee must disclose and how long beneficiaries have to challenge the trust all vary by state. In some places the trustee must file a certain notice with the court, in others nothing at all. Start with your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois), and if you are the person making a trust, ask your attorney how your state works so your family knows the rules in advance.
