Rosa signed her living trust last month. The attorney's packet included the trust, a pour-over will, powers of attorney, and two papers she did not quite understand: a page called Schedule A and a short document titled "General Assignment of Personal Property." She wonders whether they matter, or whether they are filler.

They matter, in different ways. This page explains what each does, what neither does, and how to keep them useful over time. It is part of our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts), and it sits next to the larger question of [what assets go in a living trust](/learn/trusts/what-assets-go-in-a-living-trust).

## What is Schedule A?

Schedule A is a list of assets that you, as the person creating the trust, transfer into it. It is attached to the back of the trust document. A typical Schedule A names the house by address, bank accounts by institution and last four digits, brokerage accounts, and sometimes a note that all tangible personal property is assigned.

The list serves three purposes. It tells the successor trustee what you meant to include. It gives a bank or a court a written record of your intent. And it gives you a checklist to work from while you retitle things. What it does not do, by itself, is change who legally owns an asset. Banks and county land records do not read your trust's attachments. They read their own records.

## What is a general assignment?

A general assignment of personal property is a signed statement that transfers everything you own that has no title document into the trust. That includes furniture, clothing, jewelry, art, tools, collectibles, household goods and, in many documents, other assets with no formal title. You, as owner, assign them to yourself as trustee.

The assignment solves a practical problem. A couch does not have a deed. Nobody is going to retitle a set of dishes. Without an assignment, those items sit outside the trust, and if you die, they pass under your will, which means probate for items that are often modest in value but sentimental. A pour-over will, covered in our [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) article, eventually sends them to the trust, but only after the court process the trust was meant to avoid.

A good assignment covers property you own now and property you acquire later. It also has a short list of exclusions, such as titled vehicles and retirement accounts, which need separate treatment.

## Which assets does the assignment not cover?

The assignment handles things that have no paper title. Everything else has its own transfer step, and Schedule A does not replace it.

| Asset | How it gets into the trust | Does Schedule A or the assignment do it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| House or land | Signed, notarized and recorded deed | No |
| Bank and credit union accounts | Retitle with the bank, using a certification of trust | No |
| Brokerage account | New account or retitle with the broker | No |
| Car, truck, boat | Title change with the motor vehicle agency, or leave out | No |
| Furniture, jewelry, art, tools | General assignment | Yes |
| Retirement accounts (IRA, 401(k)) | Do not retitle; name beneficiaries instead | No |
| Life insurance | Change owner or beneficiary by form | No |
| Business interest or LLC | Assignment of interest, often with company consent | Partly |

Retirement accounts deserve a warning. Retitling an IRA to a trust can be treated as a withdrawal, which makes the whole balance taxable at once. The usual approach is to keep the account in your name and name a beneficiary, possibly the trust, using the rules in our guide to [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

## An example with real-looking numbers

Rosa's trust owns, after signing, only what her paperwork actually moved. Here is her inventory, in an invented example.

- House worth $420,000: deeded to the trust by a recorded deed. In the trust.
- Checking account of $18,000: retitled to the trust. In the trust.
- Savings account of $60,000: still in her name, because she forgot. Not in the trust.
- Furniture, jewelry and art, roughly $25,000: covered by the general assignment. In the trust.
- IRA of $210,000: has a named beneficiary. Outside the trust by design.
- Car worth $14,000: left in her name, with a plan to handle it separately.

If Rosa died tomorrow, the savings account and the car would need probate unless a small estate procedure applied, even though Schedule A lists the savings account. The list showed her intention. The bank never saw it. That gap is the most common failure in do-it-yourself trusts and the subject of our article on [telling whether your trust is actually funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded).

## How do you use Schedule A well?

Treat it as a working document, not a one-time form.

1. **Start with what you own.** Write down each account, property and policy.
2. **Sort each into a path.** Deed, bank form, assignment, beneficiary designation or leave out.
3. **Do the retitling.** Use the steps in [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
4. **Update Schedule A to match reality.** List what is actually in the trust, with account numbers masked.
5. **Date each revision.** Sign and date the new schedule and keep older versions.
6. **Review every year or two.** Add new accounts, remove closed ones.

A mismatch is a warning. If Schedule A lists something that is not in the trust, either retitle it or take it off the list. If something is in the trust and missing from the schedule, add it. Either error confuses a successor trustee years later.

## What about sentimental items?

The assignment transfers ownership, but it does not decide who gets Grandma's ring. That is a separate choice, made in the trust's distribution terms, in a signed list of gifts, or in a letter to the family. Our article on [leaving jewelry, heirlooms and sentimental items](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-jewelry-heirlooms-and-sentimental-items) covers how to make those wishes stick. Be aware that a casual handwritten list, with no signature or reference in the trust, may be treated as a wish rather than a command.

## Does an assignment work in Illinois and New Jersey?

Yes, as a way to move personal property you own. Both states let you transfer personal property into your own revocable trust by a written declaration or assignment, and attorneys in both routinely use one. What differs is the surrounding paperwork: Illinois land is moved by a recorded deed, and a New Jersey deed needs the state's transfer-related forms, as explained in our article on [transferring a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust). If you move to the other state later, the assignment stays valid, though a quick review is wise. See [living trusts and property in another state](/learn/trusts/living-trusts-and-moving-or-owning-property-in-another-state).

Keep in mind that an assignment of ordinary household goods rarely matters to a creditor or the IRS. Its real value is quiet: the family does not need a court order to give away Mom's dresser, and the trustee can act the day after a death.

## What mistakes do our attorneys see?

- **Assuming the list is the transfer.** A Schedule A that names a house, without a deed, leaves the house outside the trust.
- **Assigning retirement accounts.** This can create an unexpected tax bill.
- **Listing account numbers in full.** Schedule A can end up in the hands of many people. Use the institution name and the last four digits.
- **Never updating it.** A schedule from ten years ago names accounts that no longer exist.
- **Forgetting the assignment.** Some online forms leave it out, so the household goods fall to probate.
