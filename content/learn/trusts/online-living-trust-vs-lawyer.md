You can buy a living trust online in an evening for a few hundred dollars. A lawyer will quote you more, and give you a longer to-do list. The question most people ask is whether the cheaper route is actually fine. For some families it is. For others it quietly fails, and the failure does not surface until the trustee or the family finds out.

This comparison, part of our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts), explains where online trusts work, where they break, how to tell a real educational seminar from a sales pitch, and how to decide. For price ranges, read the companion page on [what a living trust costs](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost).

## Is an online living trust legally valid?

A trust is valid in nearly every state if it is in writing, shows your intent to create it, names a trustee and beneficiaries, and is signed as your state requires. Many states ask for a notary. A trust that checks those boxes is not invalid just because software produced it.

Validity is a low bar, though. A valid trust can still do the wrong thing. It might name your estranged brother as backup trustee because the form had a default, or it might split everything equally when you wanted a house to go to the child who lives in it. The law will enforce what the document says, not what you meant.

## What do online services do well?

- **Speed and price.** You can finish a basic document in an hour.
- **Plain forms for simple cases.** One person, no real estate or one house, adult children, no unusual wishes.
- **A starting structure.** For someone who has done nothing, a basic plan beats no plan.

Honest providers say they are not law firms and cannot give legal advice. That disclaimer matters. A form asks "Who should be successor trustee?" It does not ask whether your daughter and your son get along.

## Where do DIY and online trusts go wrong?

| Problem | What happens | Who finds out |
|---|---|---|
| Never funded | House and accounts stay in your name; probate is still needed | Family, after death |
| Wrong deed form | Title company refuses it, or the transfer is defective | The buyer or title agent, years later |
| Missing incapacity language | Successor trustee cannot act quickly | Family, in a crisis |
| Generic distribution terms | Child receives a lump sum at 18 or 21 | The child |
| Beneficiary forms contradict the trust | Retirement account goes to an ex or a minor | Executor |
| Wrong state's law | Clauses that do nothing locally | A judge |
| No pour-over will | Forgotten assets drift to intestacy | Family |

The first row dwarfs the others. People sign the trust and feel finished. A trust that owns nothing does not avoid probate; see [what happens if you never funded your trust](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) and the [funding guide](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## How do online trusts compare with hiring a lawyer?

| | Online service | Estate planning attorney |
|---|---|---|
| Typical upfront cost | Lowest | Higher, often quoted as a flat fee |
| Advice on your facts | No | Yes |
| Deeds and retitling | You do it | Usually included or guided |
| State-specific terms | Template for your state | Drafted for your state and your family |
| Review of beneficiary forms | Not included | Part of a coordinated plan |
| Help if something goes wrong | Support chat | Someone who knows the file |
| Best fit | One simple situation | Real estate, blended family, a child with needs, a business |

Neither column is a verdict. A 28-year-old with a studio apartment and a 401(k) does not need a $4,000 trust, and might not need a trust at all; our page on [do I need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust) walks through that.

## When is DIY a mistake?

Hire an attorney, rather than a form, if any of these is true:

1. You own real estate, especially in more than one state.
2. You have a second marriage, stepchildren, or a child you want to treat differently.
3. A beneficiary has a disability or receives benefits; see [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).
4. You own a business or an LLC.
5. Your estate may face federal estate tax (above $15,000,000 per person in 2026) or a state estate tax.
6. A relative is likely to object, so the trust needs to hold up; see [contesting a trust](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested).
7. You want Medicaid planning, which is a different field with its own rules; see [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

Rosa, 71, bought a trust package online after her husband died. She skipped the deed because it looked intimidating. When she died four years later, her house sat in her own name. Her son spent a year in probate for a house that the trust was supposed to protect. A one-hour appointment with a lawyer to record the deed would have solved it.

## Are living trust seminars and sales pitches legitimate?

Some are. Many are marketing events. The pitch usually runs like this: probate is a nightmare, a living trust fixes it, today's price is special. The features of a pitch to be careful about include:

- Pressure to sign the same day or the same week.
- A package price that rises if you ask for changes.
- A claim that a trust saves estate taxes or protects against nursing home costs. A revocable trust does neither on its own; see [does a trust avoid taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes).
- A salesperson who is not an attorney offering to draft the documents.
- A reluctance to let you take the documents to your own attorney.

Check your state bar's website to confirm that the person drafting is licensed in your state. If you are told you must buy something to avoid "losing your house," slow down. Probate rules and costs differ widely, and for many modest estates a will plus beneficiary designations is enough; the [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) gives a ballpark.

## What should you check before you sign an online trust?

Read the successor trustee section twice. Look for who takes over if your first choice cannot serve, and whether the document says what happens when you are incapacitated. Check the distribution section for ages; a default of outright payment at 18 is common in templates and rarely what a parent wants. Confirm there is a pour-over will, and that you know where the signed originals will be kept. Finally, ask yourself who will do the retitling and by what date. If the honest answer is "probably nobody," the trust is likely to fail.

## What is a middle path?

You can use a lawyer for the parts that carry risk and handle the paperwork yourself. Some clients ask an attorney to review a document they drafted online. Others pay a flat fee for the trust and do their own account retitling with a checklist. A review is much cheaper than a full engagement, and it catches the structural mistakes.
