Leaving an inheritance outright is the simplest approach, and for many families it is perfectly fine. It is less fine when a child is in a shaky marriage, is facing lawsuits or debt, struggles with spending or addiction, or works in a profession with liability risk. In those cases the inheritance can disappear within months. A spendthrift trust is the standard way to keep it protected.

This article explains how spendthrift clauses work, how much protection they offer, the exceptions, and how they differ from asset protection trusts that people create for themselves. You can find the broader context in our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts).

## What is a spendthrift trust?

A spendthrift trust is any trust that contains a [spendthrift clause](/glossary/spendthrift-clause). The clause does two things:

1. It bars the beneficiary from voluntarily transferring or pledging their interest in the trust, for example by selling the right to future payments or using it as collateral.
2. It bars most creditors from forcing a transfer of that interest, such as by garnishing future distributions.

The money belongs to the trust until the trustee decides to pay it. A creditor with a judgment against the beneficiary generally cannot demand that the trustee turn it over. Once the trustee pays money to the beneficiary, though, it is the beneficiary's money, and creditors can pursue it.

Because the person who creates the trust (the [grantor](/glossary/grantor)) decides to restrict someone else's access, this is sometimes called a third-party trust. Almost every state enforces spendthrift clauses in this setting.

## How does discretion strengthen protection?

Spendthrift trusts fall along a spectrum:

| Trust type | Beneficiary rights | Level of protection |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed payments (e.g., income to the beneficiary each year) | Can demand required payments | Moderate; payments due may be reachable once owed |
| Discretionary distributions | Cannot demand anything | Stronger; nothing is owed until the trustee decides |
| Hybrid with standards (e.g., health, education, support) | Can ask, trustee decides within standards | Strong, and flexible |

A fully discretionary trust gives the strongest shield because a creditor can only reach what the beneficiary has a right to receive. That is why many attorneys draft heirs' trusts with a spendthrift clause and discretion. The trustee can still pay bills directly to vendors, such as a landlord or a tuition office, which keeps money away from creditors altogether.

## Who benefits from this kind of trust?

- **A beneficiary with creditor or lawsuit exposure,** such as a physician, a business owner or a contractor.
- **A beneficiary in a troubled marriage,** since a lifetime trust can keep an inheritance separate from marital property. Courts may still count the beneficiary's access to the trust when setting support, so results vary.
- **A beneficiary with addiction or spending problems,** where discretion lets the trustee pay for housing and treatment but not cash.
- **A beneficiary with a disability.** A [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) is a specialized form that also protects benefits eligibility.
- **A young adult,** where the same concept applies; see [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).
- **A surviving spouse in a second marriage,** where a trust can protect the principal for children from an earlier marriage. See [protecting children from a first marriage](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage).

## An example

Marcus, 66, has two adult children. Lena is a pediatric surgeon with malpractice exposure and a stable marriage. Tyrell has had two business failures and owes the IRS back taxes. Marcus leaves each child's share in a separate lifetime trust, with a spendthrift clause and an independent trustee. Lena can serve as her own co-trustee for flexibility, with an independent co-trustee for discretionary decisions. Tyrell's trust is fully discretionary and the trustee pays his rent and health insurance directly. A federal tax lien may still reach what Tyrell actually receives, which is one reason Marcus and his attorney discussed the exceptions before signing.

## What are the exceptions?

A spendthrift clause is strong but not absolute. Common exceptions, which vary by state, include:

- Child support and sometimes alimony
- Federal tax liens and some other government claims
- Claims for necessary goods or services in some states, such as a nursing home or hospital
- Claims by the trustee or by someone who preserved the trust property
- Tort judgments, in a few states
- Money that has already been distributed to the beneficiary

Ask an attorney how your state treats each category, and do not rely on general descriptions for a specific decision.

## What if a beneficiary controls their own trust?

Many parents want adult children to serve as their own trustee eventually. That is possible, but the protection weakens if the beneficiary can take the money whenever they want. A common compromise is for the beneficiary to serve as co-trustee with an independent trustee, with the beneficiary's powers limited to a standard such as health, education, maintenance and support, which is tax-friendly and creditor-resistant in most states. Review [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) for the tradeoffs.

## How are asset protection trusts different?

A spendthrift trust protects an inheritance you leave to someone else. An **asset protection trust** is generally meant to protect your own assets from your own future creditors, which is a much harder legal problem. Three points matter:

1. **Most states do not protect a self-settled trust.** If you can benefit from a trust you funded, the general rule is that your creditors can reach it.
2. **Some states authorize domestic asset protection trusts** (DAPTs) under strict conditions, such as an independent trustee, a waiting period, and limits on what you can keep. Other states and federal courts may not honor them, especially if you live elsewhere.
3. **Transfers made to avoid known creditors can be reversed** as fraudulent transfers. These trusts must be created well before a problem arises.

A DAPT is an [irrevocable trust](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) with complex tradeoffs, and is typically considered only for people with significant assets and real liability exposure. For long-term care planning, a different structure called a Medicaid asset protection trust is used; see [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## What should the trust document include?

1. A clear spendthrift clause that meets your state's requirements
2. A distribution standard and who decides
3. Authority for the trustee to pay vendors and others directly rather than the beneficiary
4. An independent trustee or co-trustee, with a way to replace a trustee who fails
5. A statement of what happens at the beneficiary's death (to children, siblings or other heirs)
6. Language addressing divorce, bankruptcy and guardianship of the beneficiary
7. A purpose statement or letter of wishes explaining why you set it up

Trustees have [fiduciary duties](/glossary/fiduciary-duty) to follow these instructions, and beneficiaries have legal recourse if they do not.

## How do beneficiary designations tie in?

A trust only protects assets that reach it. Life insurance, IRAs and 401(k)s pass by beneficiary designation, so the forms must name the trust. Retirement accounts need careful language to keep favorable payout rules; see [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary). If you hold property in your own revocable trust, the trust can create these protected subtrusts at your death; see [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).

State law on spendthrift clauses, support claims and trust duration varies. A state-specific starting point is our [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) for Florida, one of many; ask an attorney about the rules where the beneficiary lives.
