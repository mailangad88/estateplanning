You decide to get a living trust, and the first question is when it will actually be done. The honest answer has two parts. The document itself takes a few weeks. The part that makes the trust work takes longer, and it depends mostly on you and on a handful of institutions you do not control.

Here is a stage-by-stage timeline, what typically slows each stage, and how to shorten it.

## How long does each stage take?

A typical timeline for a married couple in Illinois or New Jersey with a house, bank accounts and retirement accounts looks like this. These are planning ranges based on how the work usually flows, not promises. A complicated family or an out-of-state property stretches them.

| Stage | What happens | Typical time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Intake | Questionnaire, asset list, first meeting | 1 to 2 weeks to schedule and complete |
| 2. Design | Decisions on trustees, shares, guardians, trust terms | A meeting or two, a week or more for you to decide |
| 3. Drafting | Attorney prepares trust, pour-over will, powers of attorney, health care directives | 1 to 2 weeks |
| 4. Review | You read the drafts, ask questions, corrections are made | 1 week, sometimes more |
| 5. Signing | Meeting with notary and witnesses | One sitting |
| 6. Funding | Deeds recorded, accounts retitled, beneficiaries updated | 2 to 8 weeks |

Add them up and you get roughly one to three months from first call to a funded plan. Most of that is waiting, not work.

## What slows down drafting?

Drafting slows when the answers are not ready. An attorney can write a trust in a few hours once the facts are known, but the facts are the hard part. The three questions that stall people most:

- **Who is the successor trustee?** Many people have not decided between a spouse, an adult child, or a professional, and they do not want to choose among their children. Our page on [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) helps with the decision.
- **How should the children receive money?** Outright at a certain age, or held in trust longer? Couples often disagree and need a conversation at home.
- **Who is the guardian for minor children?** The trust does not name one, but it is written at the same time, and the conversation about it can open old family tensions.

A good first-meeting habit is to bring a one-page list: your assets with approximate values, the full legal names of your family, and your first and second choices for each role. The [consultation checklist](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) shows what to bring.

## What happens at the signing?

Signing is a single meeting of roughly an hour. You review the final documents, sign the trust, and sign the pour-over will, power of attorney and health care directive in front of a notary and, for the will, witnesses. Illinois and New Jersey have their own formalities for each document, so the attorney's office runs the ceremony. See [signing, notary and witnesses for a trust](/learn/trusts/trust-signing-notary-witnesses-and-where-to-keep-the-documents) for what to expect.

Do not skip anything here to save time. A trust that is not properly signed or notarized can be rejected by a bank or title company years later, when you are not around to fix it.

## Why does funding take the longest?

Funding means changing the owner of each asset to the trust, and each institution has its own process. The main pieces:

1. **The house.** The attorney prepares a deed, you sign it before a notary, and it is recorded with the county. Recording itself may be quick, but preparing the deed requires the exact legal description from your current deed. If you have a mortgage, a courtesy call to the lender comes first. See [how to transfer a house into a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).
2. **Bank and brokerage accounts.** Each bank wants its own form and a certification of trust. Some retitle in a day, and others take two or three weeks and several phone calls.
3. **Retirement accounts and life insurance.** You do not retitle these. You file new beneficiary forms, which usually take effect right away but should be confirmed in writing.
4. **Vehicles, business interests, and anything else.** These follow their own rules and are often best left outside the trust.

Here is the example that comes up most. Thomas and Priya sign their trust on a Tuesday in March. Their attorney sends the deed for signature the same week, and the county records it ten days later. The brokerage firm, however, takes five weeks because Priya's signature card was lost. The family home is protected within two weeks. The brokerage account takes until late April. In the meantime, if one of them died, the account would still go through probate under the pour-over will.

The self-check on [whether your trust is actually funded](/learn/trusts/how-to-tell-if-your-trust-is-actually-funded) is the way to close out the project, and it is worth doing about 60 days after signing.

## What should you ask the attorney about timing?

Ask who owns each step. Some firms prepare the deed and send it for recording; others hand it to you. Ask whether they handle the bank letters, and what they do if an institution rejects the paperwork. Ask when you will see a draft and how many rounds of changes are included. A clear answer to those three questions is the best predictor of a smooth timeline. The wider [trusts library](/learn/trusts) has pages on each stage.

## Can a trust be done faster?

Yes, in three situations. If one spouse is ill, an attorney can often prioritize the incapacity documents and the signing, and you finish funding afterward. If the plan is simple, with a single house and a few accounts, drafting and review can fit in two weeks. And if you act on funding the same week you sign, rather than "when we get around to it," you take the longest stage out of the calendar.

Online services feel faster, since you can generate a document in an hour. What they leave out is the review and the funding. See [online living trust vs a lawyer](/learn/trusts/online-living-trust-vs-lawyer) for the trade-off. For a sense of cost per stage, [how much a living trust costs](/learn/trusts/how-much-does-a-living-trust-cost) breaks down fees.

## What should you do while you wait?

Do not assume you are protected until funding is done. People tend to relax once the signing is over, and that is the moment the project stalls. In the gap, keep your existing will and beneficiary forms in place. Keep a running list of which assets are moved. And if anyone in the family is in poor health, tell the attorney at the start, because the order of the work can change.

The simplest rule is to treat the trust as finished only when you can answer "yes" to this: if I died today, would my successor trustee be able to find, claim and distribute each asset without a judge?
