You signed the living trust, the lawyer said to "fund" it and you are standing at a bank counter wondering whether the checking account needs to change. The answer is not the same for every account. Some are worth retitling and some are better left alone with one short form.

This page compares the two ways to keep a bank account out of probate, explains the tradeoffs and shows how Illinois and New Jersey families usually handle it. It is part of our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts), and the general question of what belongs in a trust is covered in [what assets go in a living trust](/learn/trusts/what-assets-go-in-a-living-trust).

## What are the two ways to keep a bank account out of probate?

You can retitle the account in the name of your trust, or you can leave it in your name and add a payable on death beneficiary. Either way, a successor does not need a court case to collect the balance after your death. The difference lies in what happens before death and how much control the document gives your family.

| | Retitle into the trust | Keep it in your name with a POD beneficiary |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the account | The trustee, for the trust | You |
| Probate avoided at death | Yes | Yes |
| If you become incapacitated | Your successor trustee can manage it | A power of attorney agent may manage it, if the bank accepts the document |
| Follows trust instructions at death | Yes, in full | Only if the trust is the POD beneficiary |
| Effort | New signature card, trust certification | One form |
| Best for | Larger balances, main accounts | Small accounts, accounts you open often |

Our article on [payable on death and transfer on death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) explains how a POD form works, and [revocable trust versus TOD deed, joint account and beneficiary forms](/learn/trusts/revocable-trust-vs-tod-deed-joint-account-and-beneficiary-forms) compares all the options side by side.

## When does retitling to the trust make sense?

Retitling is worth it for an account that matters. Three situations stand out.

**You want the trust's instructions followed.** If your trust holds money for a grandchild until age 30, or divides assets unevenly, a POD account to a named person ignores all of that. The beneficiary gets the money outright. Putting the account in the trust, or naming the trust as the beneficiary, keeps the rules attached.

**You worry about incapacity.** If you cannot sign, the successor trustee can step in and pay your bills from a trust account. A POD account in your name is out of reach unless you have a durable power of attorney that the bank accepts, and banks can be slow about that. Our article on [creating a trust with dementia or a power of attorney](/learn/trusts/creating-a-trust-with-dementia-or-a-power-of-attorney) discusses the overlap.

**The balance is large.** The larger the account, the more it matters that the paperwork matches the plan. A trust account is clearer evidence of your intentions than a form that might be misfiled.

## When is a POD beneficiary the better choice?

For smaller accounts, or ones with a simple destination. A POD form takes minutes, costs nothing and does not require the trustee to open a new account. It also keeps your everyday banking just as it is.

A common pattern: keep a checking account in your own name for bills, add the trust as POD beneficiary, and put the savings or money market account in the trust. That gives the successor trustee a way to pay your expenses if you cannot, and sends the checking balance to the trust at death. It is also the pattern we see most often that works and avoids a pile of paperwork.

Be careful with several POD beneficiaries of an account. If you name three children and one dies before you, the share often passes to the other two, not to the deceased child's children, unless the form says otherwise. Banks and states differ, so read the form. A trust gives you much more control. See [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

## How does deposit insurance work for trust accounts?

Insurance is a practical reason to look at the details before moving money. The FDIC insures revocable trust accounts by owner and by beneficiary, with a cap that depends on how many beneficiaries the trust names. As a rule of thumb from current FDIC rules, the standard limit is $250,000 per owner per bank, and a revocable trust account can be insured at $250,000 for each beneficiary, up to five. The rules change, so confirm the numbers with the bank or at fdic.gov.

Two practical points. First, if you hold a large sum, spreading it over several banks or using the bank's reciprocal deposit program may matter more than the trust. Second, naming beneficiaries on a trust account can increase coverage, but the trust document and the bank's records need to match. A mismatch is easy to create when you change your trust and forget the bank.

## How do you move an account into a trust, step by step?

1. **Get your trust certification.** This is a short summary of the trust, usually two to four pages, that proves the trust exists without showing its terms. Your attorney can prepare it.
2. **Call the bank and ask what it requires.** Some banks need an appointment, and many require every trustee to sign.
3. **Choose how to retitle.** The usual form reads "Jane Smith, Trustee of the Jane Smith Revocable Trust dated March 4, 2026."
4. **Keep your own Social Security number on the account** while you are alive and the trust is revocable. The bank will confirm.
5. **Update direct deposits and automatic payments** if the account number changes.
6. **Ask for the new signature card and keep a copy** with your trust documents.
7. **Record it on your funding list.** The [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) article includes a checklist.

## What about joint accounts and accounts with a spouse?

A joint account with a right of survivorship already passes to the survivor without probate, so there may be no reason to retitle it. The problem comes at the second death, because the survivor then owns the account in his or her sole name. Many couples keep a joint checking account for daily use and put savings in the joint trust.

In Illinois, joint and POD accounts carry their own rules, covered in [Illinois payable on death and joint bank accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-payable-on-death-and-joint-bank-accounts). New Jersey's version is in [transfer on death options in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/transfer-on-death-options-in-new-jersey).

## What mistakes do people make?

Here are the ones that come up most often.

- **Opening a new account later in your own name** and forgetting to fund it or add a POD. This is the most common leak of money into probate. Run the [probate asset sorter](/tools/probate-asset-sorter) once a year.
- **Naming a child as joint owner for convenience.** That child owns the account at your death, can be sued by their own creditors and may not share it as you hoped.
- **Retitling an account with automatic withdrawals** and then missing a payment because the number changed.
- **Moving an IRA or 401(k) into a trust.** Retirement accounts cannot be retitled to a trust during life without tax consequences. Use the beneficiary form. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).
