Few myths about estate planning are as persistent as the idea that a trust is a tax shelter. Seminar speakers, online ads and well-meaning friends often suggest that a living trust will help you avoid estate tax, income tax or capital gains. In most cases it will not. This article sorts out what trusts do for taxes, what they do not, and the narrower situations where a trust genuinely reduces what your family owes.

If you are still choosing between planning tools, [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) compares them directly. For the overview, see our [guide to trusts](/learn/trusts).

## Does a revocable living trust reduce income tax?

No. A [revocable trust](/glossary/revocable-trust) is a "grantor trust" for income tax purposes, which means the trust and you are treated as one taxpayer. Interest, dividends, rent and gains earned by trust assets are reported on your personal return. Most revocable trusts use your Social Security number while you are alive, so you do not file a separate return.

Your deductions are unchanged too. The mortgage interest deduction, the home sale exclusion, and the rules for rental property all work as they did before you retitled the house to your trust.

## Does a living trust reduce estate tax?

No. A revocable trust is included in your taxable estate because you can take the property back at any time. For 2026, the federal estate tax applies only to estates above the basic exclusion of $15 million per person (indexed for inflation after 2026). A married couple can shelter up to $30 million by using [portability](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples), which lets a surviving spouse use the first spouse's unused exemption. The top federal rate is 40%.

Whether a trust or no trust, estate tax is computed the same way on the same value. Nearly all families are well below the federal threshold. See [the federal estate tax exemption in 2026](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

State taxes are a different matter. Some states levy an estate tax with an exemption far below the federal one, and a handful have an inheritance tax paid by heirs. A revocable trust does not change either. Review [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) to see whether you live in a state that matters.

## Does a trust avoid capital gains tax?

A trust does not avoid capital gains, but death can. Most assets you own when you die receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis), meaning your heirs' cost basis resets to the date-of-death value. If you bought a home for $120,000 and it is worth $620,000 when you die, your heirs generally owe tax only on gains after that date.

Assets held in a revocable trust get this step-up because you still owned them at death for tax purposes. That is a feature of keeping property until death, not a benefit of the trust, but it means the revocable trust does not cost you the step-up. By contrast, assets you give away during life, including to many [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts), generally keep your old basis. See [step-up in basis: why inheriting can beat gifting](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

## What are the most common trust tax myths?

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| "A living trust avoids estate tax." | It does not. Only certain irrevocable trusts can reduce estate tax. |
| "A trust lowers my income tax." | A revocable trust is ignored for income tax. |
| "A trust avoids probate, so it avoids taxes." | Probate and tax are separate systems. |
| "My heirs will not owe tax on a trust inheritance." | Inherited money is generally not income to the heir, but income earned later is taxable, and retirement account distributions are taxed. |
| "Putting my house in a trust protects the step-up." | You already keep the step-up if you own it at death. |
| "A trust avoids state inheritance tax." | Generally no. |
| "Trusts are only for the rich." | They are used by families of many sizes, for reasons other than tax. |

## Where can trusts actually reduce taxes?

Some trusts are designed for tax goals, but they are complex and usually for large estates.

- **Credit shelter or bypass trusts** use the first spouse's exemption. With portability available, they matter less for federal tax but may still help in states with their own estate tax or for blended families.
- **Irrevocable life insurance trusts** keep life insurance proceeds outside the taxable estate.
- **GRATs, QPRTs, SLATs and grantor-trust sales** shift appreciation out of an estate with the right facts.
- **Charitable remainder and lead trusts** can reduce income and estate tax while supporting charity.
- **Generation-skipping trusts** use the exemption from the [generation-skipping transfer tax](/glossary/generation-skipping-transfer-tax) to benefit grandchildren.
- **Qualified terminable interest trusts** and the [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction) postpone estate tax until the second spouse dies; see [QTIP and marital trusts](/learn/blended-families/qtip-and-marital-trusts).

Each of these involves tradeoffs, including permanence, cost and sometimes loss of the step-up. They are worth considering for estates near or above the exemption, or in states with lower thresholds.

## When can a trust create tax problems?

### Compressed brackets

An irrevocable trust that retains income pays tax at the trust rate, and the top bracket starts at a far lower income than for an individual. Trustees often distribute income to beneficiaries, who may be in lower brackets, or retain and invest for growth. Planning is needed, especially for a trust that continues for years.

### Retirement accounts

Naming a trust as the beneficiary of an IRA or 401(k) can speed up payout or cause bunched income in trust brackets if the trust is not drafted carefully. See [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary). Do not retitle retirement accounts into a trust, since that is treated as a withdrawal.

### Gift tax filings

Funding an irrevocable trust is often a gift. The annual exclusion is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, and larger gifts require a federal gift tax return, even if no tax is due. See [the gift tax annual exclusion and lifetime gifts](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).

### Final returns

After a death, trusts and estates have their own tax obligations, including a new tax ID, trust income tax returns and a possible fiduciary election about tax years. Our overview of [final tax returns](/learn/after-a-death/final-tax-returns) explains what trustees and executors file.

## How should you decide?

1. Add up your estate: home, retirement accounts, investments, business, life insurance.
2. Compare it with the federal exemption ($15 million per person in 2026) and your state's threshold, if any.
3. If you are well below both, choose a trust for non-tax reasons: avoiding probate, incapacity planning, privacy or control. See [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).
4. If you are near or above, ask about tax-driven trusts and the effect on basis.
5. Review as laws change, because exemptions and rules are updated by Congress and by states.

For example, Anita, 62, has a $2.3 million estate and lives in a state with no estate tax. A trust will not save her family any tax. She chooses one because her house is in two states, and she wants to avoid two probate cases. That is a reasonable use. Another family with a $22 million estate in a state with its own estate tax might benefit from tax-driven structures in addition.

Tax law changes, and state rules differ. Check your [state guide](/estate-planning/maryland) (Maryland, for instance, has both an estate and an inheritance tax) or ask an attorney for current numbers.
