People are often surprised to learn that signing a trust is only half the job. If your house, bank accounts and investments are still titled in your own name, the trust is an empty box, and your family can end up in probate anyway. [Trust funding](/glossary/funding) is the process of moving assets into the trust so it can do what you built it to do.

This guide covers each asset type, the paperwork involved, and the mistakes that most often leave a trust unfunded. If you are still deciding whether to create a trust, read [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) first.

## What does it mean to fund a trust?

Every asset has a legal owner, shown by a deed, account title, or registration. Funding means changing that owner from "Maria Alvarez" to "Maria Alvarez, Trustee of the Alvarez Family Trust dated March 3, 2026." You are still in control as trustee, so you can sell, spend or reinvest as before. What changes is that the trust now holds legal title, so a successor trustee can act if you cannot, and the asset does not need probate when you die.

Think of it as moving furniture into a storage unit you hold the key to. The unit (the trust) does nothing until you move things in.

## What is the funding process, step by step?

1. **Make an asset list.** For each item note the current title, approximate value, and any beneficiary designation.
2. **Sort assets into three groups:** retitle to the trust, keep in your name with a beneficiary designation, and leave out.
3. **Gather paperwork** such as the trust certificate (a short summary the trustee can give to institutions instead of the full trust), account statements and your deed.
4. **Complete each transfer** using the method for that asset type, described below.
5. **Keep proof.** Save recorded deeds, confirmation letters and new account statements showing the trust's name.
6. **Repeat when things change.** New accounts and real estate should be opened or bought in the trust's name from the start.

## How do you put your house in a trust?

You sign and record a new deed that transfers title from you to you as trustee. Common types include a quitclaim deed, a grant deed or a warranty deed, depending on state practice. The type matters because it affects title insurance and warranties. An attorney will prepare the deed so that the property description is accurate and the title company will honor it. For the full walk-through, including mortgage, title insurance and property tax questions, see [how to transfer a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust).

Before recording, check:

- **Title insurance.** Ask whether your existing policy continues to cover the trust. Many do, but confirm.
- **Mortgage.** Federal law generally prevents a lender from enforcing a due-on-sale clause when you transfer your home into a revocable trust you live in, but give the lender notice.
- **Property tax.** Many states and counties exclude transfers to your own revocable trust from reassessment, but some require a form or filing. Check locally.
- **Homestead and senior exemptions.** Ask whether the exemption continues in the trust's name.
- **Homeowner's insurance.** Add the trustee as an insured.

For a longer look at options for a house, see [leaving your house to your children: deed, trust or will](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children). If you own property in another state, a separate deed is needed there; see [owning property in more than one state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

## How do you fund bank and brokerage accounts?

Bank accounts are retitled with a form at the branch, usually after you show a trust certificate. Brokerage accounts are retitled through a transfer or re-registration form. For checking and savings accounts you use day to day, some people name the trust as the owner and others keep the account in their own name and add a payable-on-death designation to the trust or a person. Both can work, but the choice affects incapacity planning. A [payable-on-death account](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) passes at death without probate, but a successor trustee cannot manage it during your incapacity unless the trust owns it or you have a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) in place.

Certificates of deposit can usually be retitled without penalty, but ask the bank first.

## Which assets should not be retitled?

| Asset | Usually retitle to the trust? | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s | No | Name beneficiaries; see [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries) |
| Life insurance | Usually no | Name the trust or a person as beneficiary |
| Annuities | Usually no | Name beneficiaries |
| HSAs | No | Name beneficiary |
| Vehicles | Often no | Use your state's transfer process |
| Health savings and 529 accounts | No | Name successor owner |
| Business interests | Often yes | Assign with attention to operating agreements |
| Personal belongings | Yes, by assignment | A general assignment covers furniture, jewelry, art |

Moving a retirement account into a trust would be treated as a taxable withdrawal. If a trust is the beneficiary, the wording must be careful; see [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary).

## What about LLCs, business interests and personal property?

For an LLC, an assignment of membership interest to the trust is usually used, and the operating agreement may require other members' consent. Corporate stock is reissued in the trust's name. See [what happens to your LLC when you die](/learn/business-owners/llc-ownership-at-death).

For household furnishings, jewelry and other tangible items, a short general assignment signed by you transfers them to the trust without listing each one. Items with titles (boats, aircraft) follow the registration process. Items like firearms have their own legal rules; see [firearms in estate planning](/learn/property-and-assets/firearms-in-estate-planning).

## What are the most common funding mistakes?

- **Forgetting the house.** The deed is often the most valuable item, and the one most often left out.
- **Buying after signing.** A new house or brokerage account opened in your own name after the trust is signed is outside the trust unless you retitle it.
- **Retitling retirement accounts.** This can create an immediate tax bill.
- **Assuming the pour-over will fixes it.** A [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) sends leftover assets to the trust, but they still go through probate first.
- **Naming the wrong owner on a new account.** Watch spelling and date of the trust.
- **Not telling the successor trustee.** They need a list of what the trust owns.

Probate is only avoided for assets that are not in your probate estate; see [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) for the line.

## How often should you check funding?

Review it once a year and after any major purchase, sale, inheritance, marriage, or move. A simple habit: whenever you open an account or sign closing papers, ask, "Should this be in the trust's name?" Our checklist of [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) covers other triggers.

State rules on deeds, transfer taxes and property tax reassessment vary. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/texas), for example Texas, or an attorney in your state can tell you what applies where you live.
