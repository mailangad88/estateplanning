Parents rarely worry that their child will do something wrong with an inheritance on purpose. They worry about the life that surrounds the child: a marriage that may not last, a spouse who spends freely, a habit that has already cost the family dearly, or a lawsuit waiting to happen. A trust is the usual answer. It does not fix any of those problems, but it can keep your money from becoming part of them.

This page covers the questions people ask most often: divorce, a difficult spouse, addiction, creditors, a child on public benefits and the related question of leaving someone out. For the building blocks, see our pages on [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) and [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children), and our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts).

## The short version

- Protection depends on the money staying in the trust. Payouts into personal or joint accounts are exposed.
- Choose an independent trustee and make distributions discretionary where the risk is high.
- Add a spendthrift clause, and say what happens if the child divorces, is sued or relapses.
- Keep the trust separate from the child's household finances, including titles and tax records.
- Review it when the child's life changes, because a trust written for a 22-year-old fits poorly at 42.

## Does a trust protect an inheritance from a child's divorce?

A trust gives better protection than any other tool, but the result depends on how it is set up and how it is used afterward.

Start with the baseline. In most states, property one spouse receives by inheritance is that spouse's separate property, not shared with the other. The trouble is what happens next. If Carla inherits $300,000 and deposits it into the joint account she shares with her husband, then pays the mortgage from it, a court can treat the money as having been converted into marital property. Courts call this commingling, and it is one of the most common ways inherited money gets split in a divorce.

A trust interrupts that pattern. The money is owned by the trust, so there is nothing to deposit. If the trustee pays Carla's tuition bill or a medical expense directly, the money never enters the marital accounts. If the trust is discretionary, meaning Carla cannot demand a payment, courts in most states are reluctant to count the trust as an asset to divide, though they may still consider it when setting support. The details vary by state and by how much power the child holds.

The protection weakens when the child is the sole trustee with unlimited access, and the more control the child has, the more a divorce court is likely to treat the trust as theirs. For that reason, a child serving as trustee of their own inheritance is the setup most likely to disappoint.

## How do you keep an inheritance away from a bad spouse?

You can keep it out of reach, but not out of sight. A few drafting choices matter more than any others.

1. **Name a trustee who is not the child's spouse.** Even a well-meaning spouse becomes a problem in a divorce or after the child's death.
2. **Say who gets the trust if the child dies.** Without a clear instruction, a child's death can pass trust assets to the spouse. Most parents want the grandchildren or the child's siblings to receive it instead.
3. **Limit the child's power to leave the trust to someone else.** A broad power of appointment lets the child redirect assets to a new spouse. Narrow it to descendants.
4. **Pay for things directly.** A trustee who pays the contractor or the mortgage lender never puts money into a joint account.
5. **Keep a separate title.** A home bought with trust money should be titled in the trust, not in joint names, if the goal is to protect it.

Parents sometimes feel uncomfortable writing in distrust of a son-in-law they like. It helps to frame it as a rule that applies to every child and every spouse equally. The trust is not a statement about anyone's character. It is just a structure. Our article on [keeping an inheritance separate in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/keeping-an-inheritance-separate-in-a-blended-family) shows the same ideas in a remarriage setting.

## What happens to a trust if a beneficiary has an addiction?

A discretionary trust is the tool that fits best, because it lets the trustee say no. If the document gives the trustee full discretion, a beneficiary in active addiction cannot demand cash, and the trustee can pay rent, medical bills and treatment directly instead.

Some families go further and write specific triggers into the document. Common provisions include:

- Payments for treatment are always allowed, even if other distributions are paused.
- Cash distributions are held back until the beneficiary completes a program or passes a drug test, with the trustee choosing a lab and a schedule.
- A mandatory distribution at a certain age is replaced by a trustee's judgment about readiness.
- A neutral third party, such as a physician, can decide whether a trigger has been met, so that the trustee is not forced to confront a family member alone.

These provisions are a help, but they are not magic. A trustee who is a sibling may find them painful to enforce, so a professional or independent co-trustee is often wiser. And a beneficiary who is not a risk today may be one in ten years, so the trustee needs flexibility, not a rigid rule. The mechanics are described in [how a trust decides when heirs get money](/learn/trusts/trust-distribution-standards-hems-and-discretion).

## What happens if a beneficiary has creditors or is sued?

A spendthrift clause is the standard answer. It prevents the beneficiary from pledging their interest and prevents most creditors from reaching it before it is paid out. Once the trustee actually pays money to the beneficiary, it belongs to the beneficiary and a creditor can pursue it like any other cash.

Every state has exceptions to the spendthrift rule. These commonly include child support, alimony, certain tax claims and, in some states, claims for necessities such as medical care. The list is different in every state. Our guide on [creditors and inherited money](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits) explains how this works for different asset types.

## What about a beneficiary on Medicaid or SSI?

Giving a beneficiary on means-tested benefits a plain inheritance can cost them their coverage, because the money counts as their resource. A properly drafted trust, where the trustee has sole discretion and the beneficiary cannot force any payment, can avoid that. These are called supplemental or special needs trusts and they have their own rules. Our overview of the [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) explains them in detail.

## Can a trust be used to disinherit someone?

Yes, and a trust is often a better place to do it than a will. A trust can name exactly who receives each share and can include a short statement that a named person is intentionally left out. Because a trust usually avoids court filing, it is less public than a will.

There are limits. A surviving spouse often has a statutory right to a share of the estate regardless of what the documents say, and in some states that right reaches trust assets as well. Young children and dependent family members may also have claims for support. And a disinherited child can still file a challenge, which is why a clear statement and good records matter. See [disinheriting or unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) for how the same decisions work in a will.

## How state law changes this

States differ on three things that matter here: how much protection a spendthrift clause gives, which creditors are excepted, and how courts treat inherited property in divorce. A handful of states also have special self-settled trust rules, but those apply to a person's own money, not to an inheritance. An attorney in your state can tell you where your state lands. You can start with the [state estate planning guides](/estate-planning), which link each state's page.
