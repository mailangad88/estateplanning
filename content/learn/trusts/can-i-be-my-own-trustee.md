Many people hesitate before setting up a trust because they imagine handing their property to a stranger. In most cases that does not happen. With a revocable living trust, you are the person in charge, usually as grantor, trustee and beneficiary at once.

This page explains how that works, what duties come with the title, when a different trustee is the better or only choice, and how to plan for the handoff. For the full picture of how trusts work, start with our [trusts overview](/learn/trusts). If you are comparing people to take over later, [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) goes deeper on that decision.

## The short version

- You can serve as your own trustee of a revocable living trust, and that is the standard arrangement.
- You will have fiduciary duties, but they are mostly to yourself while you are the only beneficiary.
- The trust must say who takes over, and when.
- For an irrevocable trust meant to protect assets or reduce a taxable estate, you generally should not serve as trustee.
- Choose successors with as much care as you would choose a guardian.

## Why can you be trustee of your own trust?

A [trust](/glossary/trust) is a set of rules about property. Its three roles, grantor, trustee and beneficiary, can be filled by one person, as long as there are other people who will eventually take. In a revocable trust, you can also change or cancel the document. Because the law treats the property as still yours, nothing stops you from managing it.

In practice you will notice almost nothing. You still live in the house, pay the bills and sell the stock. The change is in how things are titled: the deed reads "Maria Alvarez, Trustee of the Alvarez Living Trust," and so do accounts. Our article on [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) shows what that retitling involves.

## What duties do you have as your own trustee?

A trustee is a [fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary), which means a legal duty to act in the interest of the beneficiaries. When you are the only beneficiary, that duty has little bite, but a few habits will help the people who follow you.

- **Keep trust property separate.** Do not mix trust money and unrelated personal accounts in ways that blur which is which.
- **Keep records.** A simple list of what the trust owns, updated each year, saves the successor a month of work.
- **Follow the document.** If the trust says your sister gets the cabin, do not sell it and spend the proceeds on something else without amending the trust.
- **Plan for changes.** If you add a beneficiary or an heir turns 18, the duties change.

Take Frank, 67, who runs his trust from his kitchen table. He pays the property tax, reinvests dividends and updates a one-page asset list every January. When he dies, his daughter finds the list, the account statements and a certification of trust in a folder marked with her name. She does not need a lawyer's help to find the money, and that is the whole point.

## When should someone else be trustee?

There are several situations where serving yourself is a poor idea or defeats the purpose.

| Situation | Can you be trustee? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Revocable living trust | Yes | You keep full control, which is the design |
| Irrevocable trust for Medicaid planning | Usually not | Control can make the assets count as yours |
| Irrevocable life insurance trust | Usually not | Control may bring the policy back into your taxable estate |
| Trust for a child or disabled beneficiary after your death | Not applicable | You will be gone; someone else must serve |
| You are 90 and fading | Technically yes, practically risky | Hand over to a co-trustee earlier |
| Trust holds complicated assets such as a business | Yes, with support | Add a professional co-trustee or advisors |

The two irrevocable examples are important. Our guide to [irrevocable trusts](/learn/trusts/irrevocable-trusts) explains that the benefits come from your giving up control. Serving as trustee of your own gift weakens that, and tax rules can treat the property as yours if you keep too much power. If you want to be involved, a better route is naming an independent trustee and keeping a limited, defined role, such as the ability to replace the trustee.

## What is a successor trustee and how do they take over?

A [successor trustee](/glossary/successor-trustee) is the person or institution who steps in when you stop serving. The document should say what triggers the change. The usual list is death, resignation, and incapacity, and incapacity is the one that needs careful wording.

Common triggers for incapacity include:

- Written statements from two physicians that you cannot manage your affairs.
- A court finding of incapacity, which is slower and public, so most people try to avoid it.
- A named person, such as your spouse, confirming the change in writing.

Pick one and spell it out. Vague language delays the handoff exactly when your family is under stress. Make sure the successor knows where the documents are and has a copy of the [certification of trust](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration). Name a second and third successor too, because the first choice may be unable or unwilling when the time comes.

Having a successor ready is also the reason a trust works better than a power of attorney in some cases. A [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) still matters for assets outside the trust, and banks sometimes resist it, which makes the trust a cleaner tool for what sits inside it.

## What if I would rather not be trustee at all?

Some people prefer to step back, and that is fine. You can name a trusted friend, a family member or a bank as trustee of your living trust from day one, or you can serve until a set age and then hand over. The cost of stepping back is losing day-to-day control, along with fees if you use a professional. If you are tired of managing money, or you worry about being pressured by a relative, a professional co-trustee gives you a check without taking over.

## Can both spouses be trustee?

Yes. A joint trust usually names both spouses as initial co-trustees, so either can act without the other, or both must sign for big transactions. The document should state the rule. At the first death, the survivor continues as sole trustee, and the trust may split into separate shares if there is a tax or blended-family plan. See [joint vs. separate trusts for remarriage](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage) if either of you has children from a prior relationship.

## How state law changes this

Every state allows you to be trustee of your revocable trust, but details differ. States vary on how a trustee proves authority to third parties, on whether a trustee must notify beneficiaries once the trust turns irrevocable, and on the formalities for naming and removing a successor. Look at your [state guide](/estate-planning/illinois) or your state's living trust page if one exists.
