A grandparent who wants to leave something for grandchildren faces two problems at once. The grandchildren may be toddlers or teenagers today, and the money may not be needed for twenty years. In that time a parent may divorce, a grandchild may be sued or in trouble, and a new grandchild may be born. A trust is the tool that handles all of that, and the main work is in the choices you make when you set it up.

This page explains how to set up a trust for grandchildren, what types exist, how to set the payout ages, who should be trustee and when the generation-skipping tax matters. For the broader question of leaving money to grandchildren, including 529 plans and direct gifts, see [leaving an inheritance to grandchildren](/learn/life-stages/leaving-an-inheritance-to-grandchildren). This page belongs to our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts).

## How does a trust for grandchildren work?

You, as grantor, sign a trust agreement. The trustee holds the assets and follows the instructions in the document. The grandchildren are the beneficiaries and receive money when and how the trust says. The trustee has a legal duty to follow those terms and to act for the beneficiaries.

There are three ways to create one:

1. **A gift trust now.** You transfer money into an irrevocable trust today. It lets the grandchildren benefit while you are alive. Gifts to such a trust can sometimes use the annual gift exclusion, which is $19,000 per recipient in 2026, if the trust is drafted for it.
2. **A trust inside your living trust.** Your [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) holds the assets while you live and creates separate grandchild trusts when you die.
3. **A trust inside your will.** A [testamentary trust](/learn/trusts/testamentary-trusts) is created by the will after death. It goes through probate, but it is simple to draft.

Most grandparents choose the second or third. A gift trust now makes sense when you want to move assets out of your estate or want to see the grandchildren benefit while you are alive.

## One shared trust or a separate trust for each grandchild?

This is the main design choice, and it affects how the trustee behaves.

| | Shared ("pot") trust | Separate trusts |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | One pool for all grandchildren, split later | Each grandchild has an account that is theirs alone |
| Strength | Money follows need, such as an illness or college | Each child gets an exact, equal share |
| Weakness | The trustee must say no to one grandchild in favor of another | A child with higher needs cannot draw on a sibling's share |
| Best fit | Young grandchildren, open group, uncertain needs | Older grandchildren, a closed group, strong wish for fairness |
| Common ending | Divide into separate shares at a set age or date | Pay out at set ages |

A common hybrid: keep one pot until the youngest grandchild reaches 21 or 25, then divide whatever is left into equal shares. That keeps the group together while the children are young and still treats each fairly in the end.

Think also about the "per stirpes" question. If your child dies before you, should that child's children take their parent's share? Our article on [per stirpes versus per capita](/glossary/per-stirpes) explains the difference, and the trust should state the answer in plain words.

## What ages and conditions should you set?

Ages are the part most grandparents get wrong by being too generous. A grandchild who receives $200,000 at 18 is likely to spend it differently than the grandparent hoped. Common options:

- **Staged payments.** One-third at 25, one-third at 30 and the rest at 35. The trustee holds the balance and can pay for specific needs in between.
- **Needs-based only.** The trustee pays for education, health, housing and support, and the grandchild receives nothing outright until a late age.
- **Incentive terms.** Matching a grandchild's earnings or paying for school. Keep these simple, since a trustee must be able to apply them without guessing.

Two cautions. Do not condition the gift on a religion, a marriage or a career, which courts find hard to apply and sometimes refuse to enforce. And give the trustee some discretion, since a rule that works at 25 can be a disaster at 30.

Our guide to [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) covers the payout standards, including the common health, education, maintenance and support standard, in more detail.

## Who should be trustee?

The trustee decides when and whether the grandchild gets money, so the choice matters more than the ages. Think through three candidates:

- **The grandchild's parent.** Convenient, but it can put your child in charge of the money for their own children, which may or may not be what you want. If your child has money problems or a difficult spouse, it may not be wise.
- **Another child or a sibling of the parent.** Fair, but a trustee who is also a beneficiary or the parent of a competing grandchild can face conflicts.
- **A bank or professional trustee.** Neutral and continuous, but it charges a fee. Our article on [trustee pay](/learn/after-a-death/trustee-compensation) explains what fees look like.

A good setup names a family trustee with a professional or a trusted friend as co-trustee, or gives someone the power to replace the trustee. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).

## A worked example

This is an illustration. Walter, 74, has a $900,000 estate and four grandchildren aged 3, 6, 9 and 14. He wants them to have $200,000 in total for college and a first home. His living trust leaves $200,000 in one pot with his daughter Maria as trustee and his lawyer's firm, or a bank, as successor. The trust pays for tuition and medical needs while the youngest is under 21. When the youngest turns 21, the remaining balance divides into four equal trusts, each paid out at 25, 30 and 35. A fifth grandchild born later shares in the pot. Walter wrote "all of my grandchildren, whenever born" to make sure of that.

If Walter had given each child $50,000 directly in a UTMA account, the money would go to each child at the age set by state law, which is 21 in both Illinois and New Jersey. See [Illinois UTMA accounts](/learn/illinois/illinois-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors) and [New Jersey UTMA accounts](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-utma-accounts-and-gifts-to-minors). A trust is the more flexible choice when you want control past 21.

## When does the generation-skipping tax matter?

Only for large gifts. A transfer to a grandchild, or to a trust for a grandchild, can be subject to the generation-skipping transfer tax in addition to estate or gift tax. Every person has an exemption from that tax that matches the federal estate tax exemption, which is $15,000,000 in 2026. A family whose total gifts to grandchildren are far below that amount will not pay it, though larger gifts can require a filing. See our article on the [generation-skipping transfer tax](/learn/estate-tax/generation-skipping-transfer-tax) and ask an attorney whether your gifts need a return.
