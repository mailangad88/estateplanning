Most married couples share a home, a bank account and a set of goals, so a single trust that holds all of it feels natural. And often it is. A joint living trust is the most common form of trust for couples who married once, have children together, and want the survivor to carry on without a court or a lawyer in the way.

The details are where couples get surprised. What the survivor can change, how the trust splits after the first death, and what happens to property that came into the marriage separately are all settled in the drafting. This guide covers the mechanics. It sits inside our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts), and if you or your spouse have children from an earlier relationship, read [joint vs. separate trusts for a second marriage](/learn/blended-families/joint-vs-separate-trusts-for-remarriage) too.

## What is a joint living trust?

A joint living trust is a [revocable trust](/glossary/revocable-trust) signed by both spouses as grantors. Both usually serve as co-trustees, and both are the beneficiaries during life. You can sell the house, move money or change investments with either signature or both, as the document specifies. A separate tax ID is not needed while both are alive in most cases because the trust uses a spouse's Social Security number.

At the death of the first spouse, the survivor remains trustee. At the second death, the remaining assets go to the people you named, often your children, either outright or in continuing trusts.

## How does it differ from two separate trusts?

| Feature | Joint trust | Two separate trusts |
|---|---|---|
| Documents to sign | One | Two |
| Titling | One trust name on shared assets | Each spouse funds their own |
| Cost | Usually lower | Usually higher |
| Fit | Shared assets, shared heirs | Different heirs, separate property, businesses |
| Clarity of each spouse's share | Needs tracking | Built in |
| After first death | Survivor may control all or part | Survivor controls only own trust, plus what the other trust allows |

In community property states, which include California, Texas and Arizona among others, property earned during the marriage is generally owned half and half, which makes joint trusts easy to administer. In other states, couples often hold some property individually, and the trust should identify whose is whose.

## What happens when the first spouse dies?

This is where drafting matters most. Joint trusts follow one of two models.

**Model one: the survivor takes everything and can change it all.** The trust stays revocable. The survivor can amend it, sell assets, and even disinherit the first spouse's children. This is simple and works when both spouses want the same people to inherit.

**Model two: the trust divides into two parts.** Often called an A/B or bypass design, it splits the trust into a survivor's part and a decedent's part. The survivor's part stays flexible. The decedent's part becomes irrevocable, with terms the first spouse set. That locks the plan and protects it against later changes. It also, for couples with larger estates, was historically used to make use of both spouses' estate tax exclusions, though with a federal exclusion of $15,000,000 per person in 2026 and portability available, this is a smaller reason than it once was. See [bypass trust vs. portability](/learn/estate-tax/bypass-trust-vs-portability).

The step by step in practice is something like this:

1. The survivor notifies the bank, brokerage and title company, and gets copies of the death certificate.
2. The survivor, as trustee, obtains a tax ID number for the irrevocable portion if the trust splits. See [trust EIN and the 65-day election](/learn/after-a-death/trust-ein-and-65-day-election).
3. Assets are inventoried and valued as of the date of death. Assets that receive a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis) are reset to that value; in a community property state both halves usually step up.
4. The trust is divided into parts if the document calls for it.
5. The survivor continues as trustee and may use assets according to the standards in the trust.

For a full timeline, see [what happens to a living trust when you die](/learn/trusts/what-happens-to-a-living-trust-when-you-die).

## Who has the authority to act while both spouses are alive?

The trust should say. Common options are "either trustee acting alone," which is convenient for paying bills and buying and selling, or "both trustees acting together," which gives each spouse a veto. For real estate, a title company will often ask for both signatures on a deed regardless.

If one spouse becomes incapacitated, the other typically steps in as sole trustee without court involvement. That is one of the biggest benefits. Compare it to a couple with only a power of attorney, where the bank may refuse the document. See [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do). The trust should also name a successor trustee for the day neither spouse can serve.

## Should you use a joint trust? An example

Marcus and Delia, both 58, married at 26. They have two adult children, a house, two retirement accounts and a modest brokerage account. They want the survivor to have complete access and the children to inherit equally after both are gone. A joint trust with a locked-share option fits well: the survivor keeps all flexibility except the right to change the final beneficiaries.

Compare Ellis and Joan, 64, who married in their fifties. Each has two children from earlier marriages, and Joan owns a small business before the marriage. A joint trust that lets the survivor change everything would let Ellis, after Joan's death, leave her business to his children. Separate trusts, or a locked-share design, avoid that. Saving the cost of a second document is not worth that risk.

## Does the type of home ownership change anything?

It can. Some states let spouses own a home as tenants by the entirety, which protects it from the creditors of just one spouse. Moving that home into a trust may change that protection, so ask before you record a deed. Our glossary entry on [tenancy by the entirety](/glossary/tenancy-by-the-entirety) explains the basics. The same question comes up for a house in a state with a homestead exemption. A good attorney checks these before retitling anything, not after.

## What mistakes are common with joint trusts?

- **Funding only half.** Couples retitle the house but leave the savings accounts in separate names. Use our [funding guide](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).
- **Not tracking separate property.** An inheritance one spouse received should be labeled so it stays traceable.
- **Forgetting beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts pass by form, not by trust; see [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).
- **Skipping the individual documents.** Each spouse still needs a will, a financial power of attorney and healthcare documents.
- **Divorce or separation without a plan.** The trust should say what happens, and state law in many places will revoke certain provisions for a former spouse, but not always.
- **Assuming the trust shelters assets from Medicaid.** A revocable trust does not. See [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) for the real options.
