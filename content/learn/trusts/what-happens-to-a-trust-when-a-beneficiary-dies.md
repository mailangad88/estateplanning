You set up a trust leaving your estate to your three children, and one of them dies. Or you are the trustee and a beneficiary has just died halfway through a long administration. The question lands the same way: whose share is it now?

The honest answer is that the trust document decides, and the date of death inside the trust's timeline decides which part of the document applies. This page walks through the three timing situations, then shows what clauses to look for. If you want background on how trusts are built, our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) is the place to start.

## The short version

- If the beneficiary dies **before the grantor**, the share goes to whoever the trust names as backup. A revocable trust can be rewritten.
- If they die **after the grantor but before being paid**, the answer turns on whether their right was already fixed or depended on surviving.
- If they die **during a lifetime trust**, the trust's remainder clause says who takes next.
- The trust should say what happens, in plain words, for every beneficiary. Most gaps are drafting gaps.

## When a beneficiary dies before the grantor

This is the most common case, especially in a trust created years ago by a parent. Lorraine, 80, left everything equally to her sons Paul and Greg. Greg dies at 55, leaving two children. Lorraine is alive and her trust is still revocable.

Because she is alive and competent, Lorraine can amend the trust on her own. She might keep Greg's share for his two children, or fold it into Paul's. Nothing happens automatically that she cannot change. The practical step is to see her attorney soon and not wait. If she waited until she lost capacity, her options would shrink.

If Lorraine never amends and dies later, the trust's backup language applies. A common clause reads, in effect, "if a beneficiary does not survive me, that share goes to the beneficiary's descendants who survive me, by representation." That is the wording of per stirpes, and our glossary entry on [per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes) explains how it divides. Another common clause sends the share to the other named beneficiaries. A third names a charity.

## When a beneficiary dies after the grantor but before the payout

This one trips people up, because two phrases that sound alike lead to different results.

A **vested** right means the beneficiary was entitled to the money already. The trust might say "pay my son $100,000 when I die." If he survives the grantor by even a day, that sum belongs to him and, if he then dies before it is paid, it goes to his estate. His heirs and creditors may end up with it.

A **contingent** right means the gift depends on something else, often on being alive at a later date. "To my daughter if she survives me by 30 days" is contingent. If she dies on day 12, she never owned the gift, and it passes to the alternate.

Many trusts include a survivorship period of 30 to 90 days for exactly this reason. It prevents a share from going into one person's probate and then straight to another's, and it avoids a second round of paperwork when two family members die close together. Our page on [lapsed gifts and simultaneous death](/learn/wills/lapsed-gifts-and-simultaneous-death) covers the same issue for wills.

## When a beneficiary dies during a lifetime trust

Some trusts do not pay everything out at once. A parent might hold a child's share for life, paying income and principal as needed. When that child dies, the trust document's **remainder clause** says who takes what is left.

| Situation | Typical result | Where to look in the trust |
|---|---|---|
| Child dies, has children | Share continues for grandchildren or is paid out to them | Remainder or "descendants" clause |
| Child dies, no children | Share goes to siblings or their lines | Contingent beneficiary clause |
| Surviving spouse dies | Trust divides among children | Termination clause |
| Everyone on the list is gone | Charity or the grantor's heirs under state law | Ultimate takers clause |

If the beneficiary had a **power of appointment**, they may be allowed to say who gets their share among a defined group. That power is only available if the trust gave it to them, and it is used in a will or by signed notice.

## What if the beneficiary is a minor?

A minor can be a beneficiary without any court involvement. The trustee holds the money and spends it for the child's needs, and the trust usually says at what ages the child gets the principal. No guardian of the estate is needed. This is one of the main reasons parents use trusts rather than leaving money outright, since an outright gift to a minor often triggers a court-supervised arrangement. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children) for how those ages are set.

If a child beneficiary dies before the age at which they would receive the money, the trust's alternate language applies, and the money does not pass through the child's estate unless the document says it should.

## What a trustee should do when a beneficiary dies

1. Get certified copies of the death certificate, and keep one in the trust file.
2. Stop any pending or regular distributions to that person until you know which clause applies. Do not pay the estate or the family on instinct.
3. Read the beneficiary clause, the survivorship clause and the remainder clause together, and mark which one fits the date of death.
4. Ask the trust's attorney whether the share was vested or contingent. If there is doubt, a court can interpret the trust.
5. Notify the new beneficiaries and give them the information the trustee owes them. Our page on [what trust beneficiaries can see and when](/learn/trusts/what-trust-beneficiaries-can-see-and-when) explains what must be shared.
6. Update the trust's records and tax filings, including any change to who receives income, and talk with a tax preparer about the final return for the deceased beneficiary.

A trustee who pays the wrong person can be personally responsible for fixing it. The process for stepping through a full administration is in [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration).

## What if the trust says nothing?

A trust that is silent about a beneficiary who dies first is a trust with a hole. States fill the gap in different ways. Some have anti-lapse statutes that send the share to the beneficiary's descendants. Others only apply those rules to wills, not trusts. In a few places, the share falls back to the grantor's heirs, which may mean people the grantor never meant to include. If no one on the list is living and there is no backup, a court may have to decide.

## How to word the backup so it holds

- Name a **contingent beneficiary** for each primary beneficiary, and say whether that person's descendants take instead.
- Add a **survivorship period**, commonly 30 to 90 days.
- Say whether grandchildren take **per stirpes** or equally, since the default can surprise you.
- Add an **ultimate backup**, such as a charity, so the property never falls to default heirs by accident.
- Say how a **minor's** or **disabled** descendant's share is held, since an outright gift can end government benefits.
- Review the trust after every birth, death, marriage and divorce. See [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

The same logic applies to forms that sit outside the trust. If the trust is named on a life insurance policy or retirement account, make sure those forms match, as covered in [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

## How state law changes this

Anti-lapse rules, survivorship periods, and how courts treat a silent trust differ from state to state. Some states apply the same default to wills and trusts, and others treat them differently. Our [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois) and [New Jersey](/estate-planning/new-jersey) guides show how those two states approach inheritance, and an attorney in your state can tell you which default applies to your trust.

When the gift passes under a will or by intestacy rather than a trust, see [when an heir cannot receive their share](/learn/after-a-death/when-an-heir-cannot-receive-their-share-died-minor-incapacitated-or-in-debt).
