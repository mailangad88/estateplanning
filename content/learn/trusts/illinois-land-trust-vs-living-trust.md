If you own a house or a two-flat in Chicago, a friend or a landlord group has probably told you to "put it in a land trust." If you have also met with an estate planning attorney, you heard about a living trust. People assume these are two names for one thing. They are not, and picking the wrong one leaves a gap that your family finds only after a death.

This page explains what an Illinois land trust does, what it does not do, and how to decide whether you need one, the other, or both.

## What is an Illinois land trust?

An Illinois land trust is a trust whose only job is to hold legal title to real estate. You sign a deed that transfers the property to a trustee, and you sign a trust agreement that makes you the beneficiary with the right to direct the trustee. The recorded deed reads something like "First Bank, not personally but as Trustee under Trust Agreement dated June 4, 2026, known as Trust No. 5521." Your name does not appear.

Illinois treats the beneficiary's interest in a land trust as personal property rather than as an interest in the land itself. That quirk has real effects. It is why you can assign your interest to someone else with a short paper and no new deed, and why the interest can be a probate asset when you die.

The land trust is common in Cook County and the surrounding counties, much less so downstate and almost not at all in New Jersey. Part of the reason is habit. Title companies and bank trust departments in Chicago have written them for generations, and they are comfortable with the paperwork.

## How is a land trust different from a living trust?

A [living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is a full estate planning document. A land trust is a title tool. The table shows the practical differences.

| Question | Illinois land trust | Revocable living trust |
|---|---|---|
| What does it hold? | One property, usually | Many assets: house, accounts, business interests |
| Who is on the public deed? | The trustee (often a bank) | You, as trustee of your trust |
| Does it name who gets the property at death? | Only if you add a successor beneficiary | Yes, that is its main purpose |
| Does it plan for incapacity? | No | Yes, a successor trustee takes over |
| Does it avoid probate by itself? | Only with a named successor beneficiary | Yes, for assets properly titled in it |
| Does it protect you from lawsuits? | No | No, while it is revocable |
| Typical cost | Lower, often a fee to the trustee plus a deed | Higher, a full plan |

The honest summary is that a land trust answers "who can look me up at the county recorder?" and a living trust answers "what happens to my family if I die or cannot manage my affairs?"

## Does a land trust avoid probate?

A land trust avoids probate only when it says who gets your beneficial interest. Many older land trust forms do not. They simply say the interest passes under the beneficiary's will, which means it goes through probate as personal property. A well-drafted agreement names a successor beneficiary or lets the beneficiary assign the interest to a living trust.

Consider Dolores, 74, who put her Bridgeport two-flat in a land trust in 1998 because her landlord friends all did. She has a will leaving everything to her son and daughter. The land trust agreement names no successor. When she dies, the interest is a probate asset, and her executor has to open an estate to assign it. Had she signed a one-page assignment of beneficial interest to her living trust, the property would have passed to her children on the trustee's say-so.

If you already have a land trust, pull out the agreement and read the section on death of the beneficiary. That one paragraph decides whether it avoids probate. For the wider picture of how Illinois probate runs, see the [Illinois probate process](/learn/illinois/illinois-probate-process).

## Does a land trust protect against creditors or lawsuits?

No, and this is the most common misunderstanding. Putting a property in a land trust makes your name harder to find in the county records. It does not move ownership away from you. A court can order you to assign your beneficial interest to satisfy a judgment, and a plaintiff who has reason to suspect you own the building can find it through a title search by parcel number.

If your concern is a tenant injury at a rental, the protection comes from liability insurance, an umbrella policy, and sometimes an LLC. For the tax side of rentals, see [inherited rental property taxes](/learn/property-and-assets/inherited-rental-property-taxes). Some owners layer all three: an LLC owns the building, the LLC's interest sits in a living trust, and the title company records the deed in a land trust for privacy.

## When do Illinois owners use both?

The most common setup for a family home is simple. The house stays titled in a land trust (or moves into one for the first time), and the beneficial interest is assigned to the owner's living trust. The land trust gives privacy and flexibility, since selling or refinancing can be done by the trustee without the owner appearing on a public record. The living trust supplies the plan.

This works well for rental owners with several parcels. Each parcel has its own land trust number, so a closing on one building does not touch the others. All the beneficial interests belong to one living trust, so there is one plan to read and one successor trustee to call.

For a single-family home you live in, a land trust is usually optional. The homeowner who wants probate avoidance and incapacity planning can get both from a deed straight into a living trust, and Illinois also allows a [transfer on death instrument](/learn/illinois/illinois-transfer-on-death-instrument) for the house alone.

## What should you check before moving a house into a land trust?

Run through this list first. It prevents the surprises we see after the fact.

1. **Call your lender.** A mortgage contract may require consent before title moves. Federal law protects transfers into a trust where the borrower stays the occupant, but lenders still want notice. The [transfer a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) page covers the mortgage rules.
2. **Update the insurance.** The land trust and the trustee should be named on the policy. Otherwise a claim can be disputed.
3. **Tell the county assessor.** Homeowner exemptions can continue after a transfer to a trust you benefit from, but some counties want an affidavit. See [how a trust affects homestead and property tax exemptions](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-affect-homestead-exemption-and-property-taxes).
4. **Read the death-of-beneficiary clause.** Add a successor beneficiary or an assignment to your living trust.
5. **Ask about fees.** Bank trustees charge an annual fee, and some charge to sign documents on request.
6. **Check the transfer tax exemption.** A deed into a trust for no consideration is generally exempt from Illinois transfer tax, but the deed must state the exemption.

## What does a land trust cost, and who keeps the records?

Expect a one-time setup fee when the deed and agreement are prepared, then an annual fee if a bank or title company is trustee. The trustee keeps the trust number, the deed and a record of who the beneficiary is. If the trustee is bought, merged or closes its trust department, the file moves to a successor, and you should make sure your copy of the agreement is current. Owners lose track of this more than anything else. We have seen families spend weeks trying to find out which institution holds title to a two-flat because nobody knew the trust number.

A simple habit helps: keep the land trust agreement, the recorded deed and the trustee's contact in the same binder as your [living trust](/learn/trusts), and note the trust number on your asset list.

## What about New Jersey?

New Jersey recognizes trusts that hold real estate, but the Illinois-style land trust with an anonymous beneficiary is rare. A New Jersey owner who wants a house to skip the Surrogate's court usually deeds it to the trustee of a [living trust in New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/living-trusts-in-new-jersey). The deed then names the trustee, and the trust document stays private. If you own property in both states, each parcel should follow its own state's practice.
