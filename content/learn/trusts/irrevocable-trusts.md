Most estate plans start with a revocable trust because you can change your mind. An irrevocable trust works the other way: you give something up on purpose, and in exchange you get a result that a revocable trust cannot deliver. That bargain is attractive in a handful of situations and a mistake in many others.

This article explains how irrevocable trusts work, the main types, what they cost you in flexibility, and how to tell whether one belongs in your plan. For the broader landscape, see our [guide to trusts](/learn/trusts).

## What makes a trust irrevocable?

An [irrevocable trust](/glossary/irrevocable-trust) is one where the person who funds it (the [grantor](/glossary/grantor)) gives up the power to revoke the trust, take the assets back, or change the beneficiaries. Legal ownership passes to the trustee, who must follow the trust terms for the beneficiaries.

That surrender is what produces the benefits. A [revocable trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is ignored for tax and creditor purposes because you can take the property back whenever you want. An irrevocable trust, if properly drafted and funded, is treated as a separate arrangement. Property in it may not count in your taxable estate, may not count for Medicaid, and may be out of reach of your creditors, depending on how the trust is built.

Two cautions. First, every benefit depends on drafting and on how much control you actually keep. A trust where you quietly keep the right to take assets back may not work. Second, some trusts that call themselves irrevocable carry limited powers, such as the right to swap assets of equal value, which are built in on purpose.

## What are irrevocable trusts used for?

### Estate tax planning

For 2026 the federal [estate tax](/glossary/estate-tax) exemption is $15 million per person, so most families will never owe federal estate tax. Irrevocable trusts for estate tax matter mainly for larger estates or for people in states with lower state exemptions. See [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and the overview of the [federal exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption).

Common estate tax trusts include:

- **Irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT).** The trust owns a life insurance policy so the death benefit is outside your taxable estate and can provide liquidity to pay taxes or equalize inheritances.
- **Grantor retained annuity trust (GRAT).** You transfer assets, receive annuity payments for a term, and the remainder passes to heirs with reduced gift tax.
- **Qualified personal residence trust (QPRT).** You transfer a home and keep the right to live in it for a term.
- **Spousal lifetime access trust (SLAT).** One spouse funds a trust for the other spouse and descendants, moving assets out of both estates while keeping indirect access through the spouse.

### Medicaid and long-term care planning

A properly drafted irrevocable trust, often called a Medicaid asset protection trust, can hold assets or a home so they are not counted when you apply for nursing home Medicaid. The transfer starts a look-back clock, which in most states is 60 months. Read about this in [Medicaid planning basics](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and the [five-year look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period). Because you must give up ownership and cannot simply take the money back, this works best for people planning well ahead of need.

### Providing for a person with a disability

A [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) holds money for a person who receives needs-based benefits like SSI and Medicaid. The money does not count toward eligibility limits, but only if the trust is drafted correctly. The distinction between a trust funded by a parent and one funded with the person's own money matters; see [first-party vs. third-party special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/first-party-vs-third-party-special-needs-trust).

### Protecting beneficiaries

You can leave an inheritance in a lasting irrevocable trust (often created at your death) with a spendthrift clause to shield it from a beneficiary's creditors, divorce or poor decisions. See [spendthrift and asset protection trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts).

### Charitable giving

Charitable remainder trusts and charitable lead trusts split income and remainder between you or your family and a charity, with tax advantages in some cases.

## What do you give up?

| What you gain | What you give up |
|---|---|
| Possible estate tax savings | Ability to take the asset back |
| Possible creditor or Medicaid protection | Control over investments (if trustee is independent) |
| Protection for vulnerable heirs | Ability to change beneficiaries |
| Removal of future appreciation from your estate | Possible step-up in basis at death |

The step-up in basis deserves emphasis. Assets you own at death usually receive a new, higher tax basis, which can wipe out capital gains for your heirs. Assets you gave away during life to an irrevocable trust usually keep your original basis. Giving away a house worth $700,000 that you bought for $150,000 could leave your children with a large taxable gain if they sell. Our article on [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains the math, and it is why many families with estates under the exemption skip irrevocable trusts for tax purposes. Some trusts are built with powers that allow inclusion in the estate to preserve the step-up, which is a technical drafting point for your attorney.

## What are the gift tax consequences?

Funding an irrevocable trust is often a gift. Gifts above the annual exclusion ($19,000 per recipient in 2026) use part of your lifetime exemption and generally require a gift tax return, although tax is typically not due. Some trusts are structured so contributions qualify for the annual exclusion; see [the gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion).

## Who should be the trustee?

Because you generally cannot be the trustee of an irrevocable trust without risking the intended result, you need someone independent. The best candidates are organized, impartial and willing to say no to a family member. Review [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) before deciding. Trustees owe [fiduciary duties](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), keep records, file trust tax returns and communicate with beneficiaries.

## Can an irrevocable trust be changed?

Rarely, and never casually. Possibilities include:

- **Reserved powers** written into the document, such as the power to substitute assets, or to change a trustee.
- **Decanting**, where a trustee moves assets into a new trust with updated terms, in states that allow it.
- **Nonjudicial settlement or consent** of all interested parties.
- **Court modification** when circumstances change or the trust's purpose is frustrated.

Rules differ widely. Some states have well-developed trust statutes and others have little. You should draft assuming that you will not be able to change it.

## How do you decide? A short checklist

1. What problem am I solving: estate tax, Medicaid, a vulnerable heir, or creditor risk?
2. Could a revocable trust, a will with a testamentary trust, or beneficiary designations solve the same problem more simply?
3. Am I comfortable losing access to these assets, including in a financial emergency?
4. What happens to income tax and basis?
5. Who will serve as trustee, and will they still be willing in 20 years?
6. Is the timing right, given look-back and gift reporting rules?

If the honest answer to the second question is yes, a simpler tool is usually better. Irrevocable trusts are for goals that cannot be reached any other way.

## An example

Walter, 74, has a $4.2 million estate, a house and a widowed sister he supports. He is not near the federal exemption, and he lives in a state with no estate tax, so he skips an estate tax trust. But he and his wife are worried about a long nursing home stay. After reviewing options with an attorney, they transfer their vacation condo to an irrevocable trust, accept the loss of a step-up on that property, and start the five-year clock while they are healthy. The point is not that everyone should do this, but that the trust has to match a defined goal.
