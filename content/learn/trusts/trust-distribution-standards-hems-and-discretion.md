Parents usually start with the question of age: when should my daughter get this money? The better question comes first, and it is who decides, and by what test. A trust that says "pay my son $50,000 at 30" is one kind of document. A trust that says "pay my son whatever the trustee considers appropriate for his health and education" is a very different one, even if the same dollars sit inside.

This article walks through the main distribution standards, shows how ages, conditions and incentives fit around them, and explains what to put in writing so a trustee is not left guessing. For the wider picture, start with our [trusts guide](/learn/trusts).

## What is a distribution standard?

A distribution standard is the instruction in a trust that says when the [trustee](/glossary/trustee) pays beneficiaries and how much. It sets the line between what the trustee must do, what the trustee may do, and what the trustee may not do. Every trust has one, even if it is only a single sentence.

There are three broad kinds.

| Standard | What the trustee does | Beneficiary's rights | Best when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mandatory | Pays fixed amounts or all income on a schedule | Can demand payment | The beneficiary is mature and you want predictability |
| Ascertainable (HEMS) | Pays for health, education, maintenance and support | Can ask; a court can review a refusal | You want flexibility with guardrails |
| Fully discretionary | Pays whatever the trustee thinks best | Cannot demand anything | The beneficiary is vulnerable, in debt or at risk |

Most real trusts mix them. A common design pays for HEMS needs while the child is young, shifts to a one-third payout at 30, and keeps the remainder in a discretionary trust for life.

## What does HEMS mean?

HEMS stands for health, education, maintenance and support. It is a term borrowed from tax law. The phrase "ascertainable standard" means a standard a court can measure, which matters for a technical reason: a beneficiary who is also the trustee can use HEMS-limited powers over their own trust without pulling the assets into their taxable estate. That is why you see HEMS in almost every trust that lets a child serve as trustee.

In practice, the pieces stretch further than people expect:

- **Health** covers medical, dental, mental health and treatment costs, including insurance premiums.
- **Education** reaches beyond tuition to books, housing, and often vocational or graduate study.
- **Maintenance and support** mean the beneficiary's accustomed standard of living, which can include rent, groceries and a car.

What HEMS does not cover well is a down payment on a home, seed money for a business, or a wedding. If you want those, say so. A sentence like "including the purchase of a primary residence" removes the argument.

A drafting note: HEMS is a floor for flexibility, not a mandate to pay. A trustee reading "may pay" still decides, and should consider the beneficiary's other resources unless the document tells them to ignore them. Whether to include or ignore outside income is a decision to make deliberately.

## When does full discretion make sense?

Full discretion gives the trustee the final word, and the beneficiary has no claim to any amount. That is its strength. A creditor, an ex-spouse or an addiction cannot demand something the beneficiary cannot demand either. Our article on [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts) covers the protection side in detail.

The weakness is entirely about people. Discretion works when the trustee is capable and fair and the beneficiary is willing to ask. It strains when the trustee is a sibling who controls the purse strings, or a child who feels micromanaged. Add a channel for disagreement: a [trust protector](/learn/trusts/what-is-a-trust-protector-and-decanting), a simple process to replace the trustee, or a letter of wishes that describes what you hoped the money would do. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) for the person-side of this decision.

## How do ages and staged payouts work?

Age-based payouts are the oldest design and the easiest to administer. The trustee can check a birth certificate and does not have to judge anyone's character.

Typical patterns:

1. **One age.** Everything at 30. Simple, but all the risk sits on one date.
2. **Staggered ages.** A third each at 25, 30 and 35, which gives time to recover from a bad first decision.
3. **Hold to a late age.** Nothing outright until 35 or 40, with HEMS payments in the meantime.
4. **Lifetime trust.** The money never comes out outright; the beneficiary receives distributions for life and the remainder goes to their children.

Some parents add a withdrawal right: the child may take half at 28 by giving notice, with the rest staying put. That respects the adult the child has become, and costs little. Ages in a trust also interact with state rules about when a minor takes property outright. Our article on [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) compares trusts with custodial accounts.

## Can you attach conditions or incentives?

You can, and the law gives you wide room, but the drafting is harder than it looks. The trustee owes the beneficiaries a [fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), so vague conditions put them in a bind. Typical conditions include finishing a degree, holding steady employment, completing treatment, or staying clear of drugs, with a testing protocol. Many courts enforce conditions if they are clear, lawful and not designed to control a marriage or religion in ways that offend public policy. A condition requiring a beneficiary to divorce, never marry, or marry within a faith is the type that draws litigation.

An incentive trust is a version that rewards effort. The trustee may match earned income dollar for dollar up to a cap, for example. The idea has appeal, but it has known failure points:

- Formulas can punish caregivers, artists, and anyone who chooses lower-paid but valuable work.
- A strict rule leaves the trustee no room when a beneficiary gets sick.
- A beneficiary who feels policed often resents the money.

Most of the trusts we see that work well use a softer approach. The trustee has the power to pay for education, job training and business ventures, and a statement of purpose that says what you hoped for. If you want hard conditions, leave an exception that lets the trustee waive them for illness or hardship. Our article on [conditions in a will](/learn/wills/conditions-on-an-inheritance-in-a-will) discusses how courts view them in general.

## An example

Priya and Marcus have two children, ages 7 and 10, and about $900,000 in life insurance and savings that would go to a trust if both died. They choose a single pot trust until the youngest turns 25. Until then, the trustee pays for health, education, and support under a HEMS standard, and may also pay for a first home or business start for either child up to a set amount. At 25, the trust splits into two shares. Each child receives a third at 28, a third at 33, and keeps the rest in a discretionary trust.

They add a statement of purpose: "We want our children to be able to take a chance on a career they love." That sentence does more work than any formula. A future trustee facing a request from a 26-year-old who wants to study ceramics or start a bakery has a clear signal about how Priya and Marcus would answer.

## How do you write this so a trustee can follow it?

A short checklist helps:

1. State the purpose of the trust in a paragraph, in plain words.
2. Pick the standard for each stage of life: childhood, young adulthood, later life.
3. Say whether the trustee should consider the beneficiary's other income and assets.
4. Name the trustee and a way to replace them.
5. List any special permissions, such as buying a home or funding a business.
6. Add a waiver for conditions in a medical emergency or hardship.
7. Review the document after any major life event.

Vague words cause the most trouble. "Responsible," "reasonable" and "mature" have no tested meaning in a dispute. If you cannot say how a trustee would verify a condition, rewrite it or drop it.
