Parents of young children usually worry about two things: who will raise them and who will manage the money. A [guardian](/glossary/guardian) answers the first question. A trust answers the second. If you leave money to a child directly, the law has very little to say about how it is spent once the child comes of age, and an 18-year-old with $250,000 can make choices that no parent would endorse.

This article explains how trusts for children work, common structures, the choices that matter most and a few pitfalls with life insurance and retirement accounts. For related reading, see our [overview of trusts](/learn/trusts) and the [guardianship cluster](/learn/guardianship).

## What happens if a child inherits without a trust?

Minors cannot legally manage significant property. If a child is named directly in a will or on a life insurance policy and no trust or custodian is set up, a court typically appoints a guardian or conservator of the child's estate. That person is often a parent but may need court approval for major decisions, post a bond, and file periodic accountings. The process takes time and the costs come out of the child's money.

When the child reaches the age set by state law, often 18 and sometimes 21, the court-supervised account usually ends and the child receives everything. If you have named a [UTMA custodian](/glossary/utma), the account passes to the child at the age set by your state's law, which varies from 18 to 25. Our article on [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) compares these options in more detail.

## How does a trust for children work?

You (the [grantor](/glossary/grantor)) set the rules in the trust document. A [trustee](/glossary/trustee) holds and manages the money and decides distributions according to those rules. The trust can be:

- **Part of a living trust.** If you have a [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust), it can include separate provisions that apply if you die while your children are young.
- **A testamentary trust.** A [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust) is created in your will and begins at your death. It usually goes through probate before it starts.
- **A standalone trust,** often paired with life insurance, such as an irrevocable life insurance trust.

The key point is the same in each case: the trust says who manages the money, what it can be used for, and when the children receive the rest.

## What are the common ways to structure it?

### Staged distributions by age

The trust pays out portions at ages you choose, for example one-third at 25, one-third at 30 and the balance at 35. Staging gives young adults a chance to learn from a first distribution while a remainder is still protected. A variation adds a right to withdraw earlier for specific goals like buying a home or starting a business with the trustee's approval.

### Discretionary trusts

The trustee decides when and how much to distribute, guided by a standard. The most common standard is health, education, maintenance and support. A discretionary trust gives maximum protection, but it requires an experienced, fair trustee and clear instructions about when the trust should end.

### Milestone-based trusts

You can tie distributions to milestones, such as completing a degree or holding a job. Be careful, because incentive provisions are hard to apply fairly. A clause that pays "matching earnings" can unintentionally penalize a child who becomes a teacher, a stay-at-home parent or a caregiver. Many attorneys recommend using milestones sparingly and giving the trustee flexibility.

### The "pot trust" and separate shares

For young children, many parents use a pot trust, a single fund held for all children together. The trustee pays whatever each child needs for education and support, and when the youngest reaches a set age (say 25), the remainder splits into equal shares. This avoids an older child getting less because a younger sibling had greater needs, but it also means shares can be unequal for a time. Some families prefer separate trusts, so each child's money is tracked individually from the start.

## What can the trustee pay for?

Typical language lets the trustee pay for:

1. Medical and dental care, including therapy
2. Education from preschool through graduate school, including living costs
3. Housing and basic living expenses
4. Special opportunities, such as a first home or business start-up, if you allow it

Be specific about whether payments can go to a parent or guardian to cover the child's costs. A trust can also say that distributions will not replace a legal parent's obligation to support the child.

## Who should be the trustee?

The trustee does not need to be the guardian, and sometimes should not be. A guardian is responsible for the child's daily life, and that person may be a loving grandparent who is not good with money. Others deliberately separate the roles so that no one person controls both the child and the funds. See [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee) and [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee). Consider a professional or co-trustee if the amounts are large, the trust will last many years, or family relationships are sensitive.

Name successors and include a mechanism to replace a trustee who is not working well. Tell the guardian and trustee how you expect them to coordinate, including how the guardian will request funds.

## How do life insurance and retirement accounts fit in?

A trust controls only the assets that are in it or that name it as beneficiary. If you buy life insurance to protect your children but name them directly, a court may need to appoint someone to hold the money. Better, name the trust (or your spouse first and the trust second) as beneficiary, and keep contingent beneficiaries current. See [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) for common mistakes.

Retirement accounts are more complicated, because a trust named as beneficiary must be drafted to qualify for the best payout rules. Under the SECURE Act most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years, with exceptions for a minor child of the owner until majority and for some others. A trust holding these accounts needs careful drafting. Read [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) before you sign.

## How is a children's trust taxed?

Income earned inside the trust is taxed either to the trust or to the child when distributed. Trusts reach the highest federal income tax bracket at much lower income than individuals, so trustees often distribute income to beneficiaries or invest tax-efficiently. For a trust that becomes irrevocable at your death, the trustee will obtain a tax ID and file annual returns. Inherited property usually receives a [step-up in basis](/glossary/step-up-in-basis). If you are curious about the broader tax picture, see [does a living trust save taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes).

## What about special circumstances?

- **A child with a disability.** Direct inheritance can disqualify them from SSI and Medicaid. Use a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) instead.
- **Stepchildren and blended families.** The trust must name stepchildren explicitly, because they are usually not heirs under intestacy rules. See [stepchildren inheritance](/learn/blended-families/stepchildren-inheritance).
- **A child you are worried about.** Addiction, debt or a poor marriage may call for a discretionary lifetime trust with a spendthrift clause; see [spendthrift trusts](/learn/trusts/spendthrift-trusts).
- **Unequal needs.** Dividing equally is not always fair. A trust can account for differing needs.

## How do you set one up? A short checklist

1. Decide how much each child should receive and how, at which ages.
2. Pick a trustee, a successor and a guardian, and confirm each is willing.
3. Choose standards for spending (HEMS, education, and so on).
4. Decide whether to use a pot trust or separate shares.
5. Update beneficiary designations on life insurance and retirement accounts.
6. Write a letter of wishes that explains your values and priorities.
7. Review the plan as children grow.

State laws vary on the age of majority for property, the UTMA age, and how long a trust may last. Our [state guides](/estate-planning/ohio) (for example, Ohio) outline local rules.
