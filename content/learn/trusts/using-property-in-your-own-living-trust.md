People hear that their house is "in the trust" and quietly worry that it is now locked away. They wonder whether they need permission to sell it, whether the bank will still talk to them, and whether pulling out $5,000 for a roof repair is allowed. For a revocable living trust the answer is simple: it is still your property, and you still decide.

This page covers the everyday moves, selling, renting, borrowing and withdrawing, and the small paperwork habits that keep them smooth. If you are earlier in the process, start with our overview of [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) or the steps for [funding a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust).

## The short version

- You can do with trust property what you could do with your own, as long as the trust document does not restrict you.
- You sign as trustee. Third parties ask for a certification of trust, not the whole document.
- Income, gains and losses stay on your personal tax return while the trust is revocable.
- Keep a clear record of what is in the trust, so nobody is guessing later.
- Different rules apply if the trust is irrevocable. This page is about the revocable kind.

## Why can you still do everything?

A revocable trust is a [living trust](/glossary/living-trust) you can change or cancel. The law treats the assets as yours for most purposes. You are the [grantor](/glossary/grantor) who made it, the [trustee](/glossary/trustee) who manages it, and the [beneficiary](/glossary/beneficiary) who benefits from it. That arrangement sounds odd, but it is the standard design, and it is why you do not lose control by signing.

The trust document names a successor who takes over if you become unable to act or die. Until then, the successor has no authority. Your daughter cannot sell the house just because her name appears in the document.

## How does selling trust property work?

Selling a house held in a trust looks nearly the same as any other sale, with one difference at the closing table. The deed that goes to the buyer is signed by you as trustee. A typical signature line reads "Joseph Reyes, Trustee of the Reyes Family Trust dated March 4, 2024."

The title company will want proof that you have the power to sell. A certification of trust does that. It is a short document, often one or two pages, that states the trust exists, who the trustee is, and that the trustee can sell real estate. It leaves out who inherits what. Buyers and lenders do not need to know that, and you do not have to show them.

Sale proceeds belong to the trust. You can deposit them in a trust account, or, if the money is only passing through, a personal account for a short time. The better habit is to deposit into the trust's name so the money does not wander out of the plan. Our article on [what assets belong in a living trust](/learn/trusts/what-assets-go-in-a-living-trust) explains which accounts are worth retitling.

## Can you rent out a house that is in your trust?

Yes. Rental income flows to you for tax purposes, and the lease can be signed by you as trustee. Three things deserve a phone call before the first tenant moves in.

1. **Insurance.** Ask your carrier to list the trust as an additional insured or named insured. A landlord policy that names only you can create a gap on a claim.
2. **The lease.** Use "trustee" in the landlord line, and keep your contact details on the notice section so tenants know who to call.
3. **Liability.** A trust does not shield you from a tenant's injury claim. If liability is the worry, an LLC may do more, and ask an attorney whether one makes sense before you decide.

## Can you borrow against property in a trust?

Yes, with a few practical points. Most lenders will refinance or extend a home equity line on a house held in a revocable trust. Some prefer the title to be moved back into your name for the closing and then returned to the trust afterward. A federal law protects you from a lender calling a mortgage due just because you transferred your home into your own living trust, as long as you live there. That protection is narrow. It does not clearly cover rentals or second homes, so ask your lender before you transfer a property with a loan on it.

The thing to avoid is quietly letting the deed drift back into your name for one loan and never putting it back in the trust. That is how people end up with a trust that holds nothing. A reminder in your calendar the week after closing is a cheap fix.

## Can you take money out of the trust?

You can, because it is your money. If the account is in the trust's name and you are the trustee, you write checks or transfer funds like before. The document may say the trustee can pay out income and principal to you "as you see fit," which covers nearly every ordinary case.

Consider Rosa, who is 66 and has a brokerage account titled in her trust. She moves $40,000 to her checking account to help her son with a down payment. The trust allows it, and no one has to approve it. What Rosa should think about is the gift. Moving trust money to her son is a gift, and gifts above the annual exclusion, currently $19,000 per recipient in 2026, call for a gift tax return even when no tax is due. Our page on the [gift tax annual exclusion](/learn/estate-tax/gift-tax-annual-exclusion) explains how that works.

## What changes when someone else is trustee?

If you step down or lose capacity, a successor trustee takes over and the rules become stricter. The successor must follow the document, keep trust money separate, and may only use funds for your benefit and that of the other beneficiaries. This is the point where a separate trust bank account and tidy records pay off. See [how to choose a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) for what to look for in that person.

## What about cars, accounts and household items?

Not everything needs a new title. A rough guide:

| Asset | Retitle to the trust? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| House or other real estate | Yes, by new deed | Largest probate item |
| Checking and savings | Usually yes | Easy to change, avoids probate for balances |
| Brokerage account | Yes, or name trust as TOD beneficiary | Often over small-estate limits |
| Car, truck, motorcycle | Often no | Simple transfer after death in most states |
| Retirement accounts | No, use beneficiary form | Moving them can trigger tax |
| Furniture, jewelry, art | Assignment of personal property | No title document exists |

Retirement accounts are the exception that surprises people. An IRA or 401(k) cannot be retitled into a living trust without tax consequences. You name beneficiaries on the account instead, and our page on [naming a trust as beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/naming-a-trust-as-beneficiary) covers when a trust should be one of them.

## What mistakes cause problems?

- **Signing as an individual.** A deed or account change signed in your own name after the asset moved to the trust can create confusion about who owns it.
- **Mixing funds.** Paying personal bills out of the trust account is fine for you. It becomes a problem later if a successor trustee has to untangle years of mixed records.
- **Forgetting new purchases.** A second house bought after the trust was signed is in your name unless you deed it over.
- **Assuming the trust is a creditor shield.** A revocable trust does not stop your creditors. Our guide on [creditors and living trusts](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-protect-assets-from-creditors) explains why.

## How state law changes this

Most states recognize a certification of trust by statute, so a bank or title company must accept it instead of demanding the full document. Rules for retitling vehicles, transfer taxes on deeds, and property tax reassessment vary. Start with your state guide, such as [California](/estate-planning/california), or talk to an attorney in your state before you move a house.
