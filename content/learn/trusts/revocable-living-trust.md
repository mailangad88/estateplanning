You may have heard that a living trust is the "gold standard" of estate planning, or that it is an expensive gimmick. Neither is quite right. A revocable living trust is a tool, and like any tool it fits some situations well and others poorly. This article explains how one works, what it does and does not do, and how to tell whether you are a good candidate.

If you want the bigger picture of how trusts fit together, start with our [guide to trusts](/learn/trusts), then come back here for the details on the most common type.

## What is a revocable living trust?

A [revocable living trust](/glossary/revocable-trust) is a written agreement that creates a separate legal holder for your property. Three roles matter:

- **The grantor** (also called the settlor or trustor) is the person who creates the trust and puts property into it. That is you. See [grantor](/glossary/grantor).
- **The trustee** manages the property for the benefit of the people named in the trust. While you are alive and well, you are usually the trustee.
- **The beneficiaries** are the people who benefit from the property. Often that is you during your life, then your spouse, children or others after your death.

"Living" means you create it while you are alive, as opposed to a [testamentary trust](/glossary/testamentary-trust) that is written into a will and only begins after death. "Revocable" means you keep the power to change it, move assets in and out, or cancel it entirely.

Because you keep that power, the law treats the trust assets as still yours for most purposes. That is why it saves no income tax and gives no creditor protection. What it changes is who has legal authority over the assets when you cannot act, and how the assets pass when you die.

## How does a revocable living trust work day to day?

For most people, very little changes. Take Maria, 58, who owns a house, a brokerage account and a rental condo. She signs a trust naming herself as trustee, then retitles the house, the brokerage account and the condo into "Maria Alvarez, Trustee of the Alvarez Family Trust dated March 3, 2026." She still lives in the house, collects the rent, and can sell any of it. Her tax return looks the same.

The trust document also names a successor trustee, say her daughter Elena. If Maria has a stroke, two doctors can certify that she can no longer manage her affairs (the document spells out the test), and Elena steps in to pay bills and manage the condo with no court hearing. When Maria dies, Elena follows Maria's written instructions and distributes the assets.

Retirement accounts are a common exception. Many people leave them in their own names because moving them could trigger tax, and instead review the beneficiary form on each one. Doing nothing about them is a mistake, since they are not covered by the trust.

The step that makes this work is [funding the trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust): changing the title on assets so the trust owns them. A trust that is signed but never funded controls nothing. This is the single most common way living trusts fail, and the fix is simple paperwork done at the start.

## What are the advantages of a revocable living trust?

### Avoiding probate

Assets titled in the trust do not go through [probate](/glossary/probate), the court process for settling a will. That matters because probate can take many months, is generally public, and can be expensive in some states. If you own real estate in more than one state, a trust can also avoid separate [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) proceedings in each one. Our guide to [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) compares the trust with other methods, some of which are cheaper.

### Planning for incapacity

A will does nothing while you are alive. A trust (together with a [durable power of attorney](/glossary/durable-power-of-attorney)) lets someone you trust manage your property if you become ill or develop dementia. Without that planning, your family may need to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator, a public and often slow process. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

### Privacy

A probated will becomes a public court record, so anyone can see who inherited what. A trust is a private document. Beneficiaries are entitled to certain information, but the world is not.

### Control over timing

A trust lets you say when and how heirs receive money: all at once, in stages at ages 25, 30 and 35, or only for specific needs like education. That is useful for [children and young beneficiaries](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children), for heirs who struggle with money, and for [blended families](/learn/blended-families) where you want to provide for a spouse now and your children later.

### Harder to contest

Courts review trusts too, but a will contest happens inside an already public probate case. Trust provisions are harder for a disappointed relative to find and, in practice, often harder to challenge, particularly when the trust was signed with clear capacity and good records.

## What are the disadvantages?

- **Cost.** A trust plan costs more up front than a simple will. See [how much estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost) for typical ranges.
- **Funding work.** You must retitle assets and keep doing so when you buy a new house or open an account. Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance are handled separately.
- **No tax savings.** A revocable trust is tax neutral. Our article on [whether a trust avoids taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes) covers the myths.
- **No creditor protection.** Assets you can take back are assets your creditors can reach.
- **No Medicaid qualification.** Assets in a revocable trust are generally counted when Medicaid reviews your finances. See [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).
- **Some friction with institutions.** Banks, brokerages and lenders have their own paperwork for trust accounts, though this is routine for them.
- **Ongoing maintenance.** A trust should be reviewed when your family or assets change. Our list of [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) is a good starting point.

## Revocable living trust vs. will: a quick comparison

| Feature | Revocable living trust | Will |
|---|---|---|
| Takes effect | When signed and funded | At death |
| Avoids probate | Yes, for funded assets | No |
| Helps if you become incapacitated | Yes | No |
| Private | Yes | No, once probated |
| Names guardian for minor children | No (needs a will) | Yes |
| Typical cost | Higher | Lower |
| Can be changed | Yes | Yes |

The full comparison, with examples of who should choose which, is in [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust).

## What mistakes make a trust fail?

- **Never funding the trust.** Signing the document is only half the job. See our [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist) for what to retitle.
- **Forgetting new assets.** A house bought five years later in your own name is outside the trust.
- **Naming the trust as beneficiary of a retirement account without advice.** This can work, but the trust language needs to fit the tax rules for inherited retirement accounts.
- **Picking a successor trustee without asking them.** The job takes time and organization.
- **Never updating it.** Marriage, divorce, a death in the family or a move to a new state are common reasons to review.

## What does it cost and how long does it take?

Costs vary widely by state, by attorney and by how complex your family and assets are. Many firms charge a flat fee for a package that includes the trust, a pour-over will, powers of attorney and healthcare documents. The drafting itself often takes a few weeks. Funding can take longer, because banks and county recorders each have their own process.

## Who is a good candidate?

A revocable trust tends to make the most sense if one or more of these are true:

1. You own real estate, especially in more than one state.
2. You want to plan for the possibility that you or your spouse will need help managing money.
3. You have minor children or a beneficiary who should not receive money outright.
4. Your family is blended, or you want to keep your plan private.
5. You live in a state where probate is slow or costly.

A trust is less necessary if your assets are modest, mostly pass by beneficiary designation, and you live in a state with a simple probate process or a [small estate procedure](/learn/probate/small-estate-affidavit). In that case a well-drafted will, power of attorney and healthcare documents may be enough.

## How do you set one up? A step-by-step overview

1. **Inventory your assets** and list how each is titled and who is named as beneficiary.
2. **Decide who gets what, and when.** Think through outright gifts, staged distributions and any special instructions.
3. **Choose your successor trustee** and a backup. Our guide to [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) covers what to weigh.
4. **Sign the trust and supporting documents** (pour-over will, powers of attorney, healthcare directive) following your state's signing rules, typically with a notary.
5. **Fund the trust** by retitling the house, bank and brokerage accounts, and assigning personal property.
6. **Coordinate beneficiary designations** on retirement accounts and life insurance so they match your plan.
7. **Tell your trustee where the documents are** and keep a copy somewhere safe.

## What happens to the trust after you die?

At death, the trust becomes irrevocable. The successor trustee obtains a tax ID number for the trust, notifies beneficiaries, inventories assets, pays debts and taxes, and makes distributions. Duties are detailed in our guide to [trust administration](/learn/after-a-death/trust-administration). A trustee has a legal duty to act in the beneficiaries' interest, a [fiduciary duty](/glossary/fiduciary-duty), and can be held accountable if they do not.

State rules vary on notice periods, creditor claims against trust assets and how long administration takes, so ask about your state's process. If you want to see what your state's rules look like, browse our [state guides](/estate-planning/california) such as California, where living trusts are especially common, or look up your own state.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Is a trust a good fit for my situation, or would a will and beneficiary designations be enough?
- Will you prepare the deeds to move my real estate into the trust?
- How does my state treat trusts for property tax, homestead and title insurance?
- Should my retirement accounts name the trust, my spouse or my children?
- What happens if I move to another state?
- Does your fee include help with funding?
