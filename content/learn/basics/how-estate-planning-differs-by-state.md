Ask three friends in three states how their estate plans work and you will hear three different stories. One says probate took four months and cost almost nothing. Another says a trust is a must. The third says nobody they know has had a problem. All three can be right, because the rules they are working under are not the same.

This article explains where state differences matter, where they do not, and what to do about them. It is one of the most common questions on our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) page, and the answer starts with a simple split between federal and state.

## What is federal and what is state?

Congress sets the federal estate and gift taxes. For 2026 the basic exclusion is $15 million per person, and the annual gift exclusion is $19,000 per recipient. Federal law also governs the rules for inherited retirement accounts and how Medicaid treats transfers. Almost everything else belongs to the states.

State law decides who can make a will and how many witnesses are needed. It decides whether a handwritten will counts, how probate works, and how long creditors have to file claims. It sets who inherits without a will and what a surviving spouse is entitled to. It also governs how powers of attorney and healthcare directives are signed, and how trusts are run. Fifty places, fifty versions.

## Which rules differ the most?

| Area | What varies | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Will signing | Witness number, notary or self-proving affidavit, handwritten wills | A will valid in one state may be questioned in another |
| Probate | Court procedures, small-estate limits, timelines, fees | Cost and speed of settling an estate |
| Spousal rights | Elective share, homestead, community property | How much a spouse can claim whatever the will says |
| Intestacy | Order of heirs when no will exists | Who gets what for unmarried people and blended families |
| Powers of attorney | Witnessing, gifting powers, agent duties | Whether a bank will accept the form |
| Healthcare forms | Witness rules, statutory forms, how agents are named | What hospitals will honor |
| State taxes | Estate tax, inheritance tax, both, or neither | Whether a smaller estate owes tax |
| Real estate | Transfer-on-death deeds, homestead protections | How a house can pass without probate |

Our guide to [state estate and inheritance taxes](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) shows how widely taxes vary. A number of states have an estate tax with a threshold far below the federal $15 million, and some have an inheritance tax that depends on who receives the money. Most states have neither.

## Is estate planning different in community property states?

It is. Nine states, Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin, treat most property acquired during a marriage as owned equally by both spouses. Alaska lets couples opt in. In those states, a spouse typically cannot leave the other spouse's half of the community to someone else, and each spouse's half gets treated on its own terms.

For couples, that changes how you title assets and what a will does. It can also affect income tax basis at death, which sometimes gives a benefit on both halves of community property. Moving into or out of a community property state creates questions of its own. Our page on [community property](/glossary/community-property) defines the term, and a lawyer in your state can tell you how it plays out with your house.

## What if you move?

Your will generally stays valid if it was valid where you signed it. Many states recognize a will that met the rules of the state where it was signed. But a plan that was correct in one state may be less effective in another: a spouse's rights can change, a healthcare directive may need to match local forms, a trust may need a different tax approach, and your named agents may be a long way away.

Nina and Paul move from Pennsylvania to Arizona at 62. Their wills are fine on their face. But they now live in a community property state, their house deed was written under Pennsylvania assumptions, and their old healthcare forms use a different layout. None of that is an emergency, and all of it is worth a review. Our guide on [wills when moving states](/learn/wills/will-moving-states-or-living-abroad) walks through it, and the page on [advance directives in other states](/learn/healthcare-directives/do-advance-directives-work-in-other-states) covers medical forms.

As a rule, review your plan within a year of any move.

## What if you own property in more than one state?

Real estate is governed by the law of the state where it sits. If you die owning a house in a state other than your home state, that state's court may need to be involved, which is called ancillary probate. A living trust that holds the out-of-state property often avoids it. Read [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property) and [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) for how that works.

## Can you use an attorney from another state?

For work that depends on a state's law, you want a lawyer licensed there. A lawyer in one state may be able to give general guidance about another, but a will, a deed or a trust drafted for a state's rules should be prepared or checked by someone licensed in it. Remote meetings and electronic signing make it easy to work with a lawyer anywhere, so geography is rarely the obstacle. Licensing is.

When you own property in several states, ask the attorney how the plan will be coordinated. One firm may handle everything, or it may bring in a local colleague.

## What is the Uniform Probate Code?

It is a model law, and some states have adopted large parts of it. It aims to make probate simpler and more uniform, with options for informal probate without much court supervision. Even states that adopted it have made changes, so it does not mean the rules match from state to state. If you read an article that says "under the UPC," check whether your state follows it.

## Is any state better for estate planning?

People ask because they hear about low-tax states and asset protection. Taxes matter only if your estate is large enough to reach a state threshold. For most families, what costs money or time is probate, and you can often limit that in any state with a trust, beneficiary forms and transfer-on-death deeds. The best-fitting plan is the one built on your state's law and your family's facts. See your own state's page, such as [Texas](/estate-planning/texas), [Florida](/estate-planning/florida) or [Illinois](/estate-planning/illinois), for specifics.
