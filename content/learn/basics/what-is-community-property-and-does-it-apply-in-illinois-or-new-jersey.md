If you have ever read about a divorce in California or an inheritance in Texas, you have run into the phrase "community property." It sounds as though it might apply to every married couple. It does not. Most states, including Illinois and New Jersey, use a different system.

This page explains what community property is, why it matters to estate planning, and what to check if you live in Illinois or New Jersey but have ties to a community property state. It is part of our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) series.

## What is community property?

Under community property, income earned and property acquired by either spouse during the marriage belongs to both spouses equally. The paycheck of one spouse is half the other's the moment it is earned. Neither spouse needs to be on the title for the other to own half.

Separate property is the exception. In these states you generally keep what you owned before you married, plus gifts and inheritances you receive in your own name. Mixing separate property with marital money, for example by depositing an inheritance into a joint account, can blur the line. We deal with the inheritance question in [is an inheritance marital property](/learn/after-a-death/is-an-inheritance-marital-property).

The nine states most often listed are Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin. Wisconsin's system is called marital property, and it works much the same way. Some other states let couples opt in by signing a community property agreement or using a trust.

## How do Illinois and New Jersey handle marital property?

Both follow the common law approach, under which ownership follows title. If a house is deeded to one spouse, that spouse owns it. A bank account in one name belongs to that person. The couple does not own half of each other's earnings during the marriage.

Protection comes at the edges instead.

| | Community property states | Illinois | New Jersey |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who owns earnings during marriage | Both spouses equally | The spouse who earned and titled it | The spouse who earned and titled it |
| Divorce | Generally an equal split of community property | Court divides marital property fairly, not always equally | Court divides by equitable distribution |
| Surviving spouse left out of the will | Already owns half of the community property | Can renounce the will and take one-third with descendants, one-half without | Can elect one-third of the augmented estate |
| Time limit to claim | Varies | 7 months after the will is admitted | Six months after a personal representative is appointed |

The details are in our articles on [Illinois spouse rights at death](/learn/illinois/illinois-spouse-rights-at-death) and [the New Jersey elective share](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-elective-share-for-a-surviving-spouse). The practical consequence is that, in these two states, a spouse who wants to protect the other must use titling and documents to do it. The law will not do it automatically.

## Why does community property matter to your estate plan?

There are three ways it comes up, even if you never lived in a community property state.

**You moved here.** A couple who bought a home and built savings in Texas, then retired to New Jersey, may still own property that was community property. Rules on whether it keeps that character vary. Some states have statutes to deal with it, and many have case law. If you are unsure, keep dated records of what was earned and where, and have your documents reviewed. Our article on [moving states without updating your plan](/learn/what-if/moved-states-plan-not-updated) covers the rest of that checklist.

**You own real estate there.** A vacation condo in Arizona is governed by Arizona law. If you die as an Illinois resident, your estate may face a second court case there, called [ancillary probate](/glossary/ancillary-probate), under Arizona rules about community property and your spouse's half. See [out-of-state property](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

**You might move.** If your plan was written in Illinois and you relocate to California, your spouse's rights shift. A will that gives your spouse everything works well there, but one that leaves a share to children assumes you own all of what is titled in your name. In a community property state you might only own half.

## Is there a tax advantage?

Yes, and it is the reason community property comes up in advice for surviving spouses. At the first spouse's death, the tax basis of the asset generally steps up to its date-of-death value for both halves of community property, not just the half that belonged to the person who died. With other joint ownership, only the decedent's share steps up (and, for spouses, usually one half).

An example, with invented numbers. Carlos and Elena bought a rental house for $200,000 in California. It is worth $600,000 when Carlos dies. If it is community property, the survivor's basis generally becomes $600,000. If the same house had been held as ordinary spousal joint tenants outside a community property state, Elena's basis would generally be about $400,000 (her original half of $100,000 plus $300,000 for Carlos's half). She would face a gain on any sale above that figure. The difference of $200,000 in basis can mean thousands of dollars of tax.

Basis is a federal income tax concept. It is a separate subject from federal estate tax, which has an exclusion of $15 million per person in 2026, or from state taxes: Illinois taxes estates over $4 million, and New Jersey has an inheritance tax, with spouses and children exempt.

## What should you do about it?

1. **Identify where you have lived** since you married, and where you own property. List each state.
2. **Sort assets** into things earned during the marriage in a community property state, things you brought in, and gifts or inheritances.
3. **Check titles.** Ask whether your deed or account says "community property" or "community property with right of survivorship."
4. **Consider a written agreement** with your spouse that says how you treat the property now. This is a legal document, so have it drafted.
5. **Update your will or trust** so that it matches the property you actually own. Our guide on [how estate planning differs by state](/learn/basics/how-estate-planning-differs-by-state) shows the variables.
6. **Review beneficiaries.** Retirement accounts and life insurance follow their own forms, and federal rules often override the state's community property rules for some accounts.

### Common mistake

Treating an account as the surviving spouse's alone because it was opened after the move. Where the funds originated can matter more than where the account sits.

## Can a couple opt in to community property?

A few states, among them Alaska, Tennessee and Kentucky, let couples elect community property treatment by agreement or through a trust set up under their laws, mainly for the tax reason above. Whether a couple living in Illinois or New Jersey can use one successfully is a technical question, and the answer turns on where the trust is sited and what property goes into it. It also has tradeoffs for creditors and divorce.

For most families the effort is not worth it. If your estate is well under the federal threshold and the property has not grown much in value, the basis benefit is small. If you have a large appreciated asset, such as a business or a rental building, ask an attorney and a tax advisor to run the numbers for your case before you move or retitle anything.
