You may be reading this because a friend just went through a rough probate, a new baby arrived, or a doctor's appointment reminded you that nobody else knows where anything is. Estate planning can sound like something for the very rich. It is not. It is a short list of decisions that you either make yourself or leave for the state and your family to make for you.

This guide explains what an estate plan is, what it includes, who needs one, and what happens if you skip it. For the full picture of how the pieces fit together, start with our [estate planning overview](/learn/basics).

## What is estate planning?

Estate planning is deciding in advance, and putting in writing, who will handle your affairs and receive your property when you cannot or when you die. It answers four questions:

1. **Who inherits what you own?** Your house, savings, retirement accounts, car, jewelry and keepsakes.
2. **Who manages things if you are alive but unable to?** Someone to pay the mortgage, file taxes and deal with the bank.
3. **Who makes medical decisions if you cannot speak for yourself?** And what treatment would you want or refuse?
4. **Who cares for your children, and who manages the money you leave them?**

Your "estate" is simply everything you own at death: real estate, bank and brokerage accounts, retirement plans, life insurance (in some cases), vehicles, business interests, and personal property. The legal term is [estate](/glossary/estate), and everyone has one.

## What does an estate plan include?

A typical plan combines several documents, each doing a different job. Our guide to [the core estate planning documents](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) walks through each in detail. In brief:

| Part of the plan | What it does | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| [Will](/learn/wills) | Names who inherits, names an executor, names a guardian for minor children | After death |
| [Revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) (optional) | Holds assets and passes them to beneficiaries without court involvement | During life and after death |
| [Durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) | Lets a trusted person handle money and property for you | During incapacity |
| [Healthcare directive](/learn/healthcare-directives) | Names a healthcare agent and records your treatment wishes | During incapacity |
| [Beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) | Direct retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death accounts | After death |

Notice that three of the five apply while you are still alive. That surprises people. A will does nothing for you if you are in a coma; a power of attorney and a healthcare directive do.

## How does your property actually pass at death?

Your property passes in one of three ways, and knowing which way each asset travels is the heart of estate planning.

1. **By beneficiary designation.** Life insurance, 401(k)s, IRAs and many bank accounts pass to whoever is named on the account form. The will does not control these.
2. **By how the asset is titled.** A house held as joint tenants with right of survivorship goes to the surviving owner. Assets titled in a trust follow the trust's instructions.
3. **By your will.** Anything left over, titled in your name alone with no beneficiary, passes under your will through probate.

This matters because the form beats the will. If your will says "split everything equally between my three children" but your life insurance names only your oldest, the oldest gets all the insurance.

For example, Daniel and Grace, both in their late thirties, have two children, ages 4 and 7. They own a home, two 401(k)s and a term life policy. A basic plan for them might include:

- Wills that leave everything to each other first, then to the children, and name Grace's sister as guardian.
- A trust for the children, so they do not receive a lump sum at 18.
- Beneficiary forms that name each other first, then the children's trust as the backup. Naming the minor children directly could force a court to appoint someone to manage the money.
- Financial and healthcare powers of attorney that name each other, with the sister as backup.

Without a plan, a court would choose the guardian, and each child would likely receive their share outright at 18.

## Who actually needs an estate plan?

Every adult benefits from some of these documents, but certain situations make a plan urgent rather than merely sensible.

### Parents of minor children

If both parents die or become unable to care for a child, a court decides who raises that child unless you have named a guardian. Naming one is often the single most important thing a young family does. See our [guide for parents on naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship) and [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents).

### Homeowners

A house is usually the largest asset a family owns, and it is the asset most likely to be pulled into court. How you title it, and whether you add a transfer-on-death deed or a trust, determines how easily it passes on. See [leaving your house to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children).

### Unmarried partners

Intestacy laws generally favor spouses and blood relatives. A partner of 20 years, without a will or beneficiary forms, may inherit nothing and may have no right to make medical decisions. Our page on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) covers the specifics.

### Blended families

If you have children from a prior relationship and a current spouse, a plan that is silent on the question can disinherit one side by accident. See [estate planning for blended families](/learn/blended-families).

### Business owners

An owner's death or illness can freeze a business within days. A succession plan and a buy-sell agreement keep it running. See [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

### Someone caring for a family member with a disability

An inheritance left directly to a person on SSI or Medicaid can end their benefits. A [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) avoids that.

### People with higher net worth

The federal estate tax only affects estates above the basic exclusion amount, currently $15,000,000 per person as of 2026. Most families are far below it, though some states have lower thresholds. Details are in our [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax).

## What happens if you die without a plan?

Every state has default rules, called [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession), that decide who gets your property if you have no valid will. The rules usually split your estate between a surviving spouse and children, or pass it to parents, siblings and more distant relatives in a set order. The rules do not know about the friend you wanted to help, the charity you supported, or the child who cared for you.

Consider Daniel, 62, divorced and living with his partner, Priya. He dies with a house in his name, a bank account and a 401(k) that still names his ex-wife. Without a will, his two adult children inherit the house; Priya has no claim and may have to move out. The 401(k) goes to the ex-wife because the form controls. Every piece of this could have been prevented with a few documents. Read more about [what happens when someone dies without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

Beneficiary forms cause trouble in other ways too. An ex-spouse may still be named on an old 401(k). In some states divorce revokes that automatically, but federal rules for employer plans can override state law.

The incapacity side is just as important. If you suffer a stroke and have no power of attorney, your family may have to petition a court for [guardianship or conservatorship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship). That process is public, takes weeks or months, and puts a judge in charge of whom to appoint.

## How does estate planning work step by step?

Most people complete a plan in a few weeks. The usual path is:

1. **Take inventory.** List what you own, what you owe, and how each asset is titled and who is named as beneficiary.
2. **Decide on people.** Choose your executor, agents, trustee and guardian, plus backups for each.
3. **Decide on outcomes.** Who gets what, whether kids inherit outright or in trust, and what medical care you would want.
4. **Meet with an attorney.** Bring your inventory; see [what to bring to your first meeting](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation).
5. **Sign correctly.** Wills and powers of attorney have witnessing and notary rules that vary by state.
6. **Fix the paperwork outside the documents.** Update beneficiary forms, retitle assets into a trust if you have one, and tell your agents where the documents are.
7. **Review it on a schedule.** See [when to update your plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## Do you need a lawyer, or can you do it yourself?

Online forms and kits exist, and for a very simple situation a carefully completed form may be better than nothing. The risks are real, though: signing errors that invalidate a will, forms that conflict with your state's law, trusts that are never funded, and beneficiary designations that contradict the plan. The cost comparison is covered in [how much an estate plan costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost), and our guide on [choosing an attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) lists what to ask.

## Is estate planning expensive or complicated?

Neither, for most families. A basic plan is a handful of documents, and the complexity grows only with your circumstances: a second marriage, a business, a child with special needs, property in several states. The cost of skipping a plan tends to be higher: court fees, lost time, family conflict, and outcomes you would not have chosen. Many of the avoidable errors are collected in [common estate planning mistakes](/learn/basics/common-estate-planning-mistakes).
