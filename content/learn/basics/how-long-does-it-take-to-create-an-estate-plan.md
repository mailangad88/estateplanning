You have decided to do it, and the next thing you want to know is how long the process will take. The honest answer is two to four weeks for a plan built on a will, and four to eight weeks for one built on a living trust. Those numbers come from how the work is sequenced, and you control more of the schedule than you might expect.

Below is the path from first call to finished plan, a worked example with dates, and the things that stretch the timeline. If you are still deciding whether you need a plan at all, start with our [estate planning basics guide](/learn/basics).

## What are the stages of making an estate plan?

There are six stages, and only two of them are the attorney's drafting time. The others depend on you and on third parties such as county offices.

1. **Intake.** You schedule the consultation and fill in a questionnaire about your family, property and wishes. Allow a few days to a week. Most people take longest here because the form asks about things they have never written down.
2. **Consultation.** One meeting of an hour or two, in person or by video. The attorney explains your choices and you make the big decisions. Our list of [what to bring to the meeting](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) shortens this stage.
3. **Drafting.** The attorney prepares the documents. For a will-based plan this is often a few business days. A trust, with its schedules and deeds, takes longer.
4. **Review.** You read the drafts, ask questions and request changes. This stage has no fixed length because it depends on how soon you reply.
5. **Signing.** A formal appointment with witnesses and a notary. The signing rules are in [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) and [New Jersey will requirements](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-requirements).
6. **Follow-through.** Deeds are recorded, beneficiary forms updated, and copies delivered to the people who need them.

## How long does each type of plan take?

The length of your plan depends mostly on whether a trust is involved and how many places the work has to touch. This table gives realistic ranges from first meeting to a finished file. They are estimates for ordinary situations, not promises.

| Plan | Typical time to signing | What can add time |
|---|---|---|
| Will, power of attorney, healthcare directive | 2 to 4 weeks | Slow replies on choices of agent or guardian |
| Same, plus guardian nominations for minors | 2 to 4 weeks | Family discussion about who should raise the children |
| Revocable living trust plan | 3 to 5 weeks | Collecting deeds, account numbers and the trust schedule |
| Trust plan plus retitling the house | 4 to 8 weeks | County recording time, lender or title questions |
| Plan with a special needs trust | 4 to 8 weeks | Benefit rules, trustee choice, and review of the child's programs |
| Plan with real estate in two states | add 1 to 2 weeks | Local deed wording and a second recorder |

## A worked example with dates

This example shows how a trust plan actually unfolds. Dev and Priya, both 46, live in Naperville, Illinois. They own a house, two 401(k)s and about $60,000 in a joint savings account, and they have two children under 12.

- **Monday, Week 1:** Priya calls and books a consultation for the following Tuesday. The intake form arrives by email.
- **Friday, Week 1:** They return the form but leave the guardian question blank because they disagree about Priya's sister.
- **Tuesday, Week 2:** The consultation takes about ninety minutes. They settle on Priya's sister as guardian and Dev's brother as backup. They choose a living trust so the house avoids probate.
- **Thursday, Week 3:** Drafts arrive. The attorney also requests their current deed, which they need to dig out of a file box.
- **Monday, Week 4:** They send two small corrections. Signing is set for Thursday.
- **Thursday, Week 4:** They sign the trust, pour-over wills, powers of attorney and directives. The deed is signed too.
- **Weeks 5 and 6:** The deed is recorded with the county. They update the beneficiary forms on both 401(k)s, naming the trust as contingent beneficiary on one.

Total: about four weeks to signing, six to a finished file. Notice where the time went. The attorney's drafting took a few days. The slack came from a guardian decision and a missing deed.

## What slows an estate plan down?

Four things cause nearly every delay, and all of them can be fixed ahead of time.

**Unmade decisions.** Choosing a guardian, an executor or an agent feels heavy, so people put it off. The fix is to name a first choice and a backup now. Your plan can be amended later, so a decent choice today beats a perfect one next year.

**Missing paperwork.** The attorney needs the legal description of the house, which is on the deed and not on the tax bill. They also need account types and balances. Retirement account statements and the policy numbers for life insurance help too. Our guide to [inventorying your assets](/learn/basics/how-to-inventory-your-assets-and-value-your-estate) walks through it.

**Family complications.** A second marriage, a child with a disability, a business or a property in another state all add drafting and review time. They are also the cases where a plan matters most.

**Waiting on third parties.** Counties record deeds on their own schedule. Some recorders turn documents around in days, others in weeks. A lender or title insurer may ask questions about a house going into a trust. See [how to transfer a house to a living trust](/learn/trusts/transfer-house-to-living-trust) for what to expect.

## How can you finish faster?

You can often cut a week or more by doing the following before the consultation.

1. Write down your executor, agent, healthcare agent and guardian choices, each with a backup.
2. Find the deed to your home and confirm whose names are on it.
3. Make a one-page list of accounts, with the institution and the type of account, not the passwords.
4. Pull your beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance.
5. Decide, roughly, who gets what. Equal shares to children is a fine starting point.
6. Book the signing appointment at the same time as the review call so it does not drift.

## What if you cannot wait?

When someone is about to have surgery or has received a serious diagnosis, the attorney can work in layers. The power of attorney, healthcare directive and a simple will can often be signed in days. The trust, deeds and fine tuning follow. Our article on [planning after a serious diagnosis](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-a-serious-diagnosis) lays out which documents go first.

The signing itself has to be done properly. Both Illinois and New Jersey require two witnesses for a will. A rushed signing with the wrong witnesses can undo the whole effort. Capacity matters too, and we explain how it is judged in [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).

## Does the plan finish at signing?

No, and this is where the plan most often fails quietly. A trust only controls what has been put into it. After signing, the house needs a recorded deed, the bank accounts need to be retitled or given a beneficiary, and the life insurance and retirement forms need to match your plan. Until then, those assets still go the old way. Our guide to [funding a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) lists each step.

Set a calendar reminder for thirty days after signing and check three things: the deed is recorded, each account has the right title or beneficiary, and your agents have copies of their documents. If you cannot say yes to all three, the plan is not finished.
