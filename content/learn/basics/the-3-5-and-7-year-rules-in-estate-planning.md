Someone told you there is a "three year rule" or a "five year rule" for estate planning, and they were sure about it. Then you searched and found three answers that did not agree. That is normal. The numbers belong to different parts of the law, and people compress them into one catch phrase.

This page sorts out which year rules are real in the United States, what each one covers, and which one should worry you. It sits in our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) library, and where a rule has its own full guide, we link to it.

## Which year rules actually exist?

Here is the short map. Match your situation to the row, not to the number.

| Rule | Where it comes from | What it covers | Who it matters to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 years | Federal estate tax | Gifted life insurance and gift tax paid just before death | Large estates, insurance trusts |
| 5 years | Medicaid | The look-back on gifts when you apply for long-term care help | People who may need a nursing home |
| 5 years | Retirement account law | Payout deadline for some inherited accounts, and the Roth clock | Account owners and heirs |
| 7 years | United Kingdom tax | Gifts made before death | Not U.S. families, unless they have UK ties |
| 10 years | SECURE Act | Emptying most inherited IRAs | Adult children who inherit IRAs |

The 10-year line is there because people mix it up with the others. It is its own rule, covered in our guide to the [inherited IRA 10-year rule](/learn/beneficiary-designations/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-yearly-withdrawals).

## What is the 3-year rule?

It is a federal estate tax rule that reaches back three years from the date of death. Generally, a gift you make during life is out of your estate the day you make it. A few things are the exception, and the best known is life insurance.

Suppose Raymond, 71, owns a $2 million term-to-permanent policy on his own life. He thinks the smart move is to give the policy to his daughter so the death benefit stays out of his estate. If he dies two years later, the IRS treats the full $2 million as part of his estate anyway, because he gave the policy away within three years of death. If he lives past the three-year mark, the proceeds are generally outside the estate.

The same window catches gift tax. If you paid federal gift tax on a gift and die within three years, that tax is added back into your estate. Very few families see this, because the lifetime exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026 and most gifts never produce a tax bill.

Two points make the rule less scary than it sounds.

- It does not make ordinary gifts of cash or stock come back. A completed gift of those assets stays out of the estate even if you die the next day. What you gave still uses exemption, though.
- Many people avoid the issue by having a trust buy the policy to begin with, so there is never a transfer to count. That is the idea behind an [irrevocable life insurance trust](/learn/estate-tax/irrevocable-life-insurance-trust).

If your estate is well under the exemption, the 3-year rule is usually academic. It matters in planning for the few families near the line, and our guide to [lifetime gifts and the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/do-lifetime-gifts-count-toward-the-estate-tax) shows how the pieces add up.

## What is the 5-year rule?

When most people say it in an estate planning conversation, they mean the Medicaid look-back. When you apply for Medicaid to pay for a nursing home, the state reviews the previous 60 months of your finances in most states. Gifts or sales below fair value during that window can trigger a penalty period when no benefits are paid. California handles its look-back differently, so check your state.

The penalty is not a flat five years. It is calculated by dividing the amount given away by a state figure for the average monthly cost of a nursing home, and the result is the number of months you are ineligible. A larger gift buys a longer wait. Our full guide to the [Medicaid look-back period](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-look-back-period) walks through the math, and the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) will show you the dates for your own case.

Here is how it plays out. Elena, 79, gives her son $90,000 in January. In the fall she has a stroke and needs a nursing home. She applies in November. The state sees the January gift inside the 60 months and counts it. Had she made the gift six years earlier, it would not have shown on the application at all.

Two other five-year rules turn up in this conversation, and they have nothing to do with Medicaid.

1. **Inherited accounts.** If a retirement account owner dies before required withdrawals begin and the beneficiary is not a person, such as the estate or a charity, the account generally has to be emptied by the end of the fifth year after death. A person named as beneficiary usually follows the 10-year rule instead.
2. **Roth IRAs.** Earnings come out tax free only after the account has been open five tax years and the owner reached 59 and a half, died or became disabled. The clock follows the owner's first Roth, not each new account.

## What about the 7-year rule?

It is British. Under UK inheritance tax, many gifts drop out of the taxable estate if the giver lives seven years after making them, with a sliding scale in the years between three and seven. Because articles about it travel well, plenty of Americans assume the same clock runs here.

It does not. The U.S. does not free a gift after seven years. A taxable gift is reported when you make it, counted against your lifetime exemption, and the tax is figured then. If you are a U.S. citizen or resident with property here, that is the system you are in. If you live in the UK, hold property there or inherit from someone who does, talk to a cross-border tax professional about both countries.

The seven year figure also appears in a few unrelated places, like some state rules on asset transfers or old planning folklore. If someone cites seven years to you, ask them to name the statute or agency.

## How do you decide which clock applies to you?

Use these steps before you give anything away.

1. **Name the goal.** Are you trying to cut estate tax, qualify for Medicaid, or simply help your child now? Each goal has a different clock.
2. **Check your estate against the exemption.** If your estate is far below $15,000,000 (or $30,000,000 for a married couple using portability), the 3-year rule probably does not touch you.
3. **Ask whether care is a realistic possibility.** A parent in their late 70s with a chronic condition should treat 60 months as a live issue. Someone healthy at 50 should not give away their home over it.
4. **Look at basis.** A gift keeps your tax cost basis, while inherited property usually gets a [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). Giving a house to save a Medicaid penalty can cost the child capital gains tax later.
5. **Get the paperwork right.** A gift needs a deed or a transfer form, and a dated record of its value.

## Common mistakes with the year rules

- **Giving away a home too late.** A parent who transfers a house after a diagnosis often finds the gift lands inside the look-back.
- **Treating "five years" as a magic number.** Waiting five years does nothing for estate tax and does nothing for a gift that was never a completed transfer.
- **Keeping control.** If you give a house but keep the right to live in it and manage it on your terms, a tax authority may treat the gift as incomplete.
- **Forgetting that Roth date.** Heirs cannot reconstruct it later if no one wrote it down.
