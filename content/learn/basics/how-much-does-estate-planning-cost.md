People often put off estate planning because they assume it costs thousands of dollars, or they choose a cheap online form without knowing what they are giving up. Neither assumption is quite right. The price depends on what you need, and the cheapest option on the day you sign is not always the cheapest option over a lifetime.

This guide explains typical pricing models, what drives the price, how DIY compares, and how to judge value. It is general information; actual prices vary by state and firm, so treat the figures here as orientation, not a quote. For the bigger picture, see our [estate planning guide](/learn/basics).

## What does an estate plan typically cost?

Costs vary so widely that a single number would mislead. The ranges below describe how the market is generally structured as of 2026.

| Option | Typical structure | What you get | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Online will or forms | Low fixed fee or subscription | Template documents you complete yourself | Errors in signing, wrong state rules, no advice |
| Attorney: simple will package | Flat fee, hundreds to low thousands | Will, powers of attorney, healthcare directives | Fewer protections if your situation is not simple |
| Attorney: trust-based plan | Flat fee, generally higher than a will package | Trust, pour-over will, powers of attorney, directives, funding help | Trust ineffective if assets are not retitled |
| Attorney: complex planning | Hourly or tiered flat fee | Tax planning, irrevocable trusts, business succession, special needs | Cost grows with scope |
| Probate-only help (later) | Statutory, percentage or hourly | Settling an estate after death | Often more than planning would have cost |

Couples typically pay less than double what a single person pays, since many documents are drafted together and the meeting is shared.

## Flat fee or hourly: how do attorneys charge?

**Flat fees** are standard for ordinary plans. You know the price before you start, and the quote should list the documents included. The benefit is predictability; the thing to check is scope. "Will package" at one firm may include powers of attorney and healthcare directives, while at another each costs extra.

**Hourly rates** are used for open-ended work: reviewing a complicated existing plan, negotiating with a business partner, advising on estate tax, or resolving a dispute. Ask for an estimate and how often you will be billed.

**Consultations** are sometimes free, sometimes a modest fee credited toward the plan. Ask when you book.

## What drives the price up or down?

- **Complexity of family:** A blended family, a child with a disability, or a beneficiary with creditor problems requires more drafting.
- **Type of plan:** Trusts need more documents, plus funding work.
- **Assets:** Business interests, real estate in several states, and large retirement accounts all add work.
- **Location:** Rates reflect local market costs, and some states require more formalities.
- **Tax planning:** Estates near a tax threshold need more analysis. The federal exemption is $15,000,000 per person as of 2026, but some states tax estates at lower levels.
- **How organized you are:** Arriving with an inventory shortens meetings and, on hourly work, lowers the bill. See [what to bring to your first meeting](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation).

## Is a living trust worth the extra cost?

The honest answer is that it depends on where you live and what you own. A trust costs more now. A will costs less now but leaves your heirs to pay for [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) later. Probate costs vary enormously: in some states fees are modest and the process is streamlined; in others, statutory attorney and executor fees are tied to the estate's value, and the process can take a year or more. See [how much probate costs](/learn/probate/probate-costs) and [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate).

Consider two examples. Alicia, 70, owns a paid-off house worth a substantial sum in a state with expensive probate. A trust may cost her family less in total. Ben, 34, rents an apartment, has a 401(k) with a named beneficiary and a small savings account. A will package and powers of attorney are probably sufficient. Our comparison of [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) goes through the factors.

## What about DIY and online documents?

Online document services and do-it-yourself kits are cheaper upfront and fit some situations. Their limits are structural:

1. **They do not know your facts.** A form cannot ask whether your house is jointly titled, whether your spouse's 401(k) beneficiary form conflicts, or whether your state has a community property rule that affects your wishes.
2. **Signing errors invalidate documents.** Witness and notary requirements vary by state, and a will that fails them can be rejected. See [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) and [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills).
3. **Trusts must be funded.** A DIY trust that never gets retitled assets is an expensive piece of paper.
4. **Nobody can fix it later.** Errors typically surface when you have died or become incapacitated, at which point you cannot correct them.

DIY is a reasonable stopgap for someone who would otherwise do nothing, and a signed, valid simple will beats having none. It is a weak fit for homeowners with children, blended families, unmarried partners and anyone with a business.

## How do you compare quotes?

Ask each firm the same questions and put the answers side by side:

1. Which documents are included, by name?
2. Are powers of attorney and healthcare directives in the price?
3. Does the fee include helping me retitle assets and update beneficiary forms?
4. Who signs documents, and are witnesses and notary included?
5. What are the costs after signing: recording fees, updates, a later amendment?
6. Is there a charge for the first meeting?
7. Who will actually draft the documents, and will I talk to an attorney?

Our guide on [choosing an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) has a longer list of questions about experience and process.

## What does skipping a plan cost?

The price of no plan is paid by your family. Typical costs include court filing fees and attorney fees for probate, court-supervised guardianship if you are incapacitated, a longer timeline before anyone can sell or transfer property, and the personal cost of disputes among relatives. None of these is certain to be large, but all are avoidable at a price that is usually known in advance. The bill for a plan is predictable; the bill for not having one is not.

## How can you keep the cost down without cutting corners?

- Gather information first: asset list, account statements, deeds, beneficiary forms.
- Decide on your key people (executor, agents, guardian) before the meeting.
- Ask about a package that bundles all the core documents.
- Do the follow-up work promptly. The funding and beneficiary steps are where plans succeed or fail.
- Review on a schedule rather than redoing everything. See [when to update your plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).
