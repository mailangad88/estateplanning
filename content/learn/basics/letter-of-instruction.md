When someone dies, the family usually knows the big things: there is a house, there was a bank. What they do not know is which bank, whether there is a safe deposit box, who prepared the taxes, what the person wanted for a service, or whether anyone is feeding the cat. A letter of instruction answers those questions in one place, in your own words.

This article explains what the letter is and is not, what to put in it, what to keep out, and how to store it so the right person can find it. It sits beside your legal documents, which are listed in [the core estate planning documents](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist), and does not replace any of them.

## What is a letter of instruction, and is it legally binding?

A letter of instruction is a plain, informal document, usually a few pages, addressed to your family and [executor](/glossary/executor). It carries no legal force in most situations. It is not a will, and a court will generally not enforce it the way it enforces a signed and witnessed will or a funded trust.

That limit matters. If you write "my sister gets the lake cabin" in a letter and your will says everything goes to your two children, the will usually controls. The letter cannot override a will, a trust, or a beneficiary form on a retirement account or life insurance policy. Our guide to [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) explains which assets pass outside the will, and the [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers how to make gifts that hold up.

What the letter does well is practical. It tells your executor where to look, whom to call, and what you would like done. Your executor still needs the court-issued authority described in [executor duties](/learn/after-a-death/executor-duties), but a good letter can shorten the early weeks considerably.

## How does it relate to your other documents?

Each document has a job. Mixing the jobs is where families get hurt.

| Document | Legally binding? | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Yes, if signed and witnessed to state rules | Names an executor and guardian, makes gifts of probate assets |
| Revocable living trust | Yes, for assets in the trust | Manages and distributes trust property |
| Beneficiary designations | Yes | Direct retirement accounts, insurance and payable-on-death accounts |
| [Living will](/glossary/living-will) and healthcare agent form | Yes, under state law | State your medical wishes and who decides |
| Letter of instruction | Generally no | Explains where things are, whom to call, what you hope for |

If the letter ever conflicts with a legal document, update one of them so they agree. A mismatch will not change the outcome in court, but it can start an argument at a hard time.

## What should a letter of instruction include?

Cover what a stranger would need to step into your shoes for a month. Most letters work best when they are organized by section, with short entries rather than paragraphs.

| Section | Prompts | Who needs it |
|---|---|---|
| People to call | Attorney, accountant, financial advisor, insurance agent, employer's benefits contact, clergy | Executor, spouse |
| Where documents are | Original will, trust, deed, titles, birth and marriage certificates, military discharge, tax returns, keys | Executor |
| Accounts and debts | Banks, brokerages, retirement plans, life insurance, mortgage, credit cards, loans, pensions | Executor, spouse |
| Regular bills | Utilities, subscriptions, insurance premiums, property taxes, what is on autopay | Whoever pays bills |
| Digital life | Phone and computer access, password manager, email, photo storage, social media preferences | Executor, family |
| Funeral and burial | Burial or cremation, service type, prepaid plan, cemetery plot, music, readings, obituary notes | Family |
| Personal items | Who should have heirlooms, jewelry, photos, tools, with the stories behind them | Family, executor |
| Pets and property care | Vet, food, who has agreed to take the animal, house, car, storage unit, mail | Family |
| Business or side income | Partners, clients, licenses, where the books live | Executor, partner |
| Messages | What you want people to know | Whoever you choose |

### Contacts and document locations

Start with the people. An attorney who drafted your documents, an accountant who knows your returns and an insurance agent who knows which policies exist can each save your executor hours. Then list where originals are kept: the fireproof box, the filing cabinet, the attorney's vault. For a will, see [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).

### Accounts, debts and bills

List institutions and account types, not full numbers. "Checking and savings at First Federal, joint with Dan" is enough for an executor to start. Include debts, because executors have to deal with them; [dealing with debts in probate](/learn/probate/dealing-with-debts-in-probate) explains how they are paid. Note any bills that run on autopay, since those continue after a death until someone stops them.

### Digital access

Phones are often the hardest thing to open. Say which device holds your authenticator app and where the unlock method is explained. Name your password manager, and give your executor a way in that does not depend on a note in a drawer. Our [passwords and access guide](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) and the overview of [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan) go into legal access rules.

### Funeral, burial and personal wishes

Funeral wishes are the part families most appreciate. Say whether you want burial or cremation, a religious service, a gathering, or none. Mention any prepaid arrangement and where the contract is. A family member who has to decide in a day, with no guidance, often second-guesses the choice for years.

For personal items, describe who should have what and why. These notes work as a guide to your intentions. If a gift matters, put it in the will; some states allow a will to refer to a separate signed list of tangible items, and others do not, so ask an attorney in your state.

## What should you leave out?

A letter that is too complete can become a risk. Leave these out or handle them differently.

- **Master passwords, PINs and full account numbers.** A lost or copied letter becomes a fraud tool. Point to a password manager instead.
- **Gifts you intend to be binding.** Put them in the will or trust, or on a beneficiary form.
- **Anything that contradicts your legal documents.** If you have changed your mind, update the documents with an attorney first.
- **Surprises you would rather deliver in person.** A letter read after a death cannot answer questions. Our guide to [talking to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) covers how to share the hard parts while you can.

## Where should you keep it, and who should know?

Keep one copy with your will, and give a second copy to your executor or a trusted family member. Tell the person who will need it that it exists, and where. A letter no one finds is worth nothing.

Be cautious about relying on a safe deposit box alone. Access rules after a death vary by bank and state, and your family may need paperwork before they can open it, which defeats the purpose of a document meant to help in the first days. If you store the letter digitally, make sure the person who needs it can reach it without your password.

## How often should you update it?

Review it once a year, at a regular date such as a birthday or tax time. Update it sooner when you open or close an account, change advisors, move, remarry, lose a pet or change your funeral wishes. [When to review and update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists the life events that call for a broader check, and the letter belongs on that checklist. Date every version, and destroy old copies.

## A short template to start from

1. **Heading.** Your full name, the date, and "Letter of instruction. This is not a will."
2. **Who to call first.** Name, relationship, phone, email for three people.
3. **Professionals.** Attorney, accountant, advisor, insurance agent.
4. **Where documents are.** One line each, with location and who has keys.
5. **Accounts and debts.** Institution, type, and a note on who else is on the account.
6. **Digital.** Devices, password manager, how your executor can reach it.
7. **Funeral and burial.** Your wishes and any prepaid plans.
8. **Personal items and pets.** What matters and to whom.
9. **A message.** Optional, in your own voice.
10. **Signature and date.** Not required legally, but it shows which version is current.

### Is this the same as an ethical will or a letter of intent?

Not quite. An ethical will shares values, stories and hopes, and is often written as a gift to children or grandchildren. A letter of intent most often describes the needs of a person who will depend on others for care; see [the letter of intent for special needs planning](/learn/special-needs/letter-of-intent). You can combine a message with the practical letter, but keep the practical sections easy to find.
