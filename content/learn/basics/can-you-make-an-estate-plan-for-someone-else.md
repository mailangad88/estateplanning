Your mother has been saying for years that she will get to her will. Now she has had a fall, her memory is slipping, and you are the one who keeps finding unpaid bills on the kitchen table. You want to fix it. The tempting thought is to just do it for her: pick a form, fill it in, and have her sign.

Most of the time that is the wrong move, and sometimes it is an illegal one. This article explains what you can and cannot do for another adult, how to help in ways that hold up, and what changes if your parent can no longer decide for themselves. It sits alongside our [basics library](/learn/basics) and our guide to [helping an aging parent get their plan in order](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan).

## The short version

- Estate planning documents belong to the person making them. Someone else cannot sign a will for a capable adult.
- Your role is helper: gather, schedule, drive, listen, ask questions.
- Let the attorney meet your parent alone for part of the visit.
- If capacity is already in doubt, move quickly and carefully. Timing and documentation matter.

## Why can't you make a will for another adult?

A will is a statement of what one person wants to happen with their own property. The law requires that the person have testamentary capacity, which means understanding in a general way what they own, who their family is and what the document does. It also requires that the wishes be the person's own, free from pressure.

If a child chooses the beneficiaries, a court has no way of knowing whether the plan reflects the parent or the child. Courts are cautious here, because the child who drafts the plan is often the child who benefits from it. A will built that way is a common target in will contests, and the child may end up spending far more fighting for it than a proper plan would have cost. Our article on [will contests](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will) explains the usual grounds.

There is one narrow exception worth knowing about. Many states allow another person to sign a will on the testator's behalf if the testator directs it, is present, and the formal witnessing is done. That is for a person who cannot hold a pen. It does not mean a relative can decide the terms.

## What can you do for a parent who is still capable?

You can do nearly everything except decide. Here is what that looks like in practice.

1. **Start the conversation gently.** Frame it around what you need to know to help, not around what they will leave you. Our guide on [talking to family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) offers phrasing that tends to work.
2. **Gather information.** Help your parent list accounts, insurance, property and contacts. The [consultation checklist](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) shows what an attorney will want.
3. **Find an attorney, and let your parent choose.** Offer two or three names and let them pick. Our page on [choosing an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) lists questions to ask.
4. **Attend if invited, and step out when asked.** Many parents want help hearing and remembering. The attorney still has to confirm that the instructions are your parent's.
5. **Pay attention to who pays.** If a parent pays the bill from their own funds, the arrangement is cleaner than if a child pays and then stands to benefit.
6. **Follow up.** Make sure the signed documents are stored, copies are where they should be, and beneficiary forms match the plan.

Take Marcus, 55, whose father Walter, 82, is sharp but tired. Marcus makes the call, sends a one-page list of Walter's accounts to the office, and drives him in. After greeting everyone, Marcus waits outside while the attorney spends thirty minutes with Walter alone. Walter names Marcus and his sister Dana as joint executors, which the attorney then reads back in front of both. Walter signs, and nobody can say it was Marcus's idea.

## Why will the attorney ask you to leave the room?

Because the attorney's client is your parent, and only your parent. The attorney has an ethical duty to take instructions from the client and to be satisfied that those instructions are voluntary. A child sitting in the room can make that harder to confirm, even when the child means well. This is routine, and good lawyers do it for spouses too.

It also protects you. If a sibling challenges the plan, the attorney's notes will show that your parent spoke for themselves. That record is often the best evidence the plan will hold. See our article on [whether a living trust can be contested](/learn/trusts/can-a-living-trust-be-contested) for how courts weigh this evidence.

## What are the warning signs of undue influence?

Undue influence means that someone took over a person's decisions so completely that the document reflects the influencer's wishes rather than the signer's. Courts look at several factors together:

- A person in a position of trust, such as a caregiver or the child handling finances.
- That person helped arrange the documents, picked the lawyer or sat in on the meeting.
- The person benefits unusually, especially compared with earlier plans.
- The signer is isolated, ill, dependent or easily confused.
- The change was sudden and secret.

One of these alone usually means little. A parent who likes the child who drives them, and says so, is not under undue influence. If several appear at once, speak to an attorney, and read our guide on [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse). The glossary entry for [undue influence](/glossary/undue-influence) gives the short definition.

## What if your parent can no longer make decisions?

The answer depends on what already exists.

| Situation | What is possible |
|---|---|
| Parent has a signed power of attorney and healthcare directive | The agents can act now, within the documents. They still cannot make a will. |
| Parent has capacity but no documents | An attorney can prepare them quickly. Meet while capacity is clear. |
| Capacity is borderline | An attorney may work with a doctor's letter and document the meeting carefully. |
| Parent has lost capacity and has no documents | A court guardianship or conservatorship is usually the path. |
| Parent has no will and loses capacity | State intestacy rules decide who inherits unless a court allows something else. |

The question of capacity is not all or nothing. A person can be unable to manage a stock portfolio and still understand a short will. A person with early dementia can often sign documents on a good day. We explain this in [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) and in [what to do when a parent with dementia has no power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/parent-with-dementia-no-power-of-attorney).

If a guardianship is needed, the court appoints someone to make decisions, files accountings, and keeps the matter on a public record. It costs more and takes longer than signing documents. [Power of attorney vs. guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) compares the two.

## What can an agent under a power of attorney do?

An agent can pay bills, manage accounts, file taxes, deal with insurers and, if the document allows, handle real estate. An agent cannot write the principal's will, change the beneficiary on an account for the agent's own benefit unless the document expressly allows it, or make gifts beyond the document's authority. See [what a power of attorney cannot do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-a-power-of-attorney-cannot-do) for the full list.

## How state law changes this

States differ on signing for another person, on the standard for capacity, on whether a guardian can change a will, and on the formalities for powers of attorney. Some states have a statutory short form, and others do not. If you are helping a parent in a state other than your own, look at that state's [guide](/estate-planning/california) or ask a local attorney.
