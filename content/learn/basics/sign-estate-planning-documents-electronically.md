You may be reading this because a family member is out of state, because you are recovering at home, or because an online service promises that you can finish a whole estate plan in your pajamas. The question is reasonable: if you can sign a lease on your phone, why not a will?

The short answer is that estate planning documents are treated more strictly than ordinary contracts, and each type has its own rule. This article, part of our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) library, goes through them one at a time, explains what a remote notary actually does, and gives you a safe way to proceed if you cannot get to an office. For the broader signing rules on wills, see our guide to [how to make a legally valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will).

## The short version

- Check the document type first. The answer for a will is not the answer for a power of attorney.
- A notary watching you over video is different from a witness watching you over video. Many states allow the first and fewer allow the second.
- If you cannot visit an office, a mobile notary or a signing at your home with two neighbors as witnesses is accepted in nearly every state.
- Keep the original, and know who holds it.

## Why are wills treated differently from other documents?

Wills are the one document the general e-signature laws step around. The federal ESIGN Act and the state versions of the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act make electronic signatures count for most contracts, but both exclude wills, codicils and trusts created by a will. The reasoning is old: a will takes effect when the person who signed it can no longer explain what they meant, so courts want strong proof that the signing was real and voluntary.

That leaves each state to decide whether it will accept an electronic will at all. A number of states have passed laws, some based on the Uniform Electronic Wills Act, and others rely on narrower rules or have nothing. Illinois, for example, has a specific statute with its own steps, described in our [Illinois will requirements](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-requirements) guide. If your state has no such law, a will signed only on a screen may simply fail.

Even where electronic wills are legal, they come with extra steps when the time comes to prove them. A court may ask for a tamper-evident record, an audit trail from the signing platform, or testimony about how the file was stored. Those are tools your executor may not know how to produce ten years from now.

## What does a remote online notary actually do?

Remote online notarization, often called RON, is a notary who sees you and your photo ID by live audio and video, checks your identity through the platform's tools, and applies an electronic seal. Most states now permit it, either by permanent law or through rules adopted after 2020.

It does not do what people hope. A notary confirms who you are and that you signed willingly. A notary is not a witness to the contents, and a notary does not make a will valid by sealing it. If your will needs two witnesses, those witnesses still have to meet your state's rules, whether they are in the room or on a screen.

Remote notarization also has practical limits. The notary is usually required to be in their own commissioned state when they perform the act. The platform keeps a recording, and the notary's state sets how long. Some county recorders accept electronic notarizations on deeds and some still want paper. When a deed will be recorded, ask the recorder's office what it accepts before the signing, not after.

## Which documents can be signed electronically?

| Document | Electronic signature | Remote notary | Common catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will | Only where a state electronic wills law applies | Does not replace witnesses | Original often needed in court; remote witnessing limited. |
| Revocable living trust | Often allowed under general e-signature law | Often allowed where notarization is needed | Funding steps, such as deeds, may still need paper. |
| Financial power of attorney | Often allowed | Often allowed | Banks may insist on their own form or a wet-ink original. |
| Healthcare power of attorney and living will | Many states allow it | Varies; witness rules matter | Hospitals sometimes want a clean paper copy. |
| Beneficiary forms | Almost always, through the institution's site | Not usually needed | Confirm the update posted. |
| Deed | Depends on county recorder | Depends on county recorder | Recording office sets the standard. |

Two notes on this table. It describes general patterns, not any one state, and it will change as laws are updated. And "allowed" means the signature is legally possible, not that the document will work well when someone needs it.

Take a power of attorney. Rosa, 71, signs a durable financial power of attorney through an online service and her daughter Elena later takes it to a bank to pay a hospital bill. The bank's legal department asks for the notarized original, or a form on its own letterhead. Rosa's signature may have been perfectly legal and Elena is still stuck for two weeks. For that reason, many of our clients choose to sign powers of attorney in person even when the law lets them skip it. See [why a bank might refuse a power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/bank-refuses-power-of-attorney) for more.

## Can a remote signing go wrong?

Yes, and usually in one of three ways.

1. **A required witness was not allowed to witness remotely.** The will looks fine and a court later refuses it, or the family has to go through extra hearings to prove it.
2. **The platform did not match the state.** A service built for real estate closings may not follow the rules for wills in your state.
3. **Nobody stored the original.** The document lives in an account that closes when the subscription ends or the person who set it up dies.

A related risk is pressure. When you sign across a table, a lawyer or a notary can see who else is in the room and whether you seem to understand what you are signing. When you sign alone on a screen, there is more room for someone to hover or help in ways that cross a line. Capacity and voluntariness matter more as people get older, and our article on [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives) explains why we like a calm, documented in-person signing in those cases.

## What if you cannot get to an office?

Plan around the problem rather than forcing the technology. Here is a path that works for most households.

1. **Ask your attorney what the state allows.** A good estate planning attorney will know whether e-wills and remote witnessing apply to you and will tell you if the answer is no.
2. **Use a mobile notary or a signing at your location.** Your attorney can send a staff member and a notary to your home, a hospital room or a care facility. It is ordinary and widely accepted.
3. **Choose witnesses carefully.** Use two adults who are not beneficiaries and who will remember the signing. Ask them to sign the self-proving affidavit, which usually saves them from having to testify later. Our explanation of the [self-proving affidavit](/learn/wills/self-proving-affidavit-explained) covers why it matters.
4. **Put the original somewhere known.** Tell your executor where it is. See [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
5. **Do the other half of the job.** After signing, update beneficiary forms and retitle assets as needed. A trust that is signed but never funded does little, and our page on [what happens when a trust is never funded](/learn/what-if/unfunded-trust) shows how that plays out.

If you live in one state and your attorney is in another, the attorney needs to be licensed where the document will be used or work with someone who is. The signing rules that count are generally those of the state where you sign, and sometimes where you live, so say clearly where you will be on the day.

## How state law changes this

Three kinds of rules vary. The first is whether the state has an electronic wills statute. The second is whether remote witnessing is allowed for wills or only for other documents. The third is how the state treats a notary who is in a different state from the signer. None of these has one national answer.

If you live in Illinois, our [Illinois estate planning guide](/learn/illinois) lists the signing statutes in detail. For other states, start with the [state guide](/estate-planning/texas) for yours, or ask us. We would rather tell you "come in for twenty minutes" than let you sign something that a court may reject.

## Are online will and trust services legally binding?

The documents they produce can be valid, because validity comes from your state's signing rules and not from the website. Some services give only a form, some include notary or witness features, and some will review your answers with a lawyer. Before you sign, check four things: whether the service explains your state's signing steps in plain words, whether it lets you download the finished document, whether it says what happens to the file if you stop paying, and whether anyone reads your answers for conflicts with your beneficiary forms.
