You have booked the appointment. Now the question is what to carry in. Showing up with a stack of the right papers and a short list of decisions turns a first meeting from an interview into real progress, and it can cut your total cost, especially if the firm bills by the hour.

This guide gives you a practical list. It is a companion to [choosing an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) and the [overview of estate planning](/learn/basics).

## What should you bring to an estate planning consultation?

Bring three things: **paper** (documents and statements), **numbers** (a rough inventory), and **decisions** (names and wishes). If you can only do part of it, do the inventory and the names.

### The inventory

For each asset, write the type, approximate value, how it is titled (your name alone, joint, in a trust), and whether it has a beneficiary designation.

| Category | What to list | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Real estate | Address, estimated value, mortgage balance, how title is held | Determines how it passes and whether probate is needed |
| Bank accounts | Institution, type, balance, any POD designation | Payable-on-death accounts skip your will |
| Retirement accounts | IRA, 401(k), pension; custodian and balance | Pass by beneficiary form |
| Investments | Brokerage accounts, stocks, bonds; titling | May carry transfer-on-death registration |
| Life insurance | Insurer, policy type, face amount, owner, beneficiaries | Death benefits pass by beneficiary form |
| Business interests | Entity name, ownership percentage, operating agreement | Agreements may override your will |
| Vehicles and valuables | Cars, boats, jewelry, art, collections | Often in dispute if not addressed |
| Digital assets | Email, cloud storage, crypto, online accounts | Need access instructions |
| Debts | Mortgage, loans, credit cards, taxes owed | Paid from your estate before heirs inherit |

### The paper

- Existing wills, codicils, trusts, powers of attorney and healthcare directives
- Deeds for any real estate (or the address, so your attorney can pull records)
- Most recent statements for major accounts
- Beneficiary designation forms or screenshots from each account's website
- Life insurance policy summaries
- Business documents: operating agreement, shareholder agreement, buy-sell agreement
- Prenuptial or postnuptial agreements, divorce decrees and settlement agreements, if any
- Any trust documents you are a beneficiary of
- Prior-year tax return, which shows most accounts at a glance
- A government photo ID

### The people

Write down full legal names, addresses and relationships for your spouse or partner, children (including adult children, stepchildren and any child from a previous relationship), and anyone else who would expect to inherit. Add anyone you want to include, such as a godchild or a charity.

## What decisions should you think about beforehand?

You do not need final answers, but thinking through these questions saves time.

1. **Who will be your executor?** The person who settles your estate. Choose a backup too. See [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
2. **Who will be your financial agent?** Under a [durable power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), this person manages your money if you cannot.
3. **Who will be your healthcare agent?** Often the same person as the financial agent, though it does not need to be.
4. **Who will be guardian of your minor children?** Talk to the person first if you can. See [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian).
5. **Who will manage money you leave to children?** The trustee, who is a different role from the guardian. See [guardian vs. trustee](/learn/guardianship/guardian-vs-trustee).
6. **Who gets what?** Equal shares to children? Specific gifts? Money for a charity? Special treatment for a child with a disability?
7. **At what age should children inherit?** Many people choose staged distributions rather than a lump sum at 18.
8. **What medical care would you want or refuse?** A rough sense of your views on life support and resuscitation helps the attorney draft a [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will).

## What family situations should you flag?

Mention anything that might complicate the plan. Attorneys will not judge, and these facts change the advice:

- A second marriage, or children from different relationships. See [blended families](/learn/blended-families).
- A child who has a disability, receives benefits, or struggles with money, addiction or creditors
- A child you want to treat differently from others, or one you do not want to inherit
- A relative who might contest the plan
- An unmarried partner, or a long-term caregiver you want to provide for
- Property in more than one state or country
- A business, farm or professional practice
- Expected inheritances or large gifts you may receive

Lisa, 52, a widow with two adult sons, did not mention at her first meeting that one son had a gambling problem. Her attorney drafted equal outright inheritances. Six weeks later she mentioned it, and the plan changed to a trust with an independent trustee. Tell the attorney early.

## What should you do if you cannot find everything?

Bring what you have. Missing items are common and fixable:

- **Beneficiary forms:** Log in to each account, or call the plan administrator and ask for the beneficiary designation on file.
- **Deeds:** Many counties have online property records, or your attorney can order a copy.
- **Old documents:** If you think a will exists and cannot find it, tell the attorney; it may be in a safe deposit box, with a former lawyer, or on file with a court.
- **Account lists:** A credit report and your last tax return are good reminders of what you hold.

## How should you handle passwords and digital accounts?

List accounts and note where access information is stored, but do not write passwords on documents you hand to others. A password manager with an emergency access feature is one common approach. See our article on [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan), and [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) for specifics.

## What should you expect at the meeting?

Most first meetings follow this order:

1. The attorney explains how the process works and confirms who the client is.
2. They ask about your family, finances and goals.
3. They discuss options and recommend a plan.
4. They explain the fee and next steps.
5. You agree on a timeline for a draft and a signing meeting.

You are not expected to sign anything at the first meeting. Take notes, ask for plain-language explanations, and ask what follow-up steps will fall to you. The list of core documents in [our checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) is a helpful reference for the conversation.

## What happens after the meeting?

Expect a draft within a few weeks. Read it with the people you have named in mind: does each name, spelling and share look right? Ask about anything you do not follow. After signing, complete the follow-up work, which usually includes retitling assets and updating beneficiary forms, and store the originals where your executor can reach them. Our article on [when to update your plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) explains how to keep it current.
