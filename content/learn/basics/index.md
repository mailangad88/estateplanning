Estate planning is how you tell your family, your bank, your doctors and a court what you want before anyone has to guess. It sounds like a project for wealthy people near the end of life. In practice it is for anyone who has people who depend on them, property they care about, or opinions about their own medical care.

This is the main guide to the site. It orients you across the whole subject: what a plan is, what it contains, how the major topics relate, and where to go for depth. Each section links to a more detailed guide, so read what applies to you and skip the rest.

## What is estate planning?

Estate planning is making decisions in advance, in legally valid documents, about who will handle your affairs and receive your property. It addresses four questions: who inherits, who manages your money if you cannot, who makes medical decisions if you cannot, and who raises your children. Our article [what is estate planning and who needs it](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning) takes each in turn, with examples of the people who benefit most.

Your [estate](/glossary/estate) is everything you own and owe at death. That includes a house, accounts, vehicles, retirement plans, insurance and personal belongings. A plan may also address what you cannot easily value: your wishes, your children's care, a family business, a pet.

Two things surprise people about the subject. First, much of it applies while you are still alive. A stroke or dementia can leave you alive but unable to sign or consent, and incapacity planning is as important as inheritance. Second, a will is only one part, and often not the part that controls the most money.

## What documents make up an estate plan?

A complete plan usually has these parts, summarized here and covered fully in [the core estate planning documents](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist).

| Document | What it decides | Applies |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Who inherits probate assets; executor; guardian for minor children | After death |
| Revocable living trust | Management and distribution of assets held in the trust | Life and after death |
| Durable financial power of attorney | Who manages your finances if you cannot | During incapacity |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Who makes your medical decisions | During incapacity |
| Living will | Your wishes on life-sustaining treatment | During incapacity |
| HIPAA authorization | Who can see your medical information | Now |
| Beneficiary designations | Who receives retirement accounts, insurance, POD accounts | After death |

The first thing to understand is that these documents are not interchangeable. Each controls different assets or decisions, and where they conflict, an account's beneficiary form generally beats a will. That is why the most common failures are coordination failures, discussed in [common estate planning mistakes](/learn/basics/common-estate-planning-mistakes).

## How do wills work?

A will names an executor, says who receives your property, and for parents, nominates a guardian. It must be signed and witnessed according to state rules, and it goes through probate. Our [wills guide](/learn/wills) covers the topic fully. Key reads:

- [What happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will), which explains the default inheritance rules.
- [How to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will), with signing and witness rules.
- [Choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
- [What a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do), the list of assets that pass outside it.

## When does a trust make sense?

A trust is a legal arrangement where a trustee holds property for beneficiaries under rules you set. A revocable living trust is the most common estate planning version. You keep control during life, and a successor takes over at death or incapacity, with no probate for assets held in the trust. Trusts can also stage distributions to children, protect beneficiaries from creditors or from themselves, and plan for tax or benefit eligibility.

They cost more and require follow-through, since a trust only controls what has been retitled into it. Start with [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) if you are deciding, then read [how revocable living trusts work](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust). The [trusts guide](/learn/trusts) also covers irrevocable trusts, trustees, trusts for children and the myths about taxes.

## What is probate, and can you avoid it?

Probate is the court process that validates a will, appoints an executor, pays debts and distributes what is left. Duration and cost vary by state and estate: some are quick and modest, others slow and expensive. Small estates may qualify for shortcuts. Many assets skip probate entirely because they pass by beneficiary designation, joint ownership or trust.

The [probate guide](/learn/probate) lays out the process, timing, costs and the main ways to avoid it. If you are still deciding whether it matters for you, [how to avoid probate](/learn/probate/how-to-avoid-probate) compares seven tools, and [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) shows what goes through court.

## What happens when someone dies?

Surviving relatives often have to act within days, with little experience. The [after-a-death guide](/learn/after-a-death) walks through the first weeks, the executor's job, trust administration, taxes for heirs, and final returns. If that is your situation now, start with [the week-by-week checklist](/learn/after-a-death/what-to-do-when-someone-dies-checklist).

## How do powers of attorney and healthcare directives protect you?

If you cannot manage your affairs, someone must. A [power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney) lets a person you choose handle your finances. Without it, a family may need a court to name a guardian or conservator, a slower and public alternative. Learn how [durable powers of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) work, and the difference between [springing and immediate](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney) authority.

[Healthcare directives](/learn/healthcare-directives) cover medical decisions: a living will for your treatment wishes, a healthcare agent to decide, and a HIPAA release so your family can talk to your doctors. For people worried about memory loss, see [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

## How do you protect your children?

Parents of minors should name a guardian in the will. Without one, a court chooses. Guardians raise the children; a trustee manages the money, and those can be different people. See the [guardianship guide](/learn/guardianship) and our walkthrough of [how to choose a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian). For ideas on structuring what children inherit, read [leaving money to minor children](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Which plan fits your life stage?

Needs change as life does. A new parent focuses on guardians and life insurance, a retiree on long-term care and taxes, a widow on updating everything built around two people. The [life-stages guide](/learn/life-stages) includes pages for new parents, young adults, newlyweds, divorced people, unmarried couples, single people, retirees and widows and widowers.

## What about complicated families?

Some situations need extra thought. In a [blended family](/learn/blended-families), the plan must balance a current spouse and children from earlier relationships. If you want to provide for a [loved one with a disability](/learn/special-needs) without losing benefits, a special needs trust is usually involved. Business owners need continuity planning, covered in [estate planning for business owners](/learn/business-owners).

## What is the beneficiary form problem?

A retirement account or life insurance policy goes to the person named on the form, whatever your will says. That makes [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) some of the most powerful estate documents you will sign, and the ones most often forgotten. They also interact with taxes, in particular the 10-year payout rule that applies to most non-spouse heirs of inherited IRAs.

## Will estate tax matter to you?

The federal estate and gift tax basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person as of 2026, indexed for inflation after 2026, with a top rate of 40% on amounts above it. Married couples can use portability to shelter up to $30 million. The annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient in 2026. Most families owe no federal estate tax, and some states tax at much lower levels. The [estate and gift tax guide](/learn/estate-tax) covers both layers, and [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis) explains why inheriting can be better than being given an asset during life.

## How do you plan for long-term care?

Nursing home and home care costs can consume savings quickly, and Medicaid has asset and look-back rules that reward early planning. The [elder care guide](/learn/elder-care) explains Medicaid planning, the five-year look-back in most states, and how to protect a home. Adult children helping a parent can start with [how to help an aging parent plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan).

## What about your digital life, home and other property?

Photos, email, cryptocurrency and online accounts need access instructions. See the [digital assets guide](/learn/digital-assets). Real estate, out-of-state property, firearms and pets raise their own questions, covered in the [property and special assets guide](/learn/property-and-assets).

## How do you get started? A seven-step path

1. **Read the basics.** Begin with [what estate planning is](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning).
2. **List what you own and owe,** with titles and beneficiaries.
3. **Choose your people:** executor, agents, guardian, trustee and backups.
4. **Decide your wishes** about inheritance and medical care.
5. **Learn what a plan costs** in [how much an estate plan costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost), and how to [choose an attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney).
6. **Prepare for the meeting** using [what to bring to your first consultation](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation).
7. **Finish the follow-through,** including retitling assets and updating beneficiary forms.

## What mistakes should you avoid at the start?

Three things go wrong most often with first-time planners. They sign a will and forget the beneficiary forms. They sign a trust and never retitle the house. They name people without asking them. All three are cheap to avoid when you know to look for them.
