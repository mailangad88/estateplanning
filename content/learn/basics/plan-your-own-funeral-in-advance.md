Most people have an opinion about their own funeral. Burial or cremation. A church, a beach, or nothing at all. Very few have written it down anywhere that someone will look in time. The result is a family sitting across from a funeral director on day two, guessing.

This guide covers how to make the decisions, where to record them so they are followed, whether prepaying is worth it, and how to avoid the common trap of putting everything in your will. It belongs with the rest of your [estate planning basics](/learn/basics), although it is more personal than most of what is in them.

## The short version

- Decide the big three: burial or cremation, what kind of gathering, and roughly what you want your family to spend.
- Write it in a signed, dated letter, separate from your will.
- Name one person to carry it out, in writing, and tell them.
- If you prepay, get the terms on paper and learn what happens if you move.
- Tell your family now. A conversation costs nothing.

## What decisions should you actually make?

Start with the choices that change cost and logistics the most. Burial usually involves a plot, a casket or vault, and cemetery fees. Cremation is generally less expensive and leaves more time to decide about a service, since there is no urgency to schedule a viewing. A person who wants a green burial, a body donation to a medical school, or a military honor guard needs to act earlier, because some of these require registration or acceptance in advance.

After that, the smaller things are what families remember. Music. Who speaks. Whether you want an open casket. Whether donations should go to a charity instead of flowers. Where ashes go, and whether they can be divided.

A short worksheet helps. Write down:

1. Burial, cremation, or donation.
2. Where, if you already own a cemetery plot or have a place in mind.
3. Type of service and who should be asked to take part.
4. A rough budget, stated as a ceiling rather than a target.
5. Obituary details and who should be notified first.
6. The person you want in charge.

Do not worry about getting every line right. A page that says "cremation, small gathering at the lake, no more than we can afford" is more useful than nothing.

## Why a will is the wrong place for funeral wishes

A will is a legal document about property. It becomes visible when someone files it with the probate court, and that often happens after the funeral. Maria, 71, tells her son Daniel in her will that she wants cremation with her ashes scattered at the coast. Daniel reads it a week after he arranged a burial next to her husband. Nothing in the will helped.

Some states do treat written funeral instructions as binding on the person in charge, whatever document they appear in. Others treat them as strong guidance. In no state does a will protect you from the timing problem. So use a letter. The [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) is the natural home for it, and our page on that letter explains how to store it so the right person finds it quickly. Keep a copy with the person who will arrange the service, not only in a drawer.

A short mention in your will does no harm, as long as it points to the letter. Never make a gift depend on whether the family follows your funeral wishes. That invites a dispute during the worst week of anyone's year.

## Who controls your body and the arrangements?

Most states have a statutory list of who has the right to control the disposition of remains. The list commonly runs: a person you named in writing, then your spouse, then adult children, then parents, then siblings. The exact order and the form required differ by state. Some provide a short form for naming an agent, and others accept a signed, witnessed statement.

This matters most in families that do not agree. An unmarried partner of twenty years usually has no standing on the default list. Adult stepchildren may rank below a half-sibling you have not spoken to in years. If any of that describes your family, name the person in writing and make sure it is the same person you picked for your medical decisions or your estate. Our guide on [who pays for the funeral](/learn/after-a-death/who-pays-for-the-funeral) covers the money side, including what happens when the person who arranged the service is not the one with access to the bank account.

## Should you prepay?

Prepaying gives you certainty that the plan is funded. It also puts money with a business, sometimes for decades. Think of it as a choice between three tools.

| Option | How it works | Strength | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-need contract with a funeral home | You choose goods and services and pay in advance | Fixes the choices and often the price of covered items | Refund rules, transfer if you move, what happens if the home is sold or closes |
| Pre-need insurance policy | A small whole life policy pays the funeral home | Can be paid in installments | Fees, rising cost, and who is the beneficiary |
| Payable-on-death account | Your own savings, with a named beneficiary | Fully yours and flexible; see [payable-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) | Money can be spent; the beneficiary must use it as you intended |

Funeral homes in the United States must give you an itemized price list on request under a federal rule, and you are generally free to buy only the goods and services you want. Use that. Get a written list from two or three providers before you choose, and compare the same items.

If you do sign a pre-need contract, ask these questions in writing: Is it refundable, and how much? Can it be transferred to another funeral home? Where is the money held, and who is responsible for it? What happens if my chosen cemetery or provider changes? Does the contract cover price increases for what I selected, or only for some items?

An important point for Medicaid planning: in some situations a funeral arrangement set up with an irrevocable pre-need contract can be treated differently from a countable asset. The rules vary by state, so ask an attorney before relying on this if long-term care is a possibility. Our [Medicaid planning guide](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) is a place to start.

## How to put the plan in place

1. Decide the main choices and write them on one page.
2. Pick the person in charge and check whether your state has a form to name them.
3. Compare written price lists from two or three funeral homes if you want to prepay.
4. Sign and date the letter. Store it with your other papers and give a copy to the person in charge.
5. Tell your family what you chose, and why. A brief conversation now prevents arguments later; our page on [talking to family about your plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) has ways to start.
6. Review it when your life changes. Moving, remarrying, or the death of the person you named are all reasons to revisit it.

## What about the medical side?

People often combine "funeral planning" with "end-of-life planning", but the documents are different. A living will and a healthcare agent decide how you are treated while you are alive. A funeral plan takes effect at death. Both belong in your file, and they should name people who can work together. See [documenting your end-of-life wishes](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-document-your-end-of-life-wishes) for the medical documents.

## Common mistakes

Leaving the plan with only one person, who then dies first. Prepaying with a provider who is far from where your family lives. Naming a cemetery that no longer sells plots. Forgetting that organ donation and body donation have their own registration. And writing so much detail that the plan becomes impossible to follow. The wishes that survive are the short ones.
