Most family members help an aging parent with paperwork out of kindness. A few step over a line. They steer the parent to a lawyer, keep the other children away, and leave with a new will in the file. The law has a name for that line, undue influence, and it is one of the most common reasons a will or trust ends up in court.

This page explains what undue influence is, how a court decides, what the warning signs are and how to set up a plan so that it holds. It belongs to our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) series, and the contest side is covered in [contesting a will](/learn/wills/contesting-a-will).

## What counts as undue influence?

It is pressure or manipulation strong enough that the signer's own wishes are replaced by someone else's. The law does not forbid influence. A friend can say that she thinks you should leave something to the church. A daughter can say she needs help paying for the care she is providing. Those are requests, and the signer is free to say no.

Undue influence begins when the signer cannot say no. That might be because of fear, dependence, isolation, deception or exhaustion. The test is about the effect on the person's own judgment, not the identity of the person pushing.

| Normal | Possibly undue |
|---|---|
| A child drives a parent to the lawyer's office | The child chooses the lawyer, attends the meeting and relays the instructions |
| A caregiver is thanked with a modest gift | A caregiver becomes the main beneficiary after months of controlling visitors and calls |
| A parent explains why one child gets more | A new plan appears after a hospital stay and gives nearly everything to the person who arranged it |
| A spouse discusses the plan openly | A new spouse of a few months holds the pen and keeps the other family out of the room |

## How do courts decide?

Courts in Illinois, New Jersey and most other states look at the circumstances rather than a single test. Four facts tend to matter:

1. **A relationship of trust or dependence.** Examples are a caregiver, an adult child with power of attorney, a financial adviser, a spiritual adviser or a new partner.
2. **A weakened signer.** Illness, grief, cognitive decline or dependence on others for daily needs makes pressure more effective.
3. **A large benefit to the influencer.** A plan that leaves nearly everything to one person who was not previously a major beneficiary draws attention.
4. **Involvement in making the document.** Choosing the lawyer, paying for it, sitting in on the meeting, or holding the documents.

When these facts combine, many states shift the burden of proof. In that situation the person who benefits has to show that the gift was freely made. The rule is stated differently in each state, so the details matter. Our articles on [Illinois's six-month contest deadline](/learn/illinois/illinois-will-contest-six-month-deadline) and [New Jersey will contests](/learn/new-jersey/new-jersey-will-contests-and-caveats) set out how a challenge is brought and when.

## What are the warning signs?

Families often notice these in the months before a death:

- The signer is cut off from people who used to visit, or calls go unanswered.
- A new will or trust appears soon after a diagnosis, a hospital stay or a death in the family.
- The beneficiary hires the lawyer, drives the signer to the appointment or is present for the meeting.
- The signer changes a long-standing plan for no explanation.
- The signer begins speaking in someone else's words, or seems afraid to discuss the plan.
- A power of attorney is signed at the same time, and the agent starts moving assets.
- Other relatives are told that nothing has changed.

One sign alone proves little. Several together are the kind of pattern a court notices.

### Example: a caregiver and a new will

Example only. Harold, 84, a widower in Trenton, had three adult children and a will that divided his estate equally. After a fall, his daughter Lynn moved in as his caregiver. Over eight months she stopped his other children from visiting without notice, took him to a new lawyer she found, and sat in the room while he signed a will leaving her 90 percent of the estate. Harold's doctor had recorded early memory loss. After he died, his son filed a caveat. The court would examine the lawyer's notes, the doctor's records, who paid the lawyer and whether Harold spoke to the lawyer alone. If Lynn cannot show Harold acted freely, the new will can be set aside and the older, equal will may take its place.

## What happens if a court finds undue influence?

The court can invalidate the will, the trust, the deed or the beneficiary change, or just the parts caused by the influence. If an earlier will is valid it often takes effect in its place. If there is none, the estate passes by intestacy. In some cases, a court can also order the influencer to return assets or can reduce their share. The cost of this process is considerable, and the family can be damaged for years.

## How can you protect your plan from a challenge?

Use these steps, particularly if you are older, ill, or leaving things unequally.

1. **Pick your own lawyer.** Do not use one chosen or paid for by a beneficiary. Our guide to [choosing an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) explains what to look for.
2. **Meet alone.** The attorney should talk to you privately, at least for the part of the meeting where you decide who gets what.
3. **Explain unequal gifts.** A short signed statement, or a letter like the one described in our article on [ethical wills](/learn/basics/what-is-an-ethical-will-and-how-to-write-a-legacy-letter), gives a judge your own words.
4. **Document capacity.** If there is any doubt, ask your doctor to write a note near the time of signing. See [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).
5. **Tell your family.** A family that knows the plan, and why, is less likely to sue. [Talking to your family about your plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) gives practical scripts.
6. **Choose a neutral fiduciary.** A professional or a family member who does not benefit as an executor or trustee reduces conflict. The same applies to the person who holds your power of attorney.
7. **Keep records.** Keep drafts, emails with your lawyer and any notes on why you decided as you did.

Unequal plans are legal. A parent can leave more to one child than another. The danger lies in the way the plan came to be, not the plan itself. Our article on [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares) covers the planning side.

## What if you suspect it is happening to a parent?

Act early, while the parent is alive. Stay in contact and keep records of visits and calls. Ask the parent, privately, whether they want to talk to their own attorney. Contact the parent's doctor if capacity is a concern. If you believe a parent is being exploited financially, Adult Protective Services in your state takes reports. Once the parent has died, bring the facts to a probate attorney quickly, because the deadline to contest is short. Check the state article for your state, because a missed date ends the case.
