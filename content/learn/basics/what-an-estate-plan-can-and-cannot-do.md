Plenty of people expect an estate plan to do more than it does, and a few expect it to do less. They imagine a document that wipes out taxes, locks the door on creditors and handles everything the day they die. Others assume it is pointless unless they are rich. Neither picture is right.

This page lays out what a plan reliably does, what it cannot do on its own, and where life insurance fits. It is part of our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) library, and if you want the definition first, start with [what estate planning is](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning).

## What does an estate plan do well?

A plan answers questions your family would otherwise have to answer in a crisis. Who talks to the doctors? Who pays the mortgage while you are in the hospital? Who gets the house? Who raises the children if neither parent can?

In practice that comes down to a handful of jobs.

1. **Naming decision makers.** A financial power of attorney and a healthcare directive pick the people who act if you cannot.
2. **Directing property.** A will or trust, plus up-to-date beneficiary forms, says who receives what and when.
3. **Choosing guardians.** Parents of minor children name who raises them, and who manages their money.
4. **Reducing delay and court time.** Assets in a funded trust or with a valid beneficiary form often skip probate.
5. **Putting wishes in writing.** Medical preferences, funeral wishes and the story behind a decision all go into documents your family can find.

None of that is glamorous. It is the reason a plan is worth having at any income level, and the reason [a person with few assets still needs one](/learn/basics/do-i-need-an-estate-plan-with-few-assets).

## Does an estate plan avoid taxes?

Not by itself. A will and a standard revocable trust change who receives your property, not how it is taxed. Everything in a revocable trust is still counted in your estate and still reported on your personal return while you are alive.

What matters more is that most families will not owe federal estate tax at all. In 2026 the basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, and a married couple can shelter $30,000,000 with portability. If your total wealth is well under that, the federal question is largely settled.

Two other taxes catch smaller estates by surprise.

- **State taxes.** Some states charge an estate tax or an inheritance tax at much lower thresholds than the federal one. Check your state, and our [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) is a quick way to see whether yours applies.
- **Income tax for heirs.** Inherited retirement accounts are taxed as income when withdrawn, and most non-spouse heirs have to empty them within 10 years.

Larger estates have real tools: lifetime gifts, certain irrevocable trusts, charitable giving. Those are deliberate strategies with trade-offs, not features that come with a will. Our guide on [whether a trust avoids taxes](/learn/trusts/does-a-trust-avoid-taxes) goes into the detail.

## Does an estate plan protect assets from creditors?

Generally not while you are alive, and the answer depends on the tool.

Mike, 62, puts his house in a revocable living trust because a friend told him it was "protected." A customer later wins a judgment against his contracting business. The trust does not stop her. Mike can revoke the trust at any time, so the law treats the house as his.

A few approaches do offer real distance from creditors. An irrevocable trust that you truly give up control over can work in some situations. Retirement accounts and some other assets have statutory protection in many states. Co-owning property as spouses has special treatment in states that recognize tenancy by the entirety. Each has limits, and moving assets after a lawsuit has been filed or threatened is a classic way to have the transfer reversed.

After death, creditors are paid from the estate before heirs receive anything, unless the asset passes outside the estate in a way the creditor cannot reach. We cover those rules in [whether creditors can take what a beneficiary inherits](/learn/beneficiary-designations/can-creditors-take-what-a-beneficiary-inherits) and in [whether a living trust protects assets](/learn/trusts/does-a-living-trust-protect-assets-from-creditors).

## Does life insurance replace an estate plan?

No, but it is often part of one. A policy pays its death benefit to the person named on the form. That is a useful job: it can replace income, pay off a mortgage, fund a buy-out or equalize an inheritance. But the policy does not do what the other documents do.

| Question | Life insurance | Estate plan documents |
|---|---|---|
| Who gets the money? | The named beneficiary, outside probate | Will, trust and beneficiary forms |
| Who raises minor children? | Not addressed | Guardian named in a will |
| Who makes medical decisions? | Not addressed | Healthcare directive |
| Who handles bills if you are incapacitated? | Not addressed | Financial power of attorney |
| What if the beneficiary is a minor or has a disability? | Pays directly, can cause problems | Trust can hold and manage the funds |
| What if the named person dies first? | Falls to the contingent or the estate | Backup beneficiaries and instructions |

A policy with no named contingent beneficiary, or one that names a minor, can create the very probate and guardianship problems the plan was meant to avoid. Our guide to [life insurance beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries) shows how to set it up well.

The same logic applies to retirement accounts and pay-on-death bank accounts. They are part of your plan because the beneficiary form controls them, not the will.

## What are the pros and cons?

It helps to be honest about both sides. This is how most clients weigh it, usually after a conversation about a relative whose affairs were a mess when they died. Their own plan does not need to be elaborate, but it does need to exist and be current.

**What you gain**

- A named person for each job, so no one has to ask a judge.
- Less cost and delay for your family, particularly if a trust is funded.
- A chance to write down your reasons, which cuts family disputes.
- Some control over how and when younger or vulnerable heirs receive money.

**What you give up**

- Fees now. See what a [plan typically costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost) before you decide.
- Some privacy about your decisions, if a will goes through probate and becomes a public record.
- Time. You have to choose people, which is the part most people put off.
- Upkeep. A plan that is not reviewed after a move, divorce or birth quietly stops working. See [when to update your plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## What an estate plan cannot do

A short list people tend to be surprised by:

- It cannot override a joint account, a beneficiary form or a valid prenuptial agreement that says otherwise. See [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do).
- It cannot force a family to get along. It can make the instructions clearer, and that sometimes helps.
- It cannot protect you from future Medicaid rules without separate planning.
- It cannot stay accurate on its own. People, laws and accounts change.
