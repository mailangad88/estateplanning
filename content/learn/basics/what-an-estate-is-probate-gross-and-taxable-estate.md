Someone says "the estate" at a funeral, a bank counter and a tax office, and each of them means something slightly different. A probate clerk wants a list of what the court will transfer. The IRS wants a list of everything you owned or controlled. And your family just wants to know who gets what.

Those are three different lists, and understanding them clears up a lot of confusion about why a house can be "in the estate" but "not in probate," or why a family with a seven-figure gross estate can owe nothing in federal tax. This page sorts the terms. For the starting point on the whole subject, see our [estate planning guide](/learn/basics).

## What is an estate in plain terms?

Your [estate](/glossary/estate) is the total of what you own, owe and control at death: your home, vehicles, bank and investment accounts, retirement plans, life insurance, business interests, personal belongings, and debts. Think of it as a snapshot taken at the moment of death.

What happens to that snapshot depends on what question is being asked. A court asks, "What do I have to supervise the transfer of?" The IRS asks, "What did this person control that could be taxed?" Each answer produces a different list, with a different name.

| Measure | Question it answers | Who uses it | Typically includes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Probate estate | What must a court transfer? | Probate court, executor | Assets titled in the decedent's sole name with no beneficiary |
| Gross estate | What did the person own or control? | IRS, state tax agency | Nearly everything, including trusts, insurance and retirement accounts |
| Taxable estate | What is left to tax? | IRS | Gross estate minus debts, expenses, charity and spousal transfers |

## What is the probate estate?

The [probate estate](/glossary/probate-estate) is the property that passes only through a court process. It is what the executor takes charge of, inventories and distributes under the will or, without a will, under state law. Typical examples are a bank account in one name only, a car, a house owned alone, and personal items.

It is usually smaller than people expect, because many valuable assets never enter it. A retirement account with a named beneficiary goes straight to that person. A house held in a living trust is managed by the trustee. A joint account passes to the surviving owner. Our page on [probate versus non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) goes through each type.

The size of the probate estate matters for three reasons. It affects the court filing, because many states have simplified processes below a dollar threshold. It affects the fees, since in some places fees are tied to the size. And it affects timing, because the executor cannot finish until debts are paid and the creditor period has run.

## What is the gross estate?

The gross estate is the broadest list. For federal estate tax it generally includes assets you owned outright, your share of jointly owned property, retirement accounts, the death benefit of life insurance you owned or controlled, and assets in a revocable trust. It includes property that avoids probate entirely. Our page on [what counts toward the estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/what-counts-toward-the-estate-tax) lists the categories.

This is the number people most often miscount. Raymond, 74, has $400,000 in a probate-bound bank account and house, a $900,000 IRA, and a $500,000 life insurance policy he owns. His probate estate is about $400,000. His gross estate is about $1.8 million. Neither figure is near the federal exemption, but the second one is the true size of what he leaves behind.

## What is the taxable estate?

The [taxable estate](/glossary/estate-tax) is the gross estate after allowed subtractions. These include funeral costs, administration expenses, debts, gifts to charity, and anything passing to a surviving spouse who is a U.S. citizen under the [marital deduction](/glossary/marital-deduction). What is left is measured against the exemption.

As of 2026 the federal basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000 per person, and married couples can shelter $30 million using portability. The top rate on the amount above that is 40%. Almost no family reaches that level, but a few states have their own estate or inheritance taxes at much lower thresholds. Our guide to the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) explains the rule, and the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) shows whether your state adds another layer.

## What is the residuary estate?

Inside the probate estate, the [residuary estate](/glossary/residuary-estate) is what is left after the specific gifts in the will, the debts, the taxes and the cost of administration. It is often the largest share. In a will like "my watch to my son, the rest to my daughter," the daughter is the residuary beneficiary. If the will does not say, state law decides who takes the remainder. [Specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) shows how this works, including what happens when a gift fails.

## How to sort your own assets

You can build a rough version of all three lists in an afternoon.

1. List every asset and debt with an approximate value. Our [asset inventory guide](/learn/basics/how-to-inventory-your-assets-and-value-your-estate) gives a template.
2. Next to each one, write how it is titled: your name alone, joint, in a trust, or owned by a company.
3. Next to each account, write who the named beneficiary is, if any.
4. Circle the items with sole title and no beneficiary. That is your probate estate.
5. Add every asset, including the circled ones, and add life insurance you own. That is your gross estate.
6. Compare the gross figure with the exemption. If you are far below it, federal estate tax is not a concern, though state tax might be.

## An example in full

Nadia, 68, is a widow. She owns a house worth $520,000 in her own name, a checking account of $40,000 with no payable-on-death designation, a brokerage account of $300,000 with her son as transfer-on-death beneficiary, and a 401(k) of $600,000 naming her daughter. She owns a term policy of $250,000 for the benefit of both children.

Her probate estate is the house and checking account, $560,000. Her gross estate is $1,710,000. Her taxable estate, after deducting say $30,000 of expenses and debts, is about $1,680,000, well below $15 million. No federal estate tax is due. But the house and account go through probate, while the rest passes outside it. If Nadia wants the children treated alike, she should review that split now; our page on [leaving unequal shares with beneficiary forms](/learn/beneficiary-designations/leaving-unequal-shares-with-beneficiary-forms) explains why mismatched designations cause trouble.

## Where families go wrong

- Assuming a will controls everything. It controls only the probate estate.
- Assuming "no probate" means "no tax." The two systems are separate.
- Forgetting that life insurance owned by the decedent is part of the gross estate.
- Leaving the probate estate so small that the will is nearly empty, while the beneficiary forms are years out of date.
- Never reviewing title after buying a home, getting married or retitling an account.

## How state law changes the picture

States set their own probate thresholds and procedures, so the same assets might go through full probate in one state and a short affidavit in another. A few states also add an estate or inheritance tax with a much lower exemption than the federal one.
