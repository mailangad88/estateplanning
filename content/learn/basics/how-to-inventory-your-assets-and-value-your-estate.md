Most people can say what they earn and what their house cost. Fewer can say what they would leave behind if they died next month. That gap is common, and it matters. A lawyer can only plan around what you tell them, and your family can only find what you have documented.

This guide shows what counts as your estate, how to build a working inventory in an afternoon, and how to tell which assets go through the court and which do not. It builds on the list in [what to bring to your estate planning consultation](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) and belongs to our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) library.

## The short version

- Your estate is wider than your will. It includes accounts and policies that pass by beneficiary form.
- Four facts per asset: what it is, what it is worth, how it is titled, who is named to receive it.
- An estimate is fine. A missing account is not.
- Totals decide whether estate tax is even a question for you, and for most families it is not at the federal level.

## What counts as part of my estate?

Everything you own or have the right to receive at death. Think in categories, because people forget entire categories.

- **Real estate:** your home, a vacation property, rental units, land, a timeshare.
- **Bank and cash accounts:** checking, savings, certificates of deposit, money market accounts, cash.
- **Investments:** brokerage accounts, stocks, bonds, mutual funds, crypto held at an exchange or in a wallet.
- **Retirement accounts:** 401(k), 403(b), IRA, Roth IRA, pensions.
- **Life insurance:** policies on your life, especially ones you own. Term policies through work count too.
- **Business interests:** an LLC, partnership, sole proprietorship, shares in a family company.
- **Personal property:** vehicles, jewelry, art, firearms, tools, furniture, collections.
- **Money owed to you:** a loan you made to a relative, a pending tax refund, a final paycheck, a lawsuit.
- **Digital property:** online accounts with value, domain names, loyalty points that can be transferred, photos stored in the cloud.

Then list what you owe: mortgage, car loans, student loans, credit cards, taxes, medical bills, and any co-signed debts.

The word [estate](/glossary/estate) in everyday use means all of this. Lawyers use two narrower terms, which is where confusion begins.

## Probate estate vs. gross estate: what is the difference?

The **probate estate** is the property that passes through the court under your will, or under state intestacy rules if you have no will. It is generally limited to assets titled in your name alone with no beneficiary. See [probate vs. non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

The **gross estate** is the tax measure. It adds back assets that avoid probate but still count for tax purposes, such as retirement accounts, life insurance you own, a share of jointly held property and the value of a revocable trust. Only the gross estate is compared with the federal estate tax exclusion, which is $15 million per person in 2026. Our guide to the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) explains how that works for married couples.

Here is an example. Priya, 64, owns a $380,000 house in her name alone, a $210,000 IRA with her son as beneficiary, a $500,000 term life policy that names her daughter, and a $60,000 savings account payable on death to her sister. Her probate estate is the house, which is $380,000. Her gross estate is about $1,150,000. A will would control only the house.

| Asset | Titled how | Who gets it | In probate estate? |
|---|---|---|---|
| House | Priya alone | Under her will | Yes |
| IRA | Priya, beneficiary: son | Son | No |
| Life policy | Priya owns | Daughter | No |
| Savings | Payable-on-death to sister | Sister | No. |

Five minutes with a table like this one can reveal that a will does less than you thought, or that three documents point to different people.

## How do you make an inventory? A step-by-step method

1. **Pull the paper and the logins.** Gather the last statement from every account, your latest mortgage statement, insurance declarations pages, your deed, vehicle titles and the most recent tax return.
2. **Make a simple spreadsheet or page.** Use columns for asset, institution, last four digits of the account number, approximate value, how it is titled, who is named as beneficiary, and where the paperwork lives. Do not write full account numbers or passwords in it.
3. **Add the value.** Use the latest balance for accounts. For a house, use a recent comparable sale or an online estimate and call it a range. For a business, use a rough figure and flag it as a guess.
4. **Check the title.** For real estate and accounts, note whether it is yours alone, joint with right of survivorship, tenants in common, in a trust, or in the name of a business. Title controls the outcome more than the will does. The [joint tenancy glossary entry](/glossary/joint-tenancy) gives a short definition.
5. **Check the beneficiary.** Log in or call and confirm the name, the share and the backup. A form signed in 2003 may name someone who is no longer in your life.
6. **List debts and monthly obligations.** Include who else is liable, such as a co-signer.
7. **Add the things people forget:** a safe deposit box, storage unit, safe, old pension, frequent flyer miles, prepaid funeral, unclaimed property, digital accounts.
8. **Total it, and sort it.** Total the assets and subtract debts. Then sort into three groups: goes by will, goes by title or beneficiary form, goes by trust.

If you want a faster way to sort, try our [probate asset sorter](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets), which asks about each asset and tells you which bucket it falls in.

## How accurate does the valuation need to be?

For planning, close enough is good enough. You are trying to answer three questions, and none requires an appraisal.

First, do you have enough to worry about federal estate tax? With an exclusion of $15 million per person in 2026, most households do not. Second, does your state have its own estate or inheritance tax with a lower threshold? Some do, and the thresholds are much lower than the federal figure, so check the [state estate and inheritance tax guide](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes). Third, can the plan treat children fairly? If one child will get the house and another gets cash, you need a realistic value for both.

Values change, and estate tax treats some things oddly. Assets that you hold at death generally receive a new tax basis, which is called a step-up. That can make a low-cost stock position or an old house less taxable for heirs than it seems. See [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis). A formal appraisal is worth ordering when a business, farmland, art or a house shared among siblings is in the mix, or when a tax return will be filed.

## Which assets do people forget most often?

From what we see, the usual misses are:

1. An employer retirement account from a job twenty years ago.
2. Life insurance through work, which may end when the job does.
3. Accounts held under a name before a marriage or divorce.
4. A safe deposit box in a bank the person no longer uses. See our advice on [where to keep your documents](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
5. Digital accounts and cryptocurrency. See [the online accounts checklist](/learn/digital-assets/online-accounts-after-death-checklist).
6. Money owed to a person, such as an informal loan.
7. Property in another state, which may need a separate court process. Read about [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate).

## What do you do with the inventory?

Keep it with your plan, not on the fridge. Store a paper copy with your will and your [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction), and a copy in your password manager. Tell your executor where it is. Update it once a year when you review your beneficiary forms, and after any big change such as a sale, an inheritance or a move. Our guide on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists the triggers.

An inventory also helps the attorney you hire. It lets us suggest a will, a trust or only a few form changes, and it speeds the work. It can reduce what you pay, because the first hour is not spent hunting for numbers.
