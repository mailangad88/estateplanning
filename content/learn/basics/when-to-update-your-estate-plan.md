An estate plan is a snapshot. It reflects your family, your property, your state's laws and your relationships on the day you signed. Each of those changes. The plan stays put unless you move it, and that gap between a stale plan and a changed life is where many of the problems described in [common estate planning mistakes](/learn/basics/common-estate-planning-mistakes) arise.

This guide covers the life events and routine checkpoints that call for a review, what to look at each time, and how to make changes properly. It is part of our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) series.

## How often should you review your estate plan?

Every three to five years is a reasonable baseline, even if nothing obvious has changed. Set a recurring reminder, for instance every January or on your birthday. A routine review is usually short: confirm that the people you named are still the right ones and still alive and willing, that your assets and family are accurately reflected, and that your beneficiary forms match.

Between reviews, certain events should trigger a check immediately.

## Which life events mean you should update your plan?

### Marriage

A new marriage changes who inherits under your state's default rules and may give your spouse inheritance rights even if your will says otherwise. Some states revoke an earlier will on marriage. Add your spouse to beneficiary forms, powers of attorney and healthcare documents, and think about how joint property is titled. See [estate planning after you get married](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-newlyweds).

### Divorce or separation

Divorce is a mandatory review. Many states automatically remove an ex-spouse from a will and trust, but not necessarily from beneficiary forms, a power of attorney or a healthcare proxy, and the rules vary. Update every document and account. See [updating your estate plan after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce).

### Birth or adoption

A new child means a guardian, probably life insurance, and a decision about how and when the child inherits. Without updates, the plan may not mention the new child at all. See [estate planning for new parents](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-new-parents) and [naming a guardian](/learn/guardianship).

### A death in the family

If your spouse, executor, guardian or beneficiary dies, someone must replace them. A surviving spouse often needs a complete update since the plan was built around two people. See [updating your plan after your spouse dies](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-spouse-dies).

### A child reaching adulthood, or a family change

When a child turns 18, parents lose automatic authority, so that child needs their own financial and healthcare documents. A child's marriage, divorce, disability, addiction or financial trouble can also change how you want to leave them money. See [estate planning at 18](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults).

### Moving to a new state

Estate documents are governed by state law. A will generally remains valid, but terminology, witnessing rules, the treatment of powers of attorney and the rules for community property, spouses' rights and taxes differ. Property in another state may also need separate handling; see [owning property in more than one state](/learn/property-and-assets/out-of-state-property).

### A big change in assets

A home purchase or sale, an inheritance, a business start, a sale or windfall, retirement or a large gift can shift which tools fit. A modest estate may become one that benefits from a trust. A growing estate may start to approach a [state estate tax](/learn/estate-tax/state-estate-and-inheritance-taxes) threshold. Business owners should revisit [succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning).

### Changes in health

A serious diagnosis, especially one that may affect capacity, makes timing urgent. Documents signed while you are clearly competent are far harder to challenge. See [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives). A change in a loved one's health matters too: a child with a new disability may need a [special needs trust](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust).

### Retirement and aging

Retirement shifts income sources, adds required minimum distributions, and brings questions about long-term care costs. See [estate planning in retirement](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-retirees) and [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

### Changes in law

Tax and probate laws change. The federal exemption is $15,000,000 per person as of 2026, with inflation indexing after that. Retirement account rules also change, notably the 10-year payout rule for most inherited IRAs. Law changes matter most to larger estates and retirement-heavy estates. See the [federal estate tax exemption](/learn/estate-tax/federal-estate-tax-exemption) and [retirement account beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/retirement-account-beneficiaries).

## What should you check during a review?

Use this checklist at each review:

1. **People.** Are the executor, trustee, agents and guardian still alive, willing, capable and living reasonably nearby? Are backups still right?
2. **Family.** New children, grandchildren, marriages, divorces, deaths or estrangements?
3. **Assets.** New property, sold property, new accounts, closed accounts? Are the assets still titled the way the plan assumes?
4. **Beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, POD and TOD accounts. Do they match the plan?
5. **Trust funding.** If you have a trust, are new assets in it?
6. **Wishes.** Do your distributions, specific gifts and charitable gifts still reflect what you want?
7. **Health documents.** Are your living will and healthcare agent current? Does your doctor have copies?
8. **Location.** Do the right people know where originals are?
9. **Taxes.** Has your estate or your state's rules moved near a tax threshold?

## A review that caught a problem

Rosa, 59, signed a trust in 2014. At her five-year check she realized three things: her successor trustee, her brother, had moved overseas; the house she had since refinanced had been retitled in her own name by the lender; and her IRA beneficiary form still named her late husband with no backup. None of that would have appeared on her radar without the review. Each fix was a single form or short document.

## How do you actually make changes?

The right tool depends on how big the change is.

| Change | Typical method | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Change a beneficiary on an account | Form from the account provider | Takes minutes; confirm in writing |
| Replace an agent or executor | New document or amendment | Powers of attorney are usually replaced in full |
| Small change to a will | Codicil | Must be signed with will formalities. See [codicil](/glossary/codicil) |
| Small change to a revocable trust | Trust amendment | Signed and dated as the trust requires |
| Major changes | Restate or sign a new will or trust | Cleaner and avoids confusion between versions |
| New real estate title | New deed | Recorded with the county |

A restatement keeps the original trust's name and date, so assets already titled in the trust stay there. You do not have to retitle everything.

Never write changes on a signed will or trust. Do not discard the old document until the new one is properly signed, and tell your attorney if previous versions exist so there is no conflict. If you restate or replace a document, make sure the older one is clearly revoked, and give updated copies to the people who rely on them.

## Which accounts do people forget to update?

Wills and trusts get the attention, but these items cause many problems.

- **Transfer-on-death and payable-on-death registrations** on bank and brokerage accounts. See [payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).
- **Joint accounts** opened for convenience. They pass to the co-owner, whatever your will says.
- **Employer plans and life insurance.** After a divorce, federal rules for employer plans can mean the named beneficiary is paid regardless of the divorce.
- **Digital accounts** and password managers. See the [digital assets inventory](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory).

## Does a trust make updating easier?

Often yes. A [revocable trust](/glossary/revocable-trust) can be amended without a court, and changing a successor trustee or a distribution age is usually a short amendment. The tradeoff is that assets must be kept funded, so any new account or property needs to be retitled.

## An example of several changes at once

Mark Feldman signed a will and a life insurance form in 2014, when he was married to Susan and had no children. Since then he divorced, married Jenna, had a daughter and moved to another state. His old will still named Susan as executor. His employer life insurance and his healthcare power of attorney still named Susan, and no guardian was named for his daughter.

His update included a new will, a trust for his daughter, a guardian nomination and new powers of attorney under his new state's forms. He also filed new beneficiary forms with his employer and his IRA custodian. The [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit) helps you find every account the way he did.

## What should you do with the old documents?

Once you sign new documents, the old ones can cause confusion. Many attorneys suggest keeping the old originals in a clearly marked folder, or destroying them on purpose, depending on your state's practice and your attorney's advice. Tell your executor, trustee and agents where the new originals are. Give copies of healthcare forms to your doctors and your healthcare agent. If you have a [letter of instruction](/checklists/letter-of-instruction-outline), update it at the same time so it matches the new plan.

## What if you never update?

An outdated plan can still work if nothing important changed. If something did, the possible outcomes include a deceased person named in a key role, an ex-spouse receiving an account, a child left out, or a trust that covers only a fraction of your assets. These problems usually surface after death or incapacity, when no one can change them. The cost is usually borne by your family.

## How can you make reviews a habit?

- Put a recurring reminder in your calendar.
- Keep a one-page summary of your plan: documents, dates, people, locations.
- Check beneficiary forms whenever you receive an annual account statement.
- Ask your attorney whether they offer periodic review meetings.
- Tell your family when you update, so they know which version is current. If the conversation feels hard, see [how to talk to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Is a codicil enough, or should I sign a new will?
- Should I amend my trust or restate it?
- Does my divorce automatically change anything in my state?
- Are my powers of attorney from another state valid here?
- What should I do with my old original documents?
