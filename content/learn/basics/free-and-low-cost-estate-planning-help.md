Plenty of people put off estate planning for the simplest reason: money is tight, and a lawyer sounds like a bill they cannot afford. That worry is fair. It is also the reason this article exists, because there is more free and low-cost help than most people realize, and a plain, valid document is better than none.

We will go through the main sources, who tends to qualify, what each one usually covers and where the limits are. If you want the numbers for paid work first, see [what an estate plan typically costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost). This article is part of our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) library.

## The short version

- Try the free routes first if you qualify. They are real and many are well run.
- Expect simple documents: a will, a financial power of attorney, a healthcare directive and sometimes a HIPAA release.
- If your situation has a house, a blended family, a child with a disability or a business, ask for a paid consultation even if you also use a free program.
- Do not skip the plan because you cannot find the perfect source. Your state's free healthcare form is a place to begin today.

## Where can you get a free will or power of attorney?

Here are the common sources, roughly from most to least widely available.

| Source | Who usually qualifies | What it typically covers |
|---|---|---|
| Legal aid office | Low income, set by the program | Simple wills, powers of attorney, healthcare forms, some probate help. |
| Senior legal services | Age 60 and older, often with income or need priority | Wills, powers of attorney, advance directives, benefits issues. |
| Law school clinic | Varies; sometimes open to anyone who meets income limits | Supervised student work on wills and directives. |
| Military legal assistance | Active-duty members, and often retirees and families | Wills, powers of attorney, sometimes trusts for deployment. |
| Veteran and first-responder programs | Veterans, police, fire and EMS in volunteer events | Wills and powers of attorney at scheduled clinics. |
| Employer or union legal plan | Employees and members who enrolled | A set number of document hours, sometimes with discounts on more. |
| Hospital or hospice social worker | Patients | Help completing a healthcare directive. |
| Charity will months | Often people over a certain age | A basic will through a participating attorney. |

Two reminders. These programs differ a lot by county, and most have limited staff, so you may wait a few weeks. Call, say which documents you want, and ask about eligibility before you travel anywhere.

### Legal aid

Legal aid offices are nonprofit law firms, many of them funded in part through the federal Legal Services Corporation and by state and local grants. Their first job is housing, benefits, safety and family cases. Many still do estate planning for clients who qualify, especially for older people and people with serious illness, since a missing power of attorney leads to bigger legal problems down the road. Look up your state's legal aid directory on your state bar or courts website.

### Senior programs

If you are 60 or older, start with your local Area Agency on Aging. Under the Older Americans Act, each region funds legal assistance for older adults. The attorneys usually work on wills, powers of attorney, advance directives and public benefits. There is generally no charge, though you may be invited to make a voluntary donation. If you are helping a parent, our article on [helping an aging parent get their plan in order](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan) pairs well with this one.

### Military and veteran help

Active-duty service members can usually get a will and power of attorney through a base legal assistance office at no cost. Retirees and some family members are often eligible too. Many communities hold volunteer clinics for police officers, firefighters and paramedics. Veterans' groups and some VA medical centers also host events. Eligibility rules change, so call first.

### Your job or your union

Look in your benefits portal for a legal plan. A common version is a prepaid plan where, for a small monthly premium, you can get a will, a power of attorney and an hour or two of advice. Some plans include a living trust, and some include only a basic will. Read the exclusions, and ask whether the plan lawyer will explain how your beneficiary forms fit with the will.

## What do free programs usually leave out?

Free help is built to handle common, simple cases. It is not built to solve complicated ones. These are the areas where we most often see people need more.

1. **Trusts.** Most free programs do wills, not funded living trusts. If you want to avoid probate, see our guide on [whether you need a living trust](/learn/trusts/do-i-need-a-living-trust).
2. **Special needs planning.** A child on SSI or Medicaid can lose benefits if a will leaves money directly. That requires a specific trust, covered in [leaving an inheritance to a disabled child](/learn/special-needs/leaving-an-inheritance-to-a-disabled-child-or-sibling).
3. **Blended families.** Second marriages and stepchildren create conflicts that a simple form does not address.
4. **Property in more than one state.** Out-of-state land often means a second probate.
5. **Business interests.** An LLC or sole proprietorship needs a plan for who runs it.
6. **Medicaid planning.** The five-year look-back means timing matters, and a mistake can cost months of coverage.

None of this is a reason to refuse free help. It is a reason to be clear about what you are getting, and to ask a paid attorney for a one-time review if one of those situations applies.

## What can you do yourself while you wait?

Several things cost nothing and make any later work faster and cheaper.

1. **Download your state's healthcare directive form.** Most health departments, bar associations and state hospital associations publish one. See [advance directive forms: free state forms, Five Wishes or an attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/advance-directive-forms-five-wishes-or-a-lawyer) for how the choices compare.
2. **Check every beneficiary form.** Log in to each retirement account, life insurance policy and bank account and confirm the names are current. This is free and it controls where a lot of money goes. Our page on [naming a beneficiary on a 401(k), IRA or life insurance policy](/learn/what-if/no-beneficiary-named) shows what happens when it is left blank.
3. **Write down what you own.** A single page with accounts, debts, insurance and contacts is the best head start you can give an attorney or a legal aid volunteer.
4. **Decide on your people.** Pick an executor, a guardian for minor children, a healthcare agent and a financial agent, and a backup for each.
5. **Talk to the person you named.** Ask before you write their name down.

Take Dale, 63, a retired bus driver with a small pension, a paid-off condo and a niece he trusts. He calls his Area Agency on Aging, gets a legal clinic appointment for a simple will and powers of attorney, and fills in his state's free healthcare form at home. He spends an afternoon on his asset list and beneficiary forms. His whole plan costs him a few hours and some postage, and it covers what he cares about.

## Is it worth paying for a simple plan?

Often yes, once you are past the very simplest case. A lawyer's job is not typing a will. It is asking about the things you did not mention: the old life insurance policy that still names your ex, the account you retitled in 2016, the child who could not manage a lump sum. A flat-fee package that covers a will, powers of attorney and healthcare documents is often within reach, and some firms offer payment plans. Ask for the fee in writing and what it includes. Our guide on [how to choose an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) lists the questions.

If the choice is between a free form with no review and a paid review of a free form, the second often delivers most of the value for a fraction of the full fee.

## How state law changes this

Legal aid funding, the number of senior programs and the forms published by the state are all local. So are the rules for what makes a document valid. A free form that works in one state may need a different witness or notary step in another. Your [state guide](/estate-planning/florida) and the guide for your county's senior services office are the right places to start. Our [wills and cost guide](/learn/wills/how-much-does-a-will-cost) gives a sense of what paid help runs for the most basic documents.
