If you came to the United States as an adult, you may carry a quiet worry about what happens to your family here if something happens to you. Maybe your parents are abroad. Maybe your spouse is a citizen and you are not, or the other way around. Maybe you own a small house in the country you left.

The good news is that nothing about immigration status shuts you out of estate planning. The hard parts are narrower than people fear, and you can plan for them. This page walks through the documents, the points where status changes the rules, and the mistakes we see most. It belongs to our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) library.

## Can non-citizens do estate planning at all?

Yes. The legal ability to sign a will, set up a trust or name an agent turns on being an adult with capacity, not on citizenship. Someone on a work visa, a green card holder, a person with temporary status and a naturalized citizen can all sign the same documents.

Some people avoid planning because they worry that paperwork could draw attention to their status. Wills, powers of attorney and healthcare directives are private family documents. They are not filed with immigration authorities. If you have a specific immigration case pending, talk to an immigration attorney about it separately, but do not skip your family's protection because of a vague fear.

## What documents does a non-citizen need?

The same core set as anyone else, with a few extra touches.

| Document | Why it matters | Extra point for immigrant families |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Directs property and names an executor | Consider a separate will for property abroad |
| Guardian nomination | Names who raises minor children | Name someone in the U.S. as backup if relatives are overseas |
| Financial power of attorney | Lets someone pay bills if you are incapacitated | Choose someone who can act here in person |
| Healthcare directive and HIPAA release | Names who speaks with doctors | Add language preferences and cultural or religious wishes |
| Beneficiary forms | Control retirement accounts and life insurance | Check whether the named person can legally receive funds abroad |
| Letter of instruction | Lists accounts, contacts and wishes | Include foreign accounts, relatives' contacts and translations |

Our [documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) describes each of these in detail.

## Why does the guardian choice matter so much?

Because the default is often a hard one. Suppose Amara and Joseph, both from Nigeria, have two children who are U.S. citizens. Their closest relatives live in Lagos. Neither parent has a will. If both die, a judge here decides who takes custody. A relative abroad may face visa and travel obstacles, and a friend nearby may have no legal standing.

A will can name a guardian and a backup, and it can say what the parents want if the family would prefer the children be raised overseas. That is better than leaving a court to guess. Our guide to [choosing a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) covers how to weigh the options, and a [guardian letter](/learn/guardianship/letter-of-intent-for-your-childrens-guardian) can describe the children's routines, schools and cultural traditions.

## How does a non-citizen spouse change the plan?

This is the biggest tax difference. Between spouses who are both citizens, everything left to the survivor is free of federal estate tax under the unlimited marital deduction. That deduction is not available for property left outright to a spouse who is not a U.S. citizen.

The fix is a Qualified Domestic Trust, or QDOT. The assets go into a trust with at least one U.S. trustee, and the survivor receives income. Principal distributions can trigger the tax that would have applied at the first death. The spouse can also become a citizen before the estate tax return is due, and in that case the QDOT may not be needed. It sounds technical, and it is, but it only matters when the combined estate is large enough to approach the exemption. Our guide to the [QDOT rules](/learn/estate-tax/non-citizen-spouse-qdot) explains the mechanics.

Even below the exemption, a non-citizen spouse changes a few everyday things.

- **Gifts between spouses.** The usual rule that spouses can give each other unlimited amounts applies only to citizen spouses. A non-citizen spouse has an annual limit that is larger than the standard $19,000, and it is adjusted each year.
- **Joint ownership.** Putting a house in joint names can have gift tax consequences when one spouse is a non-citizen.
- **Portability.** A surviving spouse who is a citizen can use the deceased spouse's unused exemption. Rules differ when the survivor is not a citizen, so planning ahead matters. See [portability for married couples](/learn/estate-tax/portability-for-married-couples).

## What if you are not a U.S. resident but own U.S. property?

Then you are in different territory. A U.S. citizen or someone domiciled here gets the $15,000,000 per person exclusion in 2026. A nonresident who is not a citizen is generally taxed on U.S. property only, and the exemption on that property is far smaller, historically $60,000, unless a tax treaty between your country and the U.S. says something different.

U.S. real estate, shares of U.S. companies and certain other assets count. Bank deposits and life insurance on your own life are treated more kindly. People who hold a vacation home or investment property here, while living abroad, should get advice before the first death. Our article on [foreign inheritance and U.S. estate tax for non-citizens](/learn/estate-tax/foreign-inheritance-and-us-estate-tax-for-non-citizens) goes through the tests. The [estate tax estimator](/tools/estate-tax-estimator) gives a rough starting number for citizens and residents.

## Should you have a separate will abroad?

Often, if you own real property or significant accounts in another country. Courts in that country usually apply their own succession laws to local land, sometimes with forced heirship rules that limit who you can leave it to. A U.S. will may not be recognized without extra steps, and the process can double the work for your family.

Some families sign two wills drafted to work together, each limited to its own country, so that signing the second does not accidentally revoke the first. This needs a lawyer in each country and clear language. If you do nothing, your family may need to open [ancillary probate](/learn/probate/ancillary-probate) in the second place on top of the main one.

## Practical steps to take this month

1. **List what you own in each country.** Include bank accounts, property, pensions and business interests.
2. **Decide who would raise your children.** Name a person nearby as a backup, even if your first choice is overseas.
3. **Sign a power of attorney and healthcare directive** with people who can act in person here.
4. **Check every beneficiary form** for retirement accounts and insurance.
5. **Write a letter of instruction** that lists contacts, accounts and languages. Our [letter of instruction guide](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) gives a template to follow.
6. **Ask whether a foreign will or foreign advice is needed** for property abroad.

## Mistakes we see most often

- Assuming that no will means the government keeps the property. It does not, but a long search for relatives can delay everything.
- Naming a foreign relative as the only executor, with nobody who can appear in the local court.
- Forgetting that a spouse's citizenship changes the tax picture, even for a couple who have lived here for decades.
- Leaving documents in a language the local court cannot read, with no translation.
- Treating a green card as the whole answer to the question of residency for estate tax.
