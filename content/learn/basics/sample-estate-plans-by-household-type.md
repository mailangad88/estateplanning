Most articles about estate planning explain one document at a time. That is useful, but it leaves a practical question open: when you put the pieces together, what does your plan actually look like? A single nurse in her thirties, a retired couple, and a widowed father of two do not need the same stack of paper.

This page walks through five common households and shows what a sensible basic plan contains for each. The examples are invented, and they are simplified on purpose. Think of them as the first draft an attorney might sketch on a legal pad before asking you questions. For the document-by-document explanations, start with our [estate planning documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist) and the [basics guide](/learn/basics).

## What do all five plans have in common?

Every adult plan rests on four building blocks, and the household type changes who you name in them.

1. **A will or a revocable living trust.** This says who inherits what is left and who is in charge of carrying it out.
2. **A durable financial power of attorney.** This names someone to pay bills and manage accounts if you cannot.
3. **A healthcare directive.** This names a healthcare agent and records your wishes about treatment.
4. **Correct beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts, life insurance and many bank accounts pass by the form on file, not by your will.

The fifth item, a HIPAA release, is often bundled with the third. It lets doctors speak to the people you name. Our guide to [beneficiary forms that override a will](/learn/beneficiary-designations) explains why the fourth block is the one people most often get wrong.

## What does the plan look like for each household?

The table shows the differences at a glance. The sections after it explain the reasoning.

| Household | Who usually inherits | Who handles money if you are ill | Extra pieces to add |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single, no children | Siblings, nieces and nephews, friends, charity | A sibling or trusted friend | Backup agents at every level |
| Married, no children | Spouse first, then family or charity | Spouse, with a successor | Joint review of titles and forms |
| Married with young children | Spouse first, then a trust for the children | Spouse, with a successor | Named guardian, children's trust, life insurance review |
| Single parent | A trust for the children | An adult relative or friend | Guardian, trust, trustee, life insurance |
| Unmarried couple | Each partner, by name | Each partner, by name | Partner-specific language, title review, written healthcare authority |

### A single person, no children

Dana, 34, rents an apartment and has a retirement account at work. If she dies tomorrow without a plan, state law sends everything to relatives she may barely speak with. If she is hurt in a car accident, no spouse can sign for her.

Her plan is lean. A simple will names her sister as executor and splits what is left between her sister and a niece. Her financial power of attorney names the sister first and a close friend second. Her healthcare directive names the friend first, because the sister lives overseas. Her beneficiary form at work matches the will, which is the step people skip. Our article on [estate planning for single people](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people) goes deeper on this setup.

### A married couple with no children

Raymond and Elaine, both 62, own a home together and each has a pension and an IRA. Spouses are the default choice in most states for both inheritance and medical decisions, so their plan is mostly about the second death.

They each sign a will leaving everything to the other, then to a nephew and a local charity. They name each other as financial agent and healthcare agent, with a niece and a nephew as backups. The part that deserves attention is what happens when one of them dies and the survivor is alone. The backup choices stop being theoretical. See [estate planning for couples without children](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-couples-without-children) for the details.

### A married couple with young children

Priya and Marcus have a four-year-old and a baby. The first draft of their plan looks like Raymond and Elaine's, plus three things.

First, a guardian. Each will names the same guardian and a backup, so a court does not choose for them if both parents die. Second, a children's trust, either inside the wills or as a living trust, so the money is managed by a trustee and not handed over at 18. Third, a life insurance check. Many young families own less coverage than they would need to raise two children without a second income. The [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) is a quick way to estimate it. Our guide to [choosing a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) covers the hardest decision on the list.

### A single parent

Tasha, 41, has a nine-year-old daughter and no co-parent involved. She is in the strongest need of a complete plan, because there is no spouse to be the automatic fallback.

Her will names a guardian and a backup. A trust created at her death holds her life insurance, retirement account balance and home equity for her daughter. She chooses a trustee who is different from the guardian, on the theory that one person raises the child and another checks the money. Her financial agent is her brother. Her healthcare agent is her closest friend, who lives ten minutes away. If the other parent is alive and has legal rights, the guardian question gets more complicated, and an attorney should talk it through with her. The article on [what happens if no guardian is named](/learn/guardianship/what-if-no-guardian-named) shows what is at stake.

### An unmarried couple

Owen and Kim have lived together for eleven years. They own a house as tenants in common and have a joint checking account. In the eyes of the law, they are legal strangers for most inheritance and medical purposes.

They each sign a will or trust naming the other, a power of attorney naming the other first, and a healthcare directive that says in plain words that the partner decides, even over the objection of a parent or sibling. They review how the house is titled, since title controls what happens at death more often than a will does. They also check the beneficiary forms. Their plan is the same list as a married couple's, but nothing on it can be skipped. Our article on [estate planning for unmarried couples](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-unmarried-couples) lays out what the law does not give them.

## What do the plans leave out?

A first-draft plan like these does not cover several situations, and you should know where the edges are.

- **Blended families.** If either partner has children from an earlier relationship, a simple "everything to my spouse" will can leave those children with nothing. See our [blended families guide](/learn/blended-families).
- **A child with a disability.** An inheritance can disqualify a person from needs-based benefits. A special needs trust is the usual answer. Start with the [special needs guide](/learn/special-needs).
- **Larger estates.** The federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, so most households do not owe it, but some states tax at lower levels. Our [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) shows where you stand.
- **A business or rental property.** These need their own planning.

## How do you turn a sample into your own plan?

Use this short sequence.

1. Write down who is in your household and who depends on you.
2. List your accounts and say, for each, how it passes: by will, by trust, by beneficiary form or by joint title.
3. Choose the people for each role, and a backup for each one.
4. Decide what the children, if any, receive and at what age.
5. Meet with an attorney, review the draft, and sign with the formalities your state requires.
