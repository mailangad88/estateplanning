A will answers who gets the house and the savings account. It does not answer the questions your family will actually ask: what did she want us to remember, why did he leave it this way, what would they say to us now? An ethical will, also called a legacy letter, is a place for those answers.

This page explains what it is, what it cannot do, how to write one without staring at a blank page, and where to keep it. It is part of our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) series.

## What is an ethical will?

It is a personal document that passes on values instead of property. The tradition goes back centuries in several cultures, where a parent would leave a letter of blessing and advice for children. Today it can be a letter, a notebook, a set of recorded stories or a video. Some are two paragraphs. Some are twenty pages.

The word "will" is misleading, which is why many people prefer "legacy letter." It does not need witnesses or a notary. No court sees it unless you put it in a court file. And it is not a substitute for the legal documents in our [estate planning documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist).

## What is the difference between an ethical will and a legal will?

| | Legal will | Ethical will (legacy letter) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Distribute property, name an executor and guardians | Share values, stories and wishes |
| Legal effect | Enforceable by a court once it is valid | None |
| Formalities | Written, signed, witnessed (two witnesses in Illinois and New Jersey) | None |
| Public? | Usually filed with the probate court | Private unless you choose to share it |
| Who reads it | A judge, the executor, heirs | The people you wrote it for |
| Can include instructions about property | Yes | Not enforceable; can state hopes |

The practical rule: anything you need to happen goes in the legal document. Anything you want your family to understand goes in the letter. Our article on [notes, letters and spoken wishes](/learn/wills/notes-letters-and-spoken-wishes-do-they-bind-your-family) explains why a handwritten note about a ring does not control who gets the ring.

## Why write one?

Four reasons come up in our practice more than any others.

1. **Grief is easier with words.** Families keep these letters for decades.
2. **Explanations prevent fights.** If you left one child more because that child cared for you, a plain sentence about it can help, as long as it stays kind. See [disinheriting or leaving unequal shares](/learn/wills/disinheriting-or-unequal-shares).
3. **Requests cost nothing.** You can ask that the family keep the cabin in the family, or visit your sister each month, or finish a project. The family may or may not do so, but they will know you asked.
4. **It prompts the conversation.** Many people write one and then talk. Our guide to [talking to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) helps with that.

## How do you write a legacy letter?

Start with a few prompts and write for twenty minutes without editing. A rough draft is better than none.

1. **Begin with who you are writing to.** One letter per person is often better than a single letter to everyone. A letter to a grandchild reads differently from one to a spouse.
2. **Tell one story.** Pick a moment that shows what you valued. "The summer your father and I drove to Wisconsin with eleven dollars" does more than "I valued hard work."
3. **Say thank you.** Name something specific the person did for you.
4. **State what you hope for them.** Not instructions, hopes.
5. **Say what you regret or what you forgive.** Only if you are ready. It is optional, and often the part that matters most.
6. **Add practical facts that belong in the story.** A family recipe, where the photos are, who the photo is of.
7. **End plainly.** You do not need a grand finish.

### Example: a letter that explains a decision

Example only. Gloria, 74, of Evanston, left her house to her daughter Rita, who moved in to care for her, and split her investments equally between Rita and her son Paul. She wrote Paul a page: "I want you to know I did not choose Rita over you. I chose to repay her the years she gave me. You have always had my heart, and the money is equal. When I think of you I think of the time we fixed the porch roof together." Paul read it after the funeral. The legal documents were clear. The letter let him hear the reason in his mother's voice.

## What are good sentences to start with?

Blank pages stop more legacy letters than anything else. Borrow a starter and finish it honestly.

Try "The best advice anyone gave me was." Or try "One thing I got wrong, and what I learned, is." Or "When I think about your grandmother, I remember." Others work as well. "I am proud of you for." "If money is ever tight, remember that." "What I want you to do with the family photographs is." Finish whichever one you can.

Short answers are fine. Three sentences from a grandfather about the day he started his first job can mean more to a ten-year-old than a polished essay. If writing is hard for you, record yourself answering these starters on a phone, then have someone type out what you said.

## How long should it be, and can it change?

As long as it needs to be, and yes. A page is plenty. Date it and sign it, because a date tells your family when the thoughts were written, and many people revisit the letter after a marriage, a birth, a diagnosis or a death in the family. Our guide to [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) gives a natural schedule: review the letter when you review the plan.

## Where should you keep it?

Keep a copy with your will and documents, and tell your executor it exists. Do not attach it to the will. A will is typically filed with the court after death, and a letter attached to it might become part of a record that strangers can view. Our article on [organizing your estate planning documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents) shows a folder system.

If you want the letter given to someone on a specific day, such as a grandchild's graduation, ask your attorney or a trusted friend to hold it, with written instructions. Keep digital recordings where your family can find them, and list the file in your [digital asset inventory](/learn/digital-assets/how-to-create-a-digital-asset-inventory-your-executor-can-use).

## What should an ethical will not do?

Avoid four things.

- **Do not use it to change your plan.** If you want to leave something to someone, change the will or trust. A letter that contradicts your will may sow confusion or fuel a contest.
- **Do not attack anyone.** A cruel line in a letter can turn a quiet estate into a dispute. If you cannot be kind, leave it out or write it and then do not send it.
- **Do not hide your health wishes in it.** Medical wishes belong in an advance directive. See [how to document your end-of-life wishes](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-document-your-end-of-life-wishes).
- **Do not make a promise you cannot keep.** If you say the house will always stay in the family, make sure the legal plan supports it.
