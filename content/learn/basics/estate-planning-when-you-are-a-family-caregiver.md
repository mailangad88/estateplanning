You may have started with a few errands: a ride to the cardiologist, a check on the pill organizer, a stack of mail. Now it is most of your week. Maybe your mother lives with you, or your father is fifteen minutes away and calls every afternoon. Most advice for people in your position is about the parent's plan. This page is about yours, and it builds on the [estate planning basics](/learn/basics).

A caregiver is a single point of failure. If something happens to you, the person you look after has no one else. If you run out of money, they feel it too. A good plan for the caregiver covers four things: who steps in for you, how you handle your parent's money, how you protect your own, and how you keep the rest of the family informed.

## Why does a caregiver need their own estate plan?

Because your parent's safety rests on you, and the plan has to say what happens if you cannot continue. Most estate plans assume a healthy adult who comes and goes from the picture. Yours has a built-in dependency.

Consider Walter, 57, who looks after his 84-year-old mother, Lena. He is divorced, with one adult son. Walter has a heart attack on a Tuesday. Lena has no idea where his financial records are, which pharmacy fills her prescriptions, or how to reach her doctor's office. His son lives in another state and finds out two days later. For that week, Lena depends on a neighbor.

A short list of documents would have changed that week.

1. **A financial power of attorney** for Walter, naming his son and a backup, so someone can keep paying the bills for both households.
2. **A healthcare directive and HIPAA release** for Walter, so his son can speak with his doctors.
3. **A will, or a revocable trust,** that says who inherits and who is in charge, and speaks to Lena if she lives in his home.
4. **A one-page care summary** with medications, doctors, contacts, schedules and where the passwords are kept.

The fourth item is not a legal document, and it may be the most valuable. Our guide to a [letter of instruction](/learn/basics/letter-of-instruction) shows how to build one.

## What should be in your parent's documents about you?

Your parent's plan should name a backup for you, because you are probably their agent. If you hold the power of attorney and the healthcare proxy, a single point of failure runs through both.

Ask your parent, while they can still decide, to sign documents that name you first and a second person behind you. The second person should not be someone who will fight with you. Our article on [how to choose a power of attorney agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/choosing-a-power-of-attorney-agent) covers the qualities that count, and [can you name more than one agent](/learn/power-of-attorney/can-you-name-more-than-one-power-of-attorney-agent) explains co-agents and successors.

If your parent already shows memory loss, time matters. Capacity to sign is judged at the moment of signing, and a parent in the early stages of dementia can often still sign valid documents. Do not wait for a better day that may not come. Our article on [making an estate plan for someone else](/learn/basics/can-you-make-an-estate-plan-for-someone-else) explains what you can and cannot do on a parent's behalf.

## How do you keep your parent's money separate from yours?

Keep it in an account in your parent's name, with you signing as agent, and never mix it with your own. Mixed money is the single most common reason a well-meaning caregiver ends up in a dispute with a sibling.

Here are the habits that protect you.

- Open or keep a checking account titled in your parent's name. Sign as "Your Name, agent for Parent's Name."
- Pay for your parent's costs from that account, not from yours, and save receipts.
- If you spend your own money on your parent, write down what, when and why, and ask whether your parent's documents allow reimbursement.
- Do not add yourself to your parent's account or title just to make bill paying easier. A power of attorney does that job without exposing the property to your creditors or changing who inherits.
- Send a short summary to your siblings every quarter. Transparency costs nothing and prevents most accusations.

An agent is a fiduciary, which means a legal duty to put the parent first. The article on [what a power of attorney agent has to do](/learn/power-of-attorney/what-does-a-power-of-attorney-agent-have-to-do) lists the records to keep. If a sibling already suspects the worst, see how [elder financial abuse](/learn/elder-care/elder-financial-abuse) is defined, so you know where the line is.

## How do you protect your own finances?

Caregiving costs real money, and the cost is easy to miss: reduced hours, lost raises, a smaller retirement contribution, mileage, groceries, home modifications. Over several years, the total can exceed what you would have spent on a daughter's tuition.

You have three tools.

| Tool | What it does | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Personal care agreement | Pays you a fair rate for documented services | Must be in writing, signed in advance and match local rates |
| Reimbursement records | Lets you recoup what you spent on your parent | Needs receipts and, ideally, siblings' awareness |
| Tax review | May allow a deduction or credit for care costs | Depends on your parent's income and whether you claim them as a dependent |

A personal care agreement has pitfalls. Payments for care may count as income to you, and for a parent who may need Medicaid, a poorly drafted agreement can look like a gift and trigger a penalty. Our articles on [paying a family caregiver](/learn/elder-care/paying-a-family-caregiver) and [tax breaks when you pay for a parent's care](/learn/elder-care/tax-breaks-when-you-pay-for-a-parents-care) explain the rules, and [should you quit your job to care for a parent](/learn/elder-care/should-you-quit-your-job-to-care-for-a-parent) walks through the retirement math before you decide.

## What if your parent lives in your home, or you live in theirs?

Shared housing is the situation where a caregiver's own plan matters most. If your mother lives in your house and you die, your heirs may sell it. If you live in her house and she dies, your siblings may expect the proceeds to be split.

Neither outcome is unfair in itself. The problem is surprise. Talk about it now, and write down the decision. Some families draft a life estate or a lease. Others agree that the caregiver can stay for a set time. If you intend to leave something for your parent in your will, say so in the will, not in a promise to a sibling. Our article on [moving a parent in with you](/learn/elder-care/moving-a-parent-in-with-you-or-to-another-state) lists the questions to ask first.

## How do you get the family on the same page?

Hold one meeting, with an agenda, before a crisis. Invite everyone who has a stake. Bring copies of the signed documents, a list of who does what, and a rough budget. Ask your siblings what they can contribute: money, a weekend a month, the bookkeeping, a weekly phone call.

Some caregivers resist because they fear a fight. The fight is more likely when siblings learn about decisions later. If the meeting goes badly, an attorney or a geriatric care manager can lead the next one. For a longer approach, read our guide to [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan).
