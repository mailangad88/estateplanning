When people start looking for help, they run into a pile of overlapping labels. Estate planner, elder law attorney, probate lawyer, financial advisor, wealth manager, trust officer. Each website describes itself differently. It is easy to pay for the wrong one, or to assume that the person you already work with covers everything.

This page sorts those jobs out. It is written for people at the start of the process, and it sits alongside our guide on [choosing an estate planning attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney) in the [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) library.

## How do the four fields compare?

| Field | Main question | Typical output | Who does it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Estate planning | Who decides and who inherits? | Will, trust, powers of attorney, directives, beneficiary review | Lawyer |
| Elder law | How do we pay for care and protect the family? | Medicaid plan, long-term care strategy, guardianship alternatives | Lawyer |
| Probate | How is this estate settled? | Court filings, inventory, claims, distribution | Lawyer for the executor |
| Financial planning | How do I fund my life and goals? | Investment plan, retirement income, insurance review | Planner or advisor |

None is a substitute for the others. Your plan can be well funded and legally empty, or legally tight and badly invested.

## What does estate planning cover?

Estate planning is the legal side of your finances. It decides who acts if you cannot, who receives your property, who cares for minors, and how it is done with the least delay and cost. The documents are laid out in our [basic and comprehensive plan guide](/learn/basics/basic-vs-comprehensive-estate-plan). The work happens before the event, while you can choose.

## What does elder law cover?

Elder law is estate planning's close relative with a different emphasis: what happens if you need care for years. An elder law attorney handles Medicaid eligibility, the five-year look-back for transfers in most states, protection of a spouse who stays at home, veterans benefits and guardianship questions. The subject is often urgent, because the client is already in or near a care facility.

Many estate planning attorneys do some of this, and many elder law attorneys draft wills and trusts. The names reflect where a lawyer spends most of their time. If you are under 60 and healthy, estate planning is probably the right starting point. If a parent has dementia or you are looking at a nursing home, start with [Medicaid planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and [helping aging parents plan](/learn/elder-care/helping-aging-parents-plan). The page on [what happens when a plan has to be made for someone else](/learn/basics/can-you-make-an-estate-plan-for-someone-else) explains the limits.

## What does probate involve, and who needs a probate lawyer?

Probate is not planning. It is what the executor does after a death to carry out the will under court supervision. The executor files the will, gets authority from the court, notifies creditors, pays debts and taxes, and distributes what is left. Our page on [what probate is](/learn/probate/what-is-probate) lays out the stages.

The people who need this help are executors and heirs, not the person who died. A probate attorney guides them, and a page like [do I need a probate lawyer](/learn/probate/do-i-need-a-probate-lawyer) can help you decide. Estate planning makes the executor's job easier. A thorough plan can mean a short probate, or none for most assets.

## What does a financial planner do that a lawyer doesn't?

A financial planner builds a plan for how much you can spend, how to invest, and when to claim Social Security. They may recommend life insurance or long-term care coverage. A planner is paid differently from a lawyer, whether through fees, commissions or a share of assets managed, and it helps to ask which.

What a planner cannot do is draft your legal documents. The ideal team has both: the financial advisor knows your portfolio, the lawyer sets up how it passes. When the two do not talk, beneficiary forms are the usual casualty. A planner opens an account and names the first child as beneficiary. The trust the lawyer drafted names three. Nobody notices until a death.

## Where do accountants and others fit?

An accountant prepares your tax returns and can model the income tax effect of transfers. A trust officer at a bank can serve as trustee. A life insurance agent can fund a plan. A real estate agent sells the house. Each is useful, and none replaces a lawyer for the legal documents.

## Is an "estate planning attorney" different from a regular lawyer?

It is the same license. The difference is where the lawyer spends their time. Someone who writes trusts every week has seen the failures that come from mistitled accounts or loose language. A general practice attorney may be perfectly competent, and a good one will tell you when to go elsewhere.

Ask a few direct questions before you hire anyone:

1. What share of your work is estate planning?
2. Do you also handle probate, and elder law, or will you tell me when I need different help?
3. How do you handle changes later, and what do they cost?
4. Who will actually draft my documents?

## Where do prenups and other agreements fit?

A prenuptial agreement settles what each spouse keeps. It does not replace a will, and it cannot name a guardian. In a second marriage, you need both. See [prenuptial agreements and estate plans](/learn/blended-families/prenuptial-agreements-and-estate-plans).

## What does it look like when several are needed?

Consider Helen, 72, widowed, with a house, a modest portfolio and two sons. Her financial planner keeps her retirement income steady. Her estate planning attorney updates her will, powers of attorney and healthcare directive after her husband's death. Then she is diagnosed with a progressive illness, and her sons start asking about care costs. Now an elder law attorney joins the conversation, because the question has become how to pay for care without exhausting what she hopes to leave. When Helen dies years later, the older son as executor works with a probate attorney, who files the will and settles the estate.

Four professionals, four different jobs, in order. The planning done early made the later steps shorter. Her sons found the documents, knew the advisor, and had a lawyer to call. That is the practical point of sorting these fields out: not to buy more help than you need, but to know who to call when the problem changes shape.

## Which one should you start with?

| Your situation | Start with |
|---|---|
| Healthy, working, family to protect | Estate planning attorney |
| Parent heading into a care facility | Elder law attorney |
| Someone close has died and you are the executor | Probate attorney |
| Money growing, retirement approaching | Financial planner, then your lawyer |
| Mixed, or you are not sure | Begin with one attorney and ask |
