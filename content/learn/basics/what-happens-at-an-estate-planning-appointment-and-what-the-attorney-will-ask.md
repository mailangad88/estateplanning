Most people walk into their first estate planning appointment expecting a lecture on legal terms and a pile of forms. What usually happens is closer to an interview. The attorney asks a lot of questions, many of them personal, and builds a picture of your family and finances before recommending anything.

This page, part of our [estate planning basics section](/learn/basics), explains what happens from the moment you sit down, the questions you will be asked and why, and what comes after. For a checklist of papers to carry in, see [what to bring to your first meeting](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation).

## What happens at an estate planning appointment?

A typical first meeting runs in four parts.

1. **Introductions and confidentiality.** The attorney explains who the client is, what is private, and how fees work.
2. **Your family and your goals.** You talk through who you are protecting and what you want.
3. **Your assets and how they are owned.** This is where the paperwork matters.
4. **Recommendations and next steps.** The attorney explains what documents you need, what they cost and when you can sign.

At the end you usually leave with a plan in outline, a list of anything still missing and a date for the next contact. Signing normally happens at a later appointment, after you review a draft.

## What questions will the attorney ask about my family?

Expect these, roughly in this order.

- Are you married or in a civil union? Have you been before?
- Do you have children, including from other relationships? How old are they?
- Does any child have special needs, a health issue, debts, an addiction or a rocky marriage?
- Are there grandchildren, stepchildren or others you support?
- Who would you trust to raise minor children if something happened to you and your spouse?
- Is there anyone you want to leave out, and why?

These sound intrusive, but each answer changes the documents. A child with a disability may need a trust to keep benefits. A child in a troubled marriage may need an inheritance held in trust. A second marriage may need provisions that protect children from the first. If you are in that position, read about [preventing inheritance fights in a blended family](/learn/blended-families/preventing-inheritance-fights-in-a-blended-family).

## Why does the attorney ask how my assets are titled?

Because title decides who gets the asset, regardless of what your will says. A house owned jointly with your spouse passes to the spouse. A retirement account passes to the person named on the form. A bank account with a payable-on-death designation goes to that person without probate. Only what is left in your name alone passes under your will.

The attorney will ask about:

| Asset | What they want to know |
|---|---|
| Home and other real estate | Whose name is on the deed, any mortgage, value, and which states |
| Bank and brokerage accounts | Whose name, whether joint, whether payable on death |
| Retirement accounts and life insurance | Who is the named beneficiary and contingent |
| Business interests | Entity type, partners, agreements |
| Digital assets and crypto | Which accounts matter and how heirs would reach them |
| Debts | Mortgages, loans, co-signed debts |

You do not need exact figures. Round numbers work. If you cannot find a beneficiary form, say so; the attorney can help request copies. See [how to inventory your assets](/learn/basics/how-to-inventory-your-assets-and-value-your-estate) for a worksheet.

## What will they ask about the people who will carry out my plan?

This is the part clients most often want more time on. You will be asked to name five roles.

- An **executor** to settle your estate
- A **trustee**, if a trust is involved
- An **agent** under a power of attorney, to manage money if you cannot
- A **healthcare agent** to make medical decisions
- A **guardian** for minor children

For each, a backup. Most people do not have a full set ready, and that is fine. Think about reliability, honesty, location and willingness, not about who would be offended by being left out. The guides on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [choosing a guardian](/learn/guardianship/how-to-choose-a-guardian) walk through it.

## Will anything I say be repeated to my family?

No. What you tell your attorney is protected by attorney-client privilege and confidentiality rules. That includes information about family conflict, finances and health. If your spouse or another person joins the meeting, the attorney will explain how that affects privilege and how conflicts are handled. In a joint meeting with a married couple, the attorney represents both and tells you what that means.

If you want to bring a child, ask the attorney first. A common approach is to meet alone and then invite the child for the logistics, such as where documents are kept.

## What should I be honest about?

Everything that could change the plan. Our attorneys see the same few topics held back, and each one matters:

1. A child who owes money or has a lawsuit.
2. A secret relationship or a child from outside a marriage.
3. Health conditions, including memory problems.
4. A prior will or agreement, such as a prenuptial agreement.
5. Property in another state or country.
6. Debts you do not want your spouse to know about, which affect planning for the spouse.

None of these will surprise an attorney. Withholding them can produce a plan that fails at the moment it is needed.

## An example of how a meeting goes

Tom and Grace, both 62, come in with a house in Morristown, two retirement accounts, a rental condo and two adult sons. The attorney starts by asking what prompted the visit. Grace says a friend died without a will. Tom admits he is worried about their younger son, who has had trouble holding a job.

Over the next hour the attorney learns three things that shape the plan: the condo is in Tom's name only, one retirement account still lists Tom's late first wife as beneficiary, and Tom wants Grace to be protected but also wants his sons to inherit eventually. None of that was on the intake form. The recommendation that follows, a revocable trust for each spouse with the younger son's share held in trust, comes straight out of the conversation, not a template.

## How can I prepare in ten minutes?

Write your answers to these four questions on one page before you go: who do you want to protect, who would you trust to act for you, what worries you most, and what must not happen. Then bring a rough list of assets. That is enough to make the first hour productive.

## What will I leave with?

Usually these four things:

- A recommendation: will only, will plus powers of attorney, or a living trust-based plan. The [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) can give you a head start.
- A fee quote, typically flat for standard plans. See [how much estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost).
- A list of documents or details still needed.
- A timeline for the draft and the signing meeting.

## What happens after the first meeting?

The attorney drafts the documents, you review them, and you come back to sign with witnesses and, where required, a notary. Illinois and New Jersey both have specific signing requirements for wills, and the attorney handles them. After signing, you update beneficiary forms and, for a trust, retitle your assets. [How long it takes to create an estate plan](/learn/basics/how-long-does-it-take-to-create-an-estate-plan) gives typical timelines.
