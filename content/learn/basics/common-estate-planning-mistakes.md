Most estate plans that fail do not fail because of clever legal traps. They fail because of small, ordinary oversights: a form nobody updated, a house nobody retitled, a backup nobody named. The damage appears years later, when the person who could have fixed it can no longer act.

This guide lists the most frequent mistakes, shows what each costs, and gives a fix. Use it as a self-audit alongside the [estate planning documents checklist](/learn/basics/estate-planning-documents-checklist), and see our [estate planning guide](/learn/basics) for the full picture.

## Mistake 1: Having no plan at all

Without a will, your state's intestacy rules decide who inherits. Without incapacity documents, a court may choose who manages your life. The outcomes are predictable, public and often not what you would have picked. A partner who is not married to you may inherit nothing. A child who needs special care may inherit a sum that costs them their benefits. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

**Fix:** Start with the core documents. Even a simple plan beats the default.

## Mistake 2: Outdated beneficiary designations

Retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death accounts go to whoever is named on the form, not to whoever your will names. Divorces, remarriages, deaths and births regularly leave forms stale.

Consider Walter, who divorced in 2009 and remarried in 2015. His will leaves everything to his second wife, Gail. His 401(k), worth most of his savings, still names his first wife. Federal law governing many employer plans can require the plan to follow the form, and Gail has little recourse. The family spends a year arguing; the money goes to the ex.

**Fix:** Review every beneficiary form after any family change and at least every few years. See [beneficiary designations](/learn/beneficiary-designations) and [life insurance beneficiary mistakes](/learn/beneficiary-designations/life-insurance-beneficiaries).

## Mistake 3: Naming no contingent beneficiary

If your named beneficiary dies before you and no backup exists, the account may default to your estate, which means probate, possible creditor claims and, for retirement accounts, faster taxation. Minor children named directly may require a court-supervised guardianship of the money.

**Fix:** Name primary and contingent beneficiaries on every account. See [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries).

## Mistake 4: Creating a trust and never funding it

A trust controls only what it holds. If you sign a living trust but leave your house, brokerage account and bank accounts in your own name, those assets may still go through probate, which defeats the purpose. This is one of the most common failures in plans purchased without follow-through.

**Fix:** After signing, retitle real estate, change account ownership or beneficiary designations as your attorney directs, and keep a list of what has been transferred. See [how to fund a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust). A [pour-over will](/learn/wills/pour-over-will) is a safety net, not a substitute.

## Mistake 5: Skipping the incapacity documents

Many people plan for death and forget disability. A stroke, accident or dementia can leave you alive but unable to sign checks or consent to treatment. Without a power of attorney, your family may need a court to appoint a guardian or conservator. Without a healthcare agent and directive, doctors and family may disagree about your care.

**Fix:** Sign a [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney), a [healthcare power of attorney](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney) and a [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will). If you are planning for a diagnosis that may affect capacity, timing matters; see [planning ahead for dementia](/learn/healthcare-directives/dementia-and-advance-directives).

## Mistake 6: Choosing the wrong people, or no backups

People often name the eldest child out of habit, or a spouse who would be overwhelmed. Naming one person with no alternate leaves a gap if they die, decline or refuse.

| Role | Common mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Executor | Naming a person who lives far away or dislikes paperwork | Choose for reliability and organization; name a backup |
| Agent under power of attorney | Choosing the person you trust most emotionally, not financially | Choose someone honest and capable with money |
| Guardian | Not asking the person first | Ask, and name a second choice |
| Trustee | Naming a child who has a conflict with siblings | Consider a neutral co-trustee or professional |

See guidance on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).

## Mistake 7: Adding a child to the deed or bank account

Parents commonly add an adult child to a house deed or account "to avoid probate." The shortcut can expose the asset to the child's creditors, divorce or lawsuits, can create gift tax filing issues, and can cost the child the step-up in basis that inheritance would provide. It can also put one child in control of what you meant to share. See [the hidden risks of adding a child to your deed](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) and [step-up in basis](/learn/estate-tax/step-up-in-basis).

**Fix:** Use a transfer-on-death deed (where allowed), a trust, or a will, depending on your goals. See [leaving your house to your children](/learn/property-and-assets/leaving-your-home-to-children).

## Mistake 8: Leaving an inheritance outright to young or vulnerable heirs

A child who turns 18 can receive a lump sum with no oversight. A beneficiary who has a disability may lose SSI or Medicaid. A beneficiary in debt, in an unstable marriage, or with an addiction may lose the money quickly.

**Fix:** Use a trust with a trustee and staged distributions. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children), [special needs trusts](/learn/special-needs/special-needs-trust) and [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Mistake 9: Ignoring blended-family realities

In a second marriage, leaving everything to your spouse "to take care of the kids" puts your children's inheritance in the hands of someone who may later change their own plan. Leaving everything to your children can leave your spouse without a home.

**Fix:** Decide on purpose, and use tools such as marital trusts to protect both. See [blended families](/learn/blended-families) and [how to make sure children from a first marriage inherit](/learn/blended-families/protecting-children-from-first-marriage).

## Mistake 10: Forgetting about digital assets and personal property

Email, cloud photos, cryptocurrency and online accounts have no automatic path to heirs, and some are lost forever if no one can get in. Sentimental items cause as many family fights as money does.

**Fix:** Keep an access plan for digital assets and a written list for specific personal items. See [digital assets in your estate plan](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).

## Mistake 11: Never reviewing or updating

A plan that was perfect in 2012 may name a deceased executor, assume a state tax threshold that changed, or ignore three grandchildren. Review after life events and on a schedule; see [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## Mistake 12: Not telling anyone where things are

An executor who does not know a will exists, or a family that cannot find the safe deposit key, loses weeks. Keep originals in a known place and share the location. More on this in [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will), and on starting the family conversation, see [how to talk to your family about your estate plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan).

## Mistake 13: Over-relying on DIY or generic forms

Forms are not wrong in themselves, but they cannot see your assets, your family or your state's rules. A mistake in witnessing or titling may not be discovered until it is too late. See [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) and the cost discussion in [how much an estate plan costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost).

## A quick self-audit

Answer yes or no:

1. Do I have a current will, signed with the right formalities?
2. Do I have a financial power of attorney and healthcare directive?
3. Have I checked every beneficiary form in the last three years?
4. Does every role have a backup?
5. If I have a trust, are my assets in it?
6. Do my named people know and agree?
7. Does anyone know where the originals are?
8. Has my family changed since I last signed?

Each "no" is a specific, fixable task.

For a sense of how common these gaps are, see our collection of [estate planning statistics](/learn/basics/estate-planning-statistics).
