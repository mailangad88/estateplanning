Plenty of capable people try to do their own estate planning. They are comfortable with software, they dislike paying for something that looks like fill-in-the-blank, and the stakes feel abstract. That is a reasonable instinct, and in some situations it works.

Here is where it holds up, where it breaks, and a way to get most of the savings without the worst of the risk. The question sits at the center of our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics), so it comes up with nearly every reader.

## The short version

- It is legal everywhere to make your own will, power of attorney, healthcare directive and trust.
- Validity is a low bar. A document can be valid and still do the wrong thing.
- The riskiest part is everything the form does not ask: how assets are titled, who is on beneficiary forms, what happens to a young heir.
- If you go DIY, have a lawyer review the finished set once.

## When does doing it yourself work?

It works best when your situation matches what the form assumes. Think of Tom, 34, single, renting an apartment, with a 401(k) and a savings account. His parents are healthy and he has no children. A basic will, a financial power of attorney, a healthcare directive and updated beneficiary forms cover most of what can go wrong. Mistakes are cheap to fix, and if his life gets more complicated he can revisit the plan.

Spouses with a modest estate, adult children, and no unusual wishes are similar. Our page on [having few assets](/learn/basics/do-i-need-an-estate-plan-with-few-assets) explains why even a small plan matters.

## Where do DIY estate plans go wrong?

The legal mistakes are mostly mechanical. The planning mistakes are the costly ones.

| Problem | What it looks like | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Signing errors | Too few witnesses, no notary where required, a missing date | A will can be rejected; a power of attorney can be refused by a bank |
| Beneficiary mismatch | Will says split equally; the 401(k) names one child | The form wins and the will does not change it |
| Title mismatch | House held jointly with right of survivorship | It passes to the co-owner whatever the will says |
| Unfunded trust | Trust signed, nothing retitled | Probate still happens; see [how to fund a trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust) |
| Generic terms | Lump sum to a child at 18 | The child receives it outright |
| Wrong state's rules | A form drafted for a different state | Clauses that do nothing or fail |
| No incapacity plan | Will but no power of attorney | The family may need court to manage your money |

Take the first row. A will usually needs to be signed in front of witnesses, and in some states a notary or a self-proving affidavit makes probate faster. A form can print instructions, but it cannot see you sign. Our guide on [making a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) lists what courts look for.

## What do online services actually do?

They ask questions and then assemble a document. Some are good at that. The better ones cover state differences and offer attorney review at an extra cost. None of them are your lawyer, and they say so. They cannot tell you that your brother-in-law is a poor choice as trustee, or that your house deed names your first wife.

If you use one, treat it as a starting draft. Read every page. Do not pick defaults you do not understand, especially those that name backup agents, backup guardians, or the age at which a child receives money. Our comparison of [online trusts and lawyers](/learn/trusts/online-living-trust-vs-lawyer) goes into more detail on trusts, and the page on [handwritten and DIY wills](/learn/wills/handwritten-and-diy-wills) covers wills.

## What do you get from a lawyer that a form does not give you?

Three things. First, questions. A good attorney asks what you own, how it is titled, who is in your family, who has debts, who would fight. Second, coordination: the will, trust, deeds and beneficiary forms are made to agree. Third, a signing done correctly, with witnesses and a notary, and a record that you understood what you signed, which helps if the plan is challenged.

That does not mean every lawyer's plan is flawless. An attorney can draft a plan that no longer fits your facts, and a plan is only as good as the information you gave. The difference is that you have a person to go back to. For fees, see [what estate planning costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost), and for choosing one, [how to choose an attorney](/learn/basics/how-to-choose-an-estate-planning-attorney).

## A practical middle path

1. Gather your information first: assets, titles, beneficiaries, family.
2. Draft the documents yourself, or use a reputable service.
3. Have an attorney review the set rather than create it. Many will do this for a fixed fee, though practices vary.
4. Sign with proper formalities, not at the kitchen table.
5. Change beneficiary forms and deeds to match the plan.
6. Store the originals as in our page on [organizing your documents](/learn/basics/how-to-organize-your-estate-planning-documents).

## How do you check a DIY draft before you sign?

Read it out loud, slowly, as if you were the person who has to carry it out. Then ask a short list of questions:

- If my first choice for executor or agent said no, is there a named backup?
- If I died tomorrow, who gets the house, the retirement account and the life insurance, and do the documents and forms say the same thing?
- If a young child or a person with special needs inherits, who manages the money and until when?
- If my spouse and I die together, what happens?
- Does every name, date and address match my identification?

Any answer you cannot give from the paper in front of you is a gap. Gaps are exactly what a one-hour attorney review tends to find.

## Does mental capacity matter if you do it yourself?

It matters the same either way. To sign a will you generally need to understand what you own, who your family is, and what the document does, a standard called [testamentary capacity](/glossary/testamentary-capacity). A lawyer can note in the file how you were doing on the day, which helps if the plan is challenged. A solo signing leaves no such record, so it is a poor route for anyone who is ill or in early cognitive decline.

## Situations where a template is not enough

A few facts push you toward a lawyer almost regardless of cost. A child or spouse with a disability who receives public benefits. A second marriage where you want to provide for a spouse and children from a first marriage. A business, a rental property or land in more than one state. An estate near the federal exemption, which is $15 million per person in 2026. A parent in cognitive decline. Any chance of a family dispute.
