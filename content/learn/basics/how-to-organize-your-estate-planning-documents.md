Estate planning documents only work if somebody can put a hand on them. A will in an attic trunk, a healthcare directive in a bank vault, and a power of attorney that exists only as a file on a laptop with a forgotten password: all signed correctly, none of them useful on the day the family needs them.

This page is about the unglamorous part of a plan: where things go, who holds a copy, and how to make the arrangement survive an emergency. For the will in particular, our page on [where to keep your original will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will) goes deeper. Here we cover the whole file, as part of the broader [estate planning basics](/learn/basics).

## What goes where?

Each document has a different reader, and the reader has a different deadline. A hospital wants your healthcare directive in minutes. A probate court wants the original will within weeks. A bank wants the power of attorney the day your agent walks in. Match the storage to the clock.

| Document | Who needs it, and when | Where the original lives | Who gets a copy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will | Executor, after death | Fireproof home safe, attorney's vault, or court deposit if offered | Executor (location, not necessarily text) |
| Revocable living trust | Successor trustee, at incapacity or death | Same as will | Successor trustee, your attorney |
| Financial power of attorney | Agent, possibly with no warning | Home file, easy to reach | Agent, and your bank if it asks |
| Healthcare directive and proxy | Hospital and agent, in an emergency | Home file | Agent, backup agent, your doctor |
| HIPAA authorization | Doctors and named people | Home file | Doctor's office, named people |
| Beneficiary forms | Plan administrators | Held by the institution | Your own copy in the index |
| Letter of instruction | Family, within days | With the will and with one trusted person | Executor |

That table makes one thing visible: the documents you would use while alive belong at hand, and the ones used after death belong somewhere secure. Getting that backwards is the usual mistake. A family rushing to a hospital cannot wait for a bank to open on Monday.

## How do you build a one-page index?

The index is the core of the system. It lists locations and contacts, not contents. If it is lost, it should reveal nothing a thief could use.

1. List each document, its date, and where the original is.
2. Add your attorney, accountant, financial advisor, insurance agent and the name of your primary doctor, each with a phone number.
3. List every account by institution and type, with no account numbers. "Checking at First Harbor Credit Union" is enough.
4. Add recurring bills that would lapse: property tax, homeowner's insurance, life insurance premiums, subscriptions.
5. Note the location of keys: house, safe, safe deposit box, storage unit, vehicle titles.
6. Write the location of your password manager or the instructions for getting in.
7. Date it. Replace it when anything changes.

Our guide to [inventorying your assets](/learn/basics/how-to-inventory-your-assets-and-value-your-estate) walks through the longer version if you also want values. The index is the short one your family reads first.

## Is a safe deposit box a good idea?

For the original will, it can be fine if the bank will let your executor in. Many states have a procedure that allows a named person to open a box only to remove a will, and some banks will let a co-renter do it directly. Others require a court order, and that takes time. For anything urgent, a box is the wrong place.

Rosa, 63, kept her signed healthcare directive in a safe deposit box and told her daughter where it was. When Rosa had a stroke on a Saturday, nobody could open the box until Monday, and the hospital relied on a surrogate form. The directive was valid, but it was not at hand. The fix cost nothing: a copy in a folder at home and one with her daughter.

If you do use a box, add your executor as an authorized person, and put the key somewhere your family knows about.

## What about digital copies?

Scan every signed document and keep the scans in an encrypted folder or a password manager that allows file attachments. Digital copies help in three ways: they survive a house fire, you can send them in seconds to a hospital or bank, and your attorney can check them for consistency. They do not replace paper originals for wills.

Passwords are a separate matter. A will is a poor place for them, because it is filed with the court and can become public. Use a password manager with an emergency-access feature, then tell your executor how to reach it. Our page on [passwords and access](/learn/digital-assets/passwords-and-access) covers the options. A phone with a biometric lock and no backup is the most common way digital life becomes unreachable.

## Who should know, and what should they know?

Plan for at least two people. Your executor needs to know where the will, trust and index are. Your agents under the powers of attorney need to know they have been named and where their documents are. A third person, perhaps a sibling or trusted friend, is the backup if the first two are unavailable.

Telling is different from showing. You can say "the documents are in the gray cabinet, and the index is on top" without revealing a single bequest. Whether to share the contents is a separate decision, and our page on [talking to your family about your plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) addresses it.

## What do you do when you move, or a person on the list changes?

When you change homes, the index changes. Update the keys, the locations and the storage unit. When an executor or agent dies, moves away or falls out with you, your documents need to change, not only your file. Our page on [when to update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan) lists those triggers. Replace old copies when you sign a new version, and destroy superseded drafts so nobody finds an outdated will first.

## Which mistakes come up most often?

Three show up again and again. The first is the single point of failure: one person knows everything, and that person is the one in the car accident. The second is stale paper. An old will in the drawer and a new one at the lawyer's office is how families end up arguing over which is real. The third is the locked laptop, where the scans, the account list and the password manager are all behind one passcode.

A fourth is quieter. People file documents under their own names and forget that the person reading them may not know what a "pour-over" will or a "HIPAA release" is. Put a plain sticky note on the front of each: what it is, and who should call whom. Ten words is enough.

## A quick yearly checklist

- The index is accurate and dated this year.
- The original will and trust are where the index says.
- Your agents and executor are still willing and reachable.
- The account list matches what you own.
- Beneficiary forms match your wishes.
- Password manager access instructions are current.
- Your doctor has a copy of the healthcare directive.
