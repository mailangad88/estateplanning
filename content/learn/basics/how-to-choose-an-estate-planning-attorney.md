Picking an attorney for something as personal as your family's future can feel like picking a surgeon: you are not a lawyer, you cannot easily judge the work, and the consequences show up years later. The good news is that you do not have to judge the legal drafting yourself. You can judge how an attorney listens, explains, prices and follows up, and those predict quality well.

This guide covers where to look, what to ask, and what to watch out for. It sits alongside our [estate planning overview](/learn/basics), which explains what a plan includes.

## What should you look for in an estate planning attorney?

Look for five things, roughly in this order:

1. **Licensed in your state.** Estate law is state law. Verify the license on your state bar's website and check for discipline.
2. **A focus on estate planning.** An attorney whose practice is mostly wills, trusts, powers of attorney and probate will have seen the problems your plan has to avoid. A general practitioner who drafts a will once a quarter has not.
3. **Plain explanation.** If you leave the first meeting understanding your choices better than when you arrived, that is a good sign.
4. **Clear, written pricing.** See [what an estate plan costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost) for the structures to expect.
5. **Follow-through.** Plans fail at the paperwork stage: assets not retitled, beneficiary forms not updated. Ask who handles those steps.

## Where do you find candidates?

- **Your state bar's referral service or lawyer directory**, which confirms licensing.
- **Recommendations** from an accountant, financial adviser or friends who recently completed a plan.
- **Local estate planning council or bar association sections**, which list members by area.
- **A firm's own educational content.** An attorney who publishes clear, accurate material about your state's rules is usually comfortable explaining things.

Meeting two or three firms is reasonable. Many offer an introductory call. Compare how each handles your actual situation, not just their price.

## What questions should you ask?

Bring a short list. These are grouped so you can pick the ones that matter.

### About experience

- What share of your practice is estate planning and probate?
- Have you handled situations like mine: a blended family, a small business, a child with a disability, property in another state?
- Will you personally draft my documents, and who else works on my file?

### About the plan

- Based on what I have told you, what would you recommend, and what are the alternatives?
- Do I need a trust, or would a will be enough? Why?
- How will you coordinate my beneficiary designations and the titling of my home and accounts?
- What would happen to my plan if I moved to another state?

### About cost and process

- What is the total fee, and what does it include? What would cost extra?
- How long from the first meeting to signing?
- What happens at signing: are witnesses and a notary provided?
- Do you hold the original documents, or do I?
- What is the cost of later updates?

### About the long term

- Do you offer periodic reviews?
- If my executor or trustee has questions after I am gone, will your firm be available to them?

## What should happen in a good first meeting?

A strong consultation tends to follow a pattern. The attorney asks about your family (spouse, children, any previous marriages, any member with special needs), your assets and how they are titled, your concerns, and the people you would trust as executor, agent or guardian. They then lay out two or three options, say which they recommend, and explain what each costs and does not do.

Contrast that with a meeting that starts with a product pitch. If the attorney recommends a trust to everyone, or a particular insurance or investment product alongside it, ask why. A trust is a sound tool for many families and an unnecessary expense for others. See [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust) for the factors that decide it, and prepare by reading [what to bring to your first meeting](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation).

## What are the red flags?

- **Promised outcomes.** No one can promise to avoid all taxes, probate or family disputes.
- **Pressure.** Sales events, "today only" prices, or dire warnings meant to hurry you.
- **Vague fees.** If a firm will not explain what the price covers, move on.
- **No questions.** If the attorney recommends documents without asking about your family and assets, the plan is generic.
- **Free seminars that lead to a product sale.** These are not always bad, but the plan should still come from a licensed attorney after an individual review.
- **Unlicensed "document preparers" offering advice.** They can type forms but cannot advise you on which to use.
- **No plan for funding.** A trust that is not funded does little.

## Is a bigger firm better, or a solo practitioner?

Neither, automatically. A solo practitioner may offer direct access and personal attention but could be hard to reach if they retire. A larger firm has depth, including tax and business colleagues, and continuity if one attorney leaves. Ask about continuity either way: if the attorney who drafted your plan is not around when it is needed, who has your file?

## Do you need other advisers too?

Often, yes, for part of the work. A CPA can calculate tax exposure, a financial adviser can manage investments and review beneficiary forms, and an insurance professional can evaluate coverage. An attorney should be willing to coordinate. Business owners should read about [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning), which usually requires several advisers together. Anyone caring for a family member with a disability should look for an attorney with specific experience in [special needs planning](/learn/special-needs).

## A short example

Teresa, 66, widowed, has a house, an IRA and two adult children who do not get along. She meets two attorneys. The first quotes a low price for a standard will and does not ask about the children's relationship. The second asks who she trusts as executor, whether she wants her children to share the house, and how to handle the IRA beneficiary form; recommends a trust with a corporate co-trustee; and writes out the total cost. The second quote is higher but matches Teresa's actual problem. The point is not that the second is always better, but that the right answer starts with questions about your life.

## What do you do after you pick one?

1. Confirm the fee and scope in writing.
2. Gather documents. See our [checklist of what to bring](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation).
3. Think about who you will name as executor, agents and guardian. Our articles on [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) can help.
4. Ask for a draft to read before the signing meeting.
5. Schedule the follow-up steps before you leave.
