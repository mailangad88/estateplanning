A lot of people decide estate planning is for other people. They rent an apartment, they have a modest bank balance, their 401(k) names a beneficiary, and the car is jointly titled. It feels like the paperwork is already handled.

Sometimes it nearly is. But the reasons to plan have little to do with how much you own. They have to do with who speaks for you in a hospital, who can pay your rent if you are unconscious, who raises your kids, and what happens when the person named on your form is no longer alive. This article goes through the most common "I don't need one" situations and says honestly where that is right and where it is not. This piece is part of our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics) library. If you want the big picture first, start with [what estate planning is and who needs it](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning).

## The short version

- If you are an adult, you need a healthcare agent and a financial power of attorney, whatever you own.
- If you have minor children, you need a will naming a guardian, and probably a trust for their money.
- If you own anything in your name alone, a will, or a transfer-on-death form, keeps it from the default rules.
- If everything passes by beneficiary or joint title, you still need a plan for what happens when the first person named is gone.

## Which situation sounds like yours?

| Your situation | What is usually enough | What is often missing |
|---|---|---|
| Rent, few assets, single | Healthcare directive, HIPAA release, financial power of attorney, beneficiary on any account | A named person for belongings and pets. |
| Rent, with children | All of the above plus a will naming a guardian | A trust or custodian for money the kids might receive. |
| Own a home | Will or trust, powers of attorney, directive | Title and deed review. |
| Everything jointly owned | Powers of attorney, directive, a backup plan | What happens after the first owner dies. |
| Everything has a beneficiary | Powers of attorney, directive, backups listed | Contingent beneficiaries, minors named directly. |
| Divorced | Updated will and forms | Old beneficiary designations. |
| No children | Will or trust, powers of attorney | A clear choice of who inherits. |

Use this as a first filter. Your real list depends on your state and your family.

## I rent and have almost nothing. Do I still need documents?

Yes, a short set. The documents with the greatest everyday value cost little and do not depend on your net worth.

1. **Healthcare power of attorney.** Names someone to make medical decisions if you cannot. Without it, a hospital looks to state law for a decision-maker, which might not be the person you would pick. See [what happens without an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/what-happens-without-an-advance-directive).
2. **HIPAA authorization.** Lets named people receive your medical information. Parents of an 18-year-old often find out the hard way that they cannot call the hospital. Read [what happens if your 18-year-old has an emergency and you have no release](/learn/what-if/adult-child-medical-emergency-no-hipaa).
3. **Durable financial power of attorney.** Lets someone pay your rent and close your accounts if you are in the hospital. Without one, your family may need a court proceeding, covered in [what happens if you are incapacitated without a power of attorney](/learn/what-if/incapacitated-without-power-of-attorney).
4. **Beneficiary forms.** Free, quick and the most powerful document on the list.

A will can wait if you own almost nothing and have no dependents, though it costs little and can name an executor and say who gets your belongings. Our guide for [young adults](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-young-adults) and for [single people](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-single-people) goes further.

## Doesn't a beneficiary form cover everything?

It covers the asset it is attached to, and only if it is filled out and still accurate. We often see five ways it falls short.

- **The beneficiary died first, and nobody named a backup.** The account then goes to the estate and through probate.
- **The form is blank or was never completed.** See [what happens if you never named a beneficiary](/learn/what-if/no-beneficiary-named).
- **The named person is a minor.** A court may need to supervise the money until the child is an adult. Our article on [a minor inheriting without a trust](/learn/what-if/minor-inherits-without-a-trust) explains.
- **An ex-spouse is still named.** Laws vary, and some forms, particularly employer plans, follow federal rules that can override state law. See [when an ex-spouse is still your beneficiary](/learn/what-if/ex-spouse-still-beneficiary).
- **The form and the will disagree.** The form wins for that asset. Your will cannot change it.

Beneficiary forms are a good tool. They are not a plan. A plan looks at all the forms together, and also covers the things forms cannot do, such as naming a guardian or saying who makes medical decisions.

## What if everything is jointly owned?

Joint ownership with right of survivorship passes the asset to the surviving owner outside probate. Spouses use it for a house or bank account often, and it works well for the first death.

The trouble comes later. After the first spouse dies, the survivor owns everything in one name and has no plan. After a second death, the usual probate and intestacy rules apply. Take Helen, 72, who put her house and savings in joint names with her son Greg to avoid probate. Greg gets divorced, and his ex-wife's lawyer names the house as a marital asset. Helen's own plan for equal shares among her three children is gone. Our article on [the hidden risks of adding a child to your deed](/learn/property-and-assets/joint-tenancy-risks) covers this in detail.

For married couples, joint ownership is more reliable, and in some states a special form called tenancy by the entirety gives added protection. Still, a power of attorney and a healthcare directive are needed for each person.

## What if I'm young and healthy, married, or divorced?

Each stage has its own gap.

- **Young and single:** Parents have no automatic access once you turn 18. A short set of documents closes that gap.
- **Married:** Your spouse may have rights under state law, but not necessarily the authority to manage an account in your name alone, and state law does not name a guardian for your children. See [estate planning for newlyweds](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-newlyweds).
- **Divorced:** Old documents may still name the ex, and a decree does not always change retirement beneficiaries. Our guide to [updating your plan after divorce](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-after-divorce) gives a checklist.
- **No children:** Your property goes to relatives as state law decides, and your partner, friends or a charity may get nothing. Read [estate planning for couples without children](/learn/life-stages/estate-planning-for-couples-without-children).

## Who should not skip a plan?

Four groups, in our experience, pay the most for putting it off:

1. Parents of minor children.
2. Parents of a child with a disability, where an inheritance can cost benefits.
3. Unmarried partners, who have no inheritance rights in most states.
4. Owners of property in more than one state or of a business.

If you are in one of these groups, a will and powers of attorney are the starting point, and often a trust is better. If you are not, a short set of documents is still worth the afternoon. A good rule of thumb is that the less you own, the simpler the plan, and the more it matters that it exists.

## How state law changes this

States differ in who has authority to make medical decisions when there is no directive, in whether an ex-spouse is cut out automatically, in what unmarried partners can claim, and in how community property is treated.
