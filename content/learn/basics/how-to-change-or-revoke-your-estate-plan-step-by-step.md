Your plan was right when you signed it. Then you remarried, a daughter moved to Oregon, or the person you named as executor stopped speaking to you. Now you want to change it. The good news is that most estate planning documents are built to be changed. The catch is that each type changes in its own way, and doing it the wrong way can leave you worse off than doing nothing.

This guide, part of the [estate planning basics section](/learn/basics), walks through each document one at a time. For deciding when a change is needed, see [when to review and update your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## Can I change my estate plan whenever I want?

Yes, with two conditions. You must have legal capacity when you make the change, meaning you understand what you own, who your family is, and what the document does. And the document must be revocable. Wills, revocable living trusts, powers of attorney and healthcare directives are all revocable. Irrevocable trusts, by design, are not.

The method matters more than the decision. The table shows how each document is changed.

| Document | Change it by | Revoke it by | Common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will | Signed codicil or new will | New will that revokes the old one, or destroying it on purpose | Writing changes on the original |
| Revocable trust | Written, signed amendment | Written revocation, then retitle assets | Amending but forgetting new assets |
| Power of attorney | New document | Written revocation, notice to agent and institutions | Not telling the bank |
| Healthcare directive | New document | Written revocation, notice to doctors | Old copy still in the chart |
| Beneficiary form | New form filed with the company | New form that replaces the old one | Assuming the will controls |
| Irrevocable trust | Court, consent or trust protector | Rarely possible | Assuming it can be undone |

## How do I change a will?

You have two options: a codicil or a new will.

A **codicil** is a short document that amends specific parts of your will. It must be signed and witnessed the same way a will is, in the state where you sign it. Codicils work for small changes, such as swapping an executor. They get messy when there are several.

A **new will** is cleaner. It should begin by revoking all earlier wills and codicils. For anything larger than a name change, we recommend a new will.

Do not write on the original, cross out lines, or staple notes to it. A handwritten change that is not properly signed may be ignored, and in the worst case the marks could be read as revoking the whole will. The detailed rules are in [changing a will and codicils](/learn/wills/changing-a-will-and-codicils), with state versions for [Illinois](/learn/illinois/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-illinois) and [New Jersey](/learn/new-jersey/changing-or-revoking-a-will-in-new-jersey).

### What if I just want to cancel my will?

Destroying a will with the intent to revoke it generally works, but if you do it and do not sign a new one you will be treated as dying without one. Then state law decides who inherits. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) before you tear anything up.

## How do I change or revoke a living trust?

Read the trust's amendment clause first. Most revocable trusts let the creator amend or revoke by a signed writing delivered to the trustee, who may be you. An amendment should name the trust, the date of the original, and the exact paragraph that changes. Some trusts require notarization.

If you revoke the trust, you also have to move the assets back. A house deeded to the trust stays in the trust until a new deed moves it out, even if the trust paper has been shredded. Our guide on [how to change or revoke a living trust](/learn/trusts/how-to-change-or-revoke-a-living-trust) lists the steps.

Joint trusts add a wrinkle. If a married couple signed together, changing it may need both spouses, or it may become partly irrevocable at the first death.

## How do I revoke a power of attorney?

Sign a written revocation, date it, have it notarized if the original required that, and deliver it to the agent. Then deliver copies to every bank, brokerage and financial institution that holds the old power of attorney. Until they receive notice, a bank acting in good faith can keep honoring it. If you are replacing the agent, sign the new document at the same time.

Illinois residents can read [revoking an Illinois power of attorney](/learn/illinois/revoking-an-illinois-power-of-attorney). The general rules are in [when a power of attorney ends](/learn/power-of-attorney/when-does-power-of-attorney-end). 

## How do I change a healthcare directive?

Sign a new directive that says it replaces the old one, then give copies to your healthcare agent, your primary doctor and the hospital where you are most likely to be treated. Hospitals keep the directive in your chart, so an old one sitting there can be followed by mistake. The steps, including how to revoke by telling a doctor, are in [how to change or revoke an advance directive](/learn/healthcare-directives/how-to-change-or-revoke-an-advance-directive). If you changed agents because of a falling out, tell the former agent in writing so there is no confusion in an emergency.

## How do I change beneficiaries?

Contact the company that holds the account and ask for a change-of-beneficiary form. Complete it, file it, and keep the confirmation. Your will does not change a retirement account or life insurance beneficiary, because the form controls. Many of our clients are surprised to find an ex-spouse still named on a policy decades after the divorce. Review all of them at the same time you review the rest of your plan.

## What are common mistakes when changing an estate plan?

- **Changing only one document.** A new will does not fix a trust, a POA or a beneficiary form.
- **Forgetting to retitle assets.** A new trust only controls what is in it.
- **Not collecting the old copies.** Someone may find the 2012 will and offer it for probate.
- **Waiting until capacity is in doubt.** A change signed when you are very ill invites challenges. See [signing a will with dementia, illness or family pressure](/learn/wills/signing-a-will-with-dementia-illness-or-family-pressure).
- **Skipping the signing formalities.** Witnesses and notary rules are not optional.

## What steps should I follow?

1. List every estate document and every account with a beneficiary.
2. Decide what you want to change and why.
3. Have an attorney draft the new documents so they work together.
4. Sign with the required witnesses and notary.
5. Update beneficiary forms and retitle assets.
6. Notify banks, agents, your executor and your trustee.
7. Gather old versions and mark them replaced. Keep one copy of each in a file in case of a dispute.
8. Write the date of the change on your calendar and review again in a few years.

## What if I have moved or my situation changed a lot?

A move to another state does not cancel your documents, but the other state may read them differently, so have them checked. The same holds for divorce or remarriage. In most states divorce removes an ex-spouse from a will and trust, but not from an account that still names them. Read [what to do after moving states](/learn/what-if/moved-states-plan-not-updated) if that is your case.
