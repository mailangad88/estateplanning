Almost everyone agrees an estate plan is a good idea. Fewer people have signed one. The gap is not about money or intelligence. It is about a handful of understandable feelings that keep a simple task at the bottom of the list for years.

This article goes through the reasons we hear most, says what each one costs a real family, and then gives you a seven-day plan to finish the first round. For the full picture of what a plan contains, see our [estate planning basics](/learn/basics).

## Why don't people do estate planning?

The reasons fall into seven groups. Seeing yours on the list helps, because each one has a practical answer.

| The excuse | What is really going on | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| "I do not want to think about dying" | Discomfort, not disagreement | Frame it as planning for being sick or away, which is far more likely |
| "I do not own enough" | Underestimating what you have | Add up the 401(k), life insurance, car and house equity |
| "My spouse gets everything anyway" | A belief about intestacy that is often wrong | Read what your state actually says |
| "I cannot decide who should be guardian or executor" | Fear of insulting someone | Name a first choice and a backup; change them later |
| "It costs too much" | Not knowing the price range | Ask for a flat quote, see [what a plan costs](/learn/basics/how-much-does-estate-planning-cost) |
| "I will do it when things settle down" | Waiting for a calm moment that never comes | Pick a date and book the appointment |
| "My family will work it out" | Hope that nobody will fight | Families who get along still hit legal roadblocks |

## What happens if you never do it?

The state writes your plan for you, and the result is often not what you would have picked. If you die without a will, your property passes under your state's intestacy rules, and a court picks who administers the estate.

Take Illinois. A married person who dies without a will, leaving a spouse and children, does not leave everything to the spouse. Illinois gives the surviving spouse half of the estate and the children share the other half. If those children are minors, the spouse may then need court supervision to handle their share. Our article on [who inherits without a will in Illinois](/learn/illinois/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-illinois) has the full order, and the [New Jersey version](/learn/new-jersey/who-inherits-without-a-will-in-new-jersey) shows how the rules differ when some children are not the spouse's.

The bigger gaps are the ones nobody sees coming. Without a power of attorney, a family can end up asking a court to appoint a guardian just to pay the mortgage on a parent's behalf. Without a named guardian for minor children, a judge chooses. These are the outcomes a plan prevents, and they have nothing to do with how rich you are.

## Is it true that you do not own enough?

Usually not. Count what you control. A work 401(k) or IRA, a life insurance policy (including group coverage at work), a checking account, a car and any equity in a home all pass by some rule. Take Luis, 39, a school bus mechanic in Joliet. He thinks he has nothing worth planning for. Then he adds it up: $48,000 in a 401(k), $150,000 of group life insurance through his employer, a truck, and a paid-down share of a townhouse. That is a real estate, and three of those four items pass by beneficiary form or title rather than by any will.

The better question is whether the people who will deal with it know what to do. A tidy estate of modest size is a gift to an executor. A missing list of accounts is not. Our guide to [planning with few assets](/learn/basics/do-i-need-an-estate-plan-with-few-assets) addresses this directly.

## Does your spouse really get everything?

Not automatically. Beneficiary forms and joint ownership may move some assets to your spouse without a will, but anything in your name alone follows intestacy. With children from a prior relationship, the share that goes to a new spouse can surprise everyone. The blog post on [whether a spouse inherits everything with no will](/blog/does-a-spouse-inherit-everything-if-there-is-no-will) goes through common cases.

## How do you choose a guardian or executor when you cannot decide?

Start with a ranked list and stop trying to find the perfect person. Here is the method we suggest to clients who are stuck.

1. List three adults you trust to handle money honestly for the executor role. Cross off anyone who lives far away or is overwhelmed.
2. For a guardian, think about values and stability, not wealth. Ask yourself who your children already feel safe with.
3. Pick a first choice and a backup. If the backup is unavailable too, a court takes over, which is still better than leaving it blank.
4. Call each person and ask. A yes now is worth more than a surprise later.

You can change these choices anytime. A plan with a good second pick beats a draft that sits unsigned for three years while you search for a flawless one. See [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) for the traits to look for.

## Is it too late for estate planning?

The test is whether you understand what you own, who your family is and what the document does, on the day you sign. Age alone does not matter, and a diagnosis alone does not either. If you are worried about an ill parent, read [too late for a will](/learn/what-if/too-late-for-a-will), which explains how capacity is assessed and what to do when it is in doubt.

If you are starting a plan after a bad diagnosis, say so at the first call. The attorney can sequence the paperwork so the power of attorney and healthcare directive come first.

## What can you do in one week?

This is a realistic schedule for someone with a job and a family. It aims for one thing: a signing appointment on the calendar.

1. **Day 1.** Write down who you would trust as your executor, healthcare agent and guardian, each with a backup. Do not edit yet.
2. **Day 2.** List your accounts in one page: the bank, the retirement plans, any life insurance, the house, the car. No passwords.
3. **Day 3.** Look up who is named as beneficiary on each retirement account and policy. A blank or an ex-spouse is a red flag.
4. **Day 4.** Call the people you named. Ask if they are willing and tell them where documents will be kept.
5. **Day 5.** Use the [plan finder](/plan-finder) to see which documents fit your situation.
6. **Day 6.** Book a consultation. Our guide on [what to bring](/learn/basics/what-to-bring-to-estate-planning-consultation) is a good checklist.
7. **Day 7.** Put the appointment in your calendar and tell your spouse or partner. The hardest part is over.

## What should you not wait for?

The family that waits for a calm season rarely gets one. A new baby, a job change or a parent's decline always seems to arrive first, and each of those makes the plan more necessary, not less. Do not wait for a better time, a bigger estate, or agreement from every relative. A plan reflects your wishes, and family conversations can follow. Documents can be revised in an afternoon once they exist; a conversation about a plan that does not exist tends to go in circles. Our article on [talking to your family about your plan](/learn/basics/talking-to-family-about-estate-plan) helps with that part.
