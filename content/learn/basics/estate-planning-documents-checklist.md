If you have decided to get your affairs in order, the first practical question is what, exactly, you are supposed to sign. The paperwork can look like a pile of overlapping forms with Latin names. In fact, each document answers one question, and once you see which question, the list is short.

This page is a checklist of the core documents, what each does, who signs it, and when it matters. It builds on our [introduction to estate planning](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning) and the broader [estate planning guide](/learn/basics).

## What are the core estate planning documents?

The core documents divide into two groups: those that work while you are alive but unable to act, and those that work after you die.

| Document | Question it answers | Works when |
|---|---|---|
| Last will and testament | Who inherits my probate property? Who is my executor? Who is my children's guardian? | After death |
| Revocable living trust | How can assets pass privately, and who manages them if I am incapacitated? | Now and after death |
| Durable financial power of attorney | Who pays my bills and manages my accounts if I can't? | During incapacity |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Who makes medical decisions for me if I can't? | During incapacity |
| Living will (advance directive) | What treatment do I want or refuse near the end of life? | During incapacity |
| HIPAA authorization | Who may see my medical records and talk to my doctors? | Now |
| Beneficiary designations | Who receives retirement accounts, insurance and POD accounts? | After death |

## 1. Last will and testament

A [will](/learn/wills) is the document most people think of first. It names your **executor**, the person who gathers your assets, pays debts and distributes the rest. It says who receives your property. If you have minor children, it is the usual place to nominate a guardian. A will goes through [probate](/learn/probate/what-is-probate), the court-supervised process of settling an estate, and becomes a public record.

A will only controls assets titled in your name alone. It does not control a retirement account with a named beneficiary, a jointly owned house with survivorship rights, or property already in a trust. Our article on [what a will cannot do](/learn/wills/what-a-will-cannot-do) explains the limits, and [how to make a valid will](/learn/wills/how-to-make-a-valid-will) covers signing rules.

## 2. Revocable living trust

A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) is an arrangement in which you transfer assets to a trust you control during life. You are usually the trustee. You name a successor trustee to take over when you die or become incapacitated, and that person distributes assets according to your instructions, without going through probate.

Trusts are not necessary for everyone. They tend to make sense when you own real estate, especially in more than one state, when you want privacy, when beneficiaries are young or need protection, or when you want a smooth handoff in case of incapacity. A trust only works if assets are actually retitled into it, a step called [funding](/learn/trusts/how-to-fund-a-trust). If you are weighing the two, see [will vs. living trust](/learn/trusts/will-vs-trust).

Trust-based plans also include a **pour-over will**, a backup that sweeps any forgotten assets into the trust at death. See [what a pour-over will is](/learn/wills/pour-over-will).

## 3. Durable financial power of attorney

This document lets you appoint an **agent** to handle money and property for you. "Durable" means the authority survives your incapacity, which is exactly when you need it. Without one, a spouse or child may be unable to access your accounts, sell your car, or deal with the IRS, and might have to ask a court for a conservatorship.

The agent can act on your behalf for the things you authorize: paying bills, managing investments, filing taxes, dealing with real estate. Some forms take effect immediately; others are "springing" and activate only on a stated event. See [durable financial power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney/durable-financial-power-of-attorney) and [springing vs. immediate](/learn/power-of-attorney/springing-vs-immediate-power-of-attorney). The authority ends at your death, at which point the executor or trustee takes over.

## 4. Healthcare power of attorney

Also called a healthcare proxy or healthcare agent designation, this names the person who makes medical decisions if you cannot. Hospitals ask for it constantly. If you have not named anyone, many states have a default list of relatives who may decide, but the order may not match who you would choose, and unmarried partners and friends often rank low or not at all. See [choosing someone to speak for you](/learn/healthcare-directives/healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## 5. Living will

A [living will](/learn/healthcare-directives/living-will) records your wishes on life-sustaining treatment, such as ventilators, feeding tubes and resuscitation, if you are terminally ill or permanently unconscious. It guides your healthcare agent and relieves family members of guessing. It is different from a DNR order or POLST form, which are medical orders written with a physician; see [DNR orders and POLST forms](/learn/healthcare-directives/dnr-and-polst).

Many states combine the living will and healthcare power of attorney into one form called an advance directive.

## 6. HIPAA authorization

Federal privacy law restricts what providers can tell family members. A [HIPAA authorization](/learn/healthcare-directives/hipaa-authorization) names people allowed to see your records and speak with your doctors. It matters most in the window before anyone has formal authority, such as the first hours after an accident or when an agent needs records to confirm that you are incapacitated.

## 7. Beneficiary designations

Retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s), life insurance, annuities, and bank or brokerage accounts with payable-on-death or transfer-on-death registration pass by the name on the form, not by your will. This makes them the most commonly neglected part of a plan. A form that still names an ex-spouse or a deceased parent can undo years of careful drafting. Review them with our [beneficiary designation guide](/learn/beneficiary-designations), and remember to name a [contingent beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) in case the first choice dies before you.

## What other documents might you need?

Depending on your situation, an attorney may add:

- **A transfer-on-death deed** for real estate, in states that allow one. See [transfer-on-death deeds](/learn/beneficiary-designations/transfer-on-death-deed).
- **Trusts for children** that hold an inheritance until they reach a chosen age. See [trusts for children](/learn/trusts/trusts-for-children).
- **A special needs trust** for a beneficiary receiving means-tested benefits.
- **A buy-sell agreement and succession plan** if you own part of a business.
- **A letter of instruction** with account lists, passwords locations, funeral wishes and a list of advisers. It is not legally binding, but it saves your executor weeks of searching. Also consider [digital assets](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan), which a conventional will does not address well.
- **A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement**, which interacts with your estate plan in a second marriage.

## Which documents does each person need? An example

Marcus and Elena, both 41, have two children, a house with a mortgage, 401(k)s through work and term life insurance. Their checklist looks like this:

1. Wills for each, naming each other as primary beneficiary, a guardian for the children, and a trust for the children's inheritance until age 25.
2. Financial powers of attorney naming each other, with Elena's sister as backup.
3. Healthcare powers of attorney and living wills.
4. HIPAA authorizations naming each other and a sibling.
5. Updated beneficiary forms: spouse first, a children's trust as contingent.
6. A one-page letter listing accounts and where documents are kept.

That is a complete plan for a family of their size, and it does not require a trust. If they later inherit land in another state, they might add one.

## Where do you keep the originals?

Keep originals somewhere safe but reachable. A fireproof box at home, your attorney's vault, or a county court's will-deposit program in some states all work. A safe deposit box can be a problem because access may require a court order after death. Tell your executor and agents where the documents are. More on this in [where to keep your will](/learn/wills/where-to-keep-your-will).
