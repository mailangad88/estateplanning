You sit down with a lawyer, or open a draft of your own will, and the page says "the Testator hereby devises to the Decedent's heirs." Nobody talks like that at the kitchen table. Most of the stress of reading an estate plan comes from a handful of words that sound alike and mean different things.

This page sorts them by job. Who makes the document, who manages things, who receives, and what the gifts are called. For the larger picture of how the pieces fit, start with our [estate planning guide](/learn/basics) and the plain overview in [what is estate planning](/learn/basics/what-is-estate-planning).

## Who makes the document?

The person who signs a will is the **[testator](/glossary/testator)**. You will see "testatrix" in very old forms for a woman, but nearly every modern document uses testator for anyone. The word only applies to wills. A testator has to have the mental ability to understand what they are signing, which the law calls testamentary capacity.

The person who creates a trust is the **[grantor](/glossary/grantor)**. You will also hear settlor, trustor and (in some states) trust-maker. They mean the same person. Which one appears depends on your state and on the habits of whoever drafted the form. If a trust names two grantors, that is usually a married couple who created one joint trust.

The person who signs a power of attorney is the **principal**. The person they pick to act is the agent. Those two words show up again in [types of power of attorney](/learn/power-of-attorney).

## Who manages things?

This is where most mix-ups happen. The jobs below are all described by who is in charge of what.

| Word | What the person does | When the job exists |
|---|---|---|
| Executor | Carries out a will, pays debts, distributes property | After death, named in a will |
| Administrator | Does the same work when there is no will or no usable executor | After death, appointed by a court |
| Personal representative | The general legal term that covers both of the above | After death |
| Trustee | Manages property held in a trust | During life and after death |
| Successor trustee | Steps in when the first trustee dies, resigns or cannot act | When needed |
| Agent (attorney-in-fact) | Handles money or medical decisions for someone living | While the principal is alive |
| Guardian or conservator | Court-appointed decision-maker for a child or an adult who lacks capacity | During life |

Lisa, 62, names her brother as executor in her will and her daughter as successor trustee of her living trust. Those are two different jobs under two different documents, and it is normal for them to go to different people. Our page on [executor, administrator and personal representative](/learn/after-a-death/executor-vs-administrator-vs-personal-representative) walks through how courts choose among them.

## What does fiduciary mean?

A **[fiduciary](/glossary/fiduciary)** is anyone the law trusts with another person's money or decisions and holds to a strict standard. Executors, trustees, agents and guardians are all fiduciaries. They owe duties of loyalty, honesty and care. They cannot quietly use the property for themselves, cannot mix it with their own money, and have to keep records.

The word is a standard, not a job title. When a document says a person "acts in a fiduciary capacity," it means the duty applies. A family member who accepts the role does not get a lower standard because the family is friendly. If you are weighing who to name, [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) covers how to judge that.

## Who receives?

A **[beneficiary](/glossary/beneficiary)** is anyone who gets a benefit. That includes a person named in a will, a trust, a life insurance policy or a retirement account. A **[heir](/glossary/heir)** is narrower. It is someone the law says inherits when there is no will, based on family relationship. A person can be a beneficiary without being an heir (a friend, a charity) and an heir without being a beneficiary (a child who was left out of the will).

The person who received the property after death is also sometimes called the **legatee** or **devisee**, again depending on the kind of gift. You can skip those two in daily life. If your lawyer uses them, they mean the receiver.

## What are the gifts called?

A will gift has a few traditional names.

- **Bequest:** a gift of personal property, such as money, jewelry or a car. See the [bequest entry](/glossary/bequest).
- **Devise:** a gift of real estate.
- **Legacy:** usually a gift of money, though some people use it loosely for any gift.
- **Specific gift:** a particular item, like "my mother's ring."
- **General gift:** a sum of money not tied to any account.
- **Residuary gift:** everything left over once the other gifts, debts and costs are paid.

Many attorneys now just write "I give," because courts treat the old words as interchangeable. What matters far more is the residuary clause. A will that gives away ten items by name and then says nothing about the rest leaves the rest to the law's default rules. Our page on [specific, general and residuary gifts](/learn/wills/specific-general-and-residuary-gifts) shows how that plays out.

## Which words describe the person who died?

The **[decedent](/glossary/decedent)** is the person who died. Probate papers use it constantly, because "the deceased" is considered less formal. If there was a will, the decedent was also the testator. If property passes by [intestate succession](/glossary/intestate-succession), the decedent died intestate, which means without a valid will.

The **estate** is everything the decedent owned. What counts, and what does not, is the subject of our companion page on [what an estate is: probate, gross and taxable](/learn/basics/what-an-estate-is-probate-gross-and-taxable-estate).

## Which words show up on beneficiary forms?

Bank and retirement forms use their own short list. A **primary beneficiary** is first in line. A **[contingent beneficiary](/glossary/contingent-beneficiary)** receives only if the primary cannot. **Per stirpes** and **per capita** are instructions for how a share divides when a named person has died and left children. Forms do not use the courtroom words at all, and a form usually overrides a will. That is why a short read of [contingent beneficiaries](/learn/beneficiary-designations/contingent-beneficiaries) is worth ten minutes before you fill one out.

## A worked example

Daniel, 71, signs a will and a revocable trust on the same afternoon. He is the testator of the will and the grantor of the trust. He names his daughter Ana as executor, and himself as the first trustee with Ana as successor trustee. He leaves his house to his son Marcus by devise and his savings to be split between Ana and Marcus in the residuary clause.

When Daniel dies, he becomes the decedent. Ana, now serving as both executor and successor trustee, is a fiduciary in each role and has to keep the two sets of records apart. If Daniel put the house in his trust before he died, the house never needed a devise at all. The trust documents control it, and it avoids probate. That kind of detail is why [revocable living trusts](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) and wills need to be read together.

## Common mistakes with the vocabulary

1. Treating "heir" and "beneficiary" as the same thing, then assuming a child must be included in a will because they are an heir.
2. Assuming the executor and the trustee must be the same person. They need not be, and sometimes should not be.
3. Writing "to my children" without saying what happens if a child dies first. Words like per stirpes cover that, and our glossary entry on [per stirpes](/glossary/per-stirpes) explains it.
4. Signing a form with a term you cannot define. Ask for the plain meaning in writing.
