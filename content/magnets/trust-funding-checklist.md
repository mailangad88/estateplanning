**How to use this checklist.** Do the pre-work first, then work through each asset type that applies to you. Keep proof of every change in one folder, and fill in the tracker at the end. Each institution has its own forms, and rules vary by state, so treat this as a map to take to your trust attorney.

## Why funding matters

A revocable living trust controls only the assets that are titled in the trust's name, or that name the trust or its successor trustee as beneficiary. Signing the trust document is step one. Retitling is step two, and it is the step people skip.

An asset left in your personal name typically goes through probate at death even though a trust exists, which defeats a main reason many people create one. A "pour-over" will sends such assets to the trust afterward, but only after probate.

Some states have a court process to treat unfunded assets as trust property when the trust document lists them and the person clearly intended it. California's "Heggstad petition" is one example. It costs time and money, and other states have their own approaches or none. Ask an attorney licensed in your state what exists there.

For real estate, many people ask the trust attorney to prepare the deed and funding letters. Mistakes can affect property tax reassessment, title insurance and tax basis.

## The pre-work (15 minutes)

- [ ] **Trust name and date,** exactly as written on the first page. Example: "The Jane A. Rivera Revocable Living Trust dated June 4, 2025." Institutions are strict about this.
- [ ] **Trustee names** (you, as initial trustee) and the named successor trustee.
- [ ] **Certification of Trust.** This short summary proves the trust exists without revealing its private terms. Many states have adopted Uniform Trust Code section 1013, which lets banks rely on it. Ask your attorney for one.
- [ ] **A printed copy of the tracker** at the end of this checklist.

## Asset by asset

### 1. Your home and other real estate (retitle by deed)

- [ ] **Sign and record a new deed** transferring the property from yourself to "[Your Name], Trustee of the [Trust Name]." The deed type (quitclaim, grant or warranty) depends on the state. It is filed with the county recorder or register of deeds.
- [ ] **Mortgage.** Federal law (Garn-St Germain Act, 12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)(8)) generally stops lenders from calling a loan due when a borrower moves a home into a revocable trust and remains a beneficiary and occupant. Notify your lender if your attorney advises.
- [ ] **Title insurance.** Confirm your policy continues. Some insurers need an endorsement.
- [ ] **Homeowner's insurance.** Add the trust as an additional insured or confirm coverage continues.
- [ ] **Property tax.** Many states exclude transfers into revocable trusts from reassessment or transfer tax, but rules vary, and some counties want a form. Ask your attorney or county office.
- [ ] **Homestead exemption.** Confirm with the county that it continues.
- [ ] **Out-of-state property.** Each state needs its own deed. Without one, a separate ancillary probate may be needed there.
- **Proof of completion:** a recorded deed copy with the recording number.

### 2. Bank accounts (usually retitle)

- [ ] **Checking and savings.** Ask the bank to retitle into the trust. Expect to provide the trust name and date, trustee signatures, the Certification of Trust, and a tax ID (many revocable trusts use your Social Security number). Some people instead keep one operating account in their own name with a payable-on-death designation to the trust. That is simpler and can work for small balances.
- [ ] **Joint accounts.** A joint account with your spouse probably passes to the survivor automatically. Decide whether that is what you want.
- [ ] **Certificates of deposit.** Call the bank to retitle without a penalty. Many banks allow it when the owner stays the same.
- [ ] **FDIC coverage.** Revocable trust accounts are insured up to $250,000 per owner per unique beneficiary, capped at $1,250,000 per owner per bank, under simplified rules effective April 1, 2024. FDIC's EDIE calculator can check your numbers.
- [ ] **Safe deposit box.** The box usually stays in your name. Add your successor trustee as an authorized signer.
- **Proof of completion:** a new statement showing the trust name.

### 3. Brokerage and investment accounts (retitle)

- [ ] **Taxable brokerage accounts.** Use the custodian's "transfer to trust" form. This is generally not a taxable event for a revocable grantor trust, but check for cost-basis reporting changes.
- [ ] **Stock certificates and savings bonds.** Paper certificates are reissued through the transfer agent. Paper savings bonds can be re-registered with TreasuryDirect, though forms vary.
- [ ] **Employer stock plans, restricted stock and options.** Do not retitle without the plan administrator's written approval. Transfers may be restricted or taxable.
- **Proof of completion:** a statement with the trust's name.

### 4. Retirement accounts (do not retitle; use beneficiary forms)

Never transfer ownership of an IRA or 401(k) to a living trust during your life. The transfer is treated as a distribution and can trigger income tax and, for IRAs, early-withdrawal penalties. See IRS Publication 590-B and IRC section 408.

- [ ] **Spouse as primary beneficiary.** The most common choice. A spouse can generally roll the account over and keep tax deferral. Federally governed 401(k) plans generally require spouse consent to name someone else.
- [ ] **A trust as beneficiary.** Sometimes useful for minors, disabled beneficiaries, creditor protection or payout control. The trust has to be drafted to qualify as a "see-through" trust, and the SECURE Act's 10-year payout rules affect the tax result. An attorney should review it.
- [ ] **Individuals as beneficiaries.** Often the simplest for adult children with no special needs.
- [ ] **Keep the confirmation.** The designation on file with the custodian is what counts.
- **Proof of completion:** a confirmation page showing primary and contingent beneficiaries and the date.

### 5. Life insurance (beneficiary form, sometimes ownership)

- [ ] **Term and whole life.** Ownership usually stays with you. Change the beneficiary to the trust (for minors or controlled payouts) or to individuals or a spouse. Many attorneys name the spouse as primary and the trust as contingent.
- [ ] **Estate tax.** A policy you own is included in your taxable estate (IRC section 2042). If you are near an estate tax threshold, ask about an irrevocable life insurance trust.
- [ ] **Employer group life.** Change it through the HR portal. Do not assume it follows your personal policy.
- **Proof of completion:** the insurer's confirmation.

### 6. Annuities

- [ ] **Update the beneficiary,** not the ownership. Tax rules differ for non-spouse beneficiaries and non-qualified annuities, so check the contract and ask the carrier about trust-as-beneficiary treatment.

### 7. Vehicles, boats and trailers

- [ ] **Decide whether to leave them in your name.** Many states let vehicles pass at death through a simple DMV process or small-estate affidavit below a value limit. Some offer transfer-on-death titles. Retitling to a trust can affect insurance and registration costs, so many attorneys skip vehicles unless the value is high.
- **Proof of completion:** a tracker note explaining your choice.

### 8. Business interests

- [ ] **LLC interests and corporate stock.** Assign your interest to the trust in writing. Check the operating agreement or bylaws for transfer restrictions and consent requirements, and update the membership or stock ledger.
- [ ] **S corporation stock.** A revocable grantor trust is a permitted shareholder. If the trust continues after your death, it must qualify as a QSST or ESBT within a time window to preserve S status (IRC section 1361). Involve an attorney and a CPA.
- [ ] **Sole proprietorship.** There is no separate entity, so equipment, accounts, licenses and domain names are handled one by one. Some licenses cannot be transferred.
- [ ] **Property held by an LLC.** The trust owns the LLC interest, and the deed does not change.
- [ ] **Buy-sell agreements.** Confirm what happens at death and who benefits from any funding insurance.
- **Proof of completion:** a signed assignment and the updated ledger.

### 9. Digital assets and online accounts

- [ ] **Online tools.** Most accounts cannot be retitled because terms of service govern them. Set up Google Inactive Account Manager, Apple Legacy Contact and a password manager with emergency access.
- [ ] **Cryptocurrency.** Private keys control the assets. The trust should authorize the trustee to hold digital assets. An exchange account can sometimes be retitled to the trust, and self-custody wallets need a secured key-recovery plan. Keep seed phrases out of the trust document and the will, which can become public.
- [ ] **Domain names and websites.** Transfer the registrant account to the trust where the registrar allows, or record the credentials in your locator.
- [ ] **PayPal, Venmo and similar apps.** These generally do not allow trust ownership. Plan for your fiduciary to close them and move the balance.
- **Proof of completion:** a list of platform, method and the person named.

### 10. Tangible personal property

- [ ] **General assignment of personal property.** This one-page document, often part of a trust package, assigns everything you own now and later to the trust. High-value items can be listed on a schedule.
- [ ] **Firearms.** Federal and state law may require special steps for NFA items when transferring to a trust. Ask an attorney.

### 11. Money owed to you, intellectual property and other items

- [ ] **Promissory notes and mortgages you hold.** Assign to the trust and update the servicer.
- [ ] **Royalties, patents and copyrights.** Assign in writing and register if applicable.
- [ ] **Pending lawsuits or claims.** Do not assign without legal advice.
- [ ] **Inheritances you expect.** Ask about disclaimer and titling issues before they arrive.
- [ ] **Health Savings Accounts.** Use a beneficiary form. A non-spouse beneficiary generally causes the account to stop being an HSA at death (IRS Publication 969), so a spouse is often named for tax reasons.

## Common failure points

1. A trust was signed in a lawyer's office but no deed was ever recorded.
2. A home bought after signing was titled in your personal name by the title company.
3. A new bank or brokerage account was opened in your personal name.
4. Retirement and insurance beneficiaries were never updated after the trust was signed.
5. An account was retitled to the trust, but a POD or TOD form on the same account conflicts.
6. The trust's name or date on a form differs from the trust document.
7. Property in another state was never deeded.
8. An operating agreement's transfer restrictions were ignored.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Before you transfer real estate, an LLC or S corporation interest, retirement accounts, or anything with a mortgage, a lien or unusual title.
- You signed your trust more than a few years ago and are not sure what was done.
- You moved to a new state since signing.
- The trust involves a spouse, a special needs beneficiary or a blended family.
- You want a short review of whether your trust is actually funded.
