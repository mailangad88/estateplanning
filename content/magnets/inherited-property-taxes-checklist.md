## How to use this checklist

You do not have to do all of this at once. Taxes are rarely the most urgent task after a death. Work through the sections when you are ready, and keep every paper in one folder. Nothing here is a deadline you can read off this page for your own case, so confirm dates with a tax professional.

## Which returns may be involved

- [ ] **Decedent's final Form 1040.** Covers income from January 1 to the date of death. See the [final return checklist](/free/final-tax-return-checklist).
- [ ] **Earlier years.** Make sure prior returns were filed.
- [ ] **Estate income tax return (Form 1041).** Generally needed when the estate earns more than a small gross income threshold, or has a beneficiary who is a nonresident alien. Interest, dividends and rent after death are the usual triggers.
- [ ] **Federal estate tax return (Form 706).** Only for large estates, or to elect portability for a surviving spouse.
- [ ] **State returns.** Some states have their own estate or inheritance tax. Rules vary by state.
- [ ] **Beneficiary's own return.** Inheritances themselves are usually not income to the heir. Income earned on them is.

## Get set up

- [ ] Get an EIN for the estate from the IRS (free, online).
- [ ] If you are the personal representative, send the IRS Form 56 to notify it that you are acting.
- [ ] Open an estate bank account. Keep estate and personal money separate.
- [ ] Ask the bank and brokers for date-of-death values.

## Basis and the value on the date of death

Most inherited property gets a new basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death. This is called a step-up. It usually wipes out the gain that built up during the decedent's life. Retirement accounts do not get one.

- [ ] House: order an appraisal dated near the date of death.
- [ ] Stocks and funds: print the date-of-death prices from the custodian.
- [ ] Business or land: ask a valuation professional.
- [ ] Keep these records permanently. You will need them when the asset is sold.

## Inherited IRAs and 401(k)s

Withdrawals from a traditional inherited account are generally income to the beneficiary. The timing depends on who the beneficiary is, and the rules are strict.

- [ ] Do not cash out or roll over before you know the rules. A taxable withdrawal cannot be undone.
- [ ] Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty the account by December 31 of the tenth year after the owner's death.
- [ ] If the owner had already started required minimum distributions, yearly withdrawals may also be required within that period.
- [ ] A surviving spouse often has more options, including treating the account as their own.
- [ ] Ask the plan or custodian to retitle the account as an inherited IRA.
- [ ] Missed required withdrawals can bring a penalty, so mark the calendar.

See the [inherited retirement accounts guide](/free/inherited-retirement-accounts-guide) for a deeper look.

## Selling or keeping an inherited home

- [ ] Record the date-of-death value, from a written appraisal.
- [ ] Keep records of repairs, taxes, insurance and utilities during ownership.
- [ ] Remember that inherited property is treated as held long term, so a sale soon after death usually shows little or no gain if the price is near the appraised value.
- [ ] A surviving spouse who sells may qualify for the home sale exclusion for a limited time after the death. Ask a tax professional about the timing.
- [ ] Review property tax bills and any reassessment rules in your state.

See also the [inherited home decision guide](/free/inherited-home-decision-guide).

## Timeline

| When | Task |
|---|---|
| First weeks | EIN, Form 56, estate account |
| First few months | Date-of-death values, notify retirement plans |
| Following spring | Final Form 1040 for the year of death |
| Estate's first tax year end | Decide on calendar or fiscal year for the estate |
| Each year until closed | Form 1041 if required |
| Each year | Inherited IRA withdrawals, if required |

## When to talk to an attorney

- The estate may be large enough for an estate tax return.
- There is no clear personal representative or the family disagrees.
- The decedent owned property in more than one state.
- A trust, business or farm is involved.
- Tax debts or unfiled returns turn up.
