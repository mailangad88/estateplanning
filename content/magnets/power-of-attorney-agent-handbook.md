**How to use this handbook.** Someone has trusted you with a financial power of attorney. This guide explains what that usually means and how to do the job carefully. It is general education, not legal advice. Agent duties and powers vary by state, and the exact words of your document control. Read the document itself alongside this handbook.

## What an agent is

The person who signs a power of attorney is the principal. You are the agent, sometimes called the attorney-in-fact. You are not a lawyer, and the title does not make you one.

Your authority comes from the document. It is limited to what the document and your state's law allow. Think of it as borrowed authority: you act for someone else, in their name, for their benefit.

## Your core duties in plain terms

Rules differ by state, but most places expect the same basic habits from an agent.

- **Act in the principal's interest.** Decisions are about what they would want and what helps them, not what helps you. This includes decisions where you would also benefit.
- **Stay within your authority.** If the document does not clearly allow something, treat it as not allowed until an attorney says otherwise.
- **Keep their money separate.** Never mix the principal's funds with your own. Use their accounts, in their name, with your signature shown as agent.
- **Keep records.** Track what you spend, receive and decide. Records protect you if a relative or a court asks questions later.
- **Act with reasonable care.** You are not expected to be an investment professional, but you are expected to be careful and to ask for help when a decision is beyond you.
- **Follow the principal's known wishes and plan.** If they have a will, trust or stated preferences, do not undo them through your actions.

Many documents also expect you to avoid conflicts of interest. If you are also an heir, tread carefully with any transaction that moves money toward you.

## What an agent can and cannot usually do

Most financial powers cover everyday tasks. The list below is typical, not universal.

| Often allowed | Often needs express authority or is off limits |
|---|---|
| Pay bills and routine expenses | Making gifts, including to yourself |
| Deposit income and manage bank accounts | Changing beneficiary designations |
| File tax returns | Creating or changing a trust |
| Deal with Social Security and benefit agencies | Changing or making a will (you generally cannot) |
| Manage and sell property for the principal's needs | Naming yourself as joint owner |
| Handle insurance claims | Making health decisions (that is a separate document) |

Gifts and beneficiary changes deserve extra caution. Many states require the document to say expressly that the agent may do these things. Even then, unplanned gifts can alter the principal's plan or create tax and benefit-eligibility problems. Ask an attorney first.

## Getting started: first week

- [ ] Read the whole document, start to finish.
- [ ] Note the date it was signed and whether it is effective now or only upon incapacity.
- [ ] Check whether it names a co-agent or successor agent.
- [ ] Ask the principal, if able, what matters most to them.
- [ ] Make a list of accounts, bills and income sources.
- [ ] Open a notebook or spreadsheet for your record log.
- [ ] Keep the original document safe and make several copies.

## Presenting the power of attorney to a bank

Banks differ in how they handle these documents. Some accept a copy at the branch. Others send it to their legal department and take days or weeks. This is common, so plan for it.

1. Call first and ask what they need. Ask for their process in writing.
2. Bring the original or a certified copy, plus your photo ID.
3. Ask whether they want their own form signed by the principal, if able.
4. Sign every transaction as agent, for example: "Pat Lee, as agent for Sam Lee".
5. Ask for a written reason if they refuse. Many states have laws about when a bank may reject a valid document, but rules vary.
6. Keep a note of each person you spoke with and the date.

If a bank refuses repeatedly, an attorney can often resolve it with a letter.

- **Decision made:** ______________________
- **Why:** ______________________
- **Who I consulted:** ______________________

Habits that help: keep receipts, keep bank statements, and never pay yourself without written permission in the document. If the document allows reasonable compensation or expense reimbursement, write down exactly what you took and why.

## Common mistakes

- Using the principal's debit card for personal items "just this once".
- Depositing their checks into your own account.
- Adding yourself to their accounts without clear authority.
- Making gifts to family members to "spread things out" without legal guidance.
- Skipping the records because the amounts seem small.

## When authority ends

Authority generally ends in a few ways. Rules vary, so check your document and state law.

- **The principal dies.** The power of attorney usually ends at death. After that, only the executor or successor trustee can act. Stop using the power right away and tell the bank.
- **The principal revokes it** while they still have capacity.
- **A court ends or limits it**, for example in a guardianship case.
- **The document expires** or a stated event occurs.
- **You resign, become incapacitated or die.** A successor agent may step in if one is named.
- **A divorce** may end a spouse's authority in some states.

After the principal dies, keep your records. The executor may ask you to account for what you did.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The document is unclear about gifts, compensation or what you can do.
- Family members are questioning or disputing your actions.
- A bank or institution refuses to honor the document.
- You are considering selling real estate or moving the principal into long-term care.
- You suspect someone else is taking advantage of the principal.
- You are unsure whether your authority has ended.
