## How to use this guide

We are sorry for your loss. Phones and online accounts can feel like a locked room with photos inside. There is usually a way in, but it takes paperwork and patience. Work down the list and note each request in the tracker. Policies change, so check each company's current help page before you start. This is general education, not legal advice.

## What you usually need first

- [ ] Several certified copies of the death certificate
- [ ] Proof of your role: letters of office or a will naming you executor, where a court appointment exists
- [ ] Your own photo ID
- [ ] The person's full name, email address and phone number
- [ ] A short list of what you actually want (photos, contacts, closing an account)

## What the big platforms commonly offer

| Company | What families often get | Usual requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Apple | A Legacy Contact the person named can request access. Without one, a court order is commonly required | Access key plus death certificate, or court order |
| Google | Closing the account or requesting some data in limited cases, or what the person set up through Inactive Account Manager | Death certificate and proof of relationship, then a review |
| Meta (Facebook, Instagram) | Memorializing or removing the account. Message content is generally not released | Proof of death, sometimes a legal request |
| Microsoft | Often closing the account after a review, and releasing content only by court order | Death certificate and often a court order |

Treat this table as a map, not a promise. Companies set their own rules and can say no.

## A locked phone

1. Try only the passcodes the person was known to use. Several wrong tries can erase or lock a phone, so stop early.
2. Look for a written passcode in a wallet, desk or password manager.
3. Check whether a family member set up a trusted-device sign-in.
4. Ask the carrier whether it can transfer or suspend the number. The carrier can usually not unlock the phone.
5. A court order directed to the manufacturer is the last route. It can take time and may not succeed.

Do not pay anyone who claims to unlock phones without checking them out. Many are scams.

## Two-factor codes

Many accounts send a code to the phone. If the phone is locked, you may be stuck. Before the number is cancelled:

- [ ] Keep the phone number active until the accounts are sorted
- [ ] Look for printed backup codes
- [ ] Check a laptop or tablet already signed in
- [ ] Look in a password manager for saved recovery codes

## Photos in the cloud

| Where photos may be | Try first |
|---|---|
| iCloud | Apple legacy contact request, or the family's shared album |
| Google Photos | Google's data request process, or shared albums |
| Meta | Memorialized account friends' tributes, any saved downloads |
| A computer or old phone | Back up the device before anything else |

If photos are on a phone you can open, copy them now to a drive you control.

## Finding accounts you do not know about

1. Search the email inbox for words like "receipt," "welcome" and "password reset."
2. Review credit card and bank statements for monthly charges.
3. Check the phone's installed apps.
4. Check the password manager and browser saved logins.
5. Ask close family and the person's accountant.

## Cancelling subscriptions and billing

- [ ] List every recurring charge
- [ ] Cancel by phone or chat and ask for written confirmation
- [ ] Ask for refunds of unused prepaid time
- [ ] Contact the wireless carrier last, once codes are not needed
- [ ] Dispute charges made after death with the card issuer

## Facebook and Instagram

Memorializing keeps the profile visible with "Remembering" added. Friends can leave tributes. Deleting removes it. The family decides, and the person may have set a legacy contact in advance. Apply through Meta's help center with proof of death.

## Privacy and what you may find

Not everything on a phone is meant to be read. Decide as a family who will look and what you will do about private messages. Laws also limit who may read the content of messages.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A company refuses and you believe you have the right to the account
- You need a court order for a phone or email
- There is a business, cryptocurrency or large balance behind the account
- Family members disagree about who should have access
- The person left a will that mentions digital property
