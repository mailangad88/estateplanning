**How to use this worksheet.** Fill in the inventory first, then read the which-wins table. A will is powerful, but it only reaches property titled in your name alone. Many of the larger assets people own pass by a form, a title or a trust. Rules vary by state. This is general education, not legal advice.

## What a will does

A will does four main things:

1. Names who receives property that passes through probate
2. Names an executor to carry it out
3. Names a guardian for minor children
4. Can create trusts for children or others, called testamentary trusts

It does not take effect until death, and it only controls what is in your name alone at that moment. It then goes through probate unless the estate is small enough for a shortcut. In Illinois that shortcut is the small estate affidavit, 755 ILCS 5/25-1.

## Which document wins

| Asset or wish | What controls | Does the will override it? |
|---|---|---|
| Life insurance, with a named beneficiary | The beneficiary form | No |
| IRA, 401(k), annuity with a named beneficiary | The beneficiary form | No |
| Account with payable on death or transfer on death | That designation | No |
| Joint account with right of survivorship | The joint title | No |
| Home held as joint tenants with survivorship | The title | No |
| Property in a living trust | The trust terms | No |
| Property in your name alone | The will, or intestacy if no will | Yes |
| Power of attorney | Ends at death | The POA has no power after death, the will takes over |
| Healthcare directive | Ends at death | Not relevant after death |

The rule to remember: a form or title beats a will. If your beneficiary form names your ex and your will names your child, the form usually wins. See the [beneficiary designation audit](/free/beneficiary-designation-audit).

Illinois note: a will usually cannot change who a TOD instrument or a payable on death account pays. Check the specific form and 755 ILCS 27/ for TOD instruments. <!-- verify: citation for Illinois Residential Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument Act -->

## Will versus trust, will versus power of attorney

- **Will and trust:** Trust assets follow the trust. A pour-over will catches things left out and sends them to the trust, but those pass through probate first.
- **Will and power of attorney:** A power of attorney is for your life. It ends when you die. Your executor then takes over under the will.

If you have a living trust, you usually still need a pour-over will. It names guardians for children, catches stray assets and names an executor.

## If everything has a beneficiary

You may still want a will. Beneficiaries die first, divorce, or turn out to be minors. A will provides:

- A guardian for children, which only a will or court can do
- A backup for assets with no living beneficiary
- A place for items like jewelry, collectibles and vehicles
- An executor to deal with debts and final taxes

A short will still has value. If everything is jointly owned, the last surviving owner still ends up with assets in their own name and the same need for a will.

## Funeral wishes

A will can state funeral wishes, but it is a poor place for them. Wills are often read after a funeral has already taken place. Illinois law gives a person the right to name an agent for disposition of remains by a written instrument (see the Disposition of Remains Act, 755 ILCS 65/). <!-- verify: Illinois Disposition of Remains Act citation and requirements -->

Better steps:

- [ ] Write a separate funeral wishes document and give copies to your family
- [ ] Name the person who will carry out those wishes

## Debts and a mortgaged house

A will can say which gift pays debts, but it cannot erase them. Creditors are usually paid from the estate before beneficiaries. In Illinois claims against an estate are governed by 755 ILCS 5/18-1 and following. <!-- verify: Illinois claims period citation -->

A will can leave a house that has a mortgage. The beneficiary takes the house subject to the loan. Federal law generally lets a family member who inherits a home keep the loan in place without the lender calling it due. See [mortgage after death guide](/free/mortgage-after-death-guide). A will can also say whether the estate pays off the mortgage, which is rare.

## What does not belong in a will

- Funeral directions only in the will
- Instructions for a trust you have not created
- Gifts for a person with a disability that could affect benefits
- Gifts of property owned jointly or with a named beneficiary
- Secret wishes that need to be known before death
- Personal letters (put these in a letter of instruction)

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your beneficiary forms and your will say different things
- You have a trust and a will and are not sure how they fit
- A major asset is jointly owned with a child
- You own a business or land in more than one state
- A person you want to benefit receives public benefits
