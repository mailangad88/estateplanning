## How to use this guide

Illinois has its own estate tax, separate from the federal tax. Most families owe neither, but Illinois starts at a much lower level than the federal amount. Read the sections, fill in the worksheet, then take the numbers to an attorney. Tax rules change, so this guide is general education and should be confirmed for your situation. Check the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) for Illinois as well.

## The $4 million exclusion

Illinois taxes estates above a $4,000,000 exclusion. The Illinois Attorney General describes it as a taxable threshold, not a credit against the tax. The federal exemption is far higher, so a family can owe Illinois tax and nothing to the federal government. Illinois has no inheritance tax, so heirs do not pay tax on what they receive. The tax falls on the estate.

An Illinois Form 700 return is required when the gross estate, plus adjusted taxable gifts, is above $4,000,000.

## Not portable: what that means for married couples

At the federal level, a surviving spouse can often use the unused exemption of the spouse who died. Illinois does not allow that. The Attorney General's instructions say portability does not apply to the Illinois tax.

The result: each spouse has a $4 million exclusion, and it cannot be shared. If the first spouse leaves everything to the survivor, the first exclusion goes unused. The survivor's estate then has only one $4 million exclusion, even if the couple together had more.

Here is a simple picture. A couple owns $7 million. The first spouse dies and leaves all of it to the survivor. No tax is due at the first death, but the survivor now has a $7 million estate and one exclusion. If the first spouse's share had been placed in a trust that uses the first exclusion, the family may have been able to avoid Illinois tax. This is why planning often matters here.

## The interrelated calculation

Illinois computes the tax with an interrelated calculation. The tax itself is a deduction when figuring the taxable estate, so the tax and the taxable amount depend on each other. The Attorney General offers an online calculator for this.

What this means in practice is that estates just over $4 million can face a steep effective rate on the amount above the line. The tax can take a large share of the first dollars over the threshold, and the effect eases as the estate grows. Use the Attorney General's calculator or ask an attorney to run your numbers.

## Who files and when

The personal representative of the estate files Form 700 with the Illinois Attorney General. The tax is due nine months after the date of death. Extensions are possible through the Attorney General or by a federal extension. The filing rules depend on the gross estate and adjusted taxable gifts, whether or not a federal return is required.

## Common planning responses

These are general ideas. They are not right for every family.

- **Credit shelter (bypass) trusts.** A trust funded at the first death uses the first spouse's exclusion, so it is not wasted. The survivor may have access to income, and sometimes principal.
- **Lifetime gifts.** Illinois has no gift tax. Adjusted taxable gifts, however, are added back when figuring whether the estate is over the threshold. Gifts may reduce the estate, but they have income tax effects and may come back into the calculation. <!-- verify: how Illinois treats adjusted taxable gifts in the Form 700 calculation -->
- **Charitable giving.** Gifts to charity at death are deductible.
- **Retitling between spouses.** Balancing assets so each spouse can use an exclusion.
- **Life insurance in a trust.** Policies owned by an individual can count in the estate.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your estate, with insurance and retirement accounts, is near or above $4 million
- You are married and your plan leaves everything to your spouse
- You own real estate in another state
- You want to make large gifts
- Someone has died and you are not sure Form 700 is due
