**How to use this checklist.** A timeshare can be a gift or a burden. Many carry annual fees that continue after the owner dies, and some contracts say the obligation passes to heirs. Work through the steps in order, and do not pay any company that promises to get you out before you finish Part 5. Contracts and state laws differ, so confirm what applies to yours.

## Why this asset is different

A timeshare is usually either a deeded interest (real estate) or a right-to-use or points membership. Deeded interests can pass through probate or a deed. Right-to-use contracts may end at death, or may continue as an obligation. Either way, maintenance fees and special assessments can keep coming. The result is often an unwelcome surprise for the family, and it is a common target for resellers who demand upfront fees.

Where to look:

- [ ] Original contract and deed
- [ ] Annual fee notices and tax bills
- [ ] Mail, email and bank statements for autopays
- [ ] Member portal or points statement
- [ ] County recorder's records, for deeded interests

## Part 2: Read the contract for these clauses

- [ ] Whether the obligation continues after the owner's death
- [ ] Whether heirs inherit the interest, and whether they must accept
- [ ] Any transfer fee or approval process
- [ ] Any "surrender," "deed-back" or "exit" program offered by the developer
- [ ] Rules on renting or selling
- [ ] Late fee, default and collections terms
- [ ] Whether the membership carries a loan that survives death

## Part 3: Options at a glance

| Option | What happens | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Keep and use | Heir takes ownership and pays fees | Fees may rise over time |
| Transfer to a family member | Deed or transfer form to a willing heir | Transfer fee, approval, fees pass on |
| Disclaim (refuse the inheritance) | Heir declines in writing, within the legal deadline, so the interest passes as if they had died first | Must be done before accepting benefit; rules vary by state |
| Developer surrender or deed-back | Developer takes back the interest | May be limited, may require fees to be current |
| Sell on resale market | Buyer assumes ownership | Resale value is often low; many resale offers are scams |
| Do nothing | Fees accrue; collection or foreclosure may follow | Credit and estate consequences |

A disclaimer generally has a strict time window, and you generally cannot disclaim after using or accepting the property. In many states, a disclaimer must be in writing and filed with the court or recorded. An attorney can say whether it fits your case.

## Part 4: What the estate owes, and what family does not

Fees and debts are usually the owner's debts, paid from the estate where there is money. Family members who did not sign the contract are generally not personally liable for it, though the estate may be. If a collector contacts you, ask for the account in writing, tell them who the executor is, and do not agree to pay from your own funds without advice. Do not sign anything that makes you a new owner unless you intend to be one.

## Part 5: Spotting exit and resale scams

The Federal Trade Commission warns about companies that charge large upfront fees to sell or cancel a timeshare and then do little.

Warning signs:

- [ ] They call or mail you first, claiming a buyer is waiting.
- [ ] They ask for fees upfront, before anything is sold.
- [ ] They promise a sale, a cancellation or a price. Any promise like this is a red flag.
- [ ] They pressure you to act today.
- [ ] They tell you to stop paying maintenance fees.
- [ ] They use only a phone number, with no verifiable address, license or reviews.
- [ ] They ask you to pay by wire, gift card or cryptocurrency.
- [ ] They refuse to give terms in writing.

Safer steps:

- [ ] Start with the developer's own exit or surrender program, and ask in writing.
- [ ] Check any company with your state attorney general and the Better Business Bureau.
- [ ] Use a licensed real estate broker, and ask for a written agreement with no upfront fee.
- [ ] Report fraud to the FTC and your state attorney general.

## Part 6: Sample letter to the developer

> Re: Member number ____________
> I am writing as [executor / heir] of [name], who died on [date]. Please send, in writing: the current balance and fees; whether the obligation continues after death; any surrender or deed-back program; and any deadlines. Please send all notices to me at [address]. I do not agree to assume any obligation by sending this letter.

Enclose a death certificate and proof of your authority if requested.

## Part 7: Steps for the executor

- [ ] Add the timeshare to the estate inventory.
- [ ] Notify the developer and ask for a payoff and options in writing.
- [ ] Keep paying fees from estate funds if the estate has them and the option to keep or transfer is open.
- [ ] Ask the heirs whether anyone wants it.
- [ ] If nobody does, choose between disclaimer, surrender and sale.
- [ ] Record any deed transfer with the county recorder.
- [ ] Keep copies of every letter and receipt.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The interest is a deeded property in another state.
- You are considering a disclaimer and want to meet the deadline.
- The developer refuses to accept a surrender or sends collection notices.
- You paid a company that did not deliver.
- The owner left a loan balance and little else.
