## How to use these letters

Calls and letters from creditors can arrive in the first weeks, when you are least able to deal with them. These templates give you calm, short wording. In general, a family member is not personally responsible for a deceased person's debts unless they co-signed, held a joint account, or live in a state where a spouse can be responsible. Rules vary by state. Debts are usually paid from the estate first.

Send letters by a method with a receipt. Keep a copy of each one and a log of every call.

## First rules

- [ ] Do not pay a debt from your own money until you know the estate and the law call for it.
- [ ] Do not say "I will take care of it" or "we will pay this" on the phone.
- [ ] Do not sign a new agreement, payment plan or "acknowledgment of debt".
- [ ] Do not give out the full Social Security number. Use the last four digits.
- [ ] Ask for everything in writing.
- [ ] Keep a list of who contacted you, when and what they said.

If you are the executor, you may be the right person to receive these letters. If no executor has been named yet, say so. Write "I am a family member and am gathering information. I do not yet represent the estate."

## Letter 1: Notice of death

> [Your name]
> [Address, phone, email]
> [Date]
>
> [Creditor name and address]
>
> Re: [Full name], deceased, account ending in [last four]
>
> To whom it may concern:
>
> [Full name] died on [date]. A copy of the death certificate is enclosed. I am the [executor / family member] and am writing only to notify you of the death.
>
> Please note the account as deceased, stop any statements and automatic charges to the decedent, and send all future correspondence about this account to me at the address above. Please send me a written statement of the balance owed as of the date of death, the account owner(s) and any co-signers.
>
> This letter is not an acknowledgment of the debt and is not a promise to pay. Any claim should be directed to the estate.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Signature and printed name]

## Letter 2: Request for balance and records

Use this after the first notice, or when a bill arrives with no details.

> Re: Request for account information, [full name], account ending in [last four]
>
> Please provide, in writing:
>
> 1. The original creditor and the current owner of the debt
> 2. The balance on the date of death, and how it breaks down between principal, interest and fees
> 3. A copy of the signed agreement or the last statement
> 4. The date of the last payment
> 5. Whether any other person is listed as an owner, joint holder or co-signer
> 6. Whether any insurance or credit protection applies to this account
>
> Until I receive this information, I cannot evaluate the claim.

Some accounts carry credit insurance that pays the balance after a death. Ask before assuming nothing is covered.

## Letter 3: Reply to a collector

Debt collectors may call or write about an old account. You can ask for proof and ask that contact be in writing. Federal law limits how collectors may behave, and a collector should not imply that you have to pay from your own funds when you do not share the debt.

> Re: [Full name], deceased, reference [number]
>
> I received your [call / letter] dated [date]. [Full name] died on [date]. I am not agreeing that this debt is valid or that any person other than the estate is responsible for it.
>
> Please send written proof that you own or are authorized to collect this debt, including the original agreement, the balance at the date of death and an itemization. Please contact me only in writing at the address above and do not call me.
>
> This letter is not an acknowledgment of the debt and is not a promise to pay.

If a collector keeps calling or threatens you, write down dates and what was said. A consumer protection agency or an attorney can explain your options.

## What not to promise

| Do not say | Say instead |
|---|---|
| "I will pay it off." | "Please send me the details in writing." |
| "We owe you." | "The estate will review any valid claim." |
| "I'm responsible for this." | "I am gathering information for the estate." |
| "Take it from the account." | "I cannot authorize that yet." |

## Priorities and order

In many states, the estate pays certain things first, such as funeral costs, administration costs and taxes, before many ordinary debts. If the estate does not have enough, some debts may go unpaid. Do not rush to pay a small creditor before you know what the estate holds.

In Illinois, claims against an estate are generally cut off two years after the death, and can be cut off sooner when proper notice is given. Other states use different timelines, so ask an attorney in the state where the estate is open.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The debts look larger than the estate's assets
- A collector says you personally owe the debt
- You co-signed or held a joint account with the person who died
- A mortgage, tax debt or medical bill is involved
- You are unsure whether to open a probate estate to handle claims
