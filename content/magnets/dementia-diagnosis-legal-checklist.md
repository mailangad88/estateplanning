**How to use this checklist.** A dementia diagnosis does not take away legal rights. Many people can still sign valid documents for a time. That time is the reason this list is in order of urgency. It focuses on the first legal and money steps. A broader planning guide and a pre-surgery checklist cover other ground. Work down the list, write dates in the blanks and share it with the family member who will help. The law on capacity and documents varies by state.

## First 30 days: documents while the person can still sign

Whether someone can sign depends on the day, the document and what the person understands. A diagnosis alone does not answer the question. Many people with early or mild dementia can still choose an agent and sign a power of attorney.

- [ ] **Power of attorney for property.** Names who handles money and bills. Choose with care: this person will have wide power. Consider a backup agent.
- [ ] **Power of attorney for health care.** Names who decides about medical care when the person cannot.
- [ ] **Living will.** Records wishes about life-sustaining treatment.
- [ ] **HIPAA release.** Lets doctors speak with the agent and family.
- [ ] **Will or trust review.** If a will is old, review it. If one does not exist, create one while capacity remains.
- [ ] **Beneficiary designations.** Check retirement accounts and insurance, since these override a will.

## Capacity: recording it

Signing requires that the person understand what the document does, who the people are and what they own, and what they are choosing. The question may vary from document to document. A will, for example, is judged differently from a contract.

- [ ] Ask the doctor whether they will write a short letter near the signing date about the person's understanding.
- [ ] Book signing appointments in the morning if the person is clearer then.
- [ ] Have an attorney supervise the signing, not a family member.
- [ ] Let the attorney meet the person alone for part of the meeting. This protects against later claims of pressure.
- [ ] Keep notes about the day.

If capacity is already doubtful, do not have the person sign documents anyway and hope for the best. Documents signed without capacity can be challenged, and the damage can be harder to fix. See the [power of attorney versus guardianship comparison](/compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## Money safeguards

Dementia can bring unpaid bills, duplicate payments, risky purchases and vulnerability to scams. Set up protections early.

- [ ] Add the agent as an authorized contact, or as a joint signer, if the bank allows, but understand the legal consequences of joint accounts.
- [ ] Turn on account alerts for large transactions.
- [ ] Set up automatic payments for fixed bills.
- [ ] Reduce credit card limits, or close unused cards.
- [ ] Place a freeze on credit reports.
- [ ] Use the National Do Not Call registry and opt out of junk mail.
- [ ] List recurring subscriptions and donations.
- [ ] Locate a trusted contact for investment accounts. Many firms allow a person to name one.
- [ ] Watch for new "friends," caregivers or relatives who suddenly become involved in money.

## 31 to 90 days: organize and plan

- [ ] Make an inventory of accounts, property, debts and insurance. Use the [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory).
- [ ] Gather a list of passwords and who can access them. See the [digital assets guide](/guides/digital-assets-estate-planning).
- [ ] Locate the deed, titles, tax returns and insurance policies.
- [ ] Review long-term care insurance and any rights to benefits.
- [ ] Tell the family what documents exist and where.
- [ ] Review driving, firearm and home safety questions with the doctor.
- [ ] Meet with an attorney about long-term care costs and Medicaid timing, before money is moved. See the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date).
- [ ] If the person is a veteran, ask about VA benefits.

## 91 to 180 days: build the care plan

- [ ] List who will provide care, and how it will be paid.
- [ ] Identify memory care, adult day or home care providers and get costs in writing.
- [ ] Decide who will be the main caregiver and how the family supports them.
- [ ] If a family caregiver will be paid, consider a written care agreement with legal advice.
- [ ] Update the plan after each stage of the disease.
- [ ] Set up a calendar for reviewing documents every six to twelve months.

## What to avoid

- Waiting for the person to be "ready" until the window has closed.
- Having an adult child draft or supervise the signing of a will that benefits that child.
- Moving the home or savings to children without advice. Transfers can affect Medicaid eligibility and taxes.
- Putting children on accounts to "make it easier." That can expose the accounts to the children's creditors and divorces.
- Making the person feel taken over. Involve them in every decision they can still make.

## If capacity is already gone and no one has authority

Without a power of attorney, a bank may refuse to talk to the family. The usual fallback is a court-supervised guardianship, which is public, costly and slower. Some states have simpler options for narrow tasks, but they are limited. Talk to an attorney quickly.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Documents are not signed and the window may be closing.
- Capacity is uncertain.
- There are siblings, a blended family, or someone who may challenge the documents.
- There is a home, business or large estate.
- Long-term care costs are likely and you want to plan around Medicaid.
