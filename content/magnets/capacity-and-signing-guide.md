## How to use this guide

Capacity is the legal word for having the mental ability to make a particular decision. People with early memory loss, a serious illness or a recent hospital stay often wonder whether they can still sign. Many can. Capacity is not all or nothing, and it can be shown with care. This guide explains the idea, then gives you a plan and a record sheet. Standards vary by state and by document, so use it to prepare for a conversation with an attorney.

## What the law asks

Capacity is judged at the moment of signing, for the specific document. Courts do not usually ask whether a person is a perfect decision maker. They ask whether the person understood what they were doing.

For a will, Illinois law says a person who is at least 18 and "of sound mind and memory" may leave their property by will. Other states use similar words. In practice, courts often look at whether the signer:

- Knew they were making a will, or a document that says who gets property after death
- Had a general idea of what they own
- Knew who their close family members are, such as a spouse and children
- Could connect those pieces and decide who should receive what

A trust or beneficiary change is usually treated much like a will. A power of attorney can be judged differently, since the signer must understand that they are giving another person authority over money or health decisions. A contract or a gift made during life can involve a somewhat different test. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you which one applies.

## Common misunderstandings

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| A dementia diagnosis means no capacity | A diagnosis is not a legal finding. Many people can sign in early stages |
| Capacity is the same all day | Many people think more clearly at certain times of day |
| A doctor decides capacity | Doctors give opinions. A court decides if the question is ever contested |
| Being old is enough to doubt a will | Age alone is not a reason to set a will aside |
| If a guardian was appointed, nothing can be signed | A court order can restrict signing, and some states presume documents signed after one are void unless proven otherwise. Illinois has such a presumption for wills |

## Good times and bad times

Some conditions come and go. Infections, medication changes, pain, dehydration and sleep problems can cloud thinking for a while. Memory conditions can fluctuate through the day, which is why you sometimes hear of "sundowning." If you are concerned, plan the signing for when your parent or you are usually at your sharpest, and after any acute illness has settled.

- [ ] Choose the time of day when thinking is clearest
- [ ] Schedule after meals, rest and medications, not before
- [ ] Postpone if a new infection, hospital stay or medication change is recent
- [ ] Bring glasses, hearing aids and any aids used daily
- [ ] Choose a quiet room, with no pressure to hurry

## How to document capacity

Evidence created at the time is more persuasive than recollections years later.

1. **Attorney meets the client alone.** The attorney asks open questions: What do you own? Who are your family members? Who do you want to receive your property, and why? The answers should come from the client, in the client's words.
2. **The attorney keeps notes.** Notes on what the client said and how they seemed are often the first thing reviewed in a dispute.
3. **A physician's letter may help.** If there is a diagnosis or concern, ask the doctor to see the client close to the signing and write a short letter that describes the client's understanding. Doctors do not always agree to do this, and a letter is not required.
4. **Choose witnesses who will remember.** For a will, Illinois requires attestation by two or more credible witnesses in the testator's presence. Witnesses with no stake in the will, who watch the whole signing, are more helpful later. States differ on formalities.
5. **Consider a video record, with advice.** Some attorneys use video. Others avoid it because it can cut either way. Ask yours.
6. **Keep the plan simple.** A straightforward plan that matches earlier wishes is easier to defend than a complicated one that reverses everything.

## Signing-day checklist

- [ ] The client, not a family member, set up the appointment or confirmed it
- [ ] The client meets the attorney privately before signing
- [ ] No beneficiary or agent sits in the meeting room
- [ ] The client reads or hears the document and can say what it does in their own words
- [ ] Witnesses and notary are present for the whole signing
- [ ] Any physician's letter is dated near the signing and stored with the file
- [ ] A copy of the signed documents is given to the client

## If capacity is in real doubt

Sometimes the honest answer is that the person cannot understand a complex document. Options may include:

- Signing a simpler document that is within their understanding, such as a short will that matches long-held wishes
- Using a health care power of attorney or financial power of attorney, if the person can still understand naming a trusted agent
- Using a trust the person created earlier, so a successor trustee can act without a new signing
- Asking a court for a guardianship or conservatorship, a heavier step with court oversight and costs

Rushed documents for a person who clearly cannot understand them tend to cause more conflict, not less. If you are a family member, ask the attorney what the client themself understood and said. ## Timing tips

- Do not wait until the last hospital stay. Planning in early stages is far easier.
- Review documents when health changes. A plan signed years ago is not affected by later memory loss.
- Keep earlier documents. They show long-held wishes if a new one is questioned.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A parent or spouse has memory loss, a serious diagnosis or a recent hospital stay and has no documents
- A signing is planned and a family member may object
- You want to know whether a doctor's letter makes sense in your state
- A guardianship or court order exists or is being considered
- You are unsure whether a power of attorney or a will is the right document to sign
