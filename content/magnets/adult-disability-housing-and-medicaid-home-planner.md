## How to use this planner

Where your loved one will live is often the hardest part of the plan. Start with what they like, then match it to a path. Costs and rules vary by state and by funding source, so treat each row as a question to ask your state agency. This is general education.

## Housing paths compared

| Path | What it is | Who pays | Common issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family home | Stays in the house, with a sibling or paid help | Trust, SSI, family | Shelter payments may reduce SSI; someone must own and maintain it |
| Supported living | Own apartment with staff visits | Medicaid waiver pays services; rent from SSI | Waiting lists |
| Group home | Shared home with staff | Often Medicaid waiver plus SSI | Fewer choices; placement depends on openings |
| HUD or Section 8 | Rent subsidy | Federal housing program | Waiting lists; income rules |
| Shared house with a sibling | Sibling lives with them | Rent share, trust | Sibling's life changes, divorce or sale |

## Medicaid HCBS waivers

A home and community based services (HCBS) waiver pays for services that let a person live outside an institution. Some pay for staff, therapy, day programs or home changes. They usually do not pay rent. Waivers have their own eligibility rules, which can include asset limits, and waiting lists. Ask your state's disability agency. See /free/illinois-special-needs-planning-starter-guide for Illinois.

## Can your child stay in the family home?

A person can stay if someone has legal control of the house. Without a plan, the house goes to heirs, and a sibling may sell it. Choose one:

| Option | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Leave the house to a third-party special needs trust | Trustee owns, the person lives there | The trust pays taxes and repairs; shelter payments by the trust may reduce SSI |
| Leave the house to a sibling with a written agreement | Sibling owns | Not legally binding on the sibling's creditors, spouse or sale |
| Life estate for the person | The person may live there for life | Life tenant may have taxes and repair duties; may not work with SSI <!-- verify: how a life estate affects SSI and Medicaid --> |
| Deed to the person outright | Person owns | Home may be exempt for SSI, but Medicaid may seek recovery at death |
| Sell and buy through a trust | Trust buys a new house | Cost of purchase and upkeep |

A special needs trust can own a house. Many trustees prefer that route because it protects the home from a creditor and lets a trustee sell when the person needs a different setting. A trust should hold enough cash for taxes, insurance and repairs.

## Transfer penalties and exceptions

Medicaid generally penalizes gifts made within the look-back period when someone applies for long-term care. <!-- verify: look-back period 60 months --> Federal law lists exceptions, including some transfers of a home to a disabled child and to a **caregiver child** who lived in the home and gave care for at least two years that delayed the parent's institutional care. <!-- verify: caregiver child exemption details --> The disabled child exception also covers transfers to a trust for the sole benefit of a disabled person under 65.

These are narrow rules. Do not deed the house on a hunch. See /tools/medicaid-lookback-date.

## Estate recovery

After a Medicaid recipient dies, a state may seek repayment from the estate. A home deeded to a disabled child may be protected if properly structured. A home that a disabled person owns outright may be at risk after their own Medicaid use. A third-party trust that owns the house is typically outside recovery for the person's estate. Rules vary by state, so confirm yours.

## ABLE accounts and rent

An ABLE account can pay for qualified disability expenses including housing. Using it for rent does not reduce SSI the way a trust paying rent can, but unspent housing money can count as a resource later. <!-- verify: ABLE housing rules for SSI --> See /free/able-account-planner.

## Timeline

1. **Now:** write down what the person likes and needs, tour options, join waiting lists
2. **In 1 to 3 years:** decide among paths, draft the trust, name a trustee
3. **In 5 years:** review benefits, update letter of intent, test a trial stay
4. **If a parent dies:** open the letter, call the trustee and the service coordinator the same week

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to deed or leave the home to the person
- A Medicaid waiver or look-back question is unresolved
- A sibling would inherit the house with a duty to the person
- You are considering a life estate
- A trust will buy or hold the home
