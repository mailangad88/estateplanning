**How to use this guide.** Think of one heir at a time. Read the options, tick the protections that fit that person, and use the worksheet. Leaving money outright is simple, but it gives an heir complete control, which also exposes it to their creditors, a former spouse and their own mistakes. Rules vary by state.

## What a spendthrift clause does

A spendthrift clause says the beneficiary cannot sell or pledge their future share of the trust, and creditors cannot reach it before the trustee distributes it. In most states, these clauses are enforceable. Once money is paid out to the heir, it is theirs and can be reached like any other money. So the protection works while money is held in trust, not after it leaves.

Typical language:

> No beneficiary may assign, pledge or anticipate any interest in the trust, and no interest is subject to the claims of creditors or to legal process before it is actually received.

## What it does not stop

State law carves out exceptions. These differ widely, but commonly include:

- Child support and sometimes alimony claims
- Certain government claims, such as taxes
- Claims for services that protect the beneficiary's interest in the trust
- A beneficiary who is also the person who created the trust (self-settled trust), in many states

In Illinois, the Trust Code lists exceptions to spendthrift protection, including child support. Check your own state. A spendthrift clause is a shield with edges, not a wall.

## Four ways to leave an inheritance

| Method | How it works | Protection | Heir's freedom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outright | Paid at once | None after payment | Complete |
| Staged | Paid at set ages | Some, between payments | High, with a delay |
| Discretionary trust | Trustee decides when and how much | Strong, since the heir has no right to demand | Low |
| Lifetime trust | Held for the heir's life, with distributions | Strong against creditors and divorce | Moderate, if the heir is also a co-trustee |

A discretionary trust gives the trustee decision power. This helps with creditors, because the heir cannot be forced to take money out. It also raises family tension, so choose the trustee carefully.

## Protection from divorce

If money goes outright to an heir and is mixed with marital property, a spouse may claim part of it in a divorce. If the heir keeps trust assets separate and the trust is held in trust rather than paid out, the trust interest is often treated differently. Rules vary by state, and commingling can undo the protection. A trustee who pays bills directly, such as for housing, may help keep funds separate.

## Protection from the heir

Some heirs struggle with addiction, gambling, spending or poor judgment. Options include:

- Trustee pays bills directly rather than giving cash
- Distributions based on need rather than a schedule
- Drug or alcohol testing before payments (use with care and with an attorney)
- A trustee with authority to hold back funds during a crisis
- A professional trustee, so a family member does not have to play the bad guy

See the [guide to leaving money to someone who struggles](/free/leaving-money-to-someone-who-struggles-guide).

## Incentive provisions

An incentive trust ties payments to behavior. Common ideas:

- A matching payment for each dollar the heir earns
- Funds for education, training or starting a business
- A payment on finishing a degree or a program
- Money for therapy or treatment

Ideas to approach carefully:

- Rules about marriage, religion or where someone lives. Courts in many states will not enforce terms that seriously restrict marriage or violate public policy.
- All-or-nothing deadlines that reward the wrong behavior.
- Terms that require the trustee to judge sensitive matters. Vague terms cause disputes.

Good incentive terms are clear, measurable and give the trustee room to make exceptions for illness or hardship.

Sample wording:

> For each year the beneficiary is employed or is a full-time student, the trustee may distribute an amount equal to the beneficiary's earnings, up to a limit set by the trustee. The trustee may also make distributions for medical care, housing and education regardless of employment.

## Pick the trustee with care

A trust with strong protection but a weak trustee is a bad fit. Ask:

- **Will this person be able to say no to a relative?** ______
- **Will this person keep clear records?** ______
- **Is there a backup, and can the heir remove the trustee?** ______
- **Should the heir become a co-trustee at an older age?** ______

## Common mistakes

1. Leaving a large inheritance outright and hoping it works out.
2. Naming the heir as the only trustee of their own trust, which can weaken protection.
3. Writing incentives that cannot be measured.
4. Not telling the heir what the plan is, so the trustee is the first to explain.
5. Forgetting retirement accounts, which may pass outside the trust.

## When to talk to an attorney

- An heir has creditors, a lawsuit, a risky job or a troubled marriage.
- You want incentive terms and are not sure they will be enforced.
- An heir receives government benefits.
- You want a trust to last for an heir's lifetime.
- You hold a business or real estate that will pass to an heir.
- You live in or have heirs in a state with unusual rules.
