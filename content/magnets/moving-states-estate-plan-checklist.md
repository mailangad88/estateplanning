**How to use this checklist.** Tick off items as you go and write answers in the blanks. State law is the whole story here, and it varies a great deal, so this list is for finding questions, not answering them. An attorney licensed in your new state can review your documents against local rules, often in one meeting.

## Do your documents still work?

Short answer: often, but with friction. A will that was valid where it was signed is generally honored in other states, and many states have rules to that effect. Rules vary by state, and "generally" is not "always." Small differences can still cause trouble.

| Document | Usually still valid after a move? | Practical issues to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Will** | Generally yes if validly signed | Terms such as "executor" or "personal representative" may differ. A "self-proving affidavit" may differ. Your new state may have different spousal rights. |
| **Revocable living trust** | Generally yes | Name of the trustee, powers and governing law. Trust funding may need new deeds. |
| **Financial power of attorney** | Often yes | Banks and institutions may ask for a local form, or hesitate with an out-of-state document. Some states require specific wording or signing steps. |
| **Health care power of attorney and living will** | Often yes | Hospitals may not be familiar with out-of-state forms. Some states have a statutory form that is easiest to use. |
| **HIPAA authorization** | Federal law, so generally yes | Local providers may have their own forms. |
| **Beneficiary designations** | Yes, they follow the account or policy | Not affected by a move, but good to review. |
| **Transfer on death deeds** | Depends | Some states do not allow them. A deed signed in one state may not work for property in another. |

Checklist:

- [ ] Locate all documents and list the date and state of signing.
- [ ] Ask whether each is valid in the new state.
- [ ] Ask whether health care and financial documents should be redone using local forms.
- [ ] Ask whether the will's signing steps (witnesses, notary, self-proving statement) satisfy the new state.
- [ ] Confirm the trust still has the right governing law and trustee provisions.

- **New state:** ______________________

## Community property and common law moves

States use one of two main systems for marital property. In community property states, property earned or acquired during marriage is generally treated as owned equally by both spouses. In common law (also called separate property) states, ownership generally follows title, and a surviving spouse has other protections, such as an elective share. Rules vary by state, and some states have hybrid or opt-in systems.

A move between the systems can matter:

- **From a community property state to a common law state.** Property you acquired during marriage may keep its community character in some states, but your will and titles may need a fresh look. Some states have rules to recognize this.
- **From a common law state to a community property state.** New earnings may be treated differently from what you used to expect. Some states have special rules for newcomers.
- **Estate planning documents.** A will that divided property based on one system may not produce the intended result in the other.

Questions to bring to an attorney:

- [ ] Is any of our property considered community, marital or separate in the new state?
- [ ] Does the new state give a surviving spouse a minimum share regardless of the will?
- [ ] Does the way we hold title (joint tenants, tenants by the entirety, community property with survivorship) still work?
- [ ] Should a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement be reviewed?

## Executor and trustee residency rules

Some states limit who may serve as executor (personal representative) if the person lives out of state. Common rules include requiring an out-of-state executor to appoint a local agent, post a bond or, in some places, being unable to serve unless a close relative. Rules vary by state.

- [ ] Is the person I named still a good fit, given where they live?
- [ ] Are they a resident of the state where my estate would be probated?
- [ ] Do I need a backup who lives nearby?
- [ ] Do I want a trust and trustee in place to reduce reliance on court?

See [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [executor vs trustee](/compare/executor-vs-trustee).

## Property in the old state

If you own real estate or other property in the old state, probate may be required in both states. This is called ancillary probate. It adds cost, time and paperwork, and a local attorney may be needed. People often use a trust or a transfer on death arrangement to avoid it, where their state allows.

Checklist:

- [ ] List all real estate, vehicles, bank accounts and business interests in the old state.
- [ ] Check how each is titled.
- [ ] Ask whether a trust, deed or beneficiary designation could avoid a second probate.
- [ ] Decide whether to sell, keep or transfer the property.
- [ ] If keeping a rental or vacation home, check landlord, tax and insurance rules.
- [ ] Keep contact details for a lawyer and tax preparer in the old state.

## Updating titles and accounts

- [ ] **Home deeds.** If you bought a new home, the deed should match your trust or ownership plan.
- [ ] **Trust funding.** New accounts, a new home and new vehicles may be outside your trust until retitled. See the [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist).
- [ ] **Bank and brokerage accounts.** Check names, beneficiary or payable on death designations.
- [ ] **Retirement accounts and life insurance.** Confirm beneficiaries and addresses.
- [ ] **Vehicle titles and registrations.** These change with the move and may allow a transfer on death designation in some states.
- [ ] **Driver's license and voter registration.** These establish residence, which can matter for taxes and court jurisdiction.
- [ ] **Insurance.** Homeowners, auto and umbrella policies.

## New state estate or inheritance tax

The federal estate tax applies only above a high exemption, which is $15 million per person for 2026 <!-- verify -->. Some states add their own estate tax, and a few have an inheritance tax paid by the recipient, with different thresholds, rates and relationship exemptions. A move can reduce, increase or have no effect on state tax. Rules change, so check current ones.

- [ ] Does my new state have an estate tax, inheritance tax or neither?
- [ ] Does my old state?
- [ ] Do I still own property in a state that taxes estates?
- [ ] If my estate is near a state threshold, should I ask a tax professional about options?

## Review timeline

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Before the move** | Make copies of all documents. List property in the old state. Note your signing dates. Ask the old-state attorney whether you should do anything before leaving. |
| **First week** | Store originals safely and tell your agents your new address. Update your phone's emergency contact and medical ID. |
| **First month** | Update addresses on accounts, insurance and beneficiary forms. Check titles on the new home. Update your driver's license. |
| **Within 90 days** | Book a review with an attorney in the new state. Bring the documents and the lists from this checklist. |
| **Within six months** | Sign new or updated documents if advised. Refund or retitle assets for the trust. |
| **First year** | Run the [annual estate plan review](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review). Check that agents still live nearby and accept the roles. |

## Common mistakes

- Assuming the old documents work exactly the same everywhere.
- Moving to a new home and never retitling it into the trust.
- Forgetting property still in the old state.
- Naming an out-of-state executor without checking local rules.
- Using a form from the wrong state for a power of attorney.
- Waiting years to review.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You moved between a community property and a common law state.
- You own real estate in more than one state.
- Your executor, trustee or agents live in different states from you.
- You moved to or from a state with an estate or inheritance tax.
- You have a trust and a new home, or documents signed years ago.
- You use online forms and are unsure they meet your new state's rules.
