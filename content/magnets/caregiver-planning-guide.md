**How to use this guide.** Read it once, then work through the sections in order. You do not have to finish in one sitting. Rules about powers of attorney, guardianship and Medicaid vary by state, so use this as a map for questions to ask, not a set of answers.

## Start with the conversation

Many parents resist the topic because it feels like giving up independence. Others are glad someone raised it. Either way, begin with questions about their wishes, not your worries.

### Openers that tend to work

- "I read something about powers of attorney. Do you have one?"
- "If you got sick, who would you want making decisions?"
- "What would make you feel safest as you get older?"
- "I want to follow your wishes. Can we write them down?"

### Openers that tend to backfire

- "We need to talk about your will."
- "You cannot keep living here alone."
- "What are you going to do with the house?"

Pick a calm time. Keep the first talk to twenty minutes. A second talk is fine.

## The core documents to ask about

You cannot sign these for a parent. A parent with capacity must sign them. Once capacity is lost, options may narrow to court-supervised guardianship, which is slower and costlier.

| Document | What it does | Ask about |
|---|---|---|
| Durable financial power of attorney | Lets a named agent handle money and bills | Who is the agent, is there a backup, does it take effect immediately |
| Healthcare power of attorney (proxy) | Lets a named agent make medical decisions | Does the agent know the parent's wishes |
| Living will | States preferences about life-sustaining treatment | Is it current and shared with doctors |
| HIPAA authorization | Lets doctors talk to named people | Are you on it |
| Will or trust | Directs property after death | Where is it, is it updated |
| Beneficiary forms | Direct accounts outside the will | Are they current |

Read [Powers of Attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and [Healthcare Directives](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) for details.

## Care options and costs

Common options include help at home, adult day programs, assisted living, memory care and nursing homes. Costs vary widely by region and level of care, so call local providers for current numbers.

### Ways families commonly pay

- Savings, pension and Social Security income
- Long-term care insurance, if a policy exists
- Veterans benefits, for eligible veterans
- Selling or renting a home
- Medicaid, which has income and asset limits that vary by state

Medicare generally does not pay for long-term custodial care. Ask a benefits counselor what it does and does not cover. Medicaid planning has a look-back period for gifts, 60 months in most states, so giving away assets without advice can cause a penalty. Try the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) and see [Medicaid and Long-Term Care Planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## Share the work among siblings

One child often carries most of the load. A written plan prevents resentment.

| Role | Who | What it includes |
|---|---|---|
| Medical point person | | Appointments, medications, doctor calls |
| Money manager | | Bills, taxes, records |
| Daily helper | | Visits, meals, errands |
| Out-of-town support | | Calls, research, paying for respite |
| Backup | | Takes over if someone is ill or away |

### Worked example

Three siblings share care for their father. One lives nearby and handles visits and meals. One lives out of state, manages bills and pays for a monthly cleaning service. The third attends doctor visits by video and keeps the shared calendar. They meet monthly for thirty minutes. The nearby sibling keeps a log, and the family agrees she may be reimbursed for mileage.

### Consider a caregiver agreement

Some families sign a written agreement if a child provides care and is paid. Payment can affect taxes and Medicaid eligibility, so ask an attorney before starting.

## Protect yourself too

- Track time and money you spend. Record reimbursements in writing.
- Keep your parent's money separate from yours.
- Know the signs of caregiver stress: sleep loss, irritability, health problems.
- Look into respite care, adult day programs and local Area Agency on Aging services.
- Check whether your employer offers family leave.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for a crisis. Hospital discharge planning is a poor time to write documents.
- Using a generic form without checking state requirements.
- Adding yourself to a parent's accounts or house without advice. It can affect taxes, creditors and Medicaid.
- Assuming siblings agree.
- Forgetting your parent's own wishes, which matter most.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your parent has early memory loss and has not signed a power of attorney.
- A nursing home or assisted living stay may be coming within a few years.
- Your parent owns a home or business and wants to preserve it for family.
- You are being asked to sign as an agent and are unsure of your duties.
- Siblings disagree about care or money.
- Guardianship may be required.
