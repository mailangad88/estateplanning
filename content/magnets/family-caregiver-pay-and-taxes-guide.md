## How to use this guide

Many families care for a parent for free until the cost becomes too much. Paying a family caregiver can be fair and, with paperwork, can protect the parent's Medicaid eligibility. It can also create tax duties no one expected. Tax and Medicaid rules vary and change. Use this as a map, then confirm with a tax professional and an attorney.

## Pay arrangements compared

| Arrangement | How it works | Tax effect | Medicaid effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unpaid care | No money changes hands | None | Nothing to explain |
| Gift of money for care | Parent gives the child cash | Gift tax return only if over the annual exclusion | Counts as a transfer if inside the look-back |
| Written personal care agreement | Pay for services at a fair rate, set in writing before the work | Caregiver reports the pay as income | Can be treated as fair payment, not a gift |
| Paid by a state Medicaid program | Some states pay family through a waiver | Often income to the caregiver | Program rules apply |
| Paid by long-term care insurance | Depends on the policy | Often income | None |
| Parent pays after the fact | Reimbursing past help | Reported as income if payment | Often treated as a gift |

## Who and how much

Most state Medicaid waiver programs have limits on who can be paid. Spouses are often excluded, and some states exclude adult children or limit hours. Private pay families can usually pay anyone, including a sibling, a child or a friend.

A fair rate is what an agency or independent aide would charge for the same services in your area. Overpaying looks like a disguised gift. Underpaying is fine, but then the unpaid part is a gift of time, not money.

## Setting up a written agreement

1. Write down the services: bathing, meals, driving, medication reminders, managing bills.
2. Estimate weekly hours and set an hourly rate that matches local rates.
3. Sign and date the agreement **before** care is paid for. Back pay is the problem.
4. Pay on a regular schedule from the parent's account, not in a lump sum.
5. Keep a daily log of hours and tasks. The Caregiver Daily Log on this site works.
6. Keep bank records of every payment.
7. Revisit the agreement when needs change.

Paying a lump sum in advance for years of care is a classic trap. Medicaid often treats it as a gift. Illinois and most other states look hard at these. <!-- verify: Illinois Medicaid position on prepaid personal care agreements -->

## Tax checklist

- [ ] The caregiver reports caregiver pay as income on their own return
- [ ] If the parent is the employer, decide whether the caregiver is a household employee
- [ ] A household employee generally gets a W-2 and withholding may apply when pay passes an annual threshold set by the IRS <!-- verify: IRS household employer threshold for 2026 -->
- [ ] An independent contractor generally gets a 1099 when the rules are met, but a family caregiver working set hours under the parent's direction is often an employee
- [ ] Check state unemployment and workers' compensation rules for household employees
- [ ] Ask whether the parent needs an employer identification number

When the parent is the one hiring, the parent is usually responsible for the employer side of payroll taxes, not the child.

## Claims on the child's tax return

| Question | What decides it |
|---|---|
| Can I claim my parent as a dependent? | The parent's gross income, whether you provide more than half of their support, and other IRS tests |
| Can I deduct my parent's medical costs? | You may be able to if you pay for a parent who qualifies as your dependent, and total medical costs pass the IRS floor |
| Are nursing home costs deductible? | Medical care in a facility can be deductible; the cost of room and board may be if the main reason for the stay is medical care |

These tests are technical. A tax professional can apply them to the actual numbers. <!-- verify: IRS Publication 502 and 501 current thresholds -->

If your care agreement pays less than your salary, the difference is the true cost.

## Common mistakes

- [ ] Paying in cash with no record
- [ ] Back-paying for care already given
- [ ] Forgetting that the caregiver's pay is income
- [ ] Not asking whether payments could harm a spouse's or parent's Medicaid case
- [ ] A sibling who is not told about the agreement, which can cause conflict

## When to talk to an attorney

- Medicaid is likely within five years
- A parent has dementia and cannot sign a new agreement
- Siblings disagree about pay or about the home
- A power of attorney would be paying the agent
