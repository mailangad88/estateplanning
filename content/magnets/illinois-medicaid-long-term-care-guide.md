## How to use this guide

Medicaid pays for nursing home care for people who meet strict income and asset rules. In Illinois the program is run by the Department of Healthcare and Family Services (HFS), with applications handled through the Department of Human Services (DHS). Dollar limits change every year, so this guide describes the rules and tells you where to confirm the current numbers. It is educational, not advice for your situation.

## Where the numbers come from

Illinois sets a low limit on countable assets for a single applicant, an income test, and a share of couple's assets a spouse at home may keep. Federal rules set a range for the spouse's share, and each state adjusts it every year. Third-party websites often lag or mix up states. Before relying on any figure, ask your DHS caseworker or check the current HFS notice.

- **Asset limit for a single applicant, confirmed on (date):** ____________________
- **Spouse at home may keep (confirmed on):** ____________________
- **Source of the figure:** ____________________

## What counts and what does not

Most bank accounts, investments and extra property count. Some assets are exempt or treated differently.

| Item | Usual treatment |
|---|---|
| Checking, savings, CDs, stocks | Counts |
| Home | Often protected while a spouse lives there; special rules otherwise |
| One vehicle, household goods | Often not counted |
| Prepaid funeral contract | Often protected if properly set up |
| Retirement accounts | Depends on the account and who owns it |
| Life insurance | Cash value may count |

HFS has also said certain farmland and homestead property can qualify as non-countable. If you own a farm or a second property, ask before selling.

## The 60-month look-back

When you apply, Illinois reviews transfers for the previous 60 months. The rules were extended from three to five years in 2012. A gift or a sale below fair market value can create a penalty period, a stretch of time when Medicaid will not pay for nursing home care. In Illinois the penalty starts on the later of the transfer date or the date the person enters a nursing home and is otherwise eligible. To end a penalty, all assets transferred for less than fair market value must be returned.

Use the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) to see which dates matter.

Common problems:

- Adding a child to a bank account, which may count as a gift.
- Paying a relative for care without a written agreement set up before the care.
- Selling a house to a child for less than it is worth.
- Annuities and notes, which Illinois has rules about, including no balloon or deferred payments and no cancellation at death.

## Protecting a spouse at home

If one spouse enters a nursing home, the spouse who stays at home may keep the house, a car and household furnishings. The couple's other assets are counted together, and a spouse at home may keep a protected share. The spouse at home may also keep part of the income, with a monthly allowance. Dependent family members can qualify for an additional amount. The HFS brochure on couples explains these protections, and its dollar figures are dated, so ask for the current ones.

- **Each spouse's income per month:** ____________________
- **Assets in joint names:** ____________________
- **Assets in the spouse at home's name:** ____________________

## Estate recovery basics

After a Medicaid recipient dies, Illinois may seek repayment from the estate for long-term care and related services. HFS says it recovers medical assistance paid after the 55th birthday. Recovery does not go forward when:

- A surviving spouse is living.
- A child under 21 is living.
- A child of any age is blind or permanently disabled.
- The estate is small. HFS has listed $25,000 or less as of July 2022, so confirm the current figure.

HFS also notes a hardship waiver, for example when the property is a family farm or business that provides primary income. Life insurance with a named beneficiary and payable-on-death bank accounts are listed by HFS as excluded from the estate. Public Act 102-1037 restricts the filing of new liens on real property as of June 2, 2022.

## Application documents checklist

- [ ] Photo ID, Social Security card and Medicare and insurance cards
- [ ] Proof of citizenship or lawful status
- [ ] Bank statements for the last 60 months, all accounts
- [ ] Statements for investments, CDs, annuities and retirement accounts
- [ ] Deed, property tax bill and mortgage statement for any real estate
- [ ] Vehicle titles
- [ ] Life insurance policies, with cash values
- [ ] Pension, Social Security and other income letters
- [ ] Funeral contract, if prepaid
- [ ] Records of gifts and large transfers, with who received them and why
- [ ] Power of attorney, guardianship papers or other authority to apply
- [ ] Marriage certificate and spouse's income and asset records, if married

Explain every large withdrawal in writing before the caseworker asks. It can shorten the review.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are thinking of giving away or selling anything before applying.
- A spouse will remain at home, and you want to protect the house and income.
- A parent's money is in trusts, annuities or jointly held accounts.
- A caseworker has issued a penalty period or denial you want reviewed.
- There is a farm, a business or a second property, and an estate recovery question.
