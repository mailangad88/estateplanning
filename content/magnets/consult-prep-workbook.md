**How to use this workbook.** Fill it in pencil, over a few short sittings, not one long evening. Leave a blank where you do not know the answer. A blank tells the attorney something useful, and a wrong guess can send the meeting in the wrong direction. Do not write full account numbers, Social Security numbers or passwords anywhere in this workbook. The last four digits or the name of the institution is plenty. Bring the finished pages to your meeting, and keep them somewhere private, because they describe your whole financial life.

## Why a workbook helps

A first consult often runs 60 to 90 minutes. If half of it goes to remembering the name of your sister's husband or whether the house is in one name or two, there is less time for the real questions. Attorneys see the same pattern again and again: people who arrive with their facts organized spend their time on choices, such as who should serve, how to protect a child, and what a trust would change.

This workbook does not make decisions for you. It collects what you know and surfaces what you do not. Rules vary by state, so the attorney will tell you which of your answers matter under your state's law.

## Part 2: Who would you name?

Most estate plans fill the same handful of roles. Write your first choice and at least one backup for each. If you have no idea yet, write "unsure" and note what is stopping you. The attorney can talk through it.

| Role | What they do | First choice | Backup | Backup 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Executor (personal representative) | Carries out your will and handles probate | | | |
| Successor trustee | Manages a trust if you cannot or after you die | | | |
| Agent under financial power of attorney | Pays bills and manages money if you cannot | | | |
| Health care agent | Makes medical decisions if you cannot speak | | | |
| Guardian for minor children | Raises your children if both parents die | | | |
| Trustee for children's money | Manages money held for a child | | | |
| Pet caretaker | Takes your animals | | | |
| Digital executor | Handles accounts, email and photos | | | |

Some quick checks as you fill this in:

- Does the person live nearby, or could they act from a distance?
- Would they be willing? Have you asked?
- Would two of your choices clash with each other? A co-agent arrangement can stall if they disagree.
- Is your first choice also the person you would name for everything? Many people spread roles around on purpose, since the best caregiver is not always the best money manager.

If you want a deeper look, the [executor worksheet](/checklists/choosing-an-executor-worksheet) and [guide to choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee) go through the tradeoffs.

## Part 5: Goals and worries

Write quickly and honestly. Nobody grades this.

1. **If I died next month, what would I most want to be true for the people I leave behind?** ______________________
2. **What am I most afraid will go wrong (a fight, a lawsuit, taxes, a child mishandling money, a delay)?** ______________________
3. **Who do I want to be taken care of first?** ______________________
4. **Is there anyone I want to treat differently from the others, and why?** ______________________
5. **Do I want any gifts to charity or to a school, church or cause?** ______________________
6. **What do I want to happen if I become unable to manage my own affairs?** ______________________
7. **Do I care about keeping things private, avoiding probate, or reducing cost and delay?** ______________________
8. **Are there specific items (a house, a ring, a boat) that matter more than their price?** ______________________
9. **Anything about my family situation I am nervous to say out loud?** ______________________

Number 9 is worth taking seriously. Attorneys keep what you say confidential, and the sensitive facts, such as an estranged child, a second family or a substance problem, are the ones that shape a good plan.

## Part 6: Questions for the attorney

Pre-written questions many people find useful. Circle the ones that apply and add your own.

- What do you recommend for my situation, a will, a trust or both, and what would change that advice?
- What will this cost, what does the fee include, and are there ongoing charges?
- Who prepares the documents, and who will I be working with?
- How do you handle signing: witnesses, notary, and timing?
- What happens to my plan if I move to another state?
- Which of my assets will go through probate under my state's rules?
- How often should I review the plan, and do you offer that?
- Where will the originals be stored, and can my family get them?
- What should I do about my beneficiary designations?

## Part 7: What to bring

The [documents to gather checklist](/checklists/documents-to-gather-before-your-consult) has a longer version. The short version:

- [ ] Photo ID
- [ ] This completed workbook
- [ ] Copies, not originals, of any existing estate documents
- [ ] The most recent statement for each major account, with account numbers covered or redacted
- [ ] A copy of your deed or a note of how your house is titled
- [ ] Life insurance and retirement plan beneficiary pages
- [ ] Business agreements, if you own part of a business
- [ ] A list of the people in Part 2, with phone numbers and addresses

Leave at home: passwords, full Social Security numbers, and original documents unless the attorney asks.

## Common mistakes

- **Guessing at titles and beneficiaries.** Wrong guesses mislead. "Check" is a perfectly good answer.
- **Choosing agents out of guilt.** Ask who would do the job well, not who would be offended.
- **Skipping the backup column.** Your first choice may be unable or unwilling when the time comes.
- **Treating a will as the whole plan.** Beneficiary forms, titling and a power of attorney do work the will cannot.
- **Waiting until the pages are perfect.** A half-filled workbook still saves time.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own property in more than one state or have lived abroad.
- You have a blended family, a child with special needs, or a family member who may contest.
- You own a business, farm or rental property.
- Your estate may be near the federal or a state estate tax threshold (the federal exemption is $15 million per person as of 2026<!-- verify -->, and some states tax at lower levels).
- You or a loved one has a new diagnosis and time matters.
- You already have documents but have had a marriage, divorce, birth, death or move since signing them.
