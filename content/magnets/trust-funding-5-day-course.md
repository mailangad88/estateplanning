A trust only controls what it holds. This five-day course helps you check what your trust actually owns. Each lesson ends with one small task. It is general education, not legal advice, and rules vary by state.

## Day 1: Why a signed trust can still be empty

Signing a trust creates the container. Funding means moving your assets into it, usually by changing the title or owner. If that step is skipped, the trust may hold almost nothing, and the assets could still go through probate.

Many people sign their documents, put them in a drawer and stop. That is very common, and it is fixable.

Think of three ways assets can end up:

- **Titled in the trust:** the trustee, usually you, is the owner on paper.
- **Passing by beneficiary form:** retirement accounts and insurance, which do not need retitling.
- **Still in your own name:** these are the ones to catch.

Some trusts include a pour-over will, which directs leftover assets into the trust at death. That is a useful backup, but those assets may still have to go through probate first. The [funding your trust guide](/guides/funding-your-trust) explains why.

Before changing anything, find your documents. Locate the trust itself and look for a "schedule of assets" or a list your attorney prepared. Check the exact name of the trust and the trustee, because institutions often require them to be written precisely.

If you cannot find a schedule, do not panic. Many trusts rely on a general statement that assets are meant to be held in trust, and that language alone may not move titled property. Your attorney's office may still have a file. Even a short call to ask what was funded at signing can save you a lot of guesswork later.

**Today's task:** Gather your trust document and write down the trust's full name and date. Then make a list of everything you own: home, accounts, vehicles, business interests and valuables. Use the [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist) as a template.

## Day 2: Your home and other real estate

For many families, the house is the largest asset. A trust usually owns it through a new deed. Without that deed, the house is still in your name.

The usual steps:

1. Find your current deed, or request a copy from the county recorder.
2. Ask your attorney or a title professional to prepare a deed moving the property to the trustee of your trust.
3. Sign it as required, often with a notary, and record it with the county.
4. Check what is required in your state, since some require extra forms.

Things worth checking before you move a home:

- **Mortgage:** Federal law generally protects transfers of a primary home into a revocable trust from triggering the loan due clause, but ask your lender and attorney.
- **Homeowner's insurance:** Ask your insurer to add the trust as an insured party, so coverage does not lapse.
- **Property tax rules and exemptions:** Some states or counties treat a transfer differently, and some require a form so a homestead exemption continues.
- **Title insurance:** Ask whether the existing policy continues.

Rules vary by state, and mistakes can be costly, so a short attorney conversation is common here.

If you own real estate in more than one state, each property is handled under its own state's rules.

A common mistake is recording a deed without telling the insurer or lender, which can cause surprises years later. Another is using a form copied from the internet that does not fit your state. Property taxes may also be affected by a transfer in some places, so ask first. A small upfront check often costs far less than fixing a bad deed.

**Today's task:** Find the deed for each property. Write down how title is held today and whether a deed to the trust has been recorded.

## Day 3: Bank and brokerage accounts

Bank and brokerage accounts are often the easiest to fund. Usually it is a short form and a call or branch visit.

Typically, you have two options for a bank account:

- **Retitle it** into the name of the trust, so the trustee is the owner.
- **Name the trust as the payable-on-death beneficiary,** which keeps the account in your name while you live. See [transfer on death and payable on death](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).

Each approach has tradeoffs, and banks differ in what they will accept.

For a brokerage account, ask the firm for its trust account forms. They often require a copy of the trust or a certification of trust, which is a short summary document. Do not send the whole trust unless asked.

Some practical points:

- Ask whether account numbers, tax reporting and automatic transfers will change.
- A revocable trust usually uses your Social Security number while you are alive. Confirm with your tax professional.
- Keep a record of the date each account was changed and who confirmed it.
- Do not overlook small accounts, old savings accounts and safe deposit boxes.

Some people use joint accounts with a child as a shortcut, but this can expose the account to the child's creditors or divorce and can undo your plan. Retitling to the trust keeps you in control. Ask the institution to confirm in writing that the change is complete, and keep that confirmation with your trust documents.

**Today's task:** List every bank and brokerage account. Call or visit your institution and ask for its trust retitling process. Note which ones you have moved and which are still open.

## Day 4: Retirement accounts and other assets

Retirement accounts are different. Do not retitle an IRA, 401(k) or similar account into a trust. Moving it can be treated as a withdrawal, which may trigger income tax. Instead, the account stays in your name and passes by the beneficiary form.

The real decision is who you name:

- A spouse or person directly
- A trust, which can give you more control over how funds are paid out
- A mix, with a contingent beneficiary behind the first choice

Naming a trust as beneficiary has tax and drafting rules, and it needs the trust to be written to fit. This is a good topic for your attorney and tax advisor. The [beneficiary designations guide](/guides/beneficiary-designations) gives background.

Other assets also need a look:

- **Life insurance:** Usually stays outside the trust, with the form updated.
- **Vehicles:** Many people leave these out. Some states have simple transfer methods.
- **Business interests:** Check any operating agreement and talk to your attorney first.
- **Valuables and personal items:** An assignment document can move these to the trust.
- **Digital assets and cryptocurrency:** Record how they are held and who can reach them.

Watch the annual required distribution rules on retirement accounts, since they affect how funds are paid out to heirs and have changed over time. A tax advisor can confirm what applies now. Also remember that a Roth account, a traditional account and an employer plan can each have different options, so check each one separately.

**Today's task:** For each retirement account and insurance policy, write down the current beneficiaries. Mark any that are missing, outdated, or do not match your plan.

## Day 5: Keep it funded, and where to get help

Funding is not a one-time job. New accounts, a new home or an inheritance can all end up in your own name by accident.

Build a few habits:

- When you open an account, ask for it to be opened in the trust's name.
- When you buy property, tell your closing agent about the trust ahead of time.
- Review your asset list once a year, and after any big life event.
- Keep your trust documents and funding list together, and tell your successor trustee where they are.

Here is a simple review. Take your list from day one and mark each asset: in the trust, by beneficiary form, or still personal. Anything personal and large is worth a second look.

If you have gaps you are unsure about, you can bring your list to an attorney. The [plan finder](/plan-finder) can set up a consult. Many people find a one-hour review is enough, and there is no pressure to take the next step.

If you ever sell a property or close an account, update your list at the same time so it stays accurate. A funding list that is a few years stale can be almost as confusing as none. Keep a copy somewhere your successor trustee can reach, and mention it in your letter of instruction so they know where to look.

**Today's task:** Add a yearly reminder to your calendar to review your funding list. Then use the [plan review reminder tool](/tools/plan-review-reminder) if you want an email nudge.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own real estate in more than one state
- You have a mortgage, a business or a large retirement account
- You want a trust to be the beneficiary of retirement funds
- You are unsure how an institution wants title written
- Your trust was signed many years ago or in another state
