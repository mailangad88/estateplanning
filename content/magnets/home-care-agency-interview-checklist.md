**How to use this checklist.** Start with Part 1 to define the job in writing. Use Part 2 to choose between an agency and a private hire, then interview at least two candidates or agencies using Parts 3 to 5. Ask for all costs and terms in writing. This is general information, not legal advice or tax advice, and employment rules vary by state.

## Part 1: Write down the job

- **Tasks needed (companionship, meals, bathing, medicine reminders, transfers, light housekeeping, driving):** ______________________
- **Days and hours needed, and whether overnight:** ______________________
- **Special skills (dementia, lifting, diabetes, wound care):** ______________________
- **Language, pets, smoking, home layout:** ______________________
- **Budget per week, and how long it can last:** ______________________
- **Who pays (parent's income and savings, long-term care insurance, VA benefit, Medicaid program, family):** ______________________

Non-medical home care, sometimes called personal care or home care, helps with daily living. Home health care is skilled nursing or therapy ordered by a doctor. Medicare may pay for limited home health when a doctor orders it, but generally does not pay for ongoing non-medical help. Ask the doctor which one applies.

## Part 2: Agency or private hire?

| Question | Agency | Private hire |
|---|---|---|
| Hourly cost | Usually higher | Usually lower |
| Who is the employer? | The agency | You or your parent |
| Payroll taxes, workers compensation | Agency handles | Family may owe them |
| Background checks and training | Agency usually does | Family does |
| Backup if the caregiver is sick | Agency sends someone else | Family finds a fill-in |
| Choice of caregiver | Less control | More control |
| Liability if there is an injury or theft | Agency's insurance and bonding | May fall on the family |
| Continuity | Staff turnover is possible | One person, built trust |

Neither is always right. An agency fits families who want less paperwork and need reliable cover. Private hire can suit families who want one trusted person and are ready to act as employers.

Some states license home care agencies and others do not. Check your state's rules and whether the agency you are considering is licensed.

## Part 3: Agency interview questions

### Company basics

- [ ] How long has the agency operated, and is it licensed in this state? License number: ______________________
- [ ] Is the agency insured, and are caregivers bonded? Ask for proof.
- [ ] Are caregivers employees or contractors? (Employees usually mean the agency is responsible for taxes and insurance.)
- [ ] Who supervises caregivers, and how often do they visit the home?
- [ ] Can we see a sample care plan and daily note?

### Screening and training

- [ ] What background checks are done: criminal, driving, abuse registry, references?
- [ ] What training do caregivers receive, including dementia, lifting and infection control?
- [ ] Do they do a drug screen and verify work eligibility?
- [ ] How are caregivers matched to my parent?
- [ ] Can we meet the caregiver before the first shift?

### Service and reliability

- [ ] What is the minimum number of hours per visit? Many agencies set a minimum.
- [ ] What happens if a caregiver calls in sick? How quickly is a replacement found?
- [ ] How do we reach someone on nights and weekends?
- [ ] How many different caregivers will come in a typical month?
- [ ] How do you handle complaints, and can we ask for a different caregiver?
- [ ] What tasks are not allowed (lifting, giving medicine, driving the client)?

### Money

- [ ] Hourly rate by shift, with overnight, weekend and holiday rates: ______________________
- [ ] Minimum hours, and cancellation notice: ______________________
- [ ] Assessment or setup fee, mileage, supplies: ______________________
- [ ] How often rates increase: ______________________
- [ ] Do they accept long-term care insurance, and do they help with claims?
- [ ] Do they take payment from Medicaid waiver programs or the VA?

## Part 4: Private hire interview questions

- [ ] Tell me about your last job and why it ended. Can I call two references?
- [ ] What experience do you have with my parent's condition?
- [ ] How would you handle a fall, a refusal to take medicine, or a bad day?
- [ ] Are you comfortable with the tasks in my job description?
- [ ] Do you have reliable transportation and a driver's license, and are you willing to be covered on our car insurance?
- [ ] Would you agree to a background check, and a trial period?
- [ ] What are your hours, rate and notice period?
- [ ] Are you legally authorized to work in the U.S.? (Employers must complete the federal Form I-9.)
- [ ] What is your plan if you cannot come?

Trust your own sense. A person who respects your parent, listens, and asks about their routines is a good sign. A person who dodges questions or pushes for cash and no paperwork is a warning.

## Part 5: Contract and paperwork

### For agencies

- [ ] Read the service agreement at home. Look for rate increases, cancellation rules, termination terms, and arbitration clauses.
- [ ] Confirm who is the client: your parent or you? Do you sign as agent under a power of attorney?
- [ ] Ask about a no-hire clause. Some agencies charge a fee if you hire their caregiver directly later.
- [ ] Keep a copy of the care plan and ask to update it after changes.

### For private hires

- [ ] Write a simple employment agreement: duties, hours, pay rate, overtime, paid time off, notice, house rules, and what to do in an emergency.
- [ ] Complete Form I-9 and a W-4 for the employee.
- [ ] Learn the household employer rules. In the United States, a person who hires a household worker, including a caregiver, who is an employee rather than an independent contractor may have to pay Social Security and Medicare taxes and possibly unemployment taxes once wages pass a yearly amount that changes. See IRS Publication 926 for the current threshold. Someone who works in your parent's home, on your parent's schedule, using your parent's tools, is usually an employee.
- [ ] Ask your state about minimum wage, overtime and workers compensation. Home care workers have special rules in many states.
- [ ] Ask your homeowner or renter's insurance agent whether the policy covers a worker injured in the home. Workers compensation coverage may be required in your state.
- [ ] Pay by check or bank transfer, and keep records of every payment. Use a payroll service if you are unsure.
- [ ] Do not pay cash under the table. It can create tax problems for the worker and for you, and make benefits applications harder.

## Part 6: Start-up and safety

- [ ] Keep valuables, cash, checkbooks and medicines locked or out of sight.
- [ ] Share a written routine, a medical information sheet and emergency numbers.
- [ ] Check in by phone or camera, where the law allows. Rules on recording vary by state, so tell the caregiver and ask your attorney.
- [ ] Keep a daily log of tasks and notes.
- [ ] Review the first two weeks, then monthly.
- [ ] Watch for signs of trouble: missing items, new "loans," isolation from family, caregiver asking to be added to accounts or the will.
- [ ] Do not make the caregiver a joint account holder or a beneficiary as a thank-you. Ask an attorney how to reward them properly.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You plan to hire privately and want help with an employment agreement and tax obligations.
- Your parent may need Medicaid in the future, and you are paying family or friends for care.
- A caregiver has been left a gift or has access to your parent's accounts.
- A power of attorney is not in place and your parent can no longer manage their own affairs.
- You suspect theft or abuse by a caregiver.
