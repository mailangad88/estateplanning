## How to use this guide

Price is usually the first question people have and the last one anyone answers clearly. This guide explains how pricing works so you can compare quotes. It does not quote prices. Fees vary widely by state, by firm and by how complicated your situation is, so use any figure you hear as a starting point for questions, not a standard.

## Two common ways attorneys charge

### Flat fee

A flat fee is a set price for a defined package, such as a will and powers of attorney, or a trust with its pour-over will. You know the cost before you start.

- **Good for:** Standard plans with a clear scope.
- **Watch for:** What the package leaves out, and what happens if your needs change halfway through.

### Hourly

The attorney bills for time spent, often at a stated rate with a retainer paid first. This can suit unusual, uncertain or contested matters.

- **Good for:** Complex situations where the work cannot be fixed in advance.
- **Watch for:** Estimates, billing increments and who handles routine tasks. Ask for a cap or a written estimate.

Some firms mix the two. For example, they charge a flat fee for core documents and hourly rates for extra work. Ask the firm to write down how it works.

## What drives the price

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Will versus trust | A trust package typically includes more documents and more drafting. See [will versus trust](/compare/will-vs-trust) |
| Number of documents | Powers of attorney, health care documents and extra trusts add work |
| Couples versus individuals | Joint plans may share work, though each person's choices still need attention |
| Family situation | Blended families, a child with special needs or an estranged heir often require custom drafting |
| Assets | Real estate, business interests or property in multiple states add complexity |
| Tax planning | Larger estates may need extra planning, see [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes) |
| Funding help | Retitling the home and accounts into a trust takes time. Some firms include it and others charge for it |
| Update and review policy | Some firms include updates for a period, and others bill each change |
| Your location | Costs of doing business and local practice vary |
| How prepared you are | Organized information saves time, especially on an hourly bill |

## What is usually included

Packages differ, so confirm each item below rather than assuming.

- [ ] A consult to learn your goals and family situation
- [ ] Drafting of your will or trust
- [ ] Financial power of attorney
- [ ] Health care power of attorney or directive
- [ ] HIPAA release
- [ ] A signing meeting with witnesses or a notary, as your state requires
- [ ] Instructions on beneficiary forms
- [ ] Help moving assets into the trust (funding)
- [ ] A copy of your documents in print and digital form
- [ ] Follow-up questions after signing
- [ ] Updates for a stated period

Attorneys do more than prepare forms. They ask questions that expose problems, such as a beneficiary form that conflicts with your will, or a home titled in a way that defeats your plan.

## Other options: DIY and online

Online services and do-it-yourself forms are real options, and they suit some people.

| Option | Often works well when | Tradeoffs to weigh |
|---|---|---|
| Fill-in forms or a stationery kit | Situation is very simple and you have checked your state's rules | You get no legal advice, and mistakes may not show up until after death |
| Online will or trust service | You want something fast and affordable, and your needs fit common templates | Little room for unusual wishes, and limited help if a question comes up |
| Online service with attorney review | You want some human review | Scope of review varies, so ask who the attorney is and what they check |
| Attorney-prepared plan | You own property, have a blended family, a business or special needs, or want tailored advice | Costs more up front and takes more time |

Common issues with templates, regardless of provider:

- Signing rules are strict and differ by state. A will signed incorrectly may be invalid. See [what makes a will valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid).
- A trust only works if you transfer assets into it.
- Forms do not usually check how your accounts are titled or who is on your beneficiary forms.
- Nobody may notice an error until it is too late to fix.

None of this means a template always fails. It means the savings can be real and the risks can be too. The [online will versus estate attorney](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney) comparison goes deeper.

## Looking at total cost, not just the fee

Consider the costs on both sides of the ledger.

- **Costs of planning:** Fees, notary or recording fees, your time and any retitling charges.
- **Costs of not planning or planning poorly:** Probate expenses, delays, family disputes, taxes that careful planning might reduce and a court choosing guardians or agents for you.

You can estimate part of the second list with the [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator). It is a tool, not a promise, and results depend on state rules.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own a home, a business or property in more than one state
- You have a blended family, minor children or a family member with a disability
- Your estate may be large enough to raise tax questions
- You have already used a template and want it reviewed
- You are not sure whether you want a will or a trust
- You want one person to explain the whole plan, not just sign forms
