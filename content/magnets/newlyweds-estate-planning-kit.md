## How to use this kit

Print it and work through it together, one sitting per section. Most of the tasks are paperwork, not legal work. The legal parts, such as wills and property titles, are where an attorney licensed in your state helps most. This kit is educational and is not advice for your situation.

## Part 1: Beneficiary updates after marriage

Retirement accounts and life insurance go to whoever is named on the form. Your will does not override that. Many people sign up for a job at 24 and name a parent, then never look again.

Fill in this table for each of you. Add rows as needed.

| Account or policy | Current beneficiary | Primary after update | Backup after update | Date updated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 401(k) or 403(b) | | | | |
| IRA or Roth IRA | | | | |
| Life insurance (work) | | | | |
| Life insurance (personal) | | | | |
| Bank or brokerage (POD or TOD) | | | | |
| Health savings account | | | | |

Some points to keep in mind:

- Many workplace retirement plans give a spouse automatic rights. Naming someone else may need your spouse's written consent.
- Always name a backup. If your spouse and you are in the same accident, the form with no backup may send money through probate.
- If you want a trust or children from an earlier relationship to receive money, ask an attorney how to word the form.
- Keep a copy of each confirmation page. Forms get lost.

The guide on [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) explains the mechanics in more detail.

## Part 2: Name changes on accounts

If one or both of you changed your name, the legal paperwork follows a rough order. Rules and processing times vary, so check each agency's current instructions.

- [ ] Certified copies of the marriage certificate (order several)
- [ ] Social Security record updated first
- [ ] Driver license or state ID
- [ ] Passport
- [ ] Employer payroll and benefits records
- [ ] Banks, credit cards and brokerage accounts
- [ ] Retirement accounts and insurance policies
- [ ] Mortgage, lease and property records
- [ ] Voter registration
- [ ] Email signature, professional licenses and online accounts

A name mismatch between an account and a beneficiary form can slow a claim later. Use the same legal name everywhere.

## Part 3: Joint versus separate property basics

How your assets are owned affects what happens if one of you dies, and what happens in a divorce. The categories below are general. Rules vary by state, and a few states follow community property rules.

| Type of asset | Common treatment | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Owned before marriage, kept separate | Often stays separate | Did we ever mix it into a joint account? |
| Earned during marriage | Often shared in some form | Does our state use community property or another system? |
| Gift or inheritance to one of you | Often separate if kept apart | Where is it held? |
| Home bought together | Depends on how title is held | Joint tenancy, tenancy by the entirety or something else? |
| Debts | Often depends on whose name and state law | Who signed? |

Mixing separate money into a joint account can blur the line. Some couples do this on purpose. Others want to keep things separate and use a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement. Both are legitimate choices, and an attorney can explain the tradeoffs where you live.

Write down where you stand:

- **Our home is titled as:** ______________________
- **Accounts we want fully joint:** ______________________
- **Accounts we want kept separate:** ______________________
- **Anything one of us owned before the wedding that we want protected:** ______________________

See [joint ownership versus a trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust) for how titling interacts with probate.

## Part 4: First wills together

A will names who receives your property, who serves as executor and, if you have children, who would raise them. Without one, state law decides, and the result may not match what you would choose.

Couples often sign matching wills that leave everything to each other, then name the same backup people. That is a reasonable starting point, but check these items first:

- **Executor:** ______________________ Backup: ______________________
- **If we both die, our property goes to:** ______________________
- **Specific gifts (family heirlooms, a car, a pet):** ______________________
- **Anything one of us wants to leave to children from a prior relationship:** ______________________

Also consider the two documents that matter during life. A financial power of attorney lets someone pay bills and manage accounts if you cannot. A health care directive names who speaks with doctors for you. Being married does not always give your spouse that authority automatically. Read the guides on [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will).

## Part 5: Your timeline

### First month

- [ ] Order certified marriage certificates
- [ ] Each of you list every account, policy and debt in one shared document
- [ ] Update workplace beneficiary forms and health insurance
- [ ] Decide where to store important documents and who knows the location
- [ ] Start any name change with Social Security

### Within six months

- [ ] Finish name changes on accounts and IDs
- [ ] Update beneficiaries on IRAs, personal life insurance and bank or brokerage accounts
- [ ] Review life insurance amounts. If one of you earns more, or you plan to buy a home or have children, it may be time to revisit them
- [ ] Sign powers of attorney and health care directives
- [ ] Schedule a consult to discuss wills or a trust
- [ ] Decide on a postnuptial agreement conversation, if it applies

### By the end of the first year

- [ ] Sign wills (or a trust) that reflect the marriage
- [ ] Retitle the home or accounts if you decided to
- [ ] Review the whole plan after any big change: a home purchase, a baby, a new job or a move to another state
- [ ] Put a yearly review date on both calendars

## Part 6: Conversation prompts

Money and family talks go better with a script. Pick one prompt per evening.

- If one of us died tomorrow, what would the other want to happen with our home?
- Who would each of us trust to make medical decisions if we could not?
- How do we feel about leaving money to parents, siblings or charity?
- If we have kids or plan to, who would we want as guardians?
- Is there a family business, heirloom or property that should stay in one family line?
- How much do we want the other person to know about our passwords and accounts?
- What do we want at the end of life: medical wishes, burial or cremation, a service?

Write down any disagreements and bring them to your consult. Attorneys handle these conversations every week.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Either of you has children from a prior relationship
- One of you owns a business, rental property or real estate in more than one state
- One or both of you has a large retirement account, a pension or significant assets
- You want a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement
- One of you is not a U.S. citizen, which can change tax and ownership rules
- You live in or have moved from a community property state
