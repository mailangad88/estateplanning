**How to use this guide.** Read the first two sections to understand the problem. Then fill in the inventory and compare the tools. This guide explains concepts only. State laws on probate, deeds and taxes differ, and an attorney licensed in the state where the property sits should confirm the details.

## The core problem: real estate follows the law of where it sits

Probate is the court process for passing property that has no named owner after death. A court in your home state can generally handle your personal property, such as bank accounts and cars. But land is tied to the place where it is located. If you own real estate in another state and it is in your name alone, a court in that state typically has to approve the transfer.

That second proceeding is called ancillary probate. It runs alongside the main one in your home state. It may mean:

- A second court filing, with its own forms, fees and timeline.
- A local attorney in that state, since many states restrict who can represent an estate in court.
- A local personal representative, or an out-of-state executor who must qualify there.
- Waiting for both proceedings to finish before a family can close or sell.
- Another round of public records.

If the family expects to sell the cabin quickly, a second probate can be a real delay. The cost depends on the state, the value of the property and how the court handles it.

## What does and does not need ancillary probate

| How the out-of-state property is held | Does it need ancillary probate? |
|---|---|
| In your name alone | Usually yes |
| In a revocable trust, deed recorded | Usually no |
| Joint tenancy with right of survivorship | Usually no, though a survivor may need to record paperwork |
| Tenancy by the entirety (married couples, in states that offer it) | Usually no |
| In an LLC you own | Usually no, since you own an interest in the LLC, which is personal property |
| Transfer on death deed (in states that allow it) | Usually no, if the state recognizes it and it is recorded before death |
| Real estate subject to a beneficiary designation | Rare, though some states have laws on this |

Terms differ, and states are not required to honor another state's deed form. Ask the attorney where the property is.

## Your options

### Option 1: A revocable living trust

You sign a deed moving the out-of-state property into your trust. The trustee can then sell or transfer it after your death without a court. This is the most common tool when a person owns more than one property. Pros: broad, flexible, covers disability. Cons: you must record a deed in each state, and some states charge a fee or require special forms.

### Option 2: A transfer on death deed

Some states let you name a beneficiary on a recorded deed, so the property passes at death without probate. Pros: cheap, simple. Cons: not every state allows one, requirements differ, and it covers just that property. Check that the other state's law applies to your property, not your home state's.

### Option 3: Joint ownership

Adding a spouse or child as co-owner can avoid probate, but it can expose the property to the co-owner's creditors, change your tax position and give up control. A transfer to a child is a gift that may need a tax return. Use it carefully.

### Option 4: An LLC or similar entity

Some owners of vacation property or rentals place them in a limited liability company, then transfer their interest to a trust. This can add liability protection and limit probate to personal property. It also adds fees, annual filings and complexity. A [vacation home succession guide](/free/vacation-home-succession-guide) covers family cabins in more detail.

### Option 5: Do nothing and accept ancillary probate

For a low-value lot or a simple plot, it can sometimes cost less to accept a second probate than to restructure. Small estate shortcuts may also exist in the other state. Rules vary by state.

## Taxes to keep in view

- **Federal estate tax.** For 2026, the federal exemption is $15,000,000 per person. Most families are below it. Real estate counts toward the total, wherever it sits.
- **State estate or inheritance tax.** Some states tax estates below the federal level. Some tax real property inside their borders even for nonresidents. Check each state where you own land.
- **Property tax.** A transfer can trigger a reassessment in some states, though transfers to a revocable trust are often excluded. Ask.
- **Income tax basis.** Property inherited at death usually gets a new tax basis. Gifting during life may not. Compare both before adding a child to a deed.

## What to tell your executor and family

- [ ] Every state where I own property, with the county.
- [ ] Which properties are in a trust, and which are not.
- [ ] Where deeds, insurance and tax bills are.
- [ ] The name of an attorney in each state, if I have one.
- [ ] Whether the property should be kept, rented or sold, and who decides.
- [ ] How the carrying costs, such as taxes and insurance, will be paid while it is settled.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own real estate in a state that is different from where you live.
- You want to retitle property in another state into a trust.
- You inherited out-of-state land and are not sure what to do.
- The property is held with siblings or others and no one has a written agreement.
- You are thinking of an LLC for rental or vacation property.
- You are moving, and the home you leave becomes a second property.
