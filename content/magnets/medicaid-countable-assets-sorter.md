## How to use this worksheet

Medicaid for nursing home care looks at what you own, not just what you earn. A caseworker sorts each item into **countable** (it counts toward the asset limit) or **exempt** (set aside). This worksheet lets you do that sort first. Rules vary by state, and the exact limits change, so the dollar limit is left blank for you to fill in from your state's Medicaid office. It is general education, not legal advice.

## The sort in one picture

1. List every asset with its owner and its value.
2. Mark each one countable, exempt or unclear.
3. Add up the countable column.
4. Compare the total with your state's limit.
5. Take the unclear items to an attorney before you spend or move anything.

## Quick sort table

| Asset | Usually counted? | What can change the answer |
|---|---|---|
| Checking and savings | Counted | Whose name is on it |
| CDs, stocks, bonds, brokerage | Counted | Nothing much; they are liquid |
| Main home | Often exempt | Intent to return, equity cap, spouse living there |
| Second home or vacation property | Counted | Whether it is actually the main home |
| One vehicle | Often exempt | Some states cap the value or require use for the household |
| Extra vehicles, boats, RVs | Counted | Market value less any loan |
| IRA, 401(k) | Often counted | Whether the owner is taking required payouts, state rules |
| Cash-value life insurance | Counted above a small face value | Term insurance has no cash value |
| Burial plot and funeral plan | Often exempt | The plan must usually be irrevocable |
| Personal items and furniture | Exempt | Valuable collections may count |
| Annuity | Depends | Whether it can be cashed out and who is the beneficiary |
| Trust | Depends | Who can reach the money, and if it can be revoked |

## Items that cause the most confusion

### Home and home equity

A house is often exempt while the person lives there or intends to return. Many states cap how much equity is protected. The cap adjusts yearly. <!-- verify: the current Illinois home equity limit -->

A second home does not get that protection. It counts, so owning one can block eligibility until it is sold or properly transferred.

### Vehicles

Most states set aside one car. A second car, a boat or a motorcycle typically counts at market value less the loan. So the answer to whether Medicaid can take a car is usually no for one car and yes for extras that push you over the limit.

### IRAs and retirement accounts

Some states count the full balance. Others treat an account in payout status as income instead of an asset. This is one of the most state-specific rules in the whole process, so ask before cashing anything out. Cashing out can also trigger income tax.

### Life insurance

Term insurance has no cash value and usually does not count. Whole life with cash value counts once the combined face value passes a small threshold. A policy can sometimes be converted into an exempt funeral plan.

### Joint accounts

If you share an account with a child, the caseworker will often assume all of it belongs to the applicant unless records show the child put in their own money. A child's contributions need paper proof.

### Inheritance and reverse mortgage money

An inheritance counts as soon as you receive it. Money from a reverse mortgage counts if you keep it in the bank past the month you receive it. Rental income counts as income each month, and the rental property itself counts as an asset.

### Trusts

A trust you can revoke counts. A trust you cannot touch, set up long enough ago, may not. Trusts created within the look-back window can trigger a penalty. See the transfer penalty workbook on this site for how that works.

## Common mistakes

- [ ] Forgetting that a spouse's accounts are usually counted too
- [ ] Spending money on gifts instead of items for the applicant, which can trigger a penalty
- [ ] Cashing out an IRA before learning whether it counts at all
- [ ] Leaving a second car in the applicant's name
- [ ] Not keeping statements that show whose money is in a joint account

## When to talk to an attorney

- A home, trust, annuity or business is on your list
- The total is close to your state's limit
- You are thinking of paying down a debt, remodeling or buying something to get under the limit
- Your spouse is not applying and you want to know what they keep
