**How to use this audit.** Work through the steps with your account statements beside you. The aim is simple: for each account, confirm that the person named is the person you want today. Rules differ by account type, plan and state, and this is general information, not tax advice.

## Why beneficiary forms matter more than people think

For many accounts, the beneficiary form beats your will. Retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities, and accounts with payable-on-death (POD) or transfer-on-death (TOD) designations pass by contract to the named person. Courts have repeatedly enforced old forms.

In Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan (555 U.S. 285, 2009), the U.S. Supreme Court held that a plan administrator properly paid an ex-wife named on a 401(k) form, even though a divorce decree said she had waived her interest, because the plan documents controlled. In Egelhoff v. Egelhoff (532 U.S. 141, 2001), the Court held that a state law automatically revoking an ex-spouse's designation on divorce was preempted by ERISA as applied to life insurance and pension plan benefits.

Retirement accounts and life insurance can be the largest part of what people leave, so a mistake here is rarely small.

### Step 3: Run the red-flag questions

For each account, answer these. A "yes" to any is a reason to look closer.

1. **No beneficiary named, or "estate" named?** Many accounts then default to your estate, which means probate, or to the plan's default order. For retirement accounts, naming the estate usually shortens the payout period and can cost heirs tax benefits. See IRS Publication 590-B.
2. **Form older than 5 years, or created before a major event?** Compare against marriages, divorces, births, deaths and moves.
3. **Ex-spouse named, or no update after divorce?** Many states revoke ex-spouse designations by statute on some accounts, but federal law preempts that for many employer plans (see Egelhoff). Assume the form controls.
4. **Is a person who has died still named?** If both primary and contingent have died, the plan default applies.
5. **Minor child named directly?** An insurer will generally not pay a large sum to a minor, and a court-appointed guardian of the estate may be required. A trust or UTMA custodian can solve it.
6. **A beneficiary who receives public benefits (SSI, Medicaid)?** A direct inheritance can cut off benefits. A properly drafted special needs trust can be named instead.
7. **Percentages that do not total 100?** Shares must add up. Watch for rounding.
8. **Per stirpes or per capita?** If a named child dies before you, does that child's share go to the grandchildren (per stirpes) or to the surviving siblings (per capita)? Choose on purpose, since forms default one way or the other.
9. **Do the forms contradict your will or trust?** For example, your will splits everything among three children but your IRA names only one. The form controls.
10. **Is a trust named on a retirement account?** It must be drafted with the SECURE Act in mind, or heirs may face accelerated taxes. Ask an attorney to review it.
11. **Married and naming someone other than your spouse on a 401(k)?** Federal law generally requires your spouse's written consent, witnessed by a notary or plan representative. IRAs do not have that requirement, though community property states may add rules.
12. **"My children" named generally?** Does that include a child born or adopted later, stepchildren, or a child from another relationship? Use names and relationships, and ask the custodian how class designations are treated.

### Step 4: Understand the big tax rules (short version)

- **Spouse beneficiary.** Can usually roll an inherited IRA or 401(k) into their own and keep deferring tax. See IRS Publication 590-B.
- **Non-spouse individual (adult child, friend).** Under the SECURE Act, most must empty the inherited account by the end of the 10th year after death. If the original owner had already started required distributions, annual withdrawals in years 1 to 9 may also be required. IRS final regulations (T.D. 10001, published July 19, 2024) apply to distribution calendar years beginning on or after January 1, 2025. Exceptions apply to "eligible designated beneficiaries": surviving spouses, minor children of the owner (until age 21, when the 10-year clock starts), disabled or chronically ill beneficiaries, and beneficiaries not more than 10 years younger than the owner. An attorney or CPA can confirm the wording for your situation.
- **Trust as beneficiary.** Can work well for control and protection but needs careful drafting.
- **Charity as beneficiary.** Often an efficient way to give from a pre-tax retirement account, because a charity pays no income tax on it, while heirs would pay income tax on the same dollars. A taxable brokerage account generally gets a step-up in basis at death (IRC section 1014), so people often leave charity the traditional IRA instead.
- **Roth accounts.** Inherited Roth IRAs are generally tax-free to heirs but subject to the 10-year rule.
- **Life insurance.** Death benefits are generally income tax-free to beneficiaries (IRC section 101(a)) but included in the owner's taxable estate if the owner held incidents of ownership (IRC section 2042).

### Step 5: Make the changes

- [ ] Log in to each custodian's site or request the paper form. Employer plans typically use the HR or plan administrator portal.
- [ ] Enter full legal names, relationships, dates of birth and, where asked, Social Security numbers or tax IDs. For a trust, use the exact trust name, date and trustee.
- [ ] Add contingent beneficiaries on every account.
- [ ] Download or request written confirmation and save it with your records.
- [ ] Record the date in the audit table. Repeat after each life event, and at least every 3 years.

## The six costliest mistakes and their fixes

1. **Ex-spouse still named.** Update every form after divorce, and do not rely on state automatic-revocation laws.
2. **Nobody named, so the estate gets it.** Name primary and contingent beneficiaries.
3. **Minor child named directly.** Name a trust or custodian.
4. **Special needs child named directly.** Name a special needs trust.
5. **Form says one thing and the will says another.** Align them. The form wins.
6. **Trust named as an IRA beneficiary without a see-through structure.** Ask an attorney to review.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You found any red flag above.
- You are divorced or remarried.
- You have a child with special needs, or minor children.
- A trust is named on a retirement account.
- Balances are large, or you are not sure what a form means. A CPA or tax adviser can help with the tax side.
