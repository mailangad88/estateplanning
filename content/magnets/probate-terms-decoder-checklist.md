## How to use this checklist

Probate comes with its own vocabulary, and the words change from state to state. Use the decoder to understand what the court sends you, then follow the flow. Illinois terms are noted where they differ. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Plain English decoder

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Probate | The court process that proves a will and gives someone authority to settle the estate |
| Probate court | The court that handles estates. Names vary by state. |
| Decedent | The person who died |
| Heir | Someone the law says inherits when there is no will |
| Beneficiary or legatee | Someone named in a will to receive property |
| Executor | The person named in a will to manage the estate |
| Administrator | A person the court appoints when there is no will or no named executor |
| Personal representative | The general term that covers both roles |
| Letters testamentary | The court paper that proves an executor has authority |
| Letters of administration | The same paper for an administrator |
| Bond | An insurance policy that protects the estate from an executor's misconduct |
| Inventory | A list of the estate's assets and values |
| Accounting | A report of money in and out |
| Claim | A creditor's request to be paid |
| Determination of heirship | A court finding of who the heirs are |
| Muniment of title | A Texas style order that proves a will and clears title without full administration |
| Caveat | A formal warning filed to stop a will or an appointment until the court hears the objection |

## Executor or administrator

- A will names an executor. The court confirms and issues letters testamentary.
- No will, or no named executor, means the court appoints an administrator, and issues letters of administration.
- The job is nearly the same. Illinois uses the terms "executor" for the named person and "administrator" for the appointed person, and "representative" for both. <!-- verify: Illinois terms in 755 ILCS 5/1-2 -->
- Priority to serve when no will exists often follows family order: spouse, children, then others. Check your state.

## Same court, different names

| State | Name often used |
|---|---|
| Illinois | Circuit Court, Probate Division |
| New York | Surrogate's Court (named from a historic role where the court acts as a substitute for the person who died) |
| Texas | County court, probate court |
| Pennsylvania | Orphans' Court, Register of Wills |
| California | Superior Court, Probate Department |
| Florida | Circuit Court, Probate Division |

Other states use other names. The words differ, the work is the same.

## Formal, informal, supervised and independent

| Style | What it means |
|---|---|
| Formal | Court hearing, notice to heirs, judge ruling |
| Informal | Registrar or clerk approves, little or no hearing |
| Supervised | Court approval needed for steps like selling property |
| Unsupervised or independent | The representative acts without constant approval |

Illinois offers independent administration under 755 ILCS 5/28. <!-- verify: Article XXVIII independent administration conditions --> Many estates use it. A court can still step in if an interested person asks.

## Step flow

1. **File the will and the petition.** In the county where the person lived.
2. **Court sets a hearing or approves.** Proof of the will is made.
3. **Letters are issued.** Bond may be required unless the will waives it.
4. **Notice goes out.** Heirs and legatees, and creditors by publication.
5. **Inventory the assets.**
6. **Pay claims and taxes.**
7. **Final account and distribution.**
8. **Close the estate.**

## At a probate hearing

Hearings are often brief. The judge confirms the will, the petitioner and the notice. Contested hearings take longer.

- [ ] Original will, if there is one
- [ ] Certified death certificate
- [ ] Petition and any required forms
- [ ] List of heirs with addresses
- [ ] Proof of notice
- [ ] Bond, if one is required
- [ ] Photo ID

Beneficiaries do not always have to attend. Anyone with an objection should attend or file in writing.

## What a bond is

A bond is insurance, not a payment to the court. The cost depends on the estate. A will can often waive it. Illinois courts commonly require surety unless the will waives it. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/12-2 bond requirement and waiver -->

## When to talk to an attorney

- Someone files a caveat or objection
- The court asks for forms you do not understand
- A will is contested or missing
- Property is in more than one state
- You are unsure whether to use informal or formal probate
