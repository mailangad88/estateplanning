## How to use this guide

Good intentions fail when the wording is vague. A gift to "the cancer society" can start a dispute over which one. This guide shows common ways to give, sample wording for you to discuss with your attorney, and a sheet to record each charity's exact details. If you want to plan current giving as well, see the [charitable giving planner](/free/charitable-giving-planner).

## Five ways to leave money to charity

| Method | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Gift in your will | A line in the will gives a sum, an item or a share | Simple plans, small gifts |
| Gift in a trust | Same, but private and without probate | Larger plans |
| Beneficiary designation | Name the charity on an IRA, 401(k) or life insurance | Retirement accounts |
| Transfer on death on accounts and real estate | Name the charity on a payable-on-death form | Bank and brokerage accounts |
| Donor-advised fund successor | Name a charity to receive what remains in the fund | People who already have a fund |

## Tax angle: which assets suit charity

A qualified charity pays no income tax on a traditional IRA. An individual heir does. So retirement accounts are often a natural choice for charity, while heirs receive assets that get a stepped-up basis. Property left to charity can also reduce an estate subject to estate tax. Ask your tax adviser how this applies to you.

## Sample bequest wording

These are discussion samples, not finished legal language. Have an attorney draft yours.

**A fixed sum.** "I give $10,000 to Riverside Food Pantry, a nonprofit corporation, EIN 00-0000000, located at 100 Main Street, Springfield, Illinois, for its general purposes."

**A percentage of the estate.** "I give ten percent (10%) of my residuary estate to the Lakeview Library Foundation, EIN 00-0000000."

**A specific item.** "I give my painting known as 'Harbor at Dusk' to the Springfield Art Museum, to be added to its collection."

**A restricted gift.** "I give $25,000 to Central University for scholarships to first-generation students in nursing. If the university cannot use the gift for this purpose, the gift shall be used for the closest purpose the university finds practical."

**A backup if the charity no longer exists.** "If any charity named above is not in existence at my death, its gift shall go to a charity with a similar mission chosen by my executor."

Each sample names the legal entity, an identifier and a fallback. Those three habits prevent most problems.

## Beneficiary designations

For an IRA or 401(k), the form controls, not the will. Complete the charity's section carefully.

- [ ] Get the charity's exact legal name and taxpayer ID from its development office.
- [ ] Choose percentages that add up to 100 percent across all beneficiaries.
- [ ] Use a separate beneficiary line for each charity.
- [ ] Name a contingent beneficiary.
- [ ] Ask the custodian for a confirmation copy and file it.

See the [beneficiary designations guide](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

## Qualified charitable distributions (QCDs)

If you are at least age 70 and a half, you may be able to send money directly from an IRA to a qualified charity. The amount does not count as taxable income, and it can count toward your required minimum distribution. There is an annual dollar limit the IRS adjusts for inflation. Details to know:

- The check must go directly from the IRA custodian to the charity.
- A QCD cannot go to a donor-advised fund.
- It applies to IRAs, not typically to employer plans like 401(k)s.
- Keep the charity's acknowledgment letter.

## Donor-advised funds

A donor-advised fund is a charitable account held by a sponsoring charity. You contribute, you may take a deduction in that year, and you recommend grants over time. At your death, what remains is directed by the successor advisers or charity you named. Many sponsors have a form for this, and forgetting it can leave the decision to the sponsor's default rules.

- **Account number:** ____________
- **Successor adviser or charity:** ____________

## Common mistakes

- Using a popular name instead of the legal name.
- Naming a charity on a form but leaving the will and trust inconsistent.
- Leaving a fixed dollar gift when the estate may shrink.
- Naming a charity as beneficiary of a retirement account that also has a spouse's rollover plan in mind.
- Not checking that the charity is a qualified organization.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to leave a share of a business or real estate to charity.
- You want to restrict how the money is used.
- You plan a trust that pays income to family first, and charity after.
- You want to combine charity with an estate tax strategy.
- Your will, trust and beneficiary forms do not agree.
