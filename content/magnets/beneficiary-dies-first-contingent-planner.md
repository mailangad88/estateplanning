## How to use this planner

A beneficiary form is simple until a beneficiary dies first. Then the form's wording decides who receives that share, and a vague form can send it to the wrong place or to probate. Work through the sections, make a decision for each account and fill in the plan at the end.

## Primary, contingent and secondary

- **Primary beneficiary:** the person or people who receive the account first. You can name more than one, each with a percentage.
- **Contingent beneficiary:** the backup, who receives only if the primary cannot. "Secondary" usually means the same thing.

Percentages for each tier must add up to 100.

| Tier | Example |
|---|---|
| Primary | Maria 50 percent, James 50 percent |
| Contingent | The children of whichever of them dies first |

## Per stirpes vs. per capita

Both are ways to divide a share when someone is gone. Use the example of three children (Ann, Ben, Cal) where Ben has died first, leaving two kids.

| Method | What happens to Ben's share | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Per stirpes | Passes down Ben's branch to Ben's two children | Ann 1/3, Cal 1/3, Ben's kids 1/6 each |
| Per capita (equal among survivors) | Ben's share is spread among surviving named people | Ann 1/2, Cal 1/2, Ben's kids nothing |
| Per capita at each generation | Surviving members of a class share equally, grandchildren join in | Each living person at that level gets an equal share |

"By right of representation" is the common phrase for the per stirpes idea in many forms. Companies define their own terms, so ask for the definition in writing.

Choose per stirpes when you want each family branch treated equally. Choose a per capita option when you want only surviving named people to share.

## Class designations

A form may say "my children." A class designation covers everyone who fits the label at the time of death, including children born later. That can be helpful, but some companies need names and dates of birth. Naming individuals, plus a class backup, is clearer.

## If everyone named is gone

If no listed beneficiary survives, most accounts pay the owner's estate. The estate goes through probate and may be open to creditors. Close the gap by naming a contingent whose survival you can reasonably expect, or a trust.

## Simultaneous death and the 120-hour rule

Many states follow a version of the Uniform Simultaneous Death Act. A beneficiary who does not survive the owner by 120 hours (five days) is treated as having died first. Illinois has a statute on this point. <!-- verify: Illinois simultaneous death statute cite and hour requirement --> Insurance and retirement plans may set their own survival rules, so a form can add its own requirement in writing.

## Unequal shares

To give one child more than another, name each person with a percentage on the same form. Example: Dana 60 percent, Evan 40 percent. Do not rely on a will. A form controls the account no matter what the will says.

- [ ] Percentages total exactly 100 per tier
- [ ] Each person has a full legal name and relationship
- [ ] A contingent tier is filled in
- [ ] The wording says what happens to a predeceased person's share

## Beneficiary cannot be found or says no

If a named person cannot be found, the company usually runs a search and then may pay the contingent or the estate. If a beneficiary refuses by disclaimer, the share generally passes as if that person died first. A disclaimer must meet federal and state rules, including timing. A federal qualified disclaimer must generally be made within nine months of the death. <!-- verify: nine-month rule for qualified disclaimers under 26 USC 2518 --> Do not accept benefits before disclaiming.

## Minors and incapacitated beneficiaries

If a minor is named, the company may require a court-appointed guardian of the property, which takes time. A custodian under UTMA (a person who manages money for a minor) or a trust avoids that. See [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors). For a beneficiary who receives Medicaid or SSI, an outright payment can end the benefit, so a special needs trust is usually discussed.

## Common mistakes

- Naming only one beneficiary and no contingent
- Using "my children" when one child has already died
- Leaving a minor as the contingent
- Letting the estate be the fallback by accident
- Assuming a will fixes a form that has no contingent

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your family has a stepchild, a child who died or a child with a disability
- A trust is the backup
- A plan requires survivorship terms not on the form
- Two beneficiaries disagree about how a split should work
