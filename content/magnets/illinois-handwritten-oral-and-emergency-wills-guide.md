**How to use this guide.** Find your situation in the table, then follow the emergency steps if time is short. Illinois law is described in general terms and each state sets its own rules, so a will that works in one state may fail in another. This is general education, not legal advice.

## The Illinois rule in one line

In Illinois a will generally must be in writing, signed by the maker, and attested by two credible witnesses who watch the signing. See 755 ILCS 5/4-3. <!-- verify: current text of 755 ILCS 5/4-3 and electronic will rules at 755 ILCS 6/ -->

That one rule explains most answers below.

## What counts and what does not

| Format | Valid in Illinois? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Handwritten will, signed, no witnesses | Generally no | Illinois has no general holographic will rule, so witnesses are needed |
| Handwritten will, signed, with two witnesses | Can be valid | It meets the signing rule |
| Note on a napkin or scrap | Not as a will unless witnessed | Same rule, and it is easily lost or disputed |
| Oral wishes | No | Oral (nuncupative) wills are generally not recognized |
| Typed will, signed and witnessed | Yes | Standard form |
| Will typed on a phone and emailed | Not by email alone | Needs signing, and Illinois has a separate electronic wills law |
| Electronic will under Illinois law | Can be valid | Special rules apply. <!-- verify: Illinois Electronic Wills and Remote Witnesses Act requirements --> |
| Video of you reading your wishes | Not as a will | May be evidence of intent or capacity |
| Letter attached to a will | Usually not binding | See below |

## Terms in plain English

- **Holographic will:** a will written entirely in the maker's handwriting and signed, which some states accept with no witnesses.
- **Nuncupative will:** an oral will spoken to witnesses, accepted in a few states for narrow cases, such as a final illness.

Which states accept them changes, and the limits are narrow. As a general pattern, many states do not recognize holographic wills, some accept them in limited forms, and a minority accept them broadly. Texas and California are often named as accepting them, and Florida and New York are not, with narrow exceptions. <!-- verify: current holographic will status for CA, TX, FL and NY -->

If you moved or own property in another state, ask an attorney licensed there. A will that is valid where it was signed is often accepted elsewhere, but not always.

## Why informal wills often fail

1. **Missing witnesses.** The most common reason.
2. **Unclear words.** "My stuff goes to the kids" invites a fight.
3. **No executor named.** The court picks one.
4. **Unclear date or capacity.** Disputes follow.
5. **Items that never belonged to the maker.** A will does not control property with a beneficiary form.

A will that fails goes to the intestacy rules, which may not match your wishes. See the [Illinois intestacy chart](/free/illinois-intestacy-chart).

## Emergency plan: a will in a hospital or at the end of life

Time matters, and capacity is the main issue. Illinois requires the maker to understand what they own, who the natural heirs are and what the document does. See [capacity and signing guide](/free/capacity-and-signing-guide).

Steps:

1. [ ] Call an attorney who handles wills and say the situation is urgent
2. [ ] Ask the doctor whether the person is alert and able to talk about their wishes
3. [ ] Have the attorney prepare a short will, or write one together in plain words
4. [ ] Gather two adult witnesses who are not beneficiaries and, if possible, a notary
5. [ ] Have the maker sign in front of both witnesses, and the witnesses sign in front of the maker and each other
6. [ ] Record the time, the room and who was present
7. [ ] Keep the original safe

Deathbed wills are possible if the person has capacity at signing. A will signed in a hospital is not less valid for being there. Many are challenged though, so careful steps help.

For people near the end, remember that many assets pass by form. Updating beneficiary forms can be faster than a will. See [beneficiary designation audit](/free/beneficiary-designation-audit).

## Military wills

Military families have some added options. Members of the armed forces can often get a will prepared by a military legal assistance office, at no charge. Federal law also recognizes testamentary documents prepared under military legal assistance for eligible service members. <!-- verify: federal rule on military testamentary instruments, 10 U.S.C. 1044d -->

An oral will for a service member in active service, under the traditions of some states, is a narrow rule. It varies by state and may not apply in Illinois. Do not plan on it.

Veterans can read the [military family planning guide](/free/military-family-planning-guide).

## Notes, letters and edits after signing

- **Letters attached to a will:** A letter is binding only if it was signed and witnessed with the will, or the will clearly points to it. Otherwise it is a guide to the family. Use a [personal property memorandum](/free/personal-property-memorandum-template) where the law allows it.
- **Writing on a signed will:** Cross-outs or notes made after signing can create doubt. They may be ignored, or they may revoke part of the will, depending on state law. Do not write on it. Sign a codicil with witnesses or sign a new will. See [codicil vs new will](/free/codicil-vs-new-will-decision-guide).

## A safe way to record wishes

| Wish | Where to record it |
|---|---|
| Who gets what | A properly signed will |
| Funeral preferences | A separate written document shared with family |
| Personal messages | A letter of instruction or an ethical will |
| Medical wishes | Illinois healthcare directives, not a will |

## When to talk to an attorney

- Someone is seriously ill and has no will
- You found a handwritten note after a death
- You moved from or own land in another state
- You have an old will with handwritten edits
- You are a service member with a question about military documents
