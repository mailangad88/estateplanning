**How to use this checklist.** Work through the four parts in order and tick each box when it is done. It focuses on the children, which is where divorced parents meet problems that married parents rarely face. If you want the broader divorce clean-up, such as your own will and powers of attorney, see the [divorce life event page](/life-events/divorce). Rules vary by state, so treat this as a map for your attorney meeting.

## Part 1: Know what happens if you die today

Most states give a surviving legal parent first claim to custody. That is true even when the other parent has had little role. Courts can step aside from that default, but usually only after a hearing and on proof the parent is unfit or unavailable. A name in your will does not override a living, fit parent.

So the first question is not "who do I want?" It is "what would happen if my ex stepped in?"

- [ ] I know whether my ex has legal or physical custody under our current order.
- [ ] I have read the parenting plan for any clause about death, relocation or the other parent's rights.
- [ ] I have asked an attorney how a will's guardian nomination works in my state when the other parent is alive.
- [ ] I know who would care for the kids for the first days while a court sorts it out.

## Part 2: Guardian choices

You can name a guardian in your will. The nomination matters most if the other parent is dead, cannot be found, or is found unfit. It can also carry weight if a judge must decide what is best for the child.

- **Primary guardian:** ______________________
- **Relationship to the children:** ______________________
- **Backup guardian:** ______________________
- **Second backup:** ______________________
- **Why this person (values, home, schedule, closeness to the kids):** ______________________

Questions specific to a divorced household:

- [ ] Would this guardian keep the children in contact with my ex, where that is safe?
- [ ] Would the guardian keep the kids near their school, friends and half-siblings?
- [ ] Have I asked the person, and do they agree?
- [ ] If I have concerns about my ex, have I written them down factually for my attorney, with dates and documents, not opinions?
- [ ] If the kids have different other parents, have I thought about whether each child needs a separate plan?

## Part 3: Who manages the money

A minor cannot hold a large inheritance outright. If no one is named, a court may appoint a conservator, and your ex, as parent, may end up controlling funds meant for the kids. Naming a separate money manager is often the main reason divorced parents set up a trust.

| Choice | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Custodian under a state gifts-to-minors law | Simple account, ends at an age set by state, often 18 to 21 | The child gets everything at that age |
| Trust for each child | A trustee you name manages funds under your rules | Costs more to set up, can last into adulthood |
| Leaving to the other parent directly | Easy | You lose control over how it is used |

Record your choices:

- **Trustee or custodian:** ______________________
- **Backup trustee:** ______________________
- **Age or ages for payments (for example one third at 25, 30, 35):** ______________________
- **Allowed uses before then (school, health, housing):** ______________________
- **Should my ex have any say over this money? Yes / No / Limited because:** ______________________

Tips that are easy to miss:

- Pick a trustee who is not the guardian if you want a check between the person raising the child and the person controlling the money.
- Spell out that the trustee may pay the guardian for the child's actual costs, with receipts or a regular report.
- If you pay child support, ask whether a life insurance policy or a trust is required by your decree to secure it, and whether the amount matches what is owed.

## Part 4: Beneficiary and title updates

Many accounts and policies ignore your will. A divorce does not always remove an ex from them. Some states automatically revoke an ex-spouse's rights in a will or trust after divorce, but the rules differ for retirement plans and employer benefits, and federal law governs some of them. ## Part 5: Talking to your co-parent

You do not have to share your whole plan. But a few topics are worth raising, especially if the relationship is steady.

Sample wording:

> "I am updating my will and want to make sure the kids are looked after if something happens to me. If I died, who would you want helping you, and who would you not? I would like to know before I put anything on paper."

> "I am putting money aside for the kids through a trust. I would like it used for school and health costs. Are you comfortable with that?"

If the relationship is not safe or productive, skip the conversation and take your concerns to your attorney. Do not sign any agreement about guardianship without legal advice.

- [ ] I have decided what, if anything, to share with my co-parent.
- [ ] I have a plan for the kids' first week if I were suddenly unavailable: who has keys, who has the school contacts, who knows medical details.
- [ ] I have signed a temporary medical authorization that lets a trusted adult act for the kids while they are with them.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your ex is alive and you have concerns about their fitness, safety or stability as a parent.
- Your decree requires life insurance, or you are not sure whether it does.
- You want a trust for the children and a trustee who is not the other parent.
- You have a business, a home or retirement accounts you want split across children from more than one relationship.
- Your ex lives in another state, or you may move.
- Your children have special needs and benefits to protect.
