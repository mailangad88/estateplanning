**How to use this checklist.** Many older LGBTQ adults rely on friends, partners and chosen family rather than relatives. State law does not always recognize those relationships, so documents do the work. Use this to choose agents, sign the right papers and tell the right people. It adds to, rather than repeats, the [LGBTQ families planning guide](/free/lgbtq-families-planning-guide), which focuses on younger families and parentage.

## Part 1: Who is on my team?

- **Partner or spouse:** ______________________
- **Chosen family member 1:** ______________________
- **Chosen family member 2:** ______________________
- **Biological or legal relatives who may be involved:** ______________________
- **Relatives I do not want making decisions:** ______________________
- **Friend or advocate who knows my wishes:** ______________________

Marriage, where applicable, gives legal rights, so some older couples revisit it. If you are not married, documents carry more weight. If you are, update them anyway, since agents named many years ago may no longer be right.

## Part 2: Core documents

- [ ] **Health care power of attorney.** Names the person who can speak for you if you cannot. Name a backup. State terms vary. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).
- [ ] **Living will or advance directive.** States your treatment wishes. See [healthcare directives](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).
- [ ] **Financial power of attorney.** A durable form lets someone pay bills and manage accounts.
- [ ] **HIPAA release.** Lets providers share information with the people you name.
- [ ] **Will or trust.** Names an executor or trustee and your beneficiaries. Without it, state law usually favors legal relatives, even those you are estranged from.
- [ ] **Beneficiary forms.** Retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death accounts pass to whoever is named. Update them.
- [ ] **Funeral or disposition authority.** Many states allow you to name a person to control your remains in a signed form. Without one, the right often goes to a spouse or relatives in a set order.
- [ ] **Guardian nomination.** Some states let you name who a court should appoint if one is ever needed, so chosen family is considered ahead of relatives.

## Part 3: Hospital visitation and medical access

Federal rules require hospitals that take Medicare or Medicaid to let patients choose their visitors, and to respect that choice without discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity. A written designation is still the clearest proof.

- [ ] Keep a copy of the health care power of attorney in your wallet or phone
- [ ] Give copies to your doctors, and ask to place them in your chart
- [ ] Tell agents where the original is kept
- [ ] Sign a written visitor designation at admission
- [ ] Ask providers to record your chosen name, pronouns and agent in your file

Sample wording for admission:

> "I am designating [name] as my support person and representative. Please add them to my chart and allow visitation at any time."

## Part 4: Care facilities and home care

Long-term care can be a point of concern. Some people fear returning to the closet or facing hostile staff. Prepare in advance.

- [ ] Ask facilities about their nondiscrimination policy before you need one.
- [ ] Look for those that have staff training on LGBTQ residents.
- [ ] Learn that long-term care residents have federal rights to be treated with dignity and to have visitors, and facilities may not discriminate under some state laws.
- [ ] Keep the long-term care ombudsman number handy. This is a free advocate for residents.
- [ ] Write down preferences: pronouns, partner visits, privacy, clothing and personal care.
- [ ] Ask whether partners or spouses may share a room.

## Part 5: Money and benefits

- [ ] Married couples generally have equal access to Social Security and Medicaid spousal protections. Learn how a spouse's income and assets are counted before long-term care. See [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).
- [ ] Unmarried partners generally do not receive survivor benefits. Plan accordingly.
- [ ] Check pension survivor options and whether a named partner is allowed.
- [ ] Consider long-term care insurance, and ask how the insurer treats domestic partners.
- [ ] If you inherit from or leave money to a non-spouse, tax rules differ. Ask a tax professional.
- [ ] If you are a veteran, check benefits through the VA.

Use the [funeral and burial wishes checklist](/checklists/funeral-and-burial-wishes). Share it in writing, not only verbally.

## Part 7: Reduce the risk of conflict

Relatives can challenge a will, or claim a right to make decisions. You can reduce the chance by:

- [ ] Signing documents with proper witnesses and notarization
- [ ] Choosing an attorney who understands your family
- [ ] Explaining your choices in a letter
- [ ] Keeping documents current
- [ ] Considering a trust, which can be harder to contest than a will in some states
- [ ] Having a conversation with relatives, if you are comfortable

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want chosen family to have authority and are worried about challenges from relatives.
- You and your partner are unmarried, or married recently.
- You own property together.
- You are entering long-term care or facing a diagnosis.
- You live in a state with unclear protections.
