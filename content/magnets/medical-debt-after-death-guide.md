**How to use this guide.** Bills can arrive while you are still grieving. You do not have to answer them all today. Read the short version, then use the steps and sample wording when you are ready. Rules differ by state, so check your own.

## The short version

- Medical bills are generally a debt of the person who died, paid from what they left, called the estate.
- In most cases, family members are not personally responsible for the debt just because they are related.
- Exceptions exist: someone who co-signed, a spouse in some states, or someone who agreed in writing to pay.
- If the estate has little or nothing, debts are often left unpaid. Creditors usually cannot collect from heirs.
- Do not pay from your own money simply because a collector asks. Verify first.

## Step 1: Pause and gather

- [ ] Keep every bill, letter and statement in one folder.
- [ ] Write down the date of death and the name of the executor or personal representative, if one has been named.
- [ ] Do not throw away anything marked "final notice."
- [ ] Note who is calling: the hospital, a billing company or a collector.
- [ ] Do not give out a Social Security number.
- [ ] Do not agree to pay on the phone.

## Step 2: Understand who might owe

| Situation | Who may be responsible |
|---|---|
| The person had money or property | The estate pays valid debts first, before heirs receive anything |
| The estate has nothing | Debts often go unpaid; heirs generally do not pay |
| You co-signed or agreed to pay | You may be personally responsible for that debt |
| You signed hospital admission forms as "guarantor" | Read the form; it may create a personal duty |
| Married couple, some states | Some states make a spouse responsible for necessary care; community property states treat debts differently |
| Joint account holder | You owe only if the account was joint, not just because you lived together |

Whether a spouse owes depends on state law, so ask a local attorney if the amount is large.

## Step 3: How the estate pays

Most states set an order for paying debts. Priority generally goes to costs of administration and funeral expenses, taxes, certain health care and last-illness expenses in some states, and then general unsecured creditors. Because the estate may not cover everything, an executor should not pay one creditor in a hurry. Paying out of order can create personal exposure in some states.

Many states give creditors a limited period to file claims against the estate. Ask the probate court or an attorney about the deadline.

For smaller estates, some states have simplified procedures, but the debts still come first.

## Step 4: Medicaid estate recovery

Federal law requires states to try to recover certain Medicaid costs from the estates of some people who received long-term care benefits, generally those who were 55 or older, or who lived in a nursing home or similar facility. Rules differ widely. Some states recover only from probate assets, others from a wider set. There are protections: recovery is generally delayed while a surviving spouse or a child who is under 21, blind or disabled is alive, and states must have a hardship process. Many states also place limits on recovery from a home.

## Step 5: Responding to collectors

Federal law limits how debt collectors may behave. A collector can generally contact the executor or the person who handles the estate, but should not suggest that you personally owe if you do not. They cannot harass you, call at unreasonable hours, or lie. You can ask them to stop contacting you.

Request validation, in writing, within the period stated in their first notice.

Sample letter:

> Re: Account ____________ of [name], deceased [date]
> I am writing about the above account. I do not agree that I am personally responsible for this debt. Please send written proof of the debt, the name of the original creditor, and itemized charges. Please direct further contact to the estate's [executor / attorney] at [address]. This letter is not an agreement to pay.

Sample phone wording:

> "I understand. Please send everything to me in writing. I am not able to discuss payment today."

Keep copies, and send mail with tracking.

## Step 6: Check the bills

- [ ] Ask for an itemized bill. Errors are common.
- [ ] Check whether insurance, Medicare or a supplemental plan was billed correctly.
- [ ] Look for duplicates or charges after the date of death.
- [ ] Ask the hospital about financial assistance or charity care. Many nonprofit hospitals must have a policy.
- [ ] Ask for a lower amount or payment plan, in writing.
- [ ] Do not pay a debt that has passed the legal time limit without advice. In some states a payment can restart the clock.

## Scams

Scammers read obituaries. Be wary of anyone who calls demanding immediate payment by gift card, wire or cryptocurrency, or who knows personal details. Ask for written proof and hang up if pressured.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are the executor and the estate may not cover all debts.
- A collector says you personally owe.
- You received a Medicaid estate recovery notice.
- You were a co-signer or signed a promise to pay.
- You are being sued over the debt.
