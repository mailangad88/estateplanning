## How to use this guide

HIPAA is the federal privacy rule for health information. It does not stop families from helping, but providers must have a basis to share. The right paper turns a closed door into an open one. This guide helps you pick it. State laws add rules, especially for mental health and minors. It is general education, not legal advice.

## Which document gives which access

| Document | What it allows | When it works | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| HIPAA authorization (release) | Provider may share named records with named people | As soon as signed, until it expires or is revoked | Does not let anyone make decisions |
| Health care power of attorney | Agent may make medical decisions | When the document says, often when the patient cannot decide | Agent is treated as a personal representative for records once authority begins |
| Patient portal proxy access | Family member can see the portal | When the provider sets it up | Provider forms vary |
| Verbal consent in the room | Provider may discuss with someone present if the patient does not object | At that visit | Not for records or later calls |
| Court-appointed guardian | Decides and may see records | After appointment | Slower and more costly |

The key difference: a release shares information. A power of attorney shares authority. Many people need both.

## What HIPAA does for family

HIPAA lets providers talk to family involved in care if the patient agrees or does not object, and in emergencies, if the provider judges it is in the patient's interest. That is judgment-based. Staff often say no when in doubt, so paperwork helps.

It does not give a family member a right to full records. A release does.

## Parts of a good HIPAA authorization

- [ ] **Who may share**, such as all providers or a named hospital
- [ ] **Who may receive**, by name and relationship
- [ ] **What is shared**: all records, or limited, and whether mental health, substance use and HIV records are included
- [ ] **Purpose**, which can be "at my request"
- [ ] **Expiration**: a date, an event or "until revoked"
- [ ] **Signature and date**
- [ ] **A statement that you may revoke it in writing**

Federal rules require certain statements in a valid authorization. <!-- verify: 45 CFR 164.508 required elements --> Some records, such as psychotherapy notes and certain substance use records, have extra rules.

An authorization lasts as long as it says. If it names no date, ask the provider's policy, because many set their own limit. Revoke it in writing when a relationship changes.

## Common situations

### An adult child wants a parent's records

1. If the parent can sign, ask them to sign a release and portal proxy form.
2. If the parent cannot sign, the health care agent can ask as a personal representative.
3. If there is no agent, the surrogate or guardian may qualify under state law.
4. Send the provider a copy of the document, ID and a written request.

### Parent of a young adult

At 18, a child's records are theirs. A parent has no automatic right to see them. College students are often advised to sign a HIPAA release and a health care power of attorney before leaving home. Illinois has special rules for minors' consent. <!-- verify: Illinois rules on minors' and young adults' health records -->

### After a death

HIPAA continues to protect records after death. The personal representative of the estate, usually the executor, can generally request them. Other family may obtain them in limited cases, such as for care of a living relative. <!-- verify: 45 CFR 164.502(g)(4) and Illinois rules -->

A copy of letters of office plus a death certificate is usually what the provider asks for.

### Advance directive in the record

Give a copy to your doctor and ask for it to be added to the chart. Many health systems have an online way to upload it. Make sure each facility you use has the current version.

## If a request is refused

| Reason given | What to try |
|---|---|
| "We need a HIPAA form" | Use the provider's own form or a standard one |
| "The form is too old" | Sign a new one |
| "You are not the personal representative" | Provide the power of attorney or court order |
| "Records are with another department" | Ask for the medical records office |
| "It will take weeks" | Ask about the legal time limit and fee rules <!-- verify: Illinois medical records copy fee and timing --> |

## When to talk to an attorney

- A provider will not honor a valid power of attorney
- A parent has dementia and no documents
- You need records to support a guardianship or a dispute
- A relative has died and records are needed for an estate claim
