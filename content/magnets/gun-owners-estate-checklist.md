**How to use this checklist.** Firearms are property, but they are regulated property. Federal law, state law and sometimes local law all apply, and they differ widely. This checklist helps you organize the facts and avoid surprises. It is not a statement of the law in your state. Laws change, so confirm current rules with an attorney.

## Why firearms need their own planning

Most heirs assume they can simply take a firearm home. In many places that is not true. Depending on the state, an heir may need to be eligible to possess firearms, pass a background check or use a licensed dealer. Some items, such as machine guns, short-barreled rifles, short-barreled shotguns and suppressors, are regulated under the National Firearms Act (NFA). Transfers of NFA items generally require ATF approval, even for an heir. Moving one without approval can be a federal offense.

A will usually does not solve this. It assigns ownership, but does not make a transfer legal.

## Part 2: Choose recipients with the law in mind

For each recipient, answer these questions.

- **Name:** ______________________
- **State they live in:** ______________________
- **Legally able to possess firearms?** ______________________
- **Do they want them?** ______________________
- **Do they have safe storage?** ______________________
- **Do they live with children or anyone at risk?** ______________________

Someone who is prohibited from possessing firearms, for example because of a criminal record or protective order, cannot lawfully receive them, even as a gift from an estate. If an heir lives in another state, the transfer often has to go through a licensed dealer in the heir's state. If you are unsure, ask before you name that person.

## Part 3: Gun trusts, at a high level

A gun trust is a trust designed to hold firearms, especially NFA items. People use them to name who may possess the items during life and after death, to avoid probate for the firearms, and to allow co-trustees to have access. Some states allow them for non-NFA firearms too.

Points to know:

- A trust does not make a prohibited person eligible.
- A gun trust only helps if the items are actually transferred into it, following the rules for each item.
- Registered NFA items may need ATF paperwork to move into a trust.
- Rules on responsible persons, background checks and fingerprinting for trusts have changed over the years.
- A will that leaves guns to someone does not protect them from probate or from a hold on the items.

Whether a gun trust is worth it depends on the number and type of items, your state and your family. Ask an attorney who works with firearms law.

## Part 4: Safe storage and access

- [ ] Guns are stored unloaded and locked, with ammunition stored separately.
- [ ] Only people you intend can open the safe.
- [ ] An executor or trusted person knows how to access the safe after your death or if you become incapacitated.
- [ ] Combination or key is stored in a sealed envelope or safe, not in the will.
- [ ] You told your executor what to do if guns are found that are not on the list.

Some people also name a trusted person who can hold firearms temporarily. Check whether your state allows temporary custody and for how long.

## Part 5: What the executor should do first

If you are the executor, do the following before moving anything.

- [ ] Do not sell, give or ship any firearm yet.
- [ ] Secure the home, and lock up the guns, keeping their location private.
- [ ] Call local police or a licensed dealer for temporary storage if you are unsure or uncomfortable.
- [ ] Find the will, any trust and the inventory.
- [ ] Identify NFA items and any registration paperwork.
- [ ] Check whether each recipient is legally eligible.
- [ ] Confirm the transfer method with an attorney or a licensed dealer, including any waiting period or paperwork.
- [ ] Get an appraisal where value is significant.
- [ ] Keep a record of every step, including who received which item and when.

Executors are generally responsible for estate property. Mishandling it can cause personal exposure, so take time.

## Part 6: If nobody wants the guns

Options vary by state.

- Sell through a licensed dealer, a consignment shop or an auction house.
- Transfer to a qualified heir through a dealer.
- Donate to a museum or organization that accepts firearms, where lawful.
- Turn in through a police buyback or surrender program, if offered locally.

Sale proceeds may count as estate assets. Items with collector value need an appraisal. See the [asset and account inventory checklist](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) for tracking value.

## Common mistakes

- Handing guns to a relative before probate. The estate may not yet have authority to transfer.
- Mailing a firearm. Federal and carrier rules limit this.
- Assuming a state line makes no difference.
- Forgetting NFA paperwork.
- Leaving the inventory in the safe, where no one can open it.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own any NFA item or collectible valued at a significant amount.
- Heirs live in different states, or you move between states.
- You are considering a gun trust.
- An heir may be prohibited from possessing firearms.
- You are an executor who found firearms and is unsure how to proceed.
