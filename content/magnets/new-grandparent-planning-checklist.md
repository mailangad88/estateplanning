**How to use this checklist.** A grandchild is a reason to look at your plan, not always a reason to change it. Go through each section, tick what applies and note questions for your attorney. Our broader [grandparents legacy guide](/free/grandparents-legacy-guide) covers heirlooms and values in more depth. This checklist is about the first year and the practical decisions.

## Part 1: Does your plan already cover grandchildren?

Wording matters. A will that leaves property "to my children" may not include a grandchild at all. A will that says "to my descendants" or "to my children and their children by right of representation" is different.

- [ ] I have found my current will and trust and read how they describe my heirs.
- [ ] I know whether grandchildren are included, excluded or left silent.
- [ ] I know what happens if one of my children dies before me. Do their children inherit that share?
- [ ] I know who would manage a grandchild's inheritance if the child is still a minor.
- [ ] I have a date in my calendar for the next review.

Fill in what your documents say today:

- **If a child of mine dies before me, their share goes to:** ______________________
- **A minor beneficiary's share is held by:** ______________________
- **Date my documents were last signed:** ______________________

## Part 2: Do not leave money to a baby directly

A baby cannot hold property. If your will names a grandchild outright, a court may appoint a conservator, which adds cost and gives the child the money at 18. Most families choose one of these instead.

| Option | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Trust for grandchildren in your will or living trust | Larger gifts, and control over timing | Needs drafting, and a trustee you trust |
| Custodial account under your state's gifts to minors law | Smaller gifts | Child gets the money at an age fixed by state |
| 529 college savings account | Education | Rules vary by plan and state |
| Gift to the parents, with a letter of wishes | Small, flexible gifts | No legal control after the gift |

- [ ] I have decided how I want a grandchild's share to be held.
- [ ] I have named a trustee or custodian and a backup, and it is not the grandchild's parent unless that is my choice.
- [ ] I have not named a minor grandchild as a direct beneficiary of any account or policy.

## Part 3: Gifts during your life

For 2026, the federal annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per person, per recipient. A grandparent can give that amount to each grandchild, and a couple can give twice that amount, without filing a gift tax return in most cases. Gifts above it usually require a return, although tax is generally due only after the lifetime exemption, now $15,000,000 per person, is used.

- [ ] I know the annual amount I am comfortable giving each year.
- [ ] I have a note to give an uneven amount only on purpose, such as larger help when a child buys a first home.
- [ ] I am paying tuition or medical bills directly to the school or provider. Those payments are generally not treated as gifts, apart from the annual exclusion.
- [ ] I have checked whether I should coordinate gifts with the parents, who may also be saving for the same child.
- [ ] I have spoken to a tax professional before making a large gift.

A 529 plan lets a contributor front-load several years of the annual exclusion in one year in some cases. Rules differ by plan and by state, so ask the plan administrator and a tax professional before you rely on it.

## Part 4: Your place in a guardianship plan

The parents choose a guardian for their child, not you. But you may be asked, or you may wish to offer. Think about it honestly.

- [ ] I have thought about whether I could raise a child, in health, energy and finances.
- [ ] I have said honestly to the parents whether I would want to be named, as backup, or not at all.
- [ ] If I am not named, I have offered to help in another way: a safe place for the children while a court decides, or help with money.
- [ ] I have asked the parents to leave me a copy of their emergency instructions.
- [ ] I have written down the child's medical needs, allergies and contacts, and kept them in a place the parents know about.

If you say yes to being a guardian, an attorney can help you plan your own health care and money so that a grandchild would not be left without support.

## Part 5: Beneficiaries and your own documents

- [ ] Review beneficiary forms on life insurance, retirement accounts and bank accounts. Do they still reflect your wishes?
- [ ] Add backup beneficiaries to each.
- [ ] Update your durable power of attorney if you have named a person who has since moved or changed.
- [ ] Check that your health care directive is signed and the agent knows it.
- [ ] Consider whether you want your grandchild's parents or someone else to be your executor.

## Part 6: A legacy letter

A legacy letter is not a legal document. It is a note that says who you are. Write one for each grandchild or one for all.

Prompts to start:

- **A story from my life I want you to know:** ______________________
- **Something I am proud of in your parents:** ______________________
- **What I hope for you:** ______________________
- **A family recipe, saying or object I want to pass on:** ______________________
- **Where to find important papers, in case someone needs them:** ______________________

Keep it short. A page is enough. Sign and date it. Store it with your will and tell someone where it is.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your will leaves property to "my children" and you want grandchildren to be included or protected.
- You want a trust for grandchildren, or a trust that pays for education.
- You plan large gifts, or gifts of real estate or business interests.
- One of your children has debts, a troubled marriage or a disability, and you want to leave their share in trust.
- You are considering naming yourself as backup guardian.
- You live in a different state than your grandchildren.
