**How to use this guide.** Use the first table to test a candidate, then the decision grid to shape the will's wording. Illinois rules come from the Probate Act of 1975, 755 ILCS 5/. Other states differ, and the court has the last word on who is appointed. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Who can serve in Illinois

The qualifications are in 755 ILCS 5/6-13. <!-- verify: current qualifications in 755 ILCS 5/6-13 -->

| Candidate | Usually eligible? | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Adult, 18 or older | Yes | Willingness, time, organization |
| A minor | No | Name an adult backup instead |
| Someone with a felony conviction | Often not | The statute has limits and the court can look at the circumstances |
| A person of unsound mind or under a guardianship | No | Capacity matters |
| Non-citizen who lives in the US | Often yes | Residency in the US is the usual test, not citizenship |
| Non-citizen living abroad | Usually not | The court may require a US resident |
| Illinois resident | Yes | No special step |
| Resident of another state | Often yes | The court may require a bond or an Illinois agent. <!-- verify: nonresident executor requirements in Illinois --> |
| Spouse | Yes | Common and allowed |
| A beneficiary | Yes | Common and allowed, with a conflict-of-interest caution |

A will can say who should serve, but it cannot make an ineligible person eligible. That is why a backup matters.

## Executor, beneficiary, or both

Many wills name a spouse or an adult child who is also a beneficiary. That is allowed. The risk is conflict: the executor decides what is sold, who is paid and when. Siblings who feel left out may ask hard questions.

Ways to lower friction:

- Name co-executors who are not all beneficiaries
- Write down the plan in a letter to the family
- Use a professional executor for large or divided estates
- Give each child the same information at the same time

## Should it be the oldest child?

Age order is a habit, not a rule. Test the candidate on skills.

- [ ] Lives near the home, or can travel
- [ ] Organized and good with paperwork
- [ ] Calm in a disagreement
- [ ] Has time for months of work
- [ ] Is trusted by the siblings
- [ ] Has agreed to serve

If the oldest child fails two or more, consider another child, a cousin, a friend or a corporate executor. The [executor scorecard](/free/choosing-an-executor-scorecard) turns this into a score.

## Co-executors, backups and corporate executors

| Option | When it fits | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| One executor | Simple estate, one trusted person | Illness or death with no backup |
| Co-executors | Two children who work well together | Deadlock, double the effort, may need both signatures |
| Backup (successor) executor | Always | Backup must be named in the will, in order |
| Bank or trust company | Large, complex or contested estates | Fees, less personal knowledge of the family |
| Professional individual (attorney, CPA) | Complex paperwork | Pay is usually higher than a relative would take |

How to name a backup in the will: "I name my daughter Maria as executor. If she does not serve or stops serving, I name my son James. If he does not serve, I name [third person]." Name at least two backups.

If your executor dies before you, the will's backup takes over. With no backup, the court appoints someone, often a family member who asks, or a public administrator.

## If you are named and do not want the job

Being named does not force you to serve. Options in general:

1. Decline in writing. The court appoints the next person named, or someone else.
2. Serve for a while, then ask to resign with court approval.
3. Serve with help from an attorney and an accountant.

Do not touch the estate's property before you decide. Acting as executor can imply acceptance. If a will names you, do not pay bills from the estate or move assets before you speak with an attorney.

## Pay for executors

An executor in Illinois is entitled to reasonable compensation, set by law at 755 ILCS 5/27-1. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/27-1 reasonable compensation standard --> Illinois does not set a flat percentage. Courts look at the size, complexity and the work done.

Points that apply:

- A will can set a fee or say the executor serves without pay.
- A family member can choose to waive pay. Reasons: the fee is taxable income to the executor, while an inheritance is generally not income tax.
- Records of hours and tasks help if anyone questions the fee.
- Attorney, accountant and appraiser fees are separate costs.

**To waive pay in the will**, a typical phrase is: "My executor shall serve without compensation." Think twice before you add it. A person who gives up months of work may resent it.

| Choice | Effect |
|---|---|
| Silent will | Reasonable pay allowed by law |
| Will sets fee | The will's terms control |
| Executor waives in writing | No fee, and beneficiaries keep more |

Bond: courts can ask an executor to post a surety bond to protect the estate. A will can waive bond. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/12-4 bond waiver by will -->

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your best candidate lives out of state or abroad
- The family is divided or the estate is large
- You want a bank or trust company
- The person has a felony record or health problems
- You were named and want to decline
