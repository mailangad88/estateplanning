This course covers the five pieces of an Illinois plan, one per day. Each email ends with a small task. It is general education, not legal advice, and the details change, so confirm them for your situation.

## Day 1: Your will, and what Illinois asks of it

A will names who gets your property, who serves as executor and, if you have children under 18, who becomes their guardian. Without one, Illinois law decides, and the result may not match what you would choose.

An Illinois will is generally signed by you in front of two witnesses, who also sign. The witnesses should be adults who are not receiving anything under the will. A witness who is a beneficiary can cause problems for that gift. Many people also add a "self-proving" affidavit so witnesses do not have to appear in court later.

A will only controls what passes through probate. Accounts with a named beneficiary, jointly owned property and trust assets pass outside it. That is why the other four days matter.

Some points to settle before you sign:

- **Executor:** someone organized and fair, plus a backup.
- **Guardian:** a first and second choice for any minor children.
- **Specific gifts:** who gets the house, the car, the family heirlooms.
- **Residue:** who receives everything not listed.

**Today's task:** Write down your executor, backup executor, guardian and the people who should receive your property. Read the [how to make a will guide](/guides/how-to-make-a-will) if you want more detail.

## Day 2: Powers of attorney for money and health

A will does nothing while you are alive. If an illness or accident leaves you unable to act, someone needs legal authority to pay bills and talk to doctors. Illinois has statutory forms for both jobs.

- **Property power of attorney:** your agent can manage accounts, pay bills, file taxes and handle your home. You can make it effective right away or only if you become incapacitated.
- **Health care power of attorney:** your agent speaks for you with doctors when you cannot. You can add your wishes about life support in the same document or in a living will.

Without these, your family may have to ask a court to appoint a guardian, which takes time and money and puts a judge in charge of private decisions.

Choose agents for judgment and reliability, not for who you worry will be offended. Name a backup for each. Banks and hospitals read these documents closely, so a clean, current form on the right Illinois language helps.

Also consider a HIPAA release, so your agent can see medical records.

**Today's task:** Pick your agent and a backup for money, and your agent and a backup for health. Ask each person if they are willing, and tell them where the signed copy will be. The [powers of attorney guide](/guides/powers-of-attorney) explains the choices.

## Day 3: The transfer on death instrument for your home

Illinois lets many owners of a home sign a Residential Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument, often called a TODI. It names who receives the house when you die, without probate for that property.

The rules matter. The document is typically signed with two witnesses and a notary, and it must be recorded with the county recorder before you die. You keep full control while you are alive. You can sell, mortgage or revoke it, and the beneficiary has no claim until your death.

It can be a useful tool for a simple situation, such as leaving a home to one adult child. It is less suited to complicated cases:

- Several beneficiaries who may disagree about selling
- A beneficiary who is a minor or has special needs
- A home that may need to be sold or managed for years
- Someone who might die before you, leaving no backup named

Compare it to other options in [transfer on death deed vs. trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust).

**Today's task:** Find your deed and see exactly who is on title. Write down who you would want to receive the home and whether they could take it on their own.

## Day 4: When a trust is worth considering

A revocable living trust holds assets you transfer into it. You usually serve as trustee while you are well, and a successor takes over if you cannot act or after you die. Assets titled in the trust generally avoid probate.

People often consider a trust when they:

- Own real estate in more than one state
- Have children who are young or who need managed inheritances
- Want privacy, since probate files are public
- Want a smooth handoff if they become incapacitated
- Have a blended family and want to protect each side

A trust also costs more to set up and requires follow-through. The trust only controls what you retitle into it. A trust that sits empty can leave the plan unfinished. Many small estates do fine with a will, beneficiary forms and powers of attorney.

Ask which assets would pass by probate if you did nothing. The [will vs. trust comparison](/compare/will-vs-trust) lays out the tradeoffs.

**Today's task:** List your assets in three groups: those with a named beneficiary, those jointly owned, and those in your name alone. The third group is what a will or trust must address.

## Day 5: The Illinois estate tax

Illinois has its own estate tax, separate from the federal tax. It applies when the value of an estate is above $4 million. The federal exemption is far higher ($15 million per person for 2026), so a family can owe Illinois tax and no federal tax. Illinois has no inheritance tax, so the tax falls on the estate, not the heirs.

Illinois does not allow a surviving spouse to inherit the first spouse's unused exclusion. For a married couple with a combined estate above $4 million, that can make planning worth a look. Life insurance and retirement accounts count toward the total, which surprises many families.

Common responses include trusts that use each spouse's exclusion, charitable gifts and lifetime gifts. Each has tradeoffs, so run the numbers first.

To add up your estate, include your home, accounts, retirement plans, life insurance you own, business interests and personal property. Subtract debts such as a mortgage when you do the math.

**Today's task:** Add up your estimated estate. If it is within about $500,000 of $4 million, or above it, use the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) and plan to talk with an attorney.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your estate may approach or exceed the Illinois $4 million line
- You own a home you want to leave through a TODI or a trust
- You have minor children, a blended family or a family member with special needs
- You own property in another state or a business
- Your documents are old or were signed in another state
