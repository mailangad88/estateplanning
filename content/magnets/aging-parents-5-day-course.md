Helping a parent plan is delicate. This five-day course gives you one small step per day. It is general education, not legal advice, and rules vary by state. Your parent makes the decisions. Your role is to support them.

## Day 1: Starting the conversation without a fight

Many parents resist talking about money, health and death. It can feel like a loss of independence. Your approach matters as much as your questions.

A few approaches that often work:

- **Choose a calm moment.** Avoid holidays, a crisis or a crowded room.
- **Start with your own plan.** "I just updated my will and realized I do not know what you have."
- **Lead with a story.** Mention a friend's family who struggled because nothing was written down.
- **Ask, do not tell.** "What would make you feel safe if you got sick?"
- **Keep it short.** A first talk can be ten minutes. You can return to it.

What you want to learn is simple. Does a will exist? Who is trusted to make decisions? Where are the papers? Who else is involved, such as siblings?

If your parent says no, accept it kindly and try again later. Pushing too hard can close the door. A trusted third party, such as a doctor, an attorney or a faith leader, sometimes has an easier time.

Bring siblings in early if you can. Misunderstandings between children cause many family disputes later. The [caring for aging parents guide](/life-events/caring-for-aging-parents) has more ideas.

Some families find it easier to hold a short talk with one sibling first, then widen it. If your parent seems confused or forgetful, a doctor's visit can come before any paperwork. Be patient with repeated stories and stay open to hearing their fears. Often the real worry is loss of control, and respecting that is the best way to earn trust.

**Today's task:** Pick one gentle opening line and one place and time to try it. Write down two questions you most want answered.

## Day 2: The documents to look for

Once your parent is open to it, ask about the basic documents. You are looking for what exists, not asking for private details.

The core set usually includes:

- **Will or trust:** who inherits and who is in charge
- **Financial power of attorney:** who manages money if your parent cannot
- **Health care directive and proxy:** who makes medical choices, and what care your parent wants
- **HIPAA authorization:** lets doctors talk to the named people
- **Beneficiary forms:** on retirement accounts and insurance

Learn more in the [powers of attorney guide](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and the [health care directives guide](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).

If a document is missing, that is useful to know. Powers of attorney must usually be signed while a person still has capacity, so timing matters. If capacity is already in question, an attorney can explain the options. Without one, families sometimes need a court to appoint a guardian or conservator, which can be slow and public.

Ask where originals are kept, and who has copies. Photograph the list, not the sensitive details, unless your parent agrees you may hold copies.

Also note the professionals involved: attorney, accountant, financial advisor, doctors and insurance agent.

If a parent is reluctant to share documents, offer another route, such as a sealed envelope with a trusted person, or a one-page list saying where papers are kept. Never sign for a parent unless you are legally named to do so. Even well-meant signing can cause problems with banks, doctors and other family members.

**Today's task:** With your parent's permission, fill in a simple table: document, exists (yes, no, unsure), where it is kept, and who is named.

## Day 3: Money oversight and warning signs

Older adults are targeted by scams and sometimes by people close to them. Setting up light oversight early can make problems easier to catch.

Gentle protective steps:

- Add a trusted person as a second contact on bank accounts, which in many banks allows alerts without giving access.
- Set up automatic bill pay for regular bills.
- Ask the bank about alerts on large withdrawals.
- Consider a credit freeze, which blocks new accounts being opened.
- Keep the financial power of attorney with someone honest and organized, and consider naming a backup.

Warning signs of exploitation include:

- Unpaid bills or sudden changes in spending
- New "friends" or caregivers who are very involved in money
- Pressure to sign documents or change a will
- Unusual wire transfers, gift cards or payments to a stranger
- Missing belongings or checks
- A parent who becomes secretive or anxious about money

If you suspect a scam or abuse, contact the bank, local adult protective services, and the police if needed. Your parent's doctor and an attorney can also help.

Oversight should respect your parent's dignity. Be open about what you are doing and why.

Be wary of a new caregiver, neighbor or relative who isolates your parent or steers them to change documents. Ask banks about their elder protection policies, as many have staff trained to spot odd activity. Open conversations with siblings about who handles what help prevent suspicion. Keeping records of money you spend for a parent also protects you.

**Today's task:** List your parent's accounts and bills, and note who currently sees each statement. Pick one protective step to discuss.

## Day 4: Care planning

Care costs often surprise families. Planning early gives your parent more choices.

Start by learning your parent's wishes:

- Where do they want to live as they age?
- What support, such as help at home, would they accept?
- What matters most about medical care?
- Who should be involved in decisions?

Then look at the options and costs: home care, assisted living, memory care and nursing homes. Medicare generally does not cover long-term custodial care. Medicaid may help for those who qualify, and the rules differ by state. The [Medicaid and long-term care planning guide](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning) explains the basics.

Medicaid looks back at past gifts, commonly for 60 months <!-- verify --> in most states, though California differs. Giving assets away without advice can cause a penalty. The [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) can help you understand it.

Also ask about long-term care insurance, veterans benefits and any employer or union benefits.

Write down a rough care plan: who would help, what it costs, and what happens if the first plan fails. Share it with siblings so the load is clear.

Do not assume Medicare pays for a long stay in a nursing home. It generally covers limited skilled care after a hospital stay, not ongoing custodial care. Costs and the way Medicaid treats a home and savings differ by state. Before moving money or property, talk with an elder law attorney, since a well-meant gift can create a penalty.

**Today's task:** Ask your parent the four questions above and write down their answers in their own words.

## Day 5: Keeping records, and getting help

Good records make a hard time easier for everyone. Build a shared folder, on paper or secure digital storage, that your parent approves.

Include:

- A contact list with doctors, advisors and key people
- A list of accounts and where statements go
- Document copies and where originals are kept
- Medication and insurance information
- Funeral and burial wishes
- A log of decisions and expenses, if you help with money

Use the [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list) as a template. Update it at least once a year and after any change in health.

If you pay for things on your parent's behalf, keep receipts and keep your money separate from theirs. Clear records protect you from later questions from siblings.

You now have a conversation started, a document list, an oversight plan, care wishes and a records folder. That is a strong start.

If a document is missing or things are urgent, an elder law or estate planning attorney licensed in your parent's state can help. You can book a consult through the [plan finder](/plan-finder), and there is no obligation. Your parent can attend, and so can you if they agree.

Think about your own limits as well. Caregiver stress is real, and many adult children find local resources, such as an area agency on aging, a social worker or a support group, helpful. Writing down who can do what keeps one sibling from carrying everything. Records also make it easier for you to step back, if needed.

**Today's task:** Create the shared folder, add what you have, and set a calendar date to review it in six months.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A parent may soon lose capacity and has no powers of attorney
- Long-term care or Medicaid is likely in the next few years
- You suspect financial abuse or undue influence
- Siblings disagree about care or money
- There is a family member with special needs or a blended family
