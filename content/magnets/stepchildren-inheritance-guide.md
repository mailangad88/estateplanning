## How to use this guide

Many stepparents raise a child for decades and assume the child will inherit. Often the law says otherwise. Read each section, answer the prompts and take the results to an attorney in your state. Rules differ across states and this guide is educational, not advice.

## The default rule

When someone dies without a will, state intestacy law decides who inherits. These laws are built around marriage and blood or adoption. A spouse and biological or adopted children are listed. Stepchildren usually are not, and when they appear at all, it is typically far down the list, if no closer relatives exist. Details vary by state, so ask.

Even when there is a will, a stepchild is only included if the will names them. Words like "my children" are generally read to mean biological or legally adopted children unless the document says otherwise. If you want stepchildren included, say so by name.

Four situations where stepchildren are often left out:

- **Stepparent dies first,** leaving everything to the spouse, who later leaves it to their own children
- **Parent dies first,** leaving everything to the stepparent, who has no duty to pass it on to the stepchildren
- **Both parents die** without updating plans after the second marriage
- **A sudden death** with no will, where state law sends property to blood relatives

Note the reverse as well. A stepchild with a close relationship may be left out of a grandparent's plan, and a stepparent has no automatic authority over a stepchild's medical care or finances.

## Step 2: Six ways to include a stepchild

| Method | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Name them in a will | A gift of a sum, an item or a share | Probate required, will can be challenged |
| Name them in a trust | Trust gives them a share when you die | Must be funded and kept current |
| Beneficiary forms | Name them on life insurance or retirement accounts | Form controls over the will |
| Transfer-on-death or payable-on-death | Names them on accounts or deeds | Check state rules |
| Adoption | Makes them a legal child for inheritance, in many states | Not always possible, effects on other rights |
| Lifetime gifts | Give now under the annual exclusion | Gift tax reporting if over the limit |

The annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient for 2026. Gifts under that amount need no gift tax return in most cases.

A trust is often the cleanest way to ensure that a spouse benefits first and stepchildren receive the rest. See [estate planning for blended families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families).

## Step 3: Use clear wording

Vague words cause lawsuits. Name each person and use "stepchild" intentionally. Examples for your attorney to adapt:

- "I give to my stepdaughter, Jordan Lee, born on [date], the sum of $____."
- "References to my children include my stepchildren, [names], as though they were my children by birth."
- "I leave my workshop tools to my stepson, [name], with whom I shared many projects."

Also decide what happens if the stepchild dies before you. Does the gift go to their children? Without instructions, the share may fail.

## Step 4: Cover the other gaps

- [ ] **Home:** If your spouse inherits the home, ask how a stepchild would ever receive a share.
- [ ] **Retirement account:** Your spouse may have the first claim by law. Ask about a trust.
- [ ] **Care decisions:** A stepparent may need a health care power of attorney to act for a stepchild, and a minor stepchild's guardian must be named by the parent.
- [ ] **Guardian:** If a minor stepchild's other parent is gone, a guardian must be nominated by the legal parent, not the stepparent.
- [ ] **Executor:** Pick someone fair to stepchildren and children alike.
- [ ] **Personal items:** List small keepsakes in writing.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming that "my kids" includes stepchildren
- Relying on a spouse's promise to "take care of them"
- Leaving everything to a spouse, then never updating the spouse's plan
- Naming a stepchild on a form but not matching the will
- Forgetting a stepchild's own spouse or children if the stepchild dies first
- Waiting until a health crisis

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want a stepchild to share equally with your own children
- You own a home, business or large retirement accounts with a spouse
- You are considering adoption of an adult stepchild
- You are afraid of a will contest
- A stepchild has special needs or lives overseas
