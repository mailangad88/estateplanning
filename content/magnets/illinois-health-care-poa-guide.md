## How to use this guide

A health care power of attorney names a person to speak for you if you cannot speak for yourself. Illinois has a statutory short form in the Illinois Power of Attorney Act, section 4-10 (755 ILCS 45/4-10), which was updated in 2015. This guide walks through it, then asks questions you can answer before you sign. It is education, not medical or legal advice, and other states differ.

## What the form does

Your agent can talk with your doctors, see your medical records, consent to or refuse tests and treatments, choose providers and facilities, make end-of-life decisions, and handle organ donation and the disposition of your remains, if you allow it. The form also says the agent is your personal representative under HIPAA, which gives the agent access to protected health information. Illinois lets you sign it in writing or electronically, and it can be combined with other powers of attorney.

It is not a financial document. For money, see the [Illinois property power of attorney guide](/free/illinois-power-of-attorney-for-property-guide).

## Choosing your agent

You name one agent and up to two successor agents. Each must be at least 18. Illinois law says a physician or other health care provider who is treating you cannot act as your agent, with an exception for licensed professionals who are not providing your care (755 ILCS 45/4-5).

Questions to ask about each person:

- Will they speak up to doctors, even when it is uncomfortable?
- Can they follow your wishes when their own feelings differ?
- Are they reachable, and could they travel?
- Do they know what matters to you?
- Will family members listen to them?

Then tell them. The form works best when your agent has already heard your wishes. Our [healthcare wishes conversation guide](/free/healthcare-wishes-conversation-guide) gives prompts.

## Stating your wishes on life-sustaining treatment

The statutory form offers two optional statements, and you pick the one that is closer to your view, or write your own:

- "The quality of my life is more important than the length of my life." The agent may then decline treatment that would only prolong dying or leave you with a quality of life you would not accept.
- "Staying alive is more important to me, no matter how sick I am." The agent then pursues treatment that keeps you alive.

Neither is a correct answer. Use these prompts to find your words:

- **Situations I would not want to live in:** ____________________
- **Treatments I would want tried, or not tried (CPR, ventilator, feeding tube, dialysis):** ____________________
- **How I feel about a time-limited trial of treatment:** ____________________
- **Where I would like to be cared for at the end of life:** ____________________
- **Religious or personal beliefs my agent should know:** ____________________

## When the agent starts acting

You choose when the agent's authority begins. One choice is only when you lack the ability to decide. The other is immediately, with you still taking part in your care. Many people choose the first. If you choose it, think about who decides that you cannot decide. A short, clear process in your document helps a hospital move quickly.

## Signing

The Illinois form needs your signature and a witness who is at least 18. The witness cannot be your agent or a successor agent, a member of your family, your physician or other provider, or the owner or operator of a facility that provides your care. A notary is not required for this form, though some people add one. Signatures may be written or electronic. Check the statute's current witness list or ask an attorney if there is any doubt.

- [ ] Choose a witness with no tie to the agent or to your care
- [ ] Sign and date the form with the witness present
- [ ] Give copies to your agent, successors, doctors and hospital
- [ ] Keep the original where family can find it

## HIPAA

A HIPAA authorization is a related form that lets providers share information. Under the Illinois statute, your agent is your HIPAA personal representative, but many families sign a separate HIPAA release to help while you can still decide, for example to let a spouse call the doctor. Read why in [what is a HIPAA release](/blog/what-is-a-hipaa-release-and-why-does-it-belong-in-your-plan).

## Living will and POLST

- **Living will:** States your wishes about care if you are terminally ill. It speaks to doctors directly. A health care power of attorney names a person. Many people sign both. See [living will versus health care power of attorney](/compare/living-will-vs-healthcare-power-of-attorney).
- **POLST:** The Illinois Department of Public Health publishes a Uniform Practitioner Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment form. It is a medical order about CPR and life-sustaining treatment, meant for people with serious, life-limiting illness. A POLST form is voluntary and cannot be required for treatment or admission to a facility. You complete it with a clinician, and you can change or revoke it. It is not a substitute for a power of attorney.

## If you do not sign one

Illinois has a Health Care Surrogate Act (755 ILCS 40) that names who decides for a patient with no agent and no capacity. The list begins with a court-appointed guardian of the person, then the spouse, an adult child, a parent, an adult sibling, an adult grandchild and a close friend. A surrogate may not be the person you would pick, and a family dispute can delay care. Read more in [who makes medical decisions without a directive](/blog/who-makes-medical-decisions-if-you-have-no-healthcare-directive).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You live part of the year in another state
- You have a serious illness and want a POLST discussed with your doctor
- Family members disagree about who should decide
- You have specific religious or medical limits you want written clearly
- You are a parent of an adult child with a disability
- You already have a form from another state or an older Illinois form
