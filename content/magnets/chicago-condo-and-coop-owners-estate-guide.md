## How to use this guide

A condo or co-op in Chicago is a home, an asset and a membership in a community with its own rules. Those rules can affect your estate plan. Use this guide to find out what you own, what your association says, and which tools can pass the unit on. It is educational, not advice for your situation. Documents and local practice vary between buildings.

## Condo or co-op: what you actually own

| | Condominium | Cooperative |
|---|---|---|
| What you hold | A deed to your unit plus a share of common areas | Shares in a corporation plus a proprietary lease |
| Governing papers | Declaration, bylaws, rules | Bylaws, proprietary lease, house rules |
| Real estate or personal property | Real estate | Often treated like personal property, though Illinois law has special rules |
| Typical approval to transfer | Notice or limits on leasing; sale disclosures | Board approval of a new shareholder is common |

Condos are governed in Illinois by the Condominium Property Act (765 ILCS 605). Illinois law also treats a cooperative unit as residential property in some contexts. For example, its transfer on death instrument law names condominium units and cooperative units.

## Read three documents first

Request copies of your building's governing papers from the association or management company.

- [ ] The declaration and bylaws (condo) or the proprietary lease and bylaws (co-op)
- [ ] Current rules and any leasing limits
- [ ] The latest budget, reserves and any pending special assessment

Look for the following, and write each answer in a note.

- **Do transfers need notice or approval, and from whom?** ____________________
- **Does the association have a right of first refusal on a sale?** ____________________
- **What happens if the owner dies? Is there a deadline to sell or to have an heir approved?** ____________________
- **Are rentals allowed? For how long?** ____________________

On a resale, Illinois requires the seller to give a buyer the declaration, bylaws, financial status of the unit, reserve information and other items within a set time after a written request. Heirs who inherit a condo and plan to sell should expect a request for these documents. Keep assessment payments current while a unit sits in an estate.

## Four ways a unit can pass

### Joint tenancy with survivorship

The survivor takes the unit without probate. The risks: a co-owner's creditors, a co-owner's divorce, and loss of control if the co-owner is a child.

### Revocable living trust

You sign a deed, and the trustee holds the unit. The trust passes it as you direct. Check the declaration or proprietary lease, tell the board where required, confirm the mortgage lender is informed and ask about homeowner exemptions. For co-ops, transferring shares into a trust usually needs board consent. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust).

### Transfer on death instrument

Illinois allows a recorded transfer on death instrument for real estate, including condominium units and cooperative units. You sign it in front of two witnesses and a notary, and record it with the county before you die. At death, the unit goes to the named beneficiary, who takes it subject to mortgages, assessments and creditor claims. Compare it with a trust in [transfer on death deed vs. trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust). You can revoke it only with another recorded instrument.

### Land trust

A land trust holds title in the name of a bank or title company, and the owner keeps control as beneficiary. It is common in Chicago. See the [Illinois land trust guide](/free/illinois-land-trust-guide). A lender, an association or a board may have rules about a unit in a land trust, so ask first.

## Comparison for a condo or co-op unit

| Question | Joint tenancy | Revocable trust | TOD instrument | Land trust |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Avoids probate for the unit | Yes, at the first death | Yes, if funded | Yes, if recorded in time | Beneficial interest must also be assigned |
| Private | Deed is public | Trust is private; deed is recorded | Instrument is recorded | Beneficiary is not in the public record |
| Backup for incapacity | No | Yes | No | Needs a power of attorney |
| Board or lender notice | Maybe | Maybe | Maybe | Often |

## Common mistakes

- Deeding the unit to a trust without checking association rules.
- Naming a child as joint owner, which can expose the unit to the child's debts.
- Forgetting a co-op share certificate in a safe-deposit box nobody can open.
- Leaving a unit in an estate with unpaid assessments, which can create a lien or fees.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own a co-op, where board approval and the lease can control a transfer.
- You want to put your unit in a trust or land trust, or record a transfer on death instrument.
- The unit has a mortgage, a reverse mortgage or unpaid assessments.
- Several heirs will share a unit, or one wants to keep it and another wants to sell.
- The declaration, bylaws or lease contains language you do not understand.
