## How to use this guide

Marriage comes with a bundle of legal rights that unmarried couples do not get by default. You can build many of them yourselves with documents. Read each section together, fill in the lines and take the results to an attorney in your state. Rules vary, and this guide is educational, not advice for your situation.

## Where partners are treated as strangers

Intestacy laws, which apply when someone dies without a will, usually list spouses and blood relatives. An unmarried partner, even after decades together, is typically not on the list. Property may go to parents, siblings or children from an earlier relationship. A small number of places recognize some kinds of domestic partnerships, so check your state.

The same gap shows up in other areas:

- **Medical decisions:** Hospitals often turn to a spouse or family first. A partner may be left in the waiting room.
- **Money during incapacity:** Without a power of attorney, a partner may not be allowed to pay a bill from your account.
- **Funeral decisions:** State law usually lists who decides, and partners may sit low on the list.
- **Taxes:** Gifts and inheritances between spouses often get special treatment. Unmarried partners may not get the same treatment, so ask an attorney or tax professional.
- **Benefits:** Survivor benefits under some pensions and Social Security generally depend on marriage.

Read more about [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Documents that fill the gaps

| Document | What it does for a couple | Done? |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Names who gets property, and who is executor | |
| Financial power of attorney | Lets your partner manage money if you cannot | |
| Health care power of attorney | Names your partner to speak with doctors | |
| HIPAA release | Lets providers share information with your partner | |
| Living will or directive | States your end-of-life wishes | |
| Funeral wishes or an authorization | Names who decides burial or cremation | |
| Beneficiary forms | Moves retirement accounts and insurance to your partner | |
| Revocable trust (optional) | Passes property privately, without probate | |

If you have family who may object, a signed document carries much more weight than a verbal promise. Keep copies where your partner can find them, and give a copy to your doctor.

## Hospital access

Many unmarried partners are surprised to learn that hospital visitation and decision-making are not automatic. Federal rules require many hospitals to respect a patient's choice of visitors, but decisions about care depend on state law and your documents.

- [ ] Each of you sign a health care power of attorney naming the other (and a backup)
- [ ] Sign a HIPAA release for each other
- [ ] Carry a wallet card listing your agent and phone number
- [ ] Keep copies in a shared folder and with your agent
- [ ] Tell your family who you chose, so no one is surprised

See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) for the difference between the financial and medical versions.

## Home titling options

If you buy a home together, how you hold title decides what happens if one of you dies. Options and names vary by state.

| Title type | What it generally means | Things to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Joint tenancy with survivorship | Survivor owns the whole home, without probate | Can one owner sell or borrow against their share? |
| Tenants in common | Each owns a share and can leave it by will | Will your share go to your partner or your family? |
| One partner on title only | Owner decides, and the other partner has no ownership | What protects the other partner? |
| Trust | The trust holds title and passes it as written | Who is trustee, and what happens after the first death? |

Some points to think over:

- If only one of you is on the deed, the other partner has no ownership unless there is a trust, a lease or a written agreement.
- If you own as tenants in common and have no will, your share may go to your relatives, not your partner.
- Joint tenancy passes the home at death, but it does not control what happens in a breakup. Put those answers in writing.
- Adding a partner to an existing deed can have gift tax or mortgage consequences. Ask before signing.

Read [joint ownership versus a trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust) and [buying a home](/life-events/buying-a-home).

## Beneficiary designations

Retirement accounts, life insurance and payable-on-death accounts go to the person named on the form. For unmarried partners this is often the most direct route.

- [ ] List each account that has a beneficiary line
- [ ] Name your partner, or a trust for them, and a backup
- [ ] Ask whether your plan allows partners or limits beneficiaries
- [ ] Review tax treatment of inherited retirement accounts with a professional, since an unmarried beneficiary may have fewer options than a spouse
- [ ] Keep confirmations

## Cohabitation agreements

A cohabitation or partnership agreement is a written contract about money and property during the relationship and if it ends. Courts in different states treat these differently, so an attorney should draft it.

Topics couples often cover:

- Who owns what you bring into the relationship
- How you split rent, mortgage and shared bills
- What happens to a jointly bought home or car in a breakup
- Whether one partner supports the other during a period of school or caregiving
- How you pay for debts, including student loans
- Who keeps pets

- **We agree to share these expenses:** ______________________
- **These items stay separate:** ______________________
- **If we separate, the home will be:** ______________________

Conversations to have before you sign:

- What does each of us want if one of us dies first?
- Do we want family to receive anything?
- Would either of us want to marry eventually, and would that change the plan?

## A couple's checklist

- [ ] Wills signed by both partners
- [ ] Financial powers of attorney signed
- [ ] Health care powers of attorney and HIPAA releases signed
- [ ] Beneficiary forms updated, with backups
- [ ] Home title reviewed and, if needed, changed
- [ ] Shared bank account and bills sorted out in writing
- [ ] Cohabitation agreement considered
- [ ] Emergency contacts updated at work and school
- [ ] Digital account access shared
- [ ] Review date on the calendar every year

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own a home or other property together, or only one of you is on the deed
- One partner has children from an earlier relationship
- You want to leave property to a partner and expect family to disagree
- One of you is not a U.S. citizen
- You are considering marriage or a registered partnership and want to compare the legal effects
- One of you owns a business
