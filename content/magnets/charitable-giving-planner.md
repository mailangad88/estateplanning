**How to use this planner.** Start with your reasons, then look at the methods, then fill in the worksheet. Tax results depend on your income, assets and state, so ask a tax professional before you choose a method. Rules vary by state, and this planner is educational, not tax or legal advice.

## Why you give shapes how you give

People leave money to charity for different reasons, and the reason points to different methods.

- **Support a cause for years.** A recurring gift or a trust may fit better than a single check.
- **Honor someone.** A named fund or scholarship may matter more than the amount.
- **Reduce taxes.** Some methods can reduce income or estate tax, and some do not. Do not let tax drive the whole decision.
- **Leave a small gift to a few places.** A simple bequest in your will may be all you need.
- **Involve your family.** A donor-advised fund can let children help recommend grants.

Write your reasons:

- **The causes I care about most:** ______________________
- **Why they matter to me:** ______________________
- **Do I want to give now, later or both:** ______________________
- **How much of my estate, in rough terms, I would like to give:** ______________________

## Ways to give at a glance

| Method | How it works | Typical fit | Flexibility | Cost and effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bequest in a will** | Your will names a charity to receive a set amount, a percentage or a specific item | Anyone with a will | You can change it until death | Low |
| **Beneficiary designation on a retirement account or insurance** | You name the charity on the account's form | Retirement accounts, annuities, life insurance | You can change the form any time | Low |
| **Transfer on death or payable on death account** | A bank or brokerage account passes to a charity at death | Smaller accounts | Easy to change | Low |
| **Gift from a revocable living trust** | The trust document directs gifts to charities | People who already have a trust | High | Moderate |
| **Donor-advised fund (DAF)** | You give to a fund at a sponsoring organization, then recommend grants over time | People who want to give in stages | Moderate: the gift to the fund is irrevocable | Moderate, with fees |
| **Charitable remainder trust (CRT)** | You or others receive income for a time, and the charity receives the rest | Appreciated assets, steady income wishes | Low once created | Higher, attorney needed |
| **Charitable lead trust (CLT)** | The charity receives income first, family receives the rest later | Larger estates | Low | Higher, attorney needed |
| **Gift while alive** | You donate directly to charity | Anyone | Complete, no ongoing control | Low |

### Bequests in a will

A bequest is a gift in your will. Common forms:

- **Specific:** "$10,000 to [charity]."
- **Percentage:** "5 percent of my estate to [charity]." A percentage adjusts as your estate changes.
- **Residuary:** "Whatever remains after other gifts goes to [charity]." Often used when family is not a beneficiary.
- **Contingent:** "If my spouse does not survive me, to [charity]."

A bequest is paid after probate, so it can take time. See [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will) and [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).

### Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts

Pre-tax retirement accounts, such as traditional IRAs and 401(k)s, are often taxed as income to individual heirs when withdrawn. A qualified charity generally does not pay income tax on what it receives. Because of that, many people consider leaving retirement accounts to charity and other assets, such as a home, to family. Whether this helps in your case depends on your family's tax situation, so ask a tax professional. A designation overrides your will for that account, so the form must be correct. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) and the [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).

Common mistakes:

- Naming the charity by a nickname rather than its legal name.
- Naming a charity on a form but not telling the family, who then act surprised.
- Splitting percentages that do not add up to 100.
- Forgetting to name a backup if the charity no longer exists.

### Donor-advised funds in general terms

A donor-advised fund is an account held by a public charity (often called a sponsor). You make a contribution, which is generally treated as a gift to charity when made. You can then recommend grants to other charities over time. The sponsor has legal control, but usually follows your recommendations. People sometimes name a successor advisor, such as a child, or name charities to receive what remains at death. Sponsors charge fees and have minimums, and each has its own rules.

### Charitable remainder trusts in general terms

In a charitable remainder trust, you place assets in an irrevocable trust. The trust pays you, or you and a spouse, an income for life or a set term. When the term ends, the remainder goes to the charity. People consider them when they own an appreciated asset, such as stock or land, and want income without selling it directly. These trusts have specific legal requirements and ongoing administration. An attorney drafts them, and a tax professional should review the numbers. See [irrevocable trusts explained](/guides/irrevocable-trusts-explained).

### Charitable lead trusts in general terms

A charitable lead trust works in reverse. The charity receives payments for a term, then what is left passes to your family. It is mostly a tool for larger estates and gifts to descendants. Ask a tax attorney whether it fits.

## Choosing charities

A short vetting list:

- [ ] The organization is a qualified charity under federal tax law. In the United States, a public charity usually holds a "501(c)(3)" status. The IRS has a free search tool for tax-exempt organizations.
- [ ] You have the exact legal name, not just the name used in ads.
- [ ] You have the EIN (employer identification number), a nine-digit tax ID.
- [ ] You know the address of the charity's main office or its gift planning office.
- [ ] You have read how the charity spends money, using its annual report or its public tax filing (Form 990).
- [ ] You have called the charity's gift planning office to ask if they accept your type of gift. Some cannot accept real estate or closely held business interests.
- [ ] You know whether the gift is restricted (for example, a scholarship fund) or unrestricted. Restricted gifts need a written agreement.
- [ ] You have a backup plan if the charity merges, closes or changes its mission.

A useful clause for a will or trust: "If [charity] is not in existence or is not a qualified charity when this gift is to be paid, the gift goes to another qualified charity with a similar purpose, selected by my executor." Ask your attorney for suitable wording.

## Coordinate with the rest of your plan

- **Total percentages.** If your will gives 10 percent to charity and your IRA names three charities, check that nothing conflicts.
- **Family expectations.** Children who expect to receive retirement accounts may be surprised. A short explanation, in a letter or in conversation, can prevent hurt feelings.
- **Tax considerations.** Federal estate tax applies to estates above a high exemption, which is $15 million per person for 2026 <!-- verify -->. Most estates are below that level, so the estate tax benefit of a charitable deduction may not matter. Income tax may matter more. Some states have their own estate or inheritance taxes with lower thresholds. See [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes).
- **Property that is not simple.** Real estate, a business or art may need an appraisal and may be hard for a charity to accept.
- **Pets, family and caregivers.** Make sure the charitable gift leaves enough for people who rely on you.

## Common mistakes

- Using an imprecise name, which can send a gift to the wrong organization or trigger a court dispute.
- Not checking whether the charity can accept the gift.
- Leaving a charity a percentage of an account and a specific dollar amount from the same account.
- Not updating after a charity changes its name or merges.
- Assuming a tax deduction without confirming eligibility.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to use a charitable trust or a private foundation.
- You plan to give real estate, closely held business interests or other assets that are hard to value.
- You want to leave retirement accounts to charity and other assets to family, and want the plan coordinated.
- Your estate may be subject to federal or state estate tax.
- You want a gift to fund a named scholarship or endowment with specific conditions.
- Family members may object or contest.
