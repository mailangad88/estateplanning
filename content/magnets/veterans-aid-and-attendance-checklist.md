**How to use this checklist.** Aid and Attendance is an add-on to the VA Pension, a needs-based benefit for wartime veterans and some surviving spouses who need help with daily activities. It is not the same as VA disability compensation. Work down the lists with the veteran or spouse, and write your answers. The VA sets the dollar limits and updates them every year, so confirm current figures on VA.gov before relying on any number from a website or a salesperson.

## 1. Is this the right benefit?

- [ ] This is **VA Pension** with Aid and Attendance, which looks at need and finances. It is not disability compensation for a service-connected condition.
- [ ] The person is a veteran, or the surviving spouse of one.
- [ ] The person does not receive a duplicate benefit that blocks the pension. Ask the VA.
- [ ] The VA says a person cannot receive Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefits at the same time.

## 2. Basic eligibility tests

The VA describes the pension in three parts. Check each.

**Service**

- [ ] The veteran did not receive a dishonorable discharge.
- [ ] The veteran served at least one day during a wartime period the VA recognizes, and met the minimum service length for their enlistment date.

**Age or disability**

- [ ] The veteran is age 65 or older, or is permanently and totally disabled, or lives in a nursing home due to disability, or receives certain Social Security disability benefits.

**Need for Aid and Attendance.** At least one applies:

- [ ] Needs help from another person with daily activities such as bathing, feeding or dressing
- [ ] Is bedbound or spends a significant part of the day in bed because of illness
- [ ] Lives in a nursing home because of loss of abilities
- [ ] Has severely limited eyesight, as defined by the VA

**Income and net worth**

- [ ] Income, after certain medical costs, is below the limit the VA sets.
- [ ] Net worth is below the limit the VA sets. Net worth includes assets and income. The VA says it generally excludes the home, a car and most furnishings.

## 3. The 36-month look-back

Since 2018, the VA looks at transfers made during the 36 months before the pension claim. If someone moved assets for less than fair market value and the transfer would have pushed net worth over the limit, the VA can impose a penalty period. The penalty period cannot exceed five years, and it is calculated using the amount transferred.

This means families should not rush to give money away to meet the limit. There are exceptions, and the rules are technical.

- [ ] List any gifts, sales below value, or transfers to trusts in the last 36 months.
- [ ] Do not move assets before speaking with someone who understands VA rules and your state's Medicaid rules, since the two interact.
- [ ] Remember that Medicaid has its own, longer 60-month look-back.

## 4. Documents to gather

- [ ] Discharge papers (DD-214 or equivalent)
- [ ] Marriage certificate and, for a surviving spouse, the death certificate
- [ ] Social Security number and award letters
- [ ] Income records: pensions, annuities, interest, rental income
- [ ] Bank, brokerage and retirement account statements
- [ ] Records of unreimbursed medical costs, including facility, in-home care and insurance premiums
- [ ] A doctor's statement of the need for help, on VA Form 21-2680
- [ ] If in a nursing home, VA Form 21-0779
- [ ] Direct deposit information

The VA says you can apply online, by mail or in person at a regional office. Keep copies of everything sent, and note dates.

## 5. Who may help, and who to avoid

Only three kinds of people may help prepare, present and prosecute a VA claim: accredited attorneys, accredited claims agents and representatives of recognized veterans service organizations. Check the person's name in the VA's accreditation search before sharing records.

- [ ] Look up the representative at the VA accreditation search: https://www.va.gov/ogc/apps/accreditation/index.asp
- [ ] Ask for fees in writing. The VA says representatives may charge only under a written fee agreement.
- [ ] Veterans service organizations often help at no charge.
- [ ] Be wary of anyone who offers help with the claim while selling an annuity, insurance or an investment. The VA does not allow accreditation to be used to market financial products.
- [ ] Report concerns to the VA accreditation program or the Federal Trade Commission.

## 6. After you apply

- [ ] Keep a log of calls and letters.
- [ ] Watch mail for requests for more evidence and answer by the deadline.
- [ ] Report changes in income, assets, living arrangements or medical costs.
- [ ] If the VA denies the claim, ask an accredited representative about appeal deadlines.
- [ ] Plan for care costs while waiting, since processing takes time.

## 7. How this fits with the rest of the plan

Aid and Attendance may help pay for care, but it often does not cover the whole bill. It can sit beside private funds, long-term care insurance and, sometimes, Medicaid. A power of attorney helps the family act if the veteran cannot handle paperwork. See the [powers of attorney guide](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

## When to talk to an attorney

- The veteran owns significant assets, and you are asking whether to transfer them.
- A parent is also applying for Medicaid, and the two benefits may interact.
- The VA denied or reduced a claim.
- There is a trust, an annuity or a business interest to be counted.
- A salesperson has offered "planning" for a fee.
