## How to use this workbook

Most will problems fall into a few patterns. Find your row, read the usual result, then check the next step. Rules differ by state, so treat this as a guide to questions, not a ruling. Illinois examples are included where a statute applies.

## Part 1: Was it a valid will

- [ ] The person was an adult and understood what they were signing
- [ ] It is in writing
- [ ] The person signed it, or someone signed in their presence at their direction
- [ ] The required number of witnesses signed in the person's presence
- [ ] The witnesses were not major beneficiaries

Illinois requires two credible witnesses, and a notary is not required (755 ILCS 5/4-3). <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/4-3 signing and witness rules --> A self-proving affidavit makes it easier to prove later. See [what makes a will valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid) and the [Illinois signing requirements cheat sheet](/free/illinois-signing-requirements-cheat-sheet).

## Part 2: Signing problems

| Problem | What usually happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Not signed | Usually not valid | Sign a new will with witnesses |
| No witnesses | Usually not valid, with limited exceptions for handwritten wills in some states | Re-sign with the right witnesses |
| Not dated | Often still valid in many states | Add a date in a new signing or ask an attorney |
| Not notarized | Often still valid. A notary is not what makes a will valid in many states. | Add a self-proving affidavit |
| A witness has died or cannot be found | A will can often still be proved in other ways | Collect the witness's signature affidavit or other proof |
| Signed in a hospital | Valid if capacity and signing rules were met | Keep a note of who was present |
| One child was present when it was signed | Not invalid on its own, but invites a challenge | Record the circumstances |

## Part 3: Lost or destroyed

| Problem | What usually happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Lost, but a copy exists | A court may admit a copy, but must be convinced the person did not revoke it | Ask an attorney about a lost-will petition <!-- verify: Illinois lost will procedure --> |
| Destroyed in a fire | Same as lost | Look for copies with the attorney, the bank, family |
| Last seen in the person's possession and not found at death | Courts may presume it was revoked | Gather evidence of the person's wishes |
| Nobody knows it exists | It cannot be filed | Tell your executor and keep it where it can be found |
| Executor never filed it | The estate may be handled as if there were no will | An attorney can ask the court to require filing |

### Where to store a will

- [ ] A fireproof safe at home, with the executor knowing the code
- [ ] The drafting attorney's vault
- [ ] A county or state deposit program, if available
- [ ] Record the location on your [estate plan document locator](/free/estate-plan-document-locator)

## Part 4: Content problems

| Problem | What usually happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Names an executor who has died | The court appoints someone, often a named alternate | Name two backups |
| Names a beneficiary who has died | The gift may lapse or pass to the person's descendants under state law | Add a clear fallback |
| Leaves property you no longer own | The gift fails. The beneficiary usually gets nothing for it. | Update the will |
| Left a specific item that is gone | Same as above. Often "ademption." | Describe gifts by category or by value |
| Sold the house left in the will | The specific gift usually fails | Rewrite for the sale |
| Values changed since signing | Fixed shares scale, fixed dollar gifts do not | Use percentages if you want fairness |
| Two wills, the later one revokes the earlier | The later will controls | Destroy the old original in a recorded way |
| Two wills that do not mention each other | Courts try to read them together, and conflicts can lead to a fight | Sign a new will that revokes all earlier ones |
| Written before a divorce, marriage or birth | Rules vary by state, and some effects are automatic | Review at once |

Illinois lets a person revoke a will by a later will or by destroying it with intent (755 ILCS 5/4-7). <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/4-7 revocation rules -->

If any answer here is uncertain, consider a new will. See [codicil or new will decision guide](/free/codicil-vs-new-will-decision-guide).

## When to talk to an attorney

- The original is missing and family disagree
- A will looks unsigned, undated or witnessed incorrectly
- You hold a will and the owner has died
- There may be two wills, or someone says the will is fake
- You want to repair an old will
