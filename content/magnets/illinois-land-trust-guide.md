## How to use this guide

Land trusts are common in Illinois, especially around Chicago, and they confuse many families after a death. Use this guide to learn what yours says, what happens to it when the beneficiary dies, and what to change now. It is educational, not advice for your situation. Your own land trust agreement controls, so read it first.

## How a land trust works

An Illinois land trust has three parts.

- **The trustee** (often a bank or title company) holds legal title to the real estate.
- **The beneficiary** (you) holds the right to control the property, live in it or rent it, and receive proceeds from a sale.
- **The trust agreement** sets out those rights and tells the trustee to act only on the beneficiary's written direction.

The Land Trust Beneficial Interest Disclosure Act describes the beneficiary's interest as personal property only, with the exclusive right to manage and control the real estate, possess it and receive net proceeds from rent or sale. That personal property rule is why a land trust passes differently from a deed.

## What happens at death

The beneficiary's interest is personal property, so it passes by the document that controls it. There are four common outcomes.

| How the interest is set up | What happens at death |
|---|---|
| Interest held in the owner's name, no instructions | It becomes part of the estate and may need probate to pass |
| Interest passes under a will | The executor must get court authority, then direct the trustee |
| A contingent beneficiary is named in the trust agreement | The named person steps in, as the agreement provides, without a probate case |
| Interest assigned to a revocable living trust | The successor trustee of the living trust directs the land trustee |

The details depend on the wording of your land trust agreement. Some agreements allow a contingent or successor beneficiary. Others require a written assignment. Ask the trustee bank for its current form and its requirements.

## Why people use land trusts

- Privacy, because the public record shows the trustee and not the beneficiary.
- Easier transfers of the beneficial interest by assignment.
- Co-owners can hold the interest in shares, with one trustee holding title.

A land trust does not, on its own, avoid probate, reduce taxes, stop a lender from collecting or shield against creditors. The disclosure act also requires identification of beneficiaries when a trustee or beneficiary applies to a state or local government body for certain permits, licenses or benefits related to the property.

## Land trust and other tools

- **Living trust.** Many Illinois plans assign the beneficial interest to a revocable living trust. The land trust keeps title and the living trust holds the interest. See [revocable living trust explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).
- **Transfer on death instrument.** The Illinois statute applies to real property transfers made by recorded instrument. It is not the usual tool for a land trust, since the title sits with the trustee. Ask an attorney how to coordinate the two.
- **Mortgage and exemptions.** Ask the lender and the county assessor how a land trust affects the loan and any homeowner exemptions before you set one up or change it.

## If you are the person who inherited it

- Ask for certified copies of the land trust agreement and the death certificate.
- Notify the trustee in writing and ask what it needs to recognize a new beneficiary.
- Find out whether letters of office are required.
- Keep insurance, taxes and assessments current while paperwork is pending.
- Do not sign a direction to sell or deed anything until an attorney has confirmed who has authority.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are about to buy, sell or refinance property held in a land trust.
- The beneficiary has died and no one has named a successor.
- Several heirs disagree about keeping or selling.
- The property is rented, mortgaged or used in a business.
- You are considering assigning the interest to a living trust, or changing it.
