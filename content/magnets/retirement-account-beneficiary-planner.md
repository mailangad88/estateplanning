**How to use this planner.** Retirement accounts pass by beneficiary form, not by your will. The form is often the single most important estate planning document you sign, and it is easy to overlook. Read the rules in Part 1, choose your approach in Parts 2 and 3, and fill in the table in Part 4. If you inherited an account instead, see our [inherited retirement accounts guide](/free/inherited-retirement-accounts-guide). Tax law is detailed and changes often, so check with a tax professional.

## Part 1: What the 10-year rule means

Under the SECURE Act, which took effect for deaths after 2019, most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA or 401(k) within ten years of the owner's death. The IRS says the entire balance of the account must be distributed within ten years, for owners who died after December 31, 2019.

Final IRS regulations clarified that in many cases, if the owner had already begun required minimum distributions, the beneficiary must also take yearly withdrawals during those ten years, not only empty it at the end. Whether that applies depends on the owner's age and the date of death. Ask a tax professional.

Some beneficiaries are called eligible designated beneficiaries. The 10-year rule generally does not apply to them. The IRS lists:

- A surviving spouse
- A minor child of the account owner (until the child reaches the age of majority, after which the ten-year clock begins)
- A disabled or chronically ill person
- A person not more than ten years younger than the account owner

Others, such as adult children, grandchildren, friends and most trusts, fall under the 10-year rule.

What this means in practice: an adult child who inherits a large traditional IRA may pay income tax on withdrawals, and may do it in their peak earning years. Spreading withdrawals across the ten years usually keeps the bill lower than taking it all in one year, but there are exceptions.

| Account type | Tax when beneficiary withdraws |
|---|---|
| Traditional IRA or 401(k) | Withdrawals are generally taxed as ordinary income |
| Roth IRA or Roth 401(k) | Generally not taxed if the account met the holding rules, though the 10-year emptying still applies |

## Part 2: Spouse as beneficiary

A spouse has the most options, which is one reason most people name a spouse first.

- **Roll the account into their own IRA.** The spouse then treats it as their own, with their own required distribution schedule.
- **Keep it as an inherited account.** This can help a younger spouse who needs access before age 59 and a half, since the usual early-withdrawal penalty may not apply to inherited accounts. Rules are complex.
- **Take it as cash.** This is rarely tax efficient.
- **Disclaim.** A spouse may refuse the account so it passes to the backup. Timing and form are strict.

Tip: if you are married and name someone else, check federal and state rules. Employer plans often require a spouse's written consent to name anyone else.

## Part 3: When a trust is a beneficiary

A trust can be named as beneficiary of a retirement account. It can make sense when you want to:

- Provide for a minor child or a young adult who should not receive a large sum.
- Protect a beneficiary who has creditors, a troubled marriage or an addiction.
- Provide for a person with a disability without losing government benefits.
- Control payments across the ten years instead of leaving them to a beneficiary's choice.

It can also create trouble. A trust that is poorly drafted may be treated as having no designated beneficiary, which can speed up the distribution schedule. Trusts also pay tax at compressed income tax rates, so income kept inside the trust can be taxed at higher rates than an individual would pay.

Two forms are often discussed. Names vary, but in general:

| Kind of trust | General idea | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Conduit | Required distributions flow out to the beneficiary each year | Simpler tax, less protection |
| Accumulation | Trustee may hold distributions inside the trust | More protection, possible higher tax |

Never name a trust without an attorney reviewing the trust language. A plain revocable living trust written years ago often does not address retirement accounts.

Questions to ask:

- [ ] Does the trust say the trustee can receive retirement accounts?
- [ ] Is the trust valid under state law and irrevocable at death?
- [ ] Are the trust's beneficiaries identifiable people?
- [ ] Has the attorney reviewed it for retirement accounts?

## Part 5: Mistakes that cost families money

- **Naming your estate.** This can force the account through probate and may shorten the payout period.
- **Naming a minor directly.** A court may appoint a conservator.
- **Leaving the form blank.** The plan default applies, often your spouse, then your estate.
- **Not updating after divorce or a death.** Forms control, and some are covered by federal law that ignores state divorce rules.
- **Unequal results.** A child named on a large IRA and not on other assets can upset a plan meant to be equal.
- **Splitting by percentage across accounts without checking the shares.** Use the same percentages on each form unless you mean otherwise.
- **Naming a charity last.** Charities pay no income tax on retirement money, so leaving a traditional IRA to charity and other assets to family is often more tax efficient than the reverse.

## Part 6: Checklist

- [ ] I have a list of every retirement account, including old 401(k)s.
- [ ] Each account has a primary and a backup beneficiary.
- [ ] I know which beneficiaries are spouses, which are eligible, and which fall under the 10-year rule.
- [ ] No minor is named directly.
- [ ] If I named a trust, an attorney has reviewed it.
- [ ] My spouse's consent is on file where required.
- [ ] I have asked each plan for written confirmation.
- [ ] I review the forms yearly and after any life change.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want a trust to be the beneficiary of an IRA or 401(k).
- A beneficiary is a minor, has a disability or struggles with money.
- You have a large traditional IRA and children in a high tax bracket.
- You are remarried, and want to provide for a spouse and children from a prior marriage.
- You want to leave part of an account to charity.
- You are unsure if an old employer plan still lists your former spouse.
