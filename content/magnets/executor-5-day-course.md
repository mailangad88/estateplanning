We are sorry for your loss. This is a five-email guide for someone newly named as executor. Each email is short and ends with one small task. It is general education, not legal advice, and rules vary by state. There is no deadline for reading these.

## Day 1: First steps: what to do now and what can wait

Most of what an executor does is not urgent in the first days. A few things are.

Take care of yourself and the family first. Then work through a short list:

- Get several certified copies of the death certificate. Banks, insurers and agencies commonly ask for originals.
- Secure the home. Lock it, collect mail, and make sure pets, plants and any perishable food are looked after.
- Find out if there is a will. Look in a home safe, a desk, or with the attorney who drafted it.
- Keep a notebook. Record every call, who you spoke with and what they said.

Some things can wait. You do not need to pay every bill right away, sell property, or hand out items to family. Acting too fast can cause mistakes, and in many states you only have legal authority after a court or trust document confirms it. Rules vary, so ask an attorney licensed in your state about timing.

Do not use the person's accounts for personal spending, and do not mix funds with your own. Keep a clear record of any money you pay out of pocket.

The [first 30 days checklist](/checklists/first-30-days-after-a-death) gives the full sequence.

If you have not yet agreed to serve, you are generally allowed to pause and ask questions before saying yes. Being named in a will does not always oblige a person to act, and rules vary by state. Take the time you need. Many executors find that talking to a family member or attorney early reduces stress and misunderstandings later.

**Today's task:** Start a notebook or folder for the estate. Write down the date of death, where the original will is, and who has copies of the death certificate.

## Day 2: Finding the documents

An executor often starts without a map. The goal today is to gather the paperwork that will tell you what exists.

Start with the obvious places:

- Will, trust documents, powers of attorney and any letter of instruction
- Recent tax returns, which point to accounts, income and property
- Bank, brokerage and retirement statements, by mail or email
- Insurance policies and pension paperwork
- Deeds, vehicle titles and mortgage statements
- A phone, computer and password manager, if you have legal authority to access them

Mail is a good clue. For a few weeks, note what arrives, since statements and bills reveal accounts you did not know about. A tax return can show interest or dividends from an institution you have not heard of.

Do not throw away anything yet. Keep original documents in a safe place, and make photos or scans.

Be careful with digital accounts. Laws on access to email and online accounts vary by state and by provider, so check the [digital assets guide](/guides/digital-assets-estate-planning) before logging in.

If the person had an attorney or accountant, call them. They often know where things are and can answer questions.

You may find paperwork that is confusing, such as old policies, unfamiliar accounts or documents in a language you do not read. Set those aside in a separate pile and ask the issuer or an advisor to explain. It is fine not to understand everything at once. Piles that grow slowly are better than rushed decisions about what to keep.

**Today's task:** Make a simple list of documents you have found and a second list of things you expect to exist but have not located. Do not worry if the second list is long.

## Day 3: Protecting assets while you sort things out

Between the death and the final distribution, the estate's property needs care. Think of your role as keeping everything safe and in place.

The main areas:

- **The home:** Keep the heat, insurance and utilities active. Tell the insurer the owner has died, since vacant homes can have coverage limits.
- **Vehicles:** Keep them insured, and avoid driving or selling them for now.
- **Accounts:** Once you have authority, an estate account in the estate's name can hold funds and pay bills. Avoid paying from personal funds without records.
- **Valuables and personal items:** Photograph them, secure them, and ask family to hold off on taking anything. Disputes often start here.
- **Pets, subscriptions and recurring charges:** Cancel or pause what is no longer needed.

Keep the estate's money separate from your own. Save every receipt. Executors usually have a duty to keep accurate records and treat beneficiaries fairly, and the details vary by state.

If you feel pressure from relatives to move quickly, it is fine to say you are waiting for legal authority. That is a common and reasonable answer.

Be careful about promises. Relatives may ask for a keepsake or for money soon, and saying yes before debts, taxes and the rest of the estate are clear can create problems. A polite answer is that you will share a timeline once you know more. Writing down who asked for what also helps avoid disputes later.

**Today's task:** Walk through the home or list the major assets. Write down for each one who has it now, whether it is insured and what the next step is.

## Day 4: Who to notify

Notification can feel endless. Break it into groups and work through them over a few weeks.

Common people and organizations to contact:

- **Social Security:** Funeral homes often report the death, but confirm. Payments received after death may need to be returned.
- **Banks, brokerages and insurers:** Ask what each requires to start a claim or retitle accounts.
- **Employer and pension providers:** They can explain final pay, benefits and any life insurance.
- **Credit reporting agencies:** Ask them to note the file as deceased, which can help prevent identity theft.
- **Mortgage lender, landlord and utilities:** Tell them who to contact.
- **Motor vehicle agency, voter registration and the post office**
- **Accountant, attorney and financial advisor**

Beneficiaries should also hear from you. Keep the message simple and honest: what you know, what the next steps are, and that it will take time.

Expect some calls to be slow. Keep your notebook up to date. When a company asks for proof of your authority, they may need court-issued letters or a certificate of trust, depending on the situation.

The [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list) is a handy template.

Be cautious with scams that target families after a death, such as callers claiming an unpaid debt or offering to speed up a claim. Verify any request by contacting the company through a number you look up yourself. Debts generally need to be paid from the estate, and rules about family members' responsibility vary by state, so ask before paying anything personally.

**Today's task:** Pick five names from the list above and contact them, or at least write down what each will ask for.

## Day 5: When probate is needed, and where help is

Probate is the court process that confirms an executor and oversees how a will is carried out. Whether it is needed depends on your state and on how assets were owned.

Assets often pass outside probate when they have a named beneficiary, joint owner, or are held in a trust. Examples include life insurance, retirement accounts and jointly owned property. Assets in the deceased person's sole name, without a beneficiary, usually go through probate, though many states have shortcuts for small estates. The [probate vs. non-probate assets comparison](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) explains the difference.

To see where you stand, sort each asset into one of three groups:

- Passes by beneficiary, joint title or trust
- Sole name, probably needs probate
- Unsure, to ask about

If probate is needed, the usual start is filing the will with the court and asking to be appointed. After that come inventories, notices to creditors, paying debts and taxes, and finally distributing what is left. The [guide to settling an estate](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step) lays out the steps.

You do not have to do this alone. Many executors work with an attorney, at least for the first meeting. If you would like to talk it through, you can book a consult through the [plan finder](/plan-finder), whenever you are ready.

Executors are often entitled to reasonable pay and reimbursement of costs, and many family members choose to waive it. The rules vary by state, so ask before deciding. Keep your notebook and receipts, since you may be asked to account for everything you did. Doing this carefully is a service to the family, and it protects you as well.

**Today's task:** Sort each asset you listed into the three groups above. Bring that list to any conversation with an attorney or the court clerk.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The will is missing, unclear or contested
- There is a business, real estate in more than one state, or a large estate
- Debts may exceed the assets
- Beneficiaries are in conflict
- You are unsure whether to accept the role
