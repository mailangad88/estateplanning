## How to use this guide

In a second marriage, a common goal is to take care of a spouse for life and still make sure your children receive what is left. A trust can do both. The names are confusing, so this guide sorts them by what each one does. Tax law and state law vary, so use it to prepare for a meeting with an attorney.

## The core idea

Money goes into a trust at your death. Your spouse receives income, and sometimes some principal, for life. When your spouse dies, the rest goes to the people you named. Those people are the **remainder beneficiaries**. A trustee manages the trust in between.

## Trust names, sorted

| Name | What it does | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Marital trust | Gives the spouse all income and often control over the principal | Qualifies for the estate tax marital deduction |
| QTIP trust | A marital trust where you choose who gets the principal after the spouse | Second marriages, where control over the final recipients matters |
| Bypass or credit shelter trust | Holds assets up to the estate tax exemption, outside the spouse's estate | Using both spouses' exemptions, or protecting children |
| A-B trust | A split plan: Trust A (often marital) and Trust B (bypass) | Married couples, sometimes blended families |
| Joint trust | One trust for both spouses | Simple families, but risky in a blended family |

QTIP stands for qualified terminal interest property. The trust qualifies when the spouse gets all the income at least yearly and no one else can receive principal while the spouse lives. <!-- verify: QTIP requirements, IRC 2056(b)(7) -->

## Income without principal

Yes, a trust can give a spouse the income and not the principal. For a QTIP election, the spouse must be entitled to all the income. Whether the trustee can use principal for the spouse's health, education, maintenance or support is a drafting choice. The more access the spouse has, the less you protect for the children.

| Choice | Spouse gets | Children's protection |
|---|---|---|
| Income only | Interest, dividends, rent | Strongest |
| Income plus principal for health and support | Income and limited principal | Strong |
| Income plus broad principal | Close to ownership | Weak |
| Spouse can take principal at will | Full ownership in practice | Almost none |

## Taxes

A QTIP trust used with a marital deduction delays estate tax until the surviving spouse dies. At that point the trust property is counted in the spouse's estate for tax purposes. <!-- verify: inclusion in survivor's estate, IRC 2044 --> A bypass trust is built to keep growth out of the survivor's estate.

The federal estate tax exemption is $15 million per person for 2026. <!-- verify: federal estate tax exemption 2026 --> Most families will be below it. If so, you may still use a QTIP-style trust for control, not tax. State estate taxes may apply at lower thresholds. See /tools/state-death-tax-checker.

## Worked example

Maria dies. She leaves $800,000 in a trust. Her husband Tom receives income for life. The trustee, a bank, can pay for his medical needs from principal. At Tom's death the remainder goes to Maria's two children.

| Who | What they get |
|---|---|
| Tom | Income for life, medical principal |
| Maria's children | What is left at Tom's death |
| Tom's children from his first marriage | Nothing from this trust |

If Maria had left everything to Tom outright, Tom could change his will and leave it all to his children.

## Who serves as trustee

| Choice | Pros | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Surviving spouse | Familiar, low cost | A conflict: the spouse benefits and also guards the children's share |
| Your child | Protects their share | Conflict with a step-parent they may not trust |
| Co-trustees: spouse and child | Checks and balances | Deadlock |
| Bank or professional | Neutral | Fees |
| A trusted friend | Independent | Skills and time |

A spouse can serve as trustee with the children as remainder beneficiaries. Many attorneys then add an independent co-trustee, or limit the spouse to acts that do not favor themselves. See /guides/choosing-a-trustee.

## Retirement accounts

A QTIP trust can be named as beneficiary of an IRA or 401(k) if drafted for the retirement rules. A trust that must pay out all income can raise issues with required distributions. Spousal consent may be needed to name someone other than a spouse on a 401(k). <!-- verify: ERISA spousal consent for 401(k) --> See /free/retirement-account-beneficiary-planner.

## Do you need one if under the exemption?

Under the exemption, tax is not the reason. Control is. If you want your children protected, a trust can still help. If you trust your spouse completely, a simpler plan may do.

## Separate or joint trusts

A joint trust becomes irrevocable at the first death in many plans, but some let the survivor change it. In a blended family that gap is a risk: the survivor might change who gets the money. Two separate trusts, one per spouse, keep each spouse's assets and instructions apart. They can still be coordinated. See /free/illinois-living-trust-guide for Illinois.

## Checklist

- [ ] List each spouse's assets and where they came from
- [ ] Decide what the spouse needs to live on
- [ ] Decide who the remainder beneficiaries are
- [ ] Choose a trustee and a backup
- [ ] Decide on principal access and limits
- [ ] Check retirement accounts and life insurance beneficiaries
- [ ] Ask about state estate tax
- [ ] Say what happens if the spouse remarries

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want a QTIP or A-B trust
- Retirement accounts are the largest assets
- Your spouse would be sole trustee
- A state estate tax might apply
- Your trust is already joint
