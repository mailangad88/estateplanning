**How to use this guide.** Many families learn the difference between Medicare and Medicaid at the worst moment: when a parent needs care and the bills start. Read the table first, then the sections that match your situation. The guide is general. Medicaid is run by each state, so eligibility limits, waiting lists and rules differ. Always confirm with your state Medicaid agency.

## The short version

Medicare is federal health insurance, mainly for people age 65 and older and some younger people with disabilities. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program for people with limited income and assets. People confuse them because both can touch nursing home care, but they pay for different things for different reasons.

| | Medicare | Medicaid |
|---|---|---|
| Who runs it | Federal government | Each state, with federal rules and funding |
| Who qualifies | Based on age or disability and work history | Based on income, assets and need |
| Doctor and hospital care | Yes | Yes, for those who qualify |
| Short rehab after a hospital stay | May pay, with limits | May pay |
| Long-term custodial care | Generally no | Yes, for those who qualify |
| Home and community care | Limited | Often through state waiver programs |
| Asset test | No | Yes |
| Look-back on gifts | No | Yes, for long-term care |

## What Medicare does pay for

Medicare covers hospital stays, doctor visits and, under conditions, short stays in a skilled nursing facility. For 2026, Medicare says coverage requires a medically necessary inpatient hospital stay of at least three days in a row first. Days 1 to 20 have no daily charge, after the Part A deductible. Days 21 to 100 carry a $217 daily coinsurance. After day 100, the person pays all costs. Coverage depends on continuing skilled need, such as therapy or skilled nursing. A person who is simply not able to live alone is not covered.

Medicare Advantage plans must cover what Original Medicare covers but may have their own approval rules and networks. Check the plan.

## What Medicare does not pay for

Medicare says it does not pay for long-term care, which is mostly help with bathing, dressing, eating and supervision. That applies at home, in assisted living and in nursing homes. Medigap policies, which supplement Original Medicare, generally follow Medicare's coverage, so they do not fill this gap either.

- [ ] Ask the facility whether the care is skilled or custodial.
- [ ] Ask in writing when Medicare coverage is expected to end.
- [ ] Ask whether a supplemental plan pays the daily coinsurance.

## What Medicaid pays for

Medicaid is the main public payer for long-term care in the US. Depending on the state, it can pay for nursing home care, and for home and community services that help people stay at home. To qualify, a person generally must meet:

1. **A need test:** a medical or functional need for the level of care.
2. **An income rule:** state limits, with different rules in different states.
3. **An asset rule:** countable assets must be below a state limit. A home, a car and some other items may be treated differently.

The rules for married couples include protections for the spouse who stays at home. These protections are technical, and states handle them differently.

## The look-back rule

When someone applies for Medicaid long-term care, the state reviews asset transfers. Federal law sets a look-back period of 60 months for transfers for less than fair market value. A gift or sale below value inside that window can cause a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay for long-term care.

This is why a family should not hand over a house or move savings in a hurry. Some transfers are exempt, such as certain transfers to a spouse or to a disabled child. Some may be safe, and some may be costly. Use the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) for a rough date, then ask an attorney.

## Federal home equity limit

Federal law restricts nursing home Medicaid for people whose equity in their home is above a federal threshold, with exceptions such as a spouse living there. States may set a higher limit. Ask your state agency for its current number rather than relying on an old one.

## Common gaps and how families plan around them

| Gap | Questions to consider |
|---|---|
| Medicare coverage ends but care continues | How long can private funds last? See the [Medicaid savings runway tool](/tools/medicaid-savings-runway). |
| Assets above the Medicaid limit | Is there a lawful planning option, or do funds need to be spent first on care? |
| One spouse needs care and the other stays home | How much can the healthy spouse keep? |
| Home is the main asset | What are the options for the home, and what does the state do after death? |
| Care at home is preferred | Does the state have a waiver program, and is there a waiting list? |

Planning options exist, but timing and state law decide what works. Some families buy long-term care insurance while healthy. Some use certain trusts. Some spend assets on care or home changes. Each has trade-offs.

## Estate recovery

Federal law requires states to try to recover certain Medicaid costs from the estates of some recipients after death. What counts as the estate, and what exceptions apply, varies by state. Ask your state agency about estate recovery before choosing how to hold a home.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Assuming Medicare will pay for a long stay after rehab.
- Giving assets to children before asking about the look-back.
- Applying for Medicaid without all the financial records the state asks for.
- Waiting until money runs out before learning the rules.
- Signing a facility contract as a personal guarantor without legal advice.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A parent needs care now, and savings are significant.
- You are thinking about gifting, selling a home or creating a trust to protect assets.
- One spouse needs care and the other will remain at home.
- A Medicaid application was denied or a penalty was imposed.
- You want to compare long-term care insurance, trusts and spending down.
