## How to use this guide

If you live abroad or hold two passports, several countries may claim a say in your estate. One looks at your citizenship, another at where you live, a third at where land or accounts are located. This guide explains the main questions and gives you a worksheet. It is general information. You will probably need advice from a lawyer in each country where you own property or live. A U.S. attorney can handle the U.S. part and help you coordinate.

## Four things that decide which law applies

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Where you live (domicile or habitual residence) | Many countries use it to decide which law governs your estate and who may inherit |
| Your citizenship | Some countries apply their law to citizens wherever they live. The U.S. taxes citizens on worldwide estates |
| Where assets sit (situs) | Land is almost always governed by the law where it is located. Accounts and shares may follow different rules |
| Form of the document | A will must meet the formalities of at least one place that will recognize it |

For U.S. citizens, federal estate and gift tax applies to worldwide assets. As of 2026, the estate and lifetime gift exemption is $15,000,000 per person, and the annual gift exclusion is $19,000. Many families are under those levels, but a foreign country may impose its own inheritance taxes at much lower thresholds. A tax treaty may help, though few treaties cover estate taxes.

## Forced heirship

In many countries, especially civil law systems, the law reserves part of an estate for children or a spouse. You may be free to leave only a portion by will. In some places those rules apply to land in that country, and in others they depend on your residence or citizenship. Within the European Union, a regulation lets a person choose the law of their citizenship to govern their whole estate, which can matter for an American who lives in a member state. Check how this works in the country where you live, and with a local lawyer.

- **Do local rules reserve a share for children or a spouse?** ______________________

## One will or several

There is no single answer. Common approaches include:

1. **One worldwide will** drafted to be valid in each place. Simple, but it may not satisfy local formalities or local procedures.
2. **Separate wills for separate countries**, each limited to that country's assets, with language so that one does not revoke another. Often used when local land needs a local procedure.
3. **A U.S. revocable trust** for U.S. assets, plus local wills for foreign assets.

The risk with separate wills is accidental revocation. A later will that says "I revoke all prior wills" can cancel the other country's will. Your lawyers must coordinate the wording. Do not sign a new will abroad without telling your U.S. attorney, and the reverse.

- [ ] Each lawyer knows other wills exist
- [ ] Each will states which assets it covers
- [ ] Revocation language has been reviewed by every lawyer
- [ ] Executors can communicate and have copies

## Living abroad but keeping a U.S. home

If you keep a U.S. address, bank accounts or a home, your U.S. documents still matter.

- A U.S. will can usually be probated in the state where you last lived or hold property. Some states look at residence; some at where property sits.
- Name an executor who can act from where they are, or a U.S. agent. Courts in some states limit non-resident executors, so ask.
- Make sure your power of attorney and health care directive will be accepted where you live. Foreign banks and hospitals may want local forms or a certified translation.
- Moving abroad can change your state's tax residence. Check with an attorney.

See the [guide to powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) for how an agent's authority works.

## Tax reporting that follows you

U.S. citizens abroad generally continue to file U.S. tax returns. Other reports can apply:

- A foreign bank account report, called an FBAR, is required when foreign accounts together exceed $10,000 at any time in a year
- An information return for large gifts or inheritances from foreign persons, over $100,000 in a year for gifts from nonresident individuals
- Possible reports for foreign trusts, companies and certain accounts

Heirs sometimes miss these and learn about it only after a penalty notice. Ask your tax professional to make a list of what applies to your family. The IRS pages in the sources describe the current rules.

## Dual citizens in the U.S.

If you are a U.S. citizen with a second citizenship and live in the U.S., your estate is usually governed by the law of your state. But a country of citizenship may still claim a say over land or accounts there, and it may have its own tax or procedure. If you hold a second citizenship, ask whether your other country's law reaches your U.S. estate. If an heir lives abroad, plan how money will reach them.

## Powers of attorney and incapacity

If you become unable to manage affairs, someone must act in each country. A U.S. power of attorney may not be accepted abroad.

- [ ] A local power of attorney for each country where you hold accounts or property
- [ ] A health care directive that fits where you live
- [ ] A named person in each country with authority to act
- [ ] Original documents stored where that person can reach them

## Questions to ask lawyers

- [ ] Which law governs my estate in this country, and why?
- [ ] Does forced heirship apply to me or to my property?
- [ ] Do I need a will here, and can it be coordinated with my U.S. will?
- [ ] How are wills and trusts from other countries treated?
- [ ] What taxes apply to my heirs, and is there a treaty?
- [ ] How long do estates take here, and what documents do heirs need?
- [ ] Does a power of attorney need a notary or apostille?

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own land or a business in another country
- You have a will in more than one country, or plan to sign one
- You live in a country with forced heirship rules
- Your spouse or children are citizens of different countries
- You move between countries often and are not sure where you are domiciled
- You or your heirs may have U.S. foreign-account or foreign-gift reporting duties
