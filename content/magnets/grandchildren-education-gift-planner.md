## How to use this planner

Giving while you are alive lets you see the result and can reduce what your estate owns later. The right vehicle depends on your goal: schooling, a first home, a safety net or a start in life. Fill in the tables, compare the options and review them with an attorney or tax professional. Rules vary by state and plan.

## Step 2: Know the gift tax basics

- The annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient for 2026. You can give that amount to each grandchild, and a spouse can do the same.
- Gifts above that amount generally require a gift tax return (Form 709). Tax is usually not due, because the lifetime exemption is $15,000,000 per person for 2026, but the gift reduces it.
- Paying tuition directly to a school, or medical bills directly to a provider, is generally excluded from gift tax and does not count toward the annual limit. The payment must go to the institution, not the student.
- A 529 plan allows you to front-load up to five years of exclusions in one year, reported on Form 709.
- Gifts to a grandchild's trust may qualify for the exclusion only if the trust is set up properly, which is a drafting question.

State gift or inheritance taxes may also apply in some states.

## Step 3: Compare the vehicles

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main use | Education | Anything for the child | Anything, with rules | Tuition or medical bills |

| Child gets control | Never needed | Automatically at the state age, often 18 to 25 | When the trust says | Not applicable |
| Tax on growth | Tax free for qualified use | Taxed to the child, with limits | Depends on the trust | None |
| Flexibility | Change beneficiary within family | Low, money belongs to the child | High | None |
| Cost to set up | Low | Low | Legal fees | None |
| Effect on aid | Often modest, ask the school | Counted more heavily | Varies | None |

### 529 plans

Contributions are not deductible on the federal return, though many states give a deduction. Earnings are tax free when used for qualified education costs. Unused money can generally be moved to another family member. A limited amount, up to $35,000 over a lifetime, may be rolled into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary if the account has been open at least 15 years and other conditions are met. Rollover rules are technical, so confirm before relying on them.

- [ ] Compare plan fees and investment choices
- [ ] Decide whether you or the parents own the account
- [ ] Name a successor owner in case you die

### UTMA and UGMA accounts

The gift is irrevocable. When the child reaches the age set by your state, the child controls it fully and may spend it on anything. Many grandparents like this for small gifts. For larger sums or a young adult who may not be ready, a trust may be better. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

### Trusts

A trust lets you set conditions: pay for school, delay access until age 30 or protect against a divorce or a creditor. It also adds cost and tax filings. Irrevocable trusts cannot easily be changed. See [irrevocable trusts explained](/guides/irrevocable-trusts-explained).

## Step 5: Answer these questions

- [ ] Does each parent know the plan, so no one is surprised?
- [ ] Is the money meant as a gift, or do you expect repayment? Loans differ from gifts.
- [ ] Will new grandchildren be treated the same?
- [ ] If your own children have unequal means, will gifts to grandchildren seem unfair?
- [ ] How will 529 money affect financial aid in your grandchild's situation?
- [ ] Is each grandchild's other parent included in decisions?
- [ ] Does your will mention these accounts, so the plan continues if you die?

## Step 6: Records to keep

- [ ] Date and amount of each gift
- [ ] Account statements and plan documents
- [ ] Copies of tuition payment receipts, showing payment to the school
- [ ] Any Form 709 filings
- [ ] Your list of who owns each account and who is successor

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to give more than the annual exclusion
- A grandchild has special needs, because gifts can affect benefits
- You are considering a trust for the grandchildren
- You are worried about divorce or creditors
- You live in a state with its own gift or inheritance tax
