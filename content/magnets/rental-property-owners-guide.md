**How to use this guide.** If you own rental property, your plan has to deal with more than a house. It has to deal with tenants, leases, deposits, repairs, insurance and sometimes another state's courts. Read the sections that match what you own, then fill in the successor package at the end. The goal is for someone to be able to pick up where you left off without guessing. Property, landlord and tax law vary by state and by city. This guide is educational only.

## How you hold the property matters

Three common ways to hold a rental are in your personal name, in an LLC, or in a trust. Many owners use a mix.

| Holding method | At death, in general terms | Things to weigh |
|---|---|---|
| Personal name | The property generally passes through probate under your will or state law | Simple to set up. Exposes your personal assets to claims from tenants or accidents. Probate can slow management |
| LLC | The membership interest passes under your will, your trust or the LLC agreement, rather than the real estate itself | May help separate rental liability from personal assets. Needs its own paperwork and an operating agreement that addresses death |
| Revocable living trust | The property can pass to a successor trustee without probate | Deed must actually be transferred to the trust. Check with your lender and insurer first |

**Personal name.** If you hold a rental in your own name and die, nobody has legal authority to collect rent or hire repairs until a court appoints an executor. That can leave tenants, taxes and insurance in limbo for weeks or months.

**LLC.** Many owners use an LLC to try to separate rental risk from personal assets. How well that protection works depends on state law and how the LLC is run. At death, what matters is what the operating agreement says about a member's death and who has authority to manage. A silent agreement may leave a gap.

**Revocable trust.** A trust can hold rental real estate and let a successor trustee step in right away. Deeding property to a trust can raise issues with mortgages, title insurance and property tax reassessment in some places, so ask before you record anything. See [Funding Your Trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) and [Will vs. Trust](/compare/will-vs-trust).

## What happens to tenants and leases at death

A lease generally does not end because the landlord died. In most states, the lease continues, and the owner's estate or successor takes over as landlord. Tenants usually remain responsible for rent, and the landlord's obligations, such as repairs and returning deposits, carry over.

- **Rent.** Someone must be authorized to collect it. Tell tenants where to send payments, in writing, once the right person has authority.
- **Security deposits.** Deposits are the tenant's money held in trust. Many states regulate where they must be kept. A successor needs to know where each deposit is and how much it is.
- **Repairs and emergencies.** Habitability duties continue. A broken heater in winter is still the landlord's responsibility.
- **Tenant notices.** Do not raise rent, evict or change terms until you know who has authority and what local law requires.
- **Month-to-month tenants.** Some tenants may leave. Be ready to re-rent, repair or sell.

## Out-of-state rentals and ancillary probate

If you own real estate in a state other than where you live, a court in that state may need to be involved even if your home state probate is straightforward. This second proceeding is often called **ancillary probate**. It generally means another filing, possibly another attorney and another set of fees and delays.

Ways owners commonly address this, to be discussed with an attorney, include holding the property in a revocable trust, placing it in an LLC, or using a transfer on death deed where state law allows one. Each has trade-offs, and each state sets its own rules. See [Probate vs. Non-Probate Assets](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## Property managers

If you use a property management company, review the contract for what happens when the owner dies or becomes incapacitated.

- Who is the contact when the owner is gone?
- Does the contract allow termination at death, and on how much notice?
- Does the manager hold deposits or reserves, and where?
- Can the manager keep operating until the estate has an appointed representative?
- Does the manager need a copy of the power of attorney or the letters from the court?

If you self-manage, write down who your successor would call for repairs, cleaning, leasing and legal help.

## Insurance

Rental insurance is different from homeowner insurance.

- **Landlord (dwelling fire) policy.** Check that it is in force and who is named. If the owner dies, the policy may need to be updated. Tell the insurer promptly.
- **Liability coverage.** Consider whether the limits make sense. Ask about an umbrella policy.
- **LLC or trust as named insured.** If the property is held in an LLC or trust, the policy should name it. Mismatched names are a common claim problem.
- **Vacancy.** Many policies reduce coverage after a stretch of vacancy.
- **Tenants' own renters insurance.** Many landlords require it, and it is worth confirming.

## Records your successor needs

The most useful thing you can leave is a binder, paper or digital. It saves a successor weeks.

- **Deeds and the title insurance policy:** ______________________
- **Current leases and tenant contact information:** ______________________
- **Security deposit ledger and bank account:** ______________________
- **Mortgage statements and lender contacts:** ______________________
- **Property tax bills and due dates:** ______________________
- **Insurance policies and agent contact:** ______________________
- **Rent roll with due dates and amounts:** ______________________
- **Repair history, warranties and contractor list:** ______________________
- **Income and expense records for the last three years:** ______________________
- **Entity documents (LLC operating agreement, annual filings):** ______________________
- **Property manager contract and contact:** ______________________
- **Where logins are stored:** ______________________

## Successor instructions page

Write a one-page letter and keep it with your documents.

- **Who steps in first:** ______________________
- **Who has bank account access:** ______________________
- **Who the tenants should contact:** ______________________
- **My preferences: keep, sell or hand over to a manager:** ______________________
- **Properties I want handled first:** ______________________
- **Warnings (difficult tenants, problem repairs, neighbors):** ______________________

## Tax and basis in general terms

Rental income, depreciation and sale gains each have their own tax treatment. At death, inherited property often receives a "stepped-up" tax basis, which can change the tax picture on a later sale. The details depend on how the property was owned and on current tax law. Ask a tax professional who works with landlords.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own property in more than one state.
- You hold rentals in your own name and have significant personal assets.
- You are thinking of moving rentals into an LLC or trust and have a mortgage.
- You have a partner or family member who co-owns or helps manage.
- Your LLC agreement does not address death or incapacity.
- A tenant dispute is pending.
