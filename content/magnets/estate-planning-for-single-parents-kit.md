## How to use this kit

When two parents share a home, one usually remains if the other dies. When you are the only parent, the question "who would raise my kids?" is harder, and so is "who would handle their money?" This kit walks through both. Rules about custody, parental rights and guardianship vary by state, so use it to prepare notes for an attorney in your state.

## Step 1: Understand the other parent's role

The first fact to settle is whether the other parent is living, involved and legally a parent. The answer changes everything.

| Situation | What commonly happens if you die |
|---|---|
| Other parent living, with parental rights | The other parent usually has the first claim to custody, even if they have been absent. A court can decide otherwise if they are unfit, but it requires a process |
| Other parent living, rights terminated or never established | Your named guardian may step in, subject to court approval |
| Other parent deceased | No parent remains. A guardian is needed, and a court generally follows your nomination unless it is not in the child's interest |
| Other parent unknown | Seek legal advice about establishing parentage and what your documents can do |

Your will can name a guardian, but a nomination does not usually override a fit living parent. If you are worried about the other parent, talk to an attorney about your options. Writing a guardian into your will and saying why gives a court useful information, and a court can approve your choice if the other parent cannot act.

- **Other parent's name and contact:** ______________________
- **Court orders in place (custody, support):** ______________________
- **How involved they are now:** ______________________
- **Any safety concerns the guardian should know:** ______________________

## Step 3: Separate the person who raises from the person who manages money

Many single parents split the roles. A guardian may be an aunt who is wonderful with kids but not with money. Money can be managed by a trustee, who can be a different person or a professional.

Children who inherit directly cannot manage the money. Without a plan, a court may appoint a conservator, and in many states the child receives the balance at 18. A trust lets you set the age and the rules.

## Step 4: Size your life insurance

Life insurance is often the main source of money for children when one parent raises them. A rough method:

1. Estimate yearly costs your children would have: housing, child care, food, health care, school.
2. Multiply by the years until the youngest is independent.
3. Add a lump sum for college, if you want it.
4. Add funeral costs, debts and the guardian's transition costs, such as a bigger car or a bedroom added.
5. Subtract what you already have, such as savings, survivor benefits and employer coverage.

Try the [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) and the [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator). Social Security may pay survivor benefits to children of a deceased worker, but amounts depend on work history, so check ssa.gov.

- **Years until independence:** ______________________
- **Lump sums (education, funeral, debts):** ______________________
- **Resources I already have:** ______________________
- **Gap:** ______________________

## Step 5: Name safe beneficiaries

This is where single parents most often go wrong.

- Do not name a minor child as the direct beneficiary of insurance or a retirement account. A court may have to manage the money.
- Do not name the other parent unless you trust them to use it for the children.
- Consider naming the trust you create, or a trustee under your will, so the money is used under your rules.
- Update after any change in family, such as a new partner, a move or a divorce.

## Letter to the guardian

A letter is not legally binding, but it helps. Share routines, bedtimes, favorite foods, fears, friends, school details and the family traditions you want to keep. Add hopes for who your children become. Update it every year.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The other parent is living, absent, unsafe or has disputed rights
- You have never established parentage or have a custody order
- You want a trust for your children and are not sure what ages or rules to set
- Your children have special needs
- You own a home or business and want it managed for the children
- You are in a new relationship and want to protect your children's inheritance
