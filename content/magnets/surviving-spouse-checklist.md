**How to use this checklist.** You are carrying a lot right now. This list is meant to lighten the load, not add to it. Do one section at a time, and skip anything that is not ready. Almost nothing here has to be done in the first week. Rules and benefits vary by state and by your situation, so use this as a map and confirm details with the agency, institution or attorney involved.

Ask a trusted friend or relative to sit with you for the phone calls. Many people find that having one other person listening and taking notes makes a big difference.

## Section 1: The first days

- [ ] Ask someone to stay with you, or check on you daily.
- [ ] Get the funeral home to order **certified death certificates**. Many survivors find they need 10 or more, since banks, insurers and agencies each want an original.
- [ ] Tell close family and your spouse's employer, if there was one.
- [ ] Find the will, trust and any letters of instruction. See [First 30 Days After a Death](/checklists/first-30-days-after-a-death).
- [ ] Secure the home and any vehicles. Lock up valuables and spare keys.
- [ ] Keep taking your own medications and eating meals. This counts as a task.
- [ ] Do not sign anything from a stranger, a salesperson or an unfamiliar "benefits advisor."

## Section 2: The first few months

### Notices and benefits

- [ ] **Social Security.** The funeral home often reports the death, but confirm it with the Social Security Administration. A surviving spouse may be eligible for survivor benefits, and a small one-time payment may apply. What you receive depends on your age, your work record and your spouse's. Ask Social Security directly, since the rules are detailed.
- [ ] **Do not keep any payment sent for the month of death.** Generally, the payment for the month someone dies must be returned, and Social Security handles this. Ask what to expect.
- [ ] **Employer benefits.** Ask your spouse's employer about final pay, unused vacation, life insurance and retirement accounts.
- [ ] **Pension or annuity.** Contact the plan to ask about survivor payments.
- [ ] **Veterans benefits.** If your spouse served, ask the Department of Veterans Affairs what may be available to survivors.
- [ ] **Life insurance.** File claims with each insurer. Policies are paid to the named beneficiary, usually without going through the will.

### Accounts and bills

- [ ] List every account: bank, brokerage, retirement, credit cards, loans, utilities, subscriptions.
- [ ] Note which accounts were joint with you and which were in your spouse's name alone. Joint accounts often continue with you. Others may need the executor.
- [ ] Do not close accounts in a hurry. Keep a bank account open for bills and deposits until the estate is sorted.
- [ ] Ask the three credit bureaus to add a **deceased alert** to your spouse's file, which can help prevent identity theft.
- [ ] Contact the mortgage lender. Federal rules generally allow a surviving spouse to keep an existing mortgage without paying it off immediately, but ask your servicer.
- [ ] Review credit cards. Do not assume you are responsible for debts that were only in your spouse's name. That depends on state law and how the account was opened, so ask before paying.

### Taxes

- [ ] Find the last two or three years of tax returns.
- [ ] Ask a tax professional about the final return. In general, a surviving spouse may file jointly for the year of death.
- [ ] Ask what filing status you may use for later years.

## Section 3: Retitling and your own paperwork

Retitling means changing names on accounts and property so they match reality. It can wait until the dust settles, but should not be forgotten.

- [ ] **Home.** Ask whether the deed passes to you automatically (for example, as joint owners with rights of survivorship). You may still need to record a death certificate or an affidavit.
- [ ] **Vehicles.** Ask your state's motor vehicle office about title transfer.
- [ ] **Bank and brokerage accounts.** Retitle as needed once the estate allows.
- [ ] **Retirement accounts.** A surviving spouse often has more options than other beneficiaries, such as rolling an account over into their own. The choices have tax consequences, so ask a professional before acting.
- [ ] **Health insurance.** If you were covered under your spouse's plan, ask about COBRA or other coverage options and how long you have to choose.
- [ ] **Insurance policies.** Update the named insured on home, auto and umbrella policies.
- [ ] **Your own beneficiaries.** If your spouse was named on your accounts, those designations now need new names. See [Beneficiary Designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

## Section 4: Updating your own plan

Your documents likely named your spouse as executor, trustee, agent or guardian. Those choices need new names now.

- [ ] Will: who inherits and who is executor now?
- [ ] Revocable trust: who is successor trustee?
- [ ] Financial power of attorney: who is your agent?
- [ ] Health care directive: who speaks for you?
- [ ] Guardians for minor children, if any.
- [ ] Beneficiaries on retirement accounts and life insurance.

You do not need to do all of this in month two. Many people wait until they are ready, usually within the first year, and meet an attorney then. In the meantime, if you have no one named to act for you, it may be worth speaking to an attorney sooner. See [Updating Your Estate Plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).

## Section 5: Scams aimed at the newly widowed

Scammers read obituaries. They know you are tired and your spouse's information is out there. Common tricks include:

- A caller says your spouse owed money and demands payment today.
- A "life insurance company" or "government agency" asks for a fee to release a benefit. Real benefits do not need a fee up front.
- A salesperson visits with a rushed "investment opportunity," often pushing you to move your retirement money.
- Fake sympathy cards or emails with links that ask you to log in.
- Someone offers to "handle" the paperwork for a payment.
- Identity thieves who use your spouse's number to open credit.

A few habits help. Hang up and call the agency back using a number you looked up yourself. Do not give out your Social Security number to someone who called you. Ask for everything in writing, and let someone you trust read it first.

## Section 6: Emotional pacing

Grief affects judgment, memory and sleep. Many financial professionals suggest, in general, that you avoid making large, hard-to-reverse decisions in the first year. That might mean not selling the house, not moving, not giving away large amounts and not making big investment changes.

- [ ] Park big decisions on a list marked "later."
- [ ] Pick one person you trust to talk things through with.
- [ ] Keep a notebook for calls, names and dates.
- [ ] Ask your doctor about grief support, or look for a local group.
- [ ] Mark one date three months from now to review this list again.

## Section 7: The first year

- [ ] Review your budget. Income often changes after a death.
- [ ] Confirm which tax returns apply and who is preparing them.
- [ ] Meet an attorney to complete your own estate plan.
- [ ] Update your emergency contacts.
- [ ] On the first anniversary, review what is still open.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your spouse had no will, or you cannot find one.
- Property was owned in only your spouse's name.
- There are children from a prior marriage or a blended family.
- Debts seem larger than the assets.
- You are being pressured by relatives or by a salesperson.
- You do not have your own documents in place.
