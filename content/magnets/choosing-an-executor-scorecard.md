## How to use this scorecard

The usual advice is to pick the oldest child or the closest friend. A better start is to list what the job involves and ask who fits it. The executor, also called a personal representative in some states, collects assets, pays debts, files returns and distributes what is left. For background see [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor). The [executor workload tool](/tools/executor-workload) gives a feel for the time involved.

## Step 3: Check conflicts

A high score on skills is not enough if there is a conflict. Check each candidate.

- [ ] Is this person also a beneficiary? Many states allow it. It can create tension, so discuss it.
- [ ] Is this person in a dispute with another family member?
- [ ] Is this person a spouse of a beneficiary, or someone with a business connection to the estate?
- [ ] Will they be paid, and is that clear? Rules on compensation vary by state.
- [ ] Do they live in another state? Some states limit non-resident executors or require a local agent or bond.
- [ ] Is there any history of money problems, such as bankruptcy or liens?

See also the post on [whether an executor can also be a beneficiary](/blog/can-an-executor-also-be-a-beneficiary).

## Step 4: Think about location and time

| Factor | Candidate A | Candidate B | Candidate C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Travel needed to visit the estate | | | |
| Job or care responsibilities | | | |
| Health | | | |
| Time zone or language issues | | | |

An out-of-town person can serve well if the estate is simple. A complicated estate with real estate or a business usually needs someone more hands-on, or a local professional to help.

## Step 5: Consider the professional option

A bank trust department, a trust company or a private professional fiduciary can serve. They bring experience and no family history, and they charge fees.

| | Family member | Professional |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Often waives fees, though expenses are paid | Fees and a minimum |
| Knowledge of family | High | Low |
| Neutrality | Varies | High |
| Availability | Limited by life | Staffed |
| Fit for small estate | Often | Often too costly |
| Fit for large, complex or contested estate | Sometimes | Often |

Another option is a family member as executor, with a professional hired as an agent for tax and accounting tasks.

## Step 6: Plan backups

- **First choice:** ____________
- **Second choice:** ____________
- **Third choice:** ____________
- **If none can serve, the court appoints:** [ ] Yes (default)

Co-executors can seem fair, but they must often act together and can deadlock. Some plans name a main executor and give another person an advisory role.

## Step 7: Ask the person

Choosing in secret is a common mistake. Try wording like this:

**"I'm updating my will and I would like you to be my executor. That means handling my paperwork and bills after I die, working with a lawyer, and making sure everything goes where I've said. It could take a year or more. You can say no, and I want to know honestly. Would you like to think about it?"**

- **Date asked:** ____________
- **Answer:** ____________
- **Questions they raised:** ____________

## Step 8: Prepare them

- [ ] Tell them where the will and important documents are.
- [ ] Share a list of accounts and contacts, kept updated.
- [ ] Introduce them to your attorney and financial adviser.
- [ ] Tell other family who you chose and why.
- [ ] Review the choice every few years or after a major life change.

## When to talk to an attorney

- No candidate is a clear fit.
- Family members are likely to disagree about the choice.
- You own a business, rental property or assets in several states.
- A candidate lives out of state.
- You want a professional to serve, or want co-executors.
