**How to use this guide.** Work through the sections in order and write your answers in the blanks. Nothing here is advice for your situation, and rules vary by state, so use the finished pages as a starting point for a conversation with an attorney licensed where you live.

## Start with what you want the money to do

Before choosing a tool, decide the purpose. The answer shapes everything else.

- **Education.** Tuition, books, trade school or training.
- **A head start.** A first car, a first home deposit or a business.
- **A safety net.** Money held back until a grandchild is older and steadier.
- **Equal treatment.** A share for each grandchild, however many there are.
- **Memory.** Something small that carries your name or story.

Write down your top two:

- **First purpose:** ______________________
- **Second purpose:** ______________________
- **What I do not want the money used for:** ______________________

## Why an outright gift to a minor is a problem

It feels natural to write "to my grandchildren, in equal shares" in a will or to name a child as a beneficiary on an account. For a minor, it can cause trouble.

- **Minors generally cannot hold large sums in their own name.** Banks, insurers and brokerages usually will not pay a minor directly. A court may appoint a guardian or conservator to manage the money, which adds cost and paperwork, and the process is public. Rules vary by state.
- **At the age of majority the child may receive it all.** In most states that is 18 or 21. A young adult may not be ready for a large sum.
- **Parents may be in the middle.** If you give money to a parent "for the child," it becomes the parent's money, which creditors, a divorce or a parent's own estate may reach.
- **Needs-based benefits.** A direct inheritance can affect a grandchild who receives disability benefits. A special needs trust may be a better fit. See [special needs trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts).
- **A grandparent may not be in control of timing.** If a grandchild dies before you, the will may say who gets that share. If it says nothing, state law decides.

For more on minors and money, see [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

## The main options

| Option | How it works | Control after the gift | Cost and effort | Common concern |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Outright gift while you are alive** | You give cash or assets to the grandchild's parent or the grandchild | Little or none | Low | The child may spend it, or a parent may use it |
| **Custodial account (UTMA or UGMA)** | An adult custodian manages the account for the child until a set age | The custodian controls; you can be the custodian during life | Low | Money is the child's at the termination age, which varies by state (often 18 to 25) |
| **529 college savings plan** | Tax-advantaged account for education costs | The account owner, often you, controls withdrawals and can change the beneficiary | Low | Must be used for qualified education to keep tax benefits; rules differ by plan |
| **Trust for a grandchild** | A trustee holds assets under rules you write | High: you set ages, purposes and who decides | Higher: a lawyer drafts it | Needs a trustee who is trusted and willing |
| **Trust in your will** (testamentary) | Created when you die and your will is processed | High | Moderate | The will goes through probate, which is public |
| **Beneficiary designation naming a trust** | A life insurance policy or retirement account pays into a trust | High | Moderate | Forms must be updated and coordinated with the trust |

### Custodial accounts

A UTMA (Uniform Transfers to Minors Act) or UGMA (Uniform Gifts to Minors Act) account lets an adult hold assets for a child without a court. It is simple and inexpensive. The catch is that the child receives control at a fixed age set by state law, and you cannot extend it. The money belongs to the child from the day it is given, and it may count in financial aid formulas. Ask an advisor about the tax rules for a child's investment income.

### Trusts for grandchildren

A trust lets you say when and why a grandchild receives money. Examples of features people consider:

- **Staggered ages,** such as a third at 25, a third at 30 and the rest at 35.
- **Purpose-based payments,** such as tuition, health care and a first home.
- **A trustee's discretion,** so the trustee can help the grandchild with the most need.
- **Protection** from creditors or a future ex-spouse, in many states.
- **A single trust for all grandchildren** until the youngest reaches a set age, then splitting into shares. This is sometimes called a "pot trust" and is useful when ages are far apart.

The trustee matters as much as the trust. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee). Many families name a trusted adult child, a professional trustee or both, with a backup. Rules vary by state, and a trust has to be signed correctly to work.

### 529 plans in general terms

A 529 plan is a savings account for education expenses. Contributions are usually made with after-tax money, growth is generally not taxed while in the account, and withdrawals for qualified education costs are generally free of federal income tax. States offer their own plans, and some give a state tax benefit. Contribution limits, investment choices, fees and rules about unused funds vary by plan. If a grandchild does not use the money, the owner can often switch the beneficiary to another family member. Ask a financial or tax professional about the current rules, because they change.

One planning detail: who owns the account (you or the parent) can affect financial aid and control. Ask a financial aid adviser before you open one.

## Fairness between families

Grandparents rarely have only one family to think about. Decide on your approach and write it down.

- **Equal per grandchild.** Every grandchild gets the same amount, regardless of how many children their parent has. Simple, and easy to explain.
- **Equal per family (per stirpes).** Each of your children's branches gets an equal share, then divides among that branch's children. A child with one kid gives that kid a larger share than a sibling with four.
- **Based on need.** More goes to a grandchild with a special need or higher education costs. This is flexible and can feel unfair without an explanation.
- **Different treatment for stepgrandchildren or adopted grandchildren.** Wills that say "grandchildren" may or may not include step or adopted grandchildren depending on wording and state law. Be explicit.
- **Grandchildren not yet born.** Decide whether future grandchildren will share equally. A class gift ("all my grandchildren, now living or later born") covers them. Be specific about when the class closes.

Questions to settle:

- **Do all grandchildren receive the same amount?** ______________________
- **How should stepgrandchildren and adopted grandchildren be treated?** ______________________
- **What if a grandchild dies before me?** ______________________
- **Do I want to give unequal amounts for education?** ______________________
- **Have I told my adult children, or do I want to keep it private?** ______________________

## Gifts while you are alive

Giving during life lets you see the result and avoid probate for that money. The annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient (the 2025 figure, indexed for inflation) <!-- verify -->, which means gifts below the limit generally do not require a gift tax return. Larger gifts are not necessarily taxed, because they reduce your lifetime exemption, but they may require a filing. Tuition or medical payments made directly to a school or provider are treated separately under federal rules. Ask a tax professional, since details and state rules differ.

Gifts also leave you with less. If you may need money for care later, think about that first. See [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) for how gifts can interact with eligibility for care.

## Large estates and the generation-skipping transfer tax

For very large estates, federal law has a separate tax on transfers that skip a generation, such as gifts to grandchildren that bypass the grandchild's parent. It generally applies only above a high exemption, which for 2026 follows the same $15 million per person figure used for the federal estate tax <!-- verify -->. Most families will never be affected. If your estate approaches that level, a tax attorney can explain how the rules apply. See [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## A letter to your grandchildren

Money fades. Words last. A letter is not a legal document and does not have to be formal.

Prompts:

- **What I want you to know about me:** ______________________
- **A story from when I was your age:** ______________________
- **What I hope this gift helps you do:** ______________________
- **Something I learned about money:** ______________________
- **What I am proud of in you:** ______________________
- **A family tradition I hope you continue:** ______________________

Tip: write one general letter and a short personal note for each grandchild. Date it, and update it as they grow. Keep it with your will or give it to your executor with instructions for when to hand it over.

## Common mistakes

- Naming minors directly as beneficiaries on life insurance or retirement accounts.
- Naming "my grandchildren" without defining who is included.
- Choosing a custodian or trustee without asking them first.
- Giving money to a child "for the grandchild" with no written record.
- Forgetting to update the plan after a new birth, divorce or death.
- Setting a trust to pay out at an age that is still very young.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want a trust for grandchildren or an arrangement that lasts for years.
- Any grandchild has special needs or receives benefits.
- Your grandchildren live in different states, or you own property in more than one state.
- Your estate may be large enough to raise federal or state tax questions.
- Your family is blended, or there is tension between branches.
- You want to leave retirement accounts or life insurance to a trust.
