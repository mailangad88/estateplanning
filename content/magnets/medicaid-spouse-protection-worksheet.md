## How to use this worksheet

When one spouse enters a nursing home, the couple's money is not simply split in half. Federal rules, called spousal impoverishment protections, let the spouse at home (the **community spouse**) keep a share of savings and income. The dollar amounts are reset each year and differ by state, so this worksheet leaves them blank for you to fill in from the Medicaid office. It is general education, not legal advice.

## The three protections

| Protection | What it protects | Who sets the number |
|---|---|---|
| Community spouse resource allowance (CSRA) | A share of the couple's countable savings | Federal range, state picks within it |
| Minimum monthly maintenance needs allowance (MMMNA) | Monthly income of the spouse at home | Federal formula, adjusted yearly |
| The home | The house where the community spouse lives | Generally exempt while the spouse lives there |

## Step flow

1. Add up everything the couple owns on the day the nursing home stay begins (the "snapshot").
2. Subtract the exempt items: home, one car, personal belongings, and a burial plan.
3. Divide the remaining countable total. The community spouse keeps a protected share.
4. The spouse in care must spend down to the Medicaid limit, usually by paying for care or buying exempt things.
5. Compare the community spouse's monthly income with the MMMNA. If it is short, part of the other spouse's income can be assigned to cover the gap.

## If this, then that

| If this happens | Then this |
|---|---|
| Healthy spouse owns the home alone | The home is generally exempt while the spouse lives there |
| Applicant owns the home | A transfer to the spouse is generally allowed without a penalty |
| Healthy spouse has high income | Income is not counted against the applicant, only the applicant's income is |
| Spouse at home refuses to sign or share information | The application may be denied or delayed; some states let the applicant proceed under a refusal rule. Ask. |
| Healthy spouse dies first | Assets left to the spouse in care can count and break eligibility; a will or trust can redirect them |
| Applicant dies first | Estate recovery against the home may be put off while the spouse lives, then revived |

## Common planning tools

### Spousal transfers

Moving assets between spouses is generally exempt from the transfer penalty. This is a common first move. But it does not reduce the couple's countable total at the snapshot, so timing matters.

### Medicaid compliant annuity

An annuity can turn countable savings into a monthly income stream for the spouse at home. It has strict rules about term, payout and naming the state as a remainder beneficiary. It can be a good fit or a poor one, so get a review before you buy one.

### Spousal refusal

Some states let a community spouse refuse to give the money to the care spouse and still allow Medicaid to pay, with the state later seeking repayment from the refusing spouse. Many states do not allow it. Ask whether your state has this rule. <!-- verify: Illinois treatment of spousal refusal -->

### Planning for the healthy spouse's death

The healthy spouse should have a will or trust that leaves assets to a trust for the spouse in care, such as a supplemental needs trust, not outright. Otherwise the inheritance counts and Medicaid eligibility ends.

## Common mistakes

- [ ] Signing a power of attorney or a will that leaves everything outright to the spouse in care
- [ ] Moving money into the healthy spouse's name before the snapshot
- [ ] Spending down on gifts instead of on care or exempt purchases
- [ ] Assuming the healthy spouse's income counts against the applicant
- [ ] Missing the chance to request a higher MMMNA

## Questions to bring

- What is our state's CSRA for this year?
- What is the MMMNA, and can we ask for more?
- How is the snapshot date set?
- Does our state have a spousal refusal rule?
- What happens to the home if my spouse dies first?

## When to talk to an attorney

- One spouse is in care or likely to be soon
- The couple has more than modest savings, a business or a second property
- Income of the healthy spouse is below the MMMNA
- You want to buy an annuity or move the home
- You need to update a will so it works with Medicaid
