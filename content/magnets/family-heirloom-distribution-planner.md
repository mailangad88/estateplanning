**How to use this planner.** Parents can use Parts 1 to 3 now to set the rules while everyone is calm. Siblings dividing an estate can use the same parts after a death, with Part 4 as a meeting guide. Money rarely causes the worst fights. Meaning does: Dad's watch, Mom's recipe box, the table everyone ate at. This planner is general information, not legal advice. Your will or trust controls where it names a recipient.

## Part 1: List what matters

Before choosing a method, find out what people want. Ask each person privately and in writing, so nobody is pressured by the others.

- **Item or group of items:** ______________________
- **Who has asked for it, and why it matters to them:** ______________________
- **Rough value (low, medium, high):** ______________________
- **Can it be shared, copied or photographed?** Yes / No
- **Where it is and who has it now:** ______________________

Pay attention to items more than one person asked for. Those are your hot spots. Keep a short list of them, since most things on a typical list have no competition at all.

## Part 2: Choose a method

| Method | How it works | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rotation draft | Draw numbers, then take turns choosing one item at a time. Reverse the order each round. | Many items of mixed meaning | Older or louder siblings dominating the choices |
| Sticker method | Each person gets stickers in their own color. Everyone marks items they want. Single stickers go to that person. Items with more than one sticker go to a draft. | Large households of belongings | Needs a day and a walk-through together |
| Appraisal and balance | A neutral appraiser sets values. Whoever takes more value gives credit to the others. | Valuable art, jewelry or collections | Cost, and the feeling that sentimental items were priced |
| Sell or share | Sell an item and split proceeds, or rotate it by year. | Items nobody can part with, like a vacation table or a piece of art | Rotation needs clear shipping and insurance rules |

Most families use a mix. Use a draft or stickers for ordinary things, an appraisal only for items with real market value, and an agreed way for the one or two items everyone wants.

### Rotation draft rules

- [ ] Agree on who starts. Draw numbers from a hat. Do not let age or distance decide.
- [ ] Choose in rounds. Round 2 runs in reverse order, so the last person picks twice in a row at the turn.
- [ ] Anyone may pass. A passed item goes back in the pool.
- [ ] Out-of-town siblings join by video, with a shared photo album of the items.
- [ ] Decide how a person who cannot attend makes choices, such as by naming a proxy chooser.
- [ ] Keep a written list of who took what. Photograph every item.

### Sticker method rules

- [ ] Give each person a different color and an equal number of stickers per round, such as ten.
- [ ] Do the sticker round in silence. No negotiating.
- [ ] Items with one sticker go to that person. Items with several stickers go to the draft.
- [ ] Run a second round for leftovers.
- [ ] Leave out items listed in a will or trust, and items with a beneficiary in writing.

## Part 3: Fairness and value

Equal is not always fair. Ask the family which standard they want.

- **Equal in value, equal in number, or equal in meaning?** ______________________
- **Do earlier gifts or loans count?** Yes / No. If yes, list: ______________________
- **Does a sibling who gave years of care get first pick of something?** ______________________ (an unpaid caregiver often feels their work was invisible)
- **How will step-children and grandchildren be included?** ______________________

On appraisals: a written appraisal is usually worth paying for when an item may be worth a meaningful sum, or when the estate must be valued. For ordinary household goods, an appraisal costs more than it helps. Ask any appraiser how they charge, and avoid anyone paid a percentage of the value. Estate sale companies and auction houses can price items as well, but they are also hoping to sell them. Resale values for furniture, china and collections are often much lower than families expect.

## Part 4: Conflict prevention worksheet

Fill this in together before dividing anything.

- **Who is the person in charge of the process (usually the executor):** ______________________
- **A neutral helper if we get stuck (family friend, mediator, clergy):** ______________________
- **Ground rule: nobody removes items before the group meets.**  Agreed by: ______________________
- **Ground rule: no taking an item "to keep safe."** Agreed by: ______________________
- **Date and place of the walk-through:** ______________________
- **How we handle a tie on a single item:** coin flip / draw / alternate years / sell
- **What happens to items nobody wants:** donate / sell / dispose
- **Deadline for choices:** ______________________

Sample wording for a written agreement among siblings: "We agree to divide our parents' personal belongings using the rotation draft described above. Items named in the will go to the person named. We will not remove items until all of us have met. Disputes will go to [name] for a decision, and if we cannot resolve one, to a mediator, with the cost shared equally."

## Part 5: If you are the parent

You can make this easier now.

- Put a name and a story on important items, using the [personal property memorandum template](/free/personal-property-memorandum-template).
- Write why you chose, in a sentence per item. A reason turns a decision into a gift.
- Give some things away while alive, and note it in writing so it is not counted twice.
- Tell your executor which method you prefer for everything else.
- Take photographs with names on the back for old pictures, while you still remember who is in them.

## Common mistakes

1. **Dividing before the funeral is a memory.** Most families do better waiting a few weeks, unless a house must be emptied.
2. **Letting one person hold an item "for now."** It often becomes theirs.
3. **Skipping people who live far away.** Include them through video, or an agent they choose.
4. **Skipping the estate's legal status.** Until the executor has authority, items are not yet anyone's to give away. The [settling an estate guide](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step) explains the order of steps.

## When to talk to an attorney

- An heirloom is valuable, and the estate may need a formal valuation.
- The will or trust is silent or unclear about personal property.
- Siblings disagree about who is entitled to what, or someone has already removed items.
- A blended family means a surviving spouse and children each have a claim.
- Items include firearms, a collection, or property in another state.
