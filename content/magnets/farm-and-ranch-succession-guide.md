**How to use this guide.** Read the first three sections together, then use the timeline and agenda at the end to start the family conversation. This guide is educational and not individual advice. Tax, entity and land rules vary by state, and farm succession usually involves an attorney, a tax professional and a lender working together. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you what applies.

## The core problem

A farm or ranch is a home, a business, an asset and a way of life, all at once. Most of the family's wealth may be tied up in land, equipment and livestock, which are hard to divide and slow to sell. The operation's income may support one family, not three.

That leads to the familiar tension. One child works the land and wants to stay. Others have moved away and expect a fair share. Parents want to keep the farm in the family, retire with an income, and treat everyone well. These goals pull against each other, and you cannot satisfy all of them completely. Planning means choosing, early and deliberately, what matters most.

## Separate land from operation

Treat these as two questions.

- **The land** is the long-term asset. It may be passed to all heirs, held in an entity, leased or sold.
- **The operation** is the business: machinery, livestock, crops, contracts, customers and know-how. It requires someone to run it every day.

A common structure gives the operating heir the business and a long-term lease or option on the land, while the land itself is shared among all heirs or held in a family entity. Another approach transfers land to the farming child and balances the others with insurance, other property or notes. A third sells the land and divides cash. Each fits a different family.

Ask yourself:

- **Which assets must stay together for the farm to work?** ______________________
- **Which assets could be sold or divided without hurting the operation?** ______________________
- **What does the farming heir need to own, and what could they rent?** ______________________
- **How much income do my spouse and I need in retirement, and from where?** ______________________

## On-farm and off-farm heirs

### Fair does not always mean equal

Equal shares of the land can leave the farming child as a minority co-owner with siblings who live elsewhere, want rent or cash, and may disagree about decisions. But unequal shares can feel like favoritism and may leave the other children feeling their parents valued the farm over them.

Approaches families use:

- **Give the farming heir the operation and land at a price or terms that reflect their work,** and give the off-farm heirs other assets, life insurance or a note.
- **Use life insurance** to equalize. A policy owned by a trust or entity can provide cash to off-farm children at the owner's death.
- **Pay the farming heir for work in the meantime,** in wages and equity, so their sweat is recognized and not an unpaid gift.
- **Let the off-farm heirs hold land with a lease** to the farming heir at a fair rent.
- **Give the farming heir an option to buy out the others** over time on set terms.
- **Treat in-laws and grandchildren on purpose.** A son-in-law who has worked the farm for 15 years has no automatic rights.

Whatever you choose, explain it. Silence breeds suspicion. Tell your children why, in your own words.

### A fairness check

- [ ] Has the farming heir worked years without full pay?
- [ ] Have other children received help already (a down payment, a business loan, college)?
- [ ] Does any child depend on the farm for housing?
- [ ] Would any child be left with income from the land but no say?
- [ ] Is the plan written so a spouse of an heir cannot claim a share in a divorce?

## Entity options in general terms

Entities can make ownership, management and transfers easier. They also add cost, tax issues and paperwork. Common tools, described generally:

| Tool | General idea | Things to ask about |
|---|---|---|
| Sole ownership | Simple, but passes through probate unless a trust or deed is used | Probate, and value of the land on death |
| Revocable living trust | Holds title, avoids probate, names a successor trustee | Funding the trust with land and equipment |
| Limited liability company (LLC) | Holds land or the operation, with an operating agreement | Management rules, transfer limits, tax treatment |
| Family limited partnership | Parents keep management control while shares move to the next generation | Valuation discounts, formalities |
| Corporation | Shares can be gifted or sold in pieces | Tax consequences, S corporation rules |
| Separate entities | Land in one, operation in another | Lease terms, liability protection |

Separate entities for land and operation are common because the farming heir can then own the operating business and lease the land from a family entity. Which tool fits depends on taxes, lenders, state law and family goals. Agricultural lenders and programs may also have rules about changes in ownership.

Special estate tax rules for farmland, such as special-use valuation, have been available in federal law for qualifying farms, with conditions. The federal exemption is $15 million per person as of 2026<!-- verify -->, and some states tax estates or inheritances at lower levels. See [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and ask a tax professional about what applies to you.

Basis also matters. Property inherited at death generally receives a new tax basis at the date-of-death value, while property given during life generally carries the giver's original basis. That difference can change the tax on a later sale, so gifts of land during life should be weighed carefully.

## Buy-sell terms

If one heir will buy out others, or heirs may sell to each other, a written agreement helps. Terms to settle, in general terms:

- **Who can buy?** Family members, the entity, or outsiders.
- **Triggers.** Death, disability, divorce, retirement, bankruptcy, a wish to sell.
- **Price.** A formula, a regular appraisal, or a set method for valuing land and equipment. Farmland values change, so many agreements set an appraisal method rather than a number.
- **Payment terms.** Lump sum, installment note, interest rate, security.
- **Right of first refusal** before any share goes to an outsider.
- **Use of insurance** to fund death buyouts.
- **What happens to a spouse or children of an owner who dies.**
- **How disputes get resolved,** perhaps with mediation first.

## Keeping the land together

If your goal is to keep land in the family for generations, consider:

- Holding land in an entity, so shares transfer and the land does not split into pieces
- Transfer restrictions and rights of first refusal
- A long-term trust with a trustee and rules for use and sale
- A conservation easement, which can limit development and may have tax effects, and which an attorney and a land trust can explain
- A clear process for when an heir wants out

Forcing land to stay forever can backfire if later generations cannot afford it. Many families choose flexibility with a family buyout first.

## Equipment, livestock and records

Many succession plans fail on the unglamorous details. Gather:

- [ ] Deeds, leases (including leases from others), easements, mineral and water rights
- [ ] Loan documents and lender contacts
- [ ] Equipment list with serial numbers, titles and liens
- [ ] Livestock inventory, brands, registrations and breeding records
- [ ] Crop insurance, farm program and government payment records
- [ ] Contracts with buyers and suppliers
- [ ] Tax returns for the last several years and a depreciation schedule
- [ ] Passwords and access to the farm software, grain accounts and bank
- [ ] A list of key people: the vet, the banker, the accountant, the agronomist
- [ ] Written notes on how things work: gates, wells, irrigation, equipment quirks, what each field needs

The last item matters more than most families expect. Knowledge held only in a parent's head is the asset most at risk.

## A succession timeline

Ranges are loose. Adjust to your family.

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| 10 or more years before retirement | Start the conversation. Decide who may want to farm. Gather records. |
| 7 to 10 years | Hold the first family meeting. Meet with an attorney, tax professional and lender. Set a draft goal. |
| 5 to 7 years | Give the successor real decisions and a paid role. Draft entity and buy-sell terms. |
| 3 to 5 years | Sign the documents. Transfer some management and ownership. Review insurance. |
| 1 to 3 years | Finish transfers, retirement income plan and leases. Update wills, trusts and powers of attorney. |
| After handoff | Stay available, review annually, update when family changes. |

If a health event is already on the horizon, compress the timeline and start with a power of attorney and health care directive.

## Family meeting agenda

Plan one to two hours. Consider a neutral facilitator, such as an attorney, mediator or extension agent.

1. **Welcome and ground rules** (10 minutes): listen, no interruptions, no decisions made today.
2. **Parents' goals** (15 minutes): what do you hope for the land, the family and your retirement?
3. **Each heir's goals** (30 minutes): who wants to farm, who does not, what do you need from this?
4. **The facts** (15 minutes): a simple summary of assets and debts, shared in advance if possible.
5. **Options** (20 minutes): outline the structures above, without choosing.
6. **Concerns** (15 minutes): what worries each person?
7. **Next steps** (10 minutes): who will gather records, who will see the attorney, when to meet again.

Take notes and send them afterward.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting.** Estates settle on the worst timeline: an illness or a death.
- **Assuming equal means fair, or that fair means equal.**
- **Treating the farming heir as unpaid help.**
- **Keeping the plan secret** until after a death.
- **Forgetting the retirement income** of the parents.
- **Ignoring in-laws, spouses and divorce risk.**
- **Skipping the lender.**

## When to talk to an attorney

- Land is held in more than one name or entity, or in more than one state.
- You are considering gifting land during life.
- Heirs disagree or one heir has been working without a written deal.
- You want to use an LLC, partnership or trust to hold land or the operation.
- Your estate could face federal or state estate tax.
- Medicaid planning for long-term care is a concern. See [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning).
