**How to use this worksheet.** Signing a trust does not move your house into it. A deed does. Fill in each blank, then take this page to your attorney or the person preparing the deed. Do not use a form that does not match your state, and do not record a deed until the questions in Part 3 are answered. Rules and fees vary by state and county. Where we give an Illinois example, it is labelled.

## Part 1: Facts for the deed

Copy these exactly from your current deed, tax bill and trust.

- **Owner name(s) exactly as on the current deed:** ______________________
- **Street address:** ______________________
- **County:** ______________________
- **Parcel number (PIN) from the tax bill:** ______________________
- **Full legal description from the current deed, copied word for word:** ______________________
- **How title is held now (sole, joint tenants, tenants in common, entireties):** ______________________
- **Name of the trust exactly as written:** ______________________
- **Date the trust was signed:** ______________________
- **Trustee(s) who will accept title:** ______________________
- **Where the current deed was recorded (book and page, or document number):** ______________________

Mistakes to avoid:

- Do not retype the legal description from memory. One wrong digit can leave the house out of the trust.
- A trust is not a person. The deed should name the trustee(s) as holder, "of the [name] Trust dated [date]."
- If there are two owners, both should sign unless the attorney says otherwise.

## Part 2: The deed steps

- [ ] My attorney has confirmed which deed fits (in many states a warranty, quitclaim or special trust deed).
- [ ] The draft names the correct grantor(s) and the trustee as grantee.
- [ ] The deed states that the transfer is to a revocable trust for no payment.
- [ ] I signed it in front of a notary, with a witness if my state requires.
- [ ] My attorney or I filed any transfer tax or exemption statement that the county requires. In many places a transfer to your own revocable trust is exempt, but the exemption has to be claimed.
- [ ] The deed was recorded with the county recorder or registrar.
- [ ] I received the recorded copy.
- [ ] I filed it with my trust documents and sent a copy to my attorney.

- **Recording fee quoted:** ______________________
- **Attorney or title fee for preparing the deed:** ______________________

## Part 3: Before you record

### Title insurance

Your owner's title insurance policy protects you from certain claims against ownership. Many policies continue after a transfer to your own revocable trust, but wording differs by company and policy date.

- [ ] I have found my owner's policy.
- [ ] I called the insurer or checked the policy to confirm that it continues after a transfer to my trust.
- [ ] If not, I asked about an endorsement, and what it costs.
- **Policy number and company:** ______________________

### Property tax exemptions

Homestead and senior exemptions often depend on you owning or occupying the home. A trust can affect how they are claimed.

- [ ] I identified every exemption we receive now.
- [ ] I asked the assessor's office whether a transfer to a revocable trust affects it, and whether any form must be refiled.
- [ ] I will watch for the next bill to see that the exemption is still shown.

Illinois example: In Illinois, a homeowner who lives in the home can generally keep homestead exemptions when the home is held in a revocable trust, but some counties ask for a certification or updated form. Ask the county assessor or chief county assessment officer before recording.

### Mortgage

Federal law (the Garn-St Germain Act) generally protects a transfer of an owner-occupied home into a living trust from a "due on sale" demand, where the borrower stays a beneficiary and keeps living there. That is not a reason to skip a call.

- [ ] I called or wrote to my lender.
- [ ] I asked for confirmation that the loan stays in place after the transfer.
- [ ] I kept the reply.
- **Loan servicer and loan number:** ______________________

Sample note to lender:

> "I am moving my primary residence at [address] into my revocable living trust, of which I am a trustee and beneficiary. I will continue to live in the home and make payments. Please confirm that this transfer does not change the terms of my loan, and tell me if you need a copy of the deed or trust certificate."

### Homeowners insurance

- [ ] I asked the insurer to add the trust and trustee as additional insured on the policy.
- [ ] I confirmed there is no gap in coverage.

## Part 4: After the deed is recorded

- [ ] I have a recorded copy stamped by the county.
- [ ] I confirmed on the county website that the trust is now shown as owner, if online records exist.
- [ ] My pour-over will names the trust as beneficiary.
- [ ] I added the house to my trust schedule of assets.
- [ ] I told my successor trustee where the deed and mortgage records are.
- [ ] I will review the property tax bill when it arrives.

## When something is not simple

| Situation | Why it needs a closer look |
|---|---|
| Two or more owners | Each owner must sign, and shares may need to be kept equal |
| Home in a different state from the trust | Recording rules and tax statements differ |
| Reverse mortgage or home equity line | The lender may have its own consent rules |
| Condo or co-op | The association may need notice or approval |
| Home owned partly by a trust already | Check the existing language |
| Rental or part-business use | Insurance and liability may change |

## When to talk to an attorney

- The legal description on your deed looks unclear or incomplete.
- The home is owned with someone who is not signing the trust.
- You have a reverse mortgage, a HELOC or other loans against the home.
- You are thinking about adding a child to the deed instead of using a trust.
- You may apply for Medicaid long-term care in the coming years.
- The house is in more than one state or in a cooperative.
