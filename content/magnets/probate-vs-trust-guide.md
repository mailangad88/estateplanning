**How to use this guide.** Read the comparison tables, then fill in the asset sort near the end. The goal is to understand the trade-offs, not to pick a winner. The right answer depends on your state, your assets and your family, and an attorney licensed in your state can tell you which rules apply.

## The short version

Probate is a court process that settles a will or an estate with no plan in place. A revocable living trust is a private arrangement you set up while alive, which a successor trustee runs after you die or if you cannot manage things. Assets in a properly funded trust generally skip probate. Many assets skip probate anyway, because they pass by beneficiary form or joint ownership.

## Side-by-side comparison

### How each one works

| | Probate (with a will) | Revocable living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Who is in charge after death | Executor, appointed and supervised by a court | Successor trustee, acting under the trust document |
| Court involvement | Yes, to some degree, in most states | Usually none for assets held in the trust |
| Does it work if you become incapacitated | No, a will does nothing until death | Yes, the successor trustee can step in |
| Where it starts | A will, signed with required formalities | A trust document, plus moving assets into it |
| Can you change it | A will can be changed until death | You can usually change or revoke it while alive |

### Cost, time and privacy

| | Probate | Trust |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | Often lower | Often higher |
| Cost after death | Court fees, possible attorney and executor fees, rules vary by state | Trustee costs and legal help, usually lower court costs |
| Time | Months to more than a year, depending on the state and the estate | Often faster for trust assets, though taxes and creditors still take time |
| Privacy | Wills filed with the court are generally public records | Trusts generally stay private |
| Effort during life | Little | Must retitle accounts and property into the trust |
| Contest risk | Open to challenge in a public court | Can still be contested, but process differs |

Use the [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) for a rough sense of fees, but state rules vary and these are estimates only.

### Control and flexibility

| | Will | Trust |
|---|---|---|
| Gifts at set ages | Needs a testamentary trust, which goes through probate | Can be built into the trust directly |
| Out-of-state real estate | May need a second probate in another state | Often avoids it if the property is titled to the trust |
| Protecting a beneficiary who struggles with money | Limited | Common reason families use a trust |
| Naming a guardian for kids | Yes, a will is where it usually goes | No, this is generally done in a will |

## Myths versus facts

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| A will avoids probate | A will does not avoid probate. It tells the court what to do. |
| A trust is only for the wealthy | Trusts are used by families of many sizes, but cost and effort mean they do not suit everyone. |
| A trust avoids all taxes | A revocable trust is generally tax-neutral. It does not by itself reduce income or estate tax. |
| Once I sign a trust, I am done | A trust works only for assets retitled into it or with the trust as beneficiary. This is called funding. |
| Probate always takes years | Time varies by state and by how complicated the estate is. Some are simple and short. |
| Joint ownership is a free fix | It can skip probate, but may expose property to a co-owner's creditors or cause gift and control issues. |
| A trust means no court ever | Disputes and unusual situations can still end up in court. |
| Small estates always go through full probate | Many states have simplified procedures for small estates. Limits vary. |

## What skips probate without a trust

Many assets are non-probate by design. They go to whoever is named on a form or on title.

- Retirement accounts and life insurance with a living beneficiary.
- Bank accounts with payable-on-death (POD) designations.
- Brokerage accounts with transfer-on-death (TOD) designations.
- Property owned jointly with right of survivorship.
- Real estate with a transfer-on-death deed, in states that allow them.

See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) for how these forms interact with a will.

### What to look for

- **Count the "yes" and "unsure" rows.** A short list may point toward a simple will and updated forms.
- **Look for gaps.** An account with no beneficiary falls back into probate.
- **Look for mismatches.** A will that leaves everything to one child, with a beneficiary form naming another, will usually follow the form.

## Common mistakes

- Signing a trust and never retitling assets into it.
- Naming the trust as beneficiary of a retirement account without understanding the tax rules.
- Forgetting a pour-over will, the safety net for assets that were never moved.
- Adding a child to a house title to "avoid probate" without asking about taxes and creditors.
- Assuming a family member's state rules match yours.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own real estate in more than one state.
- You have a blended family, a child with special needs or a beneficiary who may not manage money well.
- You are weighing whether the cost and effort of a trust fit your situation.
- A retirement account or business is a large part of your assets.
- You already have a trust and are not sure everything has been moved into it.
