**How to use this guide.** Read it on your own first, then share it with your brothers and sisters if you wish. You do not have to do everything at once. Grief shows up differently in each person, and it can look like anger, silence or sudden attention to small details. This guide offers ways to keep the practical work from damaging the relationships. Laws and court procedures vary by state.

## Grief and money get tangled

When a parent dies, siblings often meet again at the same moment they are asked to make decisions about money, property and memories. Old patterns come back. The oldest takes charge. The one who lives far away feels guilty. The one who lived nearby feels unseen.

None of this makes anyone a bad person. It does mean a few agreements early on can spare a lot of pain later.

## Clarify the roles

Different people may carry different jobs. Naming them out loud helps.

| Role | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Executor or personal representative** | The person named in the will, or appointed by the court, who has legal authority to manage and settle the estate |
| **Successor trustee** | If the parent had a trust, the person who manages and distributes trust assets |
| **Beneficiary** | A person who receives something from the will, the trust or an account |
| **Helper** | A sibling or relative who assists but has no legal authority |

An executor is not automatically "in charge" of the family. The role is a legal duty to follow the will, protect assets, pay debts and taxes, and distribute what is left. See [choosing an executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) and [settling an estate step by step](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step).

Questions to settle early:

- **Who is the executor or trustee, and are they willing?** ______________________
- **Who will help with which tasks (mail, house, accounts, funeral, taxes)?** ______________________
- **How often will we update each other, and how (call, group text, shared document)?** ______________________
- **Who speaks to the attorney and the bank?** ______________________

## What an executor owes the other beneficiaries

In general terms, an executor or trustee is a **fiduciary**. That means they must act honestly, in the interest of the beneficiaries, and not for personal advantage. Details vary by state, but duties commonly include:

- **Following the will or trust** and applicable law.
- **Keeping estate money separate** from personal money.
- **Protecting and managing assets** with reasonable care.
- **Keeping records** of money in and out.
- **Informing beneficiaries.** Many states require notice that the estate is open, and some require periodic updates.
- **Giving an accounting** of what came in, what was paid and what is left. Whether and when a formal accounting is required varies by state and by court.
- **Paying debts and taxes** before distributing.
- **Treating beneficiaries fairly**, even when they are family.

An executor may generally be paid reasonable compensation or can decline it. If siblings disagree about it, talk early and in writing.

## Transparency is cheaper than distrust

Most estate disputes begin when someone feels left out of the information, not because of the will itself. A few habits help.

- [ ] Share a **short summary** of the will or trust with all beneficiaries.
- [ ] Keep a **simple ledger** of income, expenses and balances. A spreadsheet is fine.
- [ ] Open a **separate estate account** and avoid using personal accounts.
- [ ] Share **statements** on request, or send a monthly summary.
- [ ] Put **significant decisions** in writing, such as sale of the house, hiring a lawyer or paying an unusual bill.
- [ ] Keep receipts for funeral costs, repairs and fees.
- [ ] Hold a **short call every few weeks** with all siblings, even when there is little news.

If you are not the executor and feel uninformed, ask in a calm, specific way: "Could you send me a list of the accounts and the current balances?" is more likely to get a response than an accusation.

## Dividing personal items

Money is often easier to divide than a coat, a recipe box or a set of dishes. These items carry stories. Do not underestimate how much they matter.

First, check the will or any written list. Many parents leave a list of who should receive specific items. A written wish does not always bind, but it is usually respected. Do not start giving things away until the executor has confirmed that doing so is allowed.

Common ways to divide:

- **Rotation draws.** Siblings take turns choosing an item. Draw lots or draw numbers for the order, then reverse the order each round (1-2-3, then 3-2-1) to balance the advantage.
- **Tags or sticky notes.** Each person places a sticker on items they want. Where two people want the same thing, use a draw, a conversation or a later swap.
- **Photo catalogs.** If siblings live far apart, photograph and number everything and select from the photos.
- **Appraisals.** For jewelry, art, antiques, collections or firearms, a professional appraisal can establish value so that division can be equalized. Appraisal costs are usually an estate expense. Some items have restrictions on transfer.
- **Sharing.** Photo albums can be scanned. A family quilt can be displayed in rotation.
- **Selling and splitting.** Items nobody wants can be donated or sold, with proceeds shared.

Ground rules that tend to help:

1. **No one removes items before the group agrees.**
2. **Everyone can ask for something and be heard.**
3. **Pause if tempers rise.** Resume another day.
4. **Take photos** of the house before anything moves, for the record.
5. **Remember that money and memory are not the same.** An item of small market value may matter greatly to someone.

## The family home

The house is often the largest and most emotional asset. There are several common options.

| Option | Things to consider |
|---|---|
| **Sell and split proceeds** | Simple and fair. Needs agreement on listing, repairs and timing. |
| **One sibling buys the others out** | Requires an appraisal and financing. Fairness depends on the price used. |
| **Keep it jointly** | Possible, but needs written rules for costs, use, and what happens if someone wants to leave. |
| **Rent it** | Brings income but adds landlord duties and risk. |
| **Hold it for a short time** | Costs continue: taxes, insurance, upkeep. Vacant homes may have insurance limits. |

Tax rules for an inherited home often differ from those for a home you have owned a long time, so a tax professional can help you understand the basis and capital gains. If the home is in a trust or passes by deed, the process differs from probate. See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).

If one sibling lived in the home as a caregiver, that person's contribution may deserve a respectful conversation. It does not, by itself, change what the will provides.

## When siblings disagree

Disagreement is common and does not mean the family is broken. Steps to try, in order:

1. **Name the issue narrowly.** "We disagree on the sale price," not "you always do this."
2. **Return to the documents.** What does the will or trust say?
3. **Ask for information** before drawing conclusions.
4. **Take a break.** Even a few days can help.
5. **Ask the estate's attorney** to explain options to everyone, keeping in mind that an attorney for the executor represents the executor, not each sibling.
6. **Try mediation.**

### When mediation helps

A mediator is a neutral person who helps people reach their own agreement. It is typically less expensive and less public than court, and more private. Many probate courts offer or encourage it.

Mediation tends to help when:

- The dispute is about items, the house, or how decisions are made rather than about legal validity.
- Siblings still want a relationship after the estate closes.
- Communication has broken down but everyone is willing to try.
- Cost and delay are growing.

Mediation may not fit when someone refuses to participate, when fraud or abuse is alleged, or when a court order is needed quickly. An attorney can help you judge.

## A short sibling agreement

You may find it useful to write a simple page you all sign, covering:

- **Who the contact person is:** ______________________
- **How often we update each other:** ______________________
- **How we will divide personal items:** ______________________
- **How we will decide about the house:** ______________________
- **How we will handle disagreements (for example, mediation before court):** ______________________

This is not a legal contract, though it can help set a tone. Have an attorney review it if you want it to be binding.

## Looking after yourself

You may be tired, grieving and handling many tasks. Taking breaks, asking for help and keeping some days free of estate talk can be as important as anything above. If a conversation is going badly, it is fine to say, "I care about this, and I need to stop for today."

## When to talk to an attorney

- You suspect the executor is not following the will, hiding information or mixing funds.
- The will or trust seems unclear, outdated or different from what your parent said.
- A sibling is contesting the will, or you are considering doing so. Deadlines can be short.
- A parent had a second marriage, a business, or property in more than one state.
- You are the executor and unsure about duties, taxes or personal liability.
- Mediation is being considered and you want to understand your rights first.
