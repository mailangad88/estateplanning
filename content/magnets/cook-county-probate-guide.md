## How to use this guide

We are sorry you are dealing with this. A probate case is paperwork, and it can be done one step at a time. This guide covers estates for people who lived in Cook County, Illinois. It pairs with the general [Illinois probate checklist](/free/illinois-probate-checklist). Court practices change, so check each detail with the clerk or an attorney before you rely on it. This is education, not legal advice.

## Does the estate belong in Cook County?

Illinois law sends a probate case to the county where the person had a known place of residence (755 ILCS 5/5-1). If they lived in Cook County, the case belongs there. If they lived elsewhere in Illinois, it belongs in that county. If they lived outside Illinois, the rules look to where their Illinois real estate or personal property sits.

Before you file anything, ask whether a court case is needed at all. A small estate affidavit may work for personal property at or under $150,000 for a death on or after August 15, 2025. Read the [small estate affidavit guide](/free/illinois-small-estate-affidavit-guide). A house in one person's name, or a dispute in the family, usually points to a court case.

## Where the Probate Division is

The Probate Division of the Circuit Court of Cook County sits in the Richard J. Daley Center, 50 West Washington Street, Chicago, IL 60602. According to the court's Probate Division page:

- The division handles estates of people who died, adult guardianship and minor guardianship.
- Judges sit in courtrooms 1802 through 1813 on the 18th floor.
- The Clerk of the Circuit Court's probate office is in Room 1202 on the 12th floor.
- The division's phone number is (312) 603-5943. The clerk's probate number is (312) 603-6441.

Cook County has suburban courthouses for other kinds of cases. The court's page names the Daley Center as the Probate Division's home, so call the clerk before you assume a suburban courthouse can accept a probate filing.

## The steps, in order

| Step | What happens | Illinois rule to know |
|---|---|---|
| 1. File the will | The person holding the original will gives it to the circuit clerk | Do it promptly. Hiding a will for 30 days after learning of the death can be a Class 3 felony (755 ILCS 5/6-1) |
| 2. Petition | A petition asks the court to admit the will and appoint an executor, or to appoint an administrator if there is no will | Ask whether independent administration fits, which means less court supervision |
| 3. Hearing | The judge admits the will and issues letters of office | A will is proved by two witnesses or by an attestation affidavit (755 ILCS 5/6-4) |
| 4. Notice to heirs | The representative mails notice to heirs and legatees | Within 14 days after the order (755 ILCS 5/6-10) |
| 5. Notice to creditors | Published once a week for three weeks, and mailed to known creditors | The claim date is the later of six months from first publication or three months from mailing (755 ILCS 5/18-3) |
| 6. Inventory | The representative files a verified list of assets | Within 60 days after letters issue (755 ILCS 5/14-1) |
| 7. Close | Pay claims and taxes, distribute, file closing papers | Claims are barred two years after death in any event (755 ILCS 5/18-12) |

Two more deadlines matter to family members. An interested person can ask for formal proof of the will within 42 days after the order admitting it (755 ILCS 5/6-21). A will contest must be filed within six months after the will is admitted (755 ILCS 5/8-1). Failing to receive notice does not stretch the six months.

## Filing and forms

Probate papers are generally filed electronically with the Clerk of the Circuit Court. The original will is a special case, so ask the clerk how and where to deliver it. The clerk's office posts probate forms, and the Probate Division page links to court orders and self-help resources. Filing fees depend on the type of case and the estate, so check the clerk's current fee schedule instead of relying on a number from a website that may be out of date.

- [ ] Original will located and delivered to the clerk
- [ ] Death certificate copies ordered
- [ ] List of heirs with addresses prepared (spouse, children, or the next relatives under [Illinois intestacy rules](/free/illinois-intestacy-chart) if there is no will)
- [ ] Petition prepared and filed
- [ ] Hearing date noted
- [ ] Bond question answered (the will may waive it)
- [ ] Certified copies of letters ordered for banks

## Hearings and Zoom

The Probate Division reopened for in-person operations after the pandemic. Remote appearances by Zoom are still allowed in some situations, and the court points to Amended General Administrative Order 2023-P-01 for the rules. Do not assume you can appear by video. Ask the courtroom or your attorney, and write down the instructions for your judge.

- [ ] Confirm whether your hearing is in person or remote
- [ ] Keep the notice of hearing and any Zoom details in one folder
- [ ] Bring photo ID and the original documents the notice lists

## Help close to the courthouse

The court's Probate Division page lists Chicago Volunteer Legal Services, which runs a Probate Court Assistance Program for people who qualify. Mediation is also available in the division. Illinois courts generally expect an estate representative to have a lawyer, so even if you look for free help, ask what the program can and cannot do.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The will names no executor, or the named person cannot or will not serve
- A family member may contest the will or disagrees about who should serve
- The estate owns real estate, a business or out-of-state property
- You are unsure whether the small estate affidavit is allowed
- A creditor sends a claim you do not understand
- You are asked to sign something at the courthouse and are not sure what it does
