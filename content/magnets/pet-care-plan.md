**How to use this planner.** Fill it out for each pet, then keep copies with your will and where a trusted person can reach them. Pets are legally treated as property in most places, so a clear plan matters. Rules about pet trusts and gifts to caregivers vary by state. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you which tools are available.

## Why pets need a plan

If you die or become unable to care for an animal, no one has the legal authority to act unless you have named them. A pet may end up in a shelter while family sorts things out. Without a will, a pet goes to your heirs as part of the estate, and they may not want it. Neighbours and friends may step in, but without money or instructions.

A plan answers three questions: who, how much, and how.

### Common mistakes

- Naming someone without asking.
- Choosing a person whose landlord forbids pets.
- Splitting bonded animals without saying so.
- Forgetting an emergency plan for the days before the caregiver arrives.
- Skipping a backup.

### Ways to leave money for pets

| Option | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Informal promise | You tell someone you will leave money | Not enforceable, and a will does not carry a verbal promise |
| Gift in a will to the caregiver | A will leaves money or the pet to a named person with a wish to care for the animal | The gift is generally not binding on what the caregiver does with it |
| Pet trust | A trust holds funds for a pet's care under written terms, with a trustee | Pet trusts are authorized in many states. Rules vary on length, enforcement and unused funds. |
| Gift to a rescue or sanctuary | A direct gift to an organization that takes the animal | Confirm the organization accepts it, and its policy |
| Life insurance or account designation | Funds pass by form, not by will | Check that the person named understands the purpose |

### How a pet trust usually works

You (the grantor) write instructions. A trustee holds the money. A caregiver has the pet. The trustee pays the caregiver, or reimburses costs, following the terms you set. Many trusts name a person to check on the pet, and say what happens to leftover money after the pet dies.

Splitting the roles, so the caregiver and trustee are different people, is a common safeguard. Some states limit how long a pet trust can last, and some courts can reduce a trust they consider too large for the animal's needs.

### Worked example

Ana has two cats, ages 6 and 9, and a dog, age 4. She estimates $3,000 per year for all three. She expects 12 years of care, so $36,000 plus a margin. She sets up a small pet trust funded by a life insurance policy. Her friend Luis is trustee, and her sister is caregiver. The trust pays $250 per month to the sister and covers vet bills on receipt. Any money left after the last pet dies goes to a local rescue.

## Step 4: Make the plan visible

- [ ] Put a wallet card or note in your wallet: "I have pets at home. Call ______"
- [ ] Give your caregiver a key and the instruction sheets
- [ ] Tell your vet who the caregiver is, in writing
- [ ] Keep records with your will and tell your executor where they are
- [ ] Update the plan after any move, health change or new pet
- [ ] Put a notice near the door listing animals inside, for first responders

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to set up a pet trust or are unsure whether your state allows one.
- You own horses, exotic animals or many animals, where costs and care are higher.
- You want to leave money in a will and keep your pet with a caregiver.
- You are in a relationship that ends or are in a blended household, and ownership is unclear.
- You are caring for an animal that is a service or emotional support animal.
