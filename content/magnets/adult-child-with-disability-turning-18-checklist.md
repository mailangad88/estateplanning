## How to use this checklist

At 18, the law generally treats your child as an adult, whatever their abilities. Parents lose automatic authority over medical, school and money decisions. This checklist helps you plan the handoff. Start around age 16 to 17 if you can. Rules vary by state and by program, so use it to prepare questions, not as legal advice.

## What changes on the 18th birthday

- **Decision rights:** Doctors, schools and banks may stop sharing information with you without your child's consent or a legal document.
- **SSI:** Social Security generally stops counting your income and resources and looks only at your child's own. Many young adults who were turned down as children qualify at 18. Social Security also reviews the disability using the adult standard, usually within a year of the birthday.
- **Medicaid:** Eligibility often follows SSI or a separate state rule. Ask your state agency how it will treat your child as an adult.
- **School services:** Special education rights usually transfer to the student at the age set by state law, and often last until a later age. Ask the school district.
- **Privacy:** Medical and school records are protected from you unless your child signs releases.

## Step 2: Compare guardianship with alternatives

Guardianship means a court finds that an adult cannot make some or all decisions and appoints someone to make them. It can be limited to certain areas in many states. It removes legal rights, so many courts expect families to consider less restrictive options first. Rules vary widely by state.

| Option | What it does | Keeps your child's rights? |
|---|---|---|
| Health care power of attorney | Names who speaks for medical choices | Yes, while they have capacity |
| Financial power of attorney | Lets an agent handle money | Yes, while they have capacity |
| HIPAA release | Lets doctors talk with you | Yes |
| Supported decision-making | Child decides with chosen helpers (some states have laws for it) | Yes |
| Representative payee | Manages Social Security benefits only | Yes |
| Limited guardianship | Court gives a guardian only the powers needed | Partly |
| Full guardianship | Court transfers most decisions | Largely no |

Your child must be able to understand and sign a power of attorney. An attorney can tell you whether that is realistic. See [power of attorney vs guardianship](/compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) and [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

- [ ] Ask the attorney whether your child can sign a power of attorney
- [ ] If guardianship is likely, ask when to file and how long it takes in your county
- [ ] Ask whether a limited guardianship is available
- [ ] Ask who must be notified and whether a medical report is required

## Step 3: Prepare for SSI and Medicaid

- [ ] Apply or update the SSI application on or near the 18th birthday, as Social Security directs
- [ ] Gather medical records, school evaluations and the IEP
- [ ] Open a bank account in your child's own name for benefits, if they can use one
- [ ] Ask whether you should become the representative payee
- [ ] Learn the SSI resource limit, which is $2,000 for an individual, and what counts
- [ ] Keep written records of every report you make to Social Security
- [ ] Ask what happens to Medicaid and any waiver services at 18

Money in your child's name can end SSI eligibility, so do not simply retitle accounts to them or leave them money directly.

## Step 4: Set up an ABLE account

An ABLE account lets money be saved for disability costs without counting against SSI up to a limit. Social Security excludes the first $100,000. Beginning in 2026, the disability generally must have started before age 46. Annual contributions are tied to the annual gift tax exclusion, which is $19,000 for 2026. Confirm the current limit with the plan.

- [ ] Compare state ABLE programs, since you can often use another state's plan
- [ ] Open the account and name your child as the owner
- [ ] Decide who may contribute, such as grandparents
- [ ] Keep receipts for spending

Read more in [special needs trust vs ABLE account](/compare/special-needs-trust-vs-able-account).

## Step 5: Plan the money your child will inherit

A direct inheritance can disrupt benefits. A supplemental needs trust can hold it instead. See [special needs trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts).

- [ ] Update your will so your child is not a direct beneficiary
- [ ] Ask relatives to leave gifts to the trust, not to your child
- [ ] Review beneficiary forms on life insurance and retirement accounts
- [ ] Name a trustee and a backup

## Step 6: Build the transition plan

- [ ] Ask the school for the transition plan section of the IEP by age 16, or earlier if state law requires
- [ ] Contact your state vocational rehabilitation office
- [ ] Ask about day programs, supported employment and housing waiting lists, which can be long
- [ ] Write a letter of intent describing routines, medical needs and preferences

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are thinking about guardianship or want to avoid it
- Your child may not be able to sign a power of attorney
- You want a supplemental needs trust written into your plan
- Your child already has savings, a settlement or an inheritance
- You are unsure how your state's Medicaid waiver treats income or assets
