**How to use this kit.** Distance makes everything harder: you cannot see small changes, and you cannot drop by. This kit covers the practical parts. Work through it in order over a few weeks. It focuses on logistics and paperwork and complements the [aging parent conversation kit](/free/aging-parent-conversation-kit), which covers how to start the talk.

## Part 1: Your local team

You cannot be everywhere, so build a network of people who are.

A geriatric care manager is a professional, often a nurse or social worker, who assesses needs and coordinates services for a fee. Fees and quality vary, so ask for references. Local Area Agencies on Aging can point to services and can be reached through the Eldercare Locator.

## Part 2: Legal access

Without paperwork, banks, doctors and agencies may not talk to you, however close you are.

- [ ] **Health care power of attorney** naming you or a nearby sibling. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).
- [ ] **Durable financial power of attorney** so you can pay bills and manage accounts if your parent cannot.
- [ ] **HIPAA release** so doctors can discuss care with you.
- [ ] **Living will** so wishes are known.
- [ ] **Bank forms** adding you as a contact or agent, or using the bank's own power of attorney form.
- [ ] **Authorization at the pharmacy and insurer** to speak with you.
- [ ] **Medicare and Social Security** authorization, where offered, to talk with you.

Your parent needs mental capacity to sign. If dementia is a worry, do this soon. If capacity is already in question, ask an attorney about options.

## Part 3: What to watch for from afar

Check in by phone or video on a regular schedule. Watch for:

- [ ] Unopened mail or unpaid bills
- [ ] Weight loss, missed medications or confusion on the phone
- [ ] A messy house, spoiled food or a neglected yard
- [ ] Unexplained bruises or falls
- [ ] New people in the parent's life asking for money
- [ ] Repeated stories, missed appointments or changes in mood
- [ ] A car with dents

Ask neighbors to tell you what they notice. Report suspected exploitation to Adult Protective Services in your parent's county.

## Part 4: Make each visit count

Before you go:

- [ ] Ask your parent what they want to do or discuss.
- [ ] Schedule doctor and bank appointments ahead.
- [ ] Bring a notebook or a laptop.
- [ ] Bring blank forms from this kit.

During the visit:

- [ ] Look at the fridge, medicine cabinet, mail pile and car.
- [ ] Walk the house for fall hazards: rugs, stairs, lighting, bathrooms.
- [ ] Meet the neighbors and helpers.
- [ ] Review the medication list with the pharmacy.
- [ ] Set up bill pay, mail forwarding or autopay.
- [ ] Update the contacts list and emergency numbers.
- [ ] Spend time together that is not about tasks.

## Part 5: The emergency plan

Prepare before it is urgent.

- **Who can reach my parent's home in an hour:** ______________________
- **Hospital my parent prefers:** ______________________
- **Where the medication list and documents are:** ______________________
- **Spare key location:** ______________________
- **My travel plan: airline, drive time, lodging:** ______________________
- **My employer's family leave policy:** ______________________
- **Money for emergency travel:** ______________________

Check whether the Family and Medical Leave Act applies to your job. It can give eligible employees unpaid, job-protected leave to care for a parent, but eligibility depends on your employer and your hours. Some states and employers offer more.

Put the key facts on a single page and send it to siblings and local helpers. The [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list) can serve as a base.

## Part 6: Money and costs

- **Parent's income and benefits:** ______________________
- **Monthly expenses:** ______________________
- **Insurance coverage (Medicare, supplement, long-term care):** ______________________
- **Cost of local help per hour:** ______________________
- **Who is paying what:** ______________________

Keep a record of anything you pay for your parent. If siblings share the work, agree on how costs and time are counted. Caregiving costs may be a tax issue in some cases, so ask a tax professional. If long-term care costs loom, read [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) before moving or giving away assets, because gifts can trigger a look-back penalty.

## Part 7: Hard conversations

Sample wording for a parent who resists help:

> "I am not trying to take over. I want you to stay in your home as long as it is safe. Can we try this for a month and see how it goes?"

Sample wording for siblings:

> "I cannot be there every week, but I can handle bills and calls. Can you cover visits? Let's write down who does what."

## Part 8: Take care of yourself

- [ ] Set limits on calls and visits you can sustain.
- [ ] Ask siblings and friends for specific help.
- [ ] Join a caregiver support group.
- [ ] Keep your own documents up to date.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your parent has not signed powers of attorney, and memory is slipping.
- A bank or doctor will not accept your paperwork.
- You suspect financial exploitation.
- Your parent may need long-term care or Medicaid.
- Siblings disagree about care or money.
