## How to use this guide

We are sorry you are dealing with this. A house shared by heirs can hold memories and strain. Most disputes are about money and time, and they settle better with a plan on paper. Work through the options in order and use the ledgers. This is general education, and an attorney can advise on your family's facts.

## Who owns what

Heirs who receive a house together usually hold it as tenants in common. Each owns a share, even if the shares are not equal. Each owner may use the whole house, and no owner can force the others to leave. If an heir dies, that share passes to their own heirs, not to the surviving siblings.

If the estate is still open, the house belongs to the estate first, and the executor controls it. See the [Illinois probate checklist](/free/illinois-probate-checklist).

## The ladder of options

1. **Agree to share.** Keep it, split costs, and write the rules down.
2. **One heir buys out the others.** Price it with an appraisal.
3. **Sell and divide.** Everyone signs the sale contract.
4. **Mediation.** A neutral person helps owners reach a deal.
5. **Partition.** A court orders a sale or a division.

Each step costs more and takes longer. Try the earlier ones first.

## Who pays upkeep

All co-owners share costs of property taxes, insurance, and necessary repairs in proportion to their ownership, in most cases. An owner who pays more than their share can often seek credit for the extra when the house is sold. Keep receipts.

## A sibling living in the house

If one heir lives in the house and others do not, the others may ask for rent or a credit. The law on this varies, and courts look at whether the others asked or objected. Settle it in writing: the occupant pays an agreed amount, or takes a larger share of expenses.

## When one heir wants to sell and another does not

A co-owner generally cannot be forced to sell to a buyer by another co-owner without a court. The remedy is a partition action. In Illinois, partition is governed by the Partition Article of the Code of Civil Procedure, 735 ILCS 5/17-101 and following. <!-- verify: Illinois partition statute citation and whether Illinois has adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act --> A court may divide the property if it can be divided fairly, or order a sale and split the proceeds.

Some states have adopted a uniform act that gives other heirs a right to buy out a forced seller. Whether and how your state has done so differs.

## What partition costs

Court filing fees, attorney fees, a commissioner or appraiser, and often a sale discount. Costs commonly come out of the sale proceeds. Contested cases cost more and take longer than a negotiated sale, and the price can end up lower than a market sale. Ask an attorney for an estimate for your county.

## Selling with many heirs

1. Get everyone's names and addresses, including heirs who are not local.
2. Agree on one agent to deal with the broker.
3. Get title clear first. See the [clear title guide](/free/illinois-inherited-property-clear-title-guide).
4. Sign the listing and the sale contract together.
5. Divide proceeds by a written statement.

## If a co-owner dies

Their share passes under their will or by intestacy. You may find yourself co-owning with a nephew or a spouse. A written co-owner agreement that binds heirs, and a buy-sell clause, avoids that surprise.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Heirs disagree and cannot talk
- One heir has lived there or paid a lot
- A partition action has been filed or threatened
- A mortgage, lien or tax sale is a risk
- An heir cannot be found
