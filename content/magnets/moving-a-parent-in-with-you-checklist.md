**How to use this checklist.** Work through the parts in order, with your parent, your spouse or partner, and your siblings in the loop. Each part ends with fill-in lines to record decisions. Treat the first few months as a trial, and write that down. For a parent's care agreement in detail, see our [personal care agreement guide](/free/personal-care-agreement-guide). This is general information, not legal advice.

## Part 1: Is this the right move?

Answer honestly, and ask your parent and household to do the same.

- **Why is this move happening now (health, money, loneliness, safety):** ______________________
- **What does my parent want?** ______________________ (their wishes come first when they can express them)
- **What care does my parent need now, and likely in two years:** ______________________
- **What does my spouse or partner think, and what limits do they set:** ______________________
- **What about my children, my work and my own health:** ______________________
- **Is my home suitable (stairs, bathrooms, bedroom on the ground floor, space):** ______________________
- **What alternatives did we look at (assisted living, a nearby apartment, home care at their own house):** ______________________
- **Our trial period:** ______ months, then we review

Checklist of honest questions:

- [ ] We have talked, as a household, about privacy, noise, meals, visitors, pets and the television.
- [ ] We know what happens if care needs grow past what we can give.
- [ ] I have a plan for rest and breaks, and a person who covers me.
- [ ] We have visited the doctor together to learn about likely needs.

## Part 2: Legal documents before the move

Sign these while your parent still has capacity. If they cannot, an attorney can advise what is possible.

- [ ] Durable power of attorney for property, naming an agent and a backup
- [ ] Health care power of attorney, and a living will or advance directive
- [ ] HIPAA release naming the people who may speak with doctors
- [ ] A will or trust that is current
- [ ] Beneficiary designations reviewed (bank accounts, life insurance, retirement accounts)
- [ ] Copies of all documents in a safe place, and the originals' location written down
- [ ] A list of accounts, passwords and contacts, kept private but findable

Rules vary by state. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and the guide to [health care directives](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).

- **Agent for health care:** ______________________
- **Where originals are kept:** ______________________

## Part 3: Money, taxes and benefits

### Parent's home

- [ ] If your parent owns a home, decide whether to keep, rent or sell it, and the cost to keep it up while empty.
- [ ] Do not add your name to the deed as a shortcut. It can create gift tax paperwork, lose tax advantages, and expose the house to your creditors. Ask an attorney first.
- [ ] If your parent sells, ask about the tax result and how the proceeds affect Medicaid eligibility.
- [ ] Notify the insurer that the home is empty or rented.

### Household costs

- **Parent's monthly contribution to rent, food and utilities:** ______________________
- **Costs for home changes (grab bars, ramp, bathroom changes), and who pays:** ______________________
- **Costs of care help (aide, adult day program):** ______________________
- **Insurance: confirm your homeowner and auto policies cover your parent living there:** ______________________

### Government help and taxes

- [ ] Ask whether your parent receives benefits that change with household size or income, such as SSI or food assistance. Rules vary.
- [ ] Ask a tax professional whether you can claim your parent as a dependent or deduct medical costs. The tests include how much the parent earns and how much of their support you provide.
- [ ] Ask whether any state program pays family caregivers. Some states and the VA have such programs.
- [ ] Check long-term care insurance, if any, for home care benefits.
- [ ] If Medicaid may be needed, talk to an attorney before any large gift or transfer. Federal law has a look-back period for gifts, which can block eligibility for a time. Details vary by state. See the [guide to Medicaid and long-term care](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning) and the [look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date).

Money rules that prevent fights:

- [ ] A separate bank account for the parent's money. Never mix it with yours.
- [ ] Receipts and a simple monthly record of what was spent, and why.
- [ ] No cash gifts or "loans" from the parent to you without a written record.

## Part 4: Home safety

Walk through the home with your parent's eyes. Check each item.

- [ ] Remove loose rugs and cords. Add night lights between the bedroom and bathroom.
- [ ] Grab bars at the toilet and in the shower, and a non-slip mat.
- [ ] Handrails on both sides of the stairs, or a bedroom on the main floor.
- [ ] Good lighting, clear walkways, and a chair in the bathroom if balance is a concern.
- [ ] Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors that work, and a fire plan.
- [ ] Medicines locked or stored out of reach, and a pill organizer.
- [ ] Hot water heater set to a safe temperature.
- [ ] A phone, an emergency pendant, or a smart speaker for calling help.
- [ ] For memory loss: door chimes, a plan for wandering, and locked cabinets for chemicals and tools.
- [ ] Firearms stored unloaded and locked, or removed. Ask about local rules.
- [ ] A way for your parent to have privacy, with their own furniture and photos.

- **Changes to make before move-in, with cost:** ______________________
- **Changes to make later if needs grow:** ______________________

## Part 5: Siblings and fairness

Talk before the move, not after. A parent living with one child can leave the others feeling shut out, or the caregiver feeling taken for granted.

- [ ] Tell all siblings about the plan and the reasons.
- [ ] Agree on what each sibling will contribute, in money, time or visits.
- [ ] Agree how the caregiver sibling is thanked, and whether they are paid. Use our [sibling caregiving agreement worksheet](/free/sibling-caregiving-agreement-worksheet).
- [ ] Agree that the parent's money pays for the parent's costs, with records.
- [ ] Decide whether time given counts when the estate is divided. If you want it to count, ask an attorney how to write it down while your parent is alive.
- [ ] Set a monthly call, and a place to share updates.

- **Siblings and what each will do:** ______________________
- **Date of first review call:** ______________________

## Part 6: Written care agreement

A written agreement turns a good intention into something everyone can refer to. It does not need to be long. It can cover:

- **Start date and trial period:** ______________________
- **What the parent pays toward housing and care:** ______________________
- **What care the child provides, and what is paid help:** ______________________
- **Whether the child is paid, and how much:** ______________________
- **House rules (privacy, visitors, alcohol, smoking, pets):** ______________________
- **Time off and respite for the caregiver:** ______________________
- **What happens if care needs rise, or the arrangement fails:** ______________________
- **Signatures and date:** ______________________

If any payments will be made to a family caregiver, talk to an attorney before they start. Payments made without a written agreement can be treated as gifts for Medicaid purposes and may raise tax questions.

## Part 7: The first 90 days

- [ ] Week 1: settle routines, and write down what is going well and what is hard.
- [ ] Week 4: hold a family check-in with your parent, your household and siblings.
- [ ] Month 2: review the money records and the care agreement.
- [ ] Month 3: decide to continue, change, or plan the next step. Update the agreement.
- [ ] Notice your own health. Caregiver burnout is common, and asking for help is part of the plan.
- [ ] Keep a short [caregiver daily log](/free/caregiver-daily-log-template) of changes and costs.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your parent has not signed powers of attorney or a health care directive, or may be losing capacity.
- You are thinking of putting your name on the deed, selling the home, or accepting money for care.
- Your parent may need Medicaid within the next few years.
- Siblings disagree about the move, the money, or how the estate will be divided.
- You are worried about undue pressure on your parent, or about financial abuse.
