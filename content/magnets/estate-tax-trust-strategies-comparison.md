## How to use this guide

Estate tax planning matters most when an estate is near or above an exemption. Federal exemption: $15 million per person in 2026. <!-- verify: 2026 federal basic exclusion amount --> Some states, including Illinois, tax at a much lower level. Read the table, find the rows that fit your situation, then use the questions at the end in a meeting. Every strategy here has a cost. This is general education, and none of it is advice for your facts.

## The core idea

Estate tax applies to what you own or control at death. Strategies work by moving assets, and their future growth, out of your estate, or by using exemptions more than once. Most of them require giving up control.

## Comparison table

| Strategy | How it works | Main risk or cost | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bypass (credit shelter) trust | At the first death, assets up to the exemption go into a trust that skips the survivor's estate | Less flexibility, trust tax returns | Couples, mostly in states with a state tax |
| Portability | Survivor adds unused federal exemption | Not available for state estate tax in Illinois, no GST | Couples under the federal level |
| SLAT | One spouse gives assets to a trust for the other, out of the estate | Divorce, or the beneficiary spouse's death | Married couples with a comfortable surplus |
| GRAT | Put assets in a trust, receive annuity payments for set years, remainder passes on | Grantor must survive the term | Appreciating assets, wealth transfer |
| QPRT | Give a home to a trust, keep living there for a term | Lose step-up, must survive the term, rent after | A valuable home, a long horizon |
| ILIT | Trust owns life insurance so proceeds are outside the estate | Three-year rule, premium gifts | Large policies |
| Charitable remainder trust | Income to you, remainder to a charity | Irrevocable, charity gets the remainder | Appreciated assets, charitable goals |
| Family LP / LLC | Hold assets in an entity, gift interests, claim valuation discounts | IRS challenges, running costs | Family business, real estate |

## Explanations

### SLAT: spousal lifetime access trust

One spouse gives assets to an irrevocable trust for the other spouse and children. The gift uses the donor's exemption and the assets leave the donor's estate. The beneficiary spouse can usually take distributions.

Risks:

- If the beneficiary spouse dies first, the donor loses indirect access to the money.
- In a divorce, the trust usually stays, and the donor cannot take the assets back.
- Two SLATs for each other can face the reciprocal trust doctrine, so they need to differ.

### GRAT: grantor retained annuity trust

You put assets in a trust for a set term and receive fixed annuity payments. What is left at the end passes to heirs with little or no gift tax. It works when the assets grow faster than the IRS interest rate used in the calculation. If you die during the term, the assets are pulled back into your estate.

GRAT vs. SLAT:

| | GRAT | SLAT |
|---|---|---|
| Access to the money | Annuity to you | Through a spouse |
| Uses the exemption | Minimal | Yes |
| Survival requirement | Yes | No |
| Best for | A short-term bet on growth | Locking in an exemption |

### QPRT

You give your home to a trust and keep living in it for a term. The gift is valued at a discount because you keep the right to use it. If you outlive the term, the house is out of your estate. After that you pay rent to stay. The heirs take your basis, not a stepped-up one. Few families use this today.

### ILIT: irrevocable life insurance trust

The trust, not you, owns the policy. Proceeds are not in your estate. A new policy bought by the trust avoids the three-year rule. If you transfer an existing policy and die within three years, the proceeds are pulled back into the estate (IRC section 2035). Premium payments are gifts, and Crummey letters can make them qualify for the annual exclusion. An ILIT may need its own tax ID and tax return.

### Bypass trust and the portability question

A bypass trust holds assets up to the exemption for the family at the first death, so growth is not taxed at the second. Portability is easier and often cheaper. The bypass trust wins when there is a state estate tax with no portability, when growth is expected, when there are children from an earlier marriage, or when you want to control where assets end up. Illinois offers no portability, so a trust may be used to use the Illinois exemption at the first death.

### Charitable remainder trust

You give appreciated assets, receive income for life or a term, and the charity gets what is left. You may receive an income tax deduction. The assets leave your estate. A donor-advised fund is simpler. See the [charitable vehicle comparison](/free/donor-advised-fund-vs-foundation-worksheet).

### Family limited partnership

Family members own interests in an entity that holds assets. Gifts of minority interests can be valued at a discount for lack of control and marketability. The IRS challenges aggressive discounts, and a partnership with no business purpose is a target. Keep personal and partnership funds separate.

## Choose by situation

| If this | Then consider |
|---|---|
| Married, estate under the federal level but over the state level | Bypass trust, state-focused planning |
| Married, estate over the federal level | SLAT, ILIT, annual gifts |
| Single, estate over the exemption | Lifetime gifts, GRAT, ILIT, charitable trusts |
| Business owner | Family entity, installment tax options, succession plan |
| Charitably inclined | Charitable remainder trust or donor-advised fund |
| Big life insurance policy | ILIT |

## Questions to take to your attorney and CPA

- [ ] Which exemption applies to my state and my family?
- [ ] What does this strategy cost to set up and maintain?
- [ ] What if I die during the term, or my spouse does?
- [ ] What happens to the income tax basis of the assets?
- [ ] Can I still reach the money if I need it?
- [ ] What is the plan if tax law changes?

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your estate is near or above an exemption
- You are considering an irrevocable gift or a trust that holds a policy
- You own a business or highly appreciated assets
- You want to keep using the same strategy after a divorce or remarriage
