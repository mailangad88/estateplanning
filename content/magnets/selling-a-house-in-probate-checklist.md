## How to use this checklist

We are sorry for your loss. A house is often the largest asset in an estate, and the sale rarely moves as fast as families hope. This checklist keeps the steps in order. Rules differ by state. In some states an executor can sell right after being appointed. In others the court, or the beneficiaries, may need to approve the sale. Ask the clerk or an attorney before you list the property.

## Step 1: Confirm who can sell

Nobody can sell a house until the law says they have authority. A buyer and a title company will ask for proof.

- [ ] Find out how the house was titled: one name, joint with someone, or in a trust
- [ ] If it was in one name, you generally need court-issued authority, often called letters of office
- [ ] If it passed to a co-owner or to a trust, ask the attorney whether probate is needed at all
- [ ] Check whether the will gives the executor power to sell, or whether court approval is required
- [ ] Order certified copies of the letters for the title company

## Step 2: Secure the house now

An empty house draws trouble.

- [ ] Change or re-key the locks and keep a list of who has keys
- [ ] Forward the mail
- [ ] Take photos of every room and the outside, with dates
- [ ] Remove valuables, medication, firearms, and important papers
- [ ] Keep utilities on so pipes do not freeze and mold does not grow
- [ ] Arrange lawn care, snow removal and regular visits
- [ ] Ask a neighbour to watch the house, and give them your number

## Step 3: Check insurance

Standard homeowner policies often limit or end coverage after the owner dies and the house sits vacant. Policies differ, so call the insurer and ask in plain words.

- [ ] Tell the insurer about the death and ask what the policy covers now
- [ ] Ask whether a vacancy clause applies and after how many days
- [ ] Ask about a vacant-property or estate policy if the house will be empty for a while
- [ ] Keep paying premiums from the estate account
- [ ] Ask who is listed as the insured and fix the name if needed

- **Insurer and policy number:** ____________________
- **Coverage confirmed in writing on:** ____________________

## Step 4: Handle the mortgage and taxes

- [ ] Find the mortgage servicer and send them notice of death with a copy of your authority
- [ ] Ask what payments are due and keep paying them if you can, from estate funds
- [ ] Ask about the lender's rules for heirs, since federal law gives certain family members some protection from an immediate call of the loan
- [ ] Pay property taxes and utilities on time

See [what happens to a mortgage when the owner dies](/blog/what-happens-to-a-mortgage-when-the-owner-dies).

## Step 5: Get a value

- [ ] Hire an independent appraiser to give a date-of-death value
- [ ] Ask a real estate agent for a market opinion of the likely sale price
- [ ] Keep both in the estate file

The date-of-death value is useful for tax purposes. Heirs who inherit property often receive a new basis based on that value. A tax professional can explain how it applies in your case.

- **Appraised value and date:** ____________________
- **Agent's suggested list price:** ____________________

## Step 6: Prepare for sale

- [ ] Decide what stays and what goes. See the [cleaning out a parent's home planner](/free/cleaning-out-a-parents-home-planner)
- [ ] Remove personal property or hold an [estate sale](/free/estate-sale-planner)
- [ ] Fix safety issues first and cosmetic items second
- [ ] Ask the agent whether to sell as is
- [ ] Gather utility bills, repair records and keys for the buyer

## Step 7: Choose an agent and set terms

- [ ] Interview at least two agents
- [ ] Ask about experience with estate and probate sales
- [ ] Ask about the commission and what it covers
- [ ] Review the listing agreement length, and ask how to end it
- [ ] Confirm that the listing disclosures mention the sale is by the estate

## Step 8: Timing with buyers

Probate sales may take longer because of court dates and title checks. Tell buyers early.

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Court approval | Some sales are not final until a judge approves them |
| Buyer financing | Lenders may want letters and proof of authority |
| Title search | Old liens or unpaid taxes can delay closing |
| Inspection | Estates often sell as is, but buyers may still ask for repairs |
| Closing date | Match it to the court calendar |

## Step 9: Close and pay out

- [ ] Review the settlement statement with the attorney
- [ ] Pay off the mortgage and any liens at closing
- [ ] Deposit the net proceeds into the estate account
- [ ] Record the sale in the [estate ledger](/free/estate-accounting-ledger-template)
- [ ] Hold the funds until claims and taxes are resolved, unless an attorney says otherwise
- [ ] Tell beneficiaries when the sale is done

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are unsure who has authority to sell
- Several heirs want different things, such as keeping the house versus selling
- The mortgage is larger than the home's value
- The home has liens, unpaid taxes or title problems
- Someone is living in the house and refuses to leave
- Court approval of the sale is required
