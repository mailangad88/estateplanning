## How to use this guide

Names for special needs trusts are confusing because the same trust can be called by a statute, a nickname or its source of funds. Use the table to match the label to the trust. Tax and Medicaid rules change and vary by state, so treat this as a map for your attorney meeting.

## Which trust is which

| Label | Whose money? | Payback to Medicaid at death? | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Third-party (supplemental needs) trust | Parents, grandparents, others | No | Inheritance or gifts |
| First-party, called (d)(4)(A) | The person's own money | Yes | Settlement, back pay, own inheritance |
| Pooled, called (d)(4)(C) | The person's own money, held by a nonprofit | Often yes, with some retained by the nonprofit | When no family trustee is available |
| Sole benefit trust | Often used for transfers to or for a disabled person | Rules depend on the transfer | Medicaid planning transfers |

"Supplemental needs" and "discretionary" are descriptions of how the trustee may pay. A discretionary trust gives the trustee choice, so the beneficiary cannot demand money. That choice is what keeps the assets from counting.

## The (d)(4)(A) trust

It gets its name from the Medicaid statute, 42 U.S.C. 1396p(d)(4)(A). It holds the person's own money. The person must be under 65 when it is set up, and disabled under SSA's definition. <!-- verify: age 65 rule at establishment --> Parent, grandparent, guardian or court can create it. The trust must repay the state for Medicaid paid on the person's behalf, at death.

## The (d)(4)(C) pooled trust

A nonprofit holds many accounts and invests together. Each person has a separate account. It is often used when a family cannot name a trustee or the amount is small. Age rules vary and a pooled trust may be available to people over 65 in some states. <!-- verify: Illinois rule on pooled trust for those 65 and older --> At death, the nonprofit may keep part and repay Medicaid for the rest.

## Third-party trust

Money comes from others. No Medicaid payback is required in most cases, so the document can name family or charity as the remainder beneficiary. Use it in a will or living trust so relatives can leave money to the trust, not to the person. See /free/special-needs-trust-funding-worksheet.

## Sole benefit trust

The name comes from a Medicaid rule that treats a transfer to a trust for the sole benefit of a disabled person as not a penalty, under some conditions. It often requires the trust to pay out over a set time or repay Medicaid. Used for planning, not for everyday spending. <!-- verify: sole benefit trust rules under 42 U.S.C. 1396p(c)(2)(B)(iv) -->

## Revocable or irrevocable

A special needs trust is generally irrevocable. If the person could cancel it and take the money, the money would count. A parent can still change a will or a living trust that feeds the trust. After it is funded, changes usually need a court order, a trust protector or a built-in amendment power. A third-party trust that a parent can amend while alive may be a revocable trust until death. Ask an attorney how yours works.

## Taxes

Rules differ by trust and by year, so confirm with a tax preparer.

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Does the trust pay tax? | It may. Trusts reach top tax rates at low income |
| Does the beneficiary pay on distributions? | Distributions carry income out to the beneficiary to the extent of the trust's income |
| Does it need its own tax ID? | Often yes. First-party trusts are often reported under the beneficiary's SSN as a grantor trust |
| Is there a special exemption? | A qualified disability trust may qualify for a larger exemption |
| Who files? | The trustee |

The qualified disability trust (QDisT) rule is in Internal Revenue Code section 642(b)(2)(C). The exemption amount ties to the personal exemption, which has been set at zero in recent years, so the benefit may be small. <!-- verify: QDisT exemption amount for current year --> Illinois taxes trust income too, so ask about state returns. <!-- verify: Illinois fiduciary income tax treatment -->

## What happens at death

| Trust type | Who gets what is left |
|---|---|
| Third-party | Whoever the document names: siblings, charity, a descendant |
| First-party | State Medicaid agency first, then any named beneficiary |
| Pooled | The nonprofit and the state, per the account agreement |

Funeral costs and taxes may come first. Read the clause titled "remainder" or "termination."

## Checklist for the attorney meeting

- [ ] Whose money will fund the trust (family, the person, both)?
- [ ] Is the person under 65?
- [ ] Which benefits does the person receive?
- [ ] Do we want a family trustee or an institution?
- [ ] Who gets the remainder, and do we want payback or not?
- [ ] Who is the trust protector?
- [ ] Will the trust need a tax ID?
- [ ] Which state's law applies?

## When to talk to an attorney

- The person already has money in their own name
- You want to use a pooled trust and need to compare nonprofits
- Medicaid planning transfers are involved
- You are unsure whether the trust is revocable
- You expect taxes on trust income
