**How to use this checklist.** Skip to your life stage and read that section plus the "Everyone" section. Tick what you have, circle what you do not, and do not try to finish in one sitting. The last page, "Fill in what you can," is the one to bring to an attorney.

A few terms first. A **will** says who gets your property and who raises your minor children, and it generally goes through probate. A **revocable living trust** holds property during your life and passes it at death without probate. A **durable power of attorney** lets someone manage money if you cannot. A **health care directive** (also called an advance directive, health care proxy or living will) states your medical wishes and names who speaks for you.

## Section 1: Everyone, at any age

These items apply to most adults, married or single, with a lot of assets or a little.

- [ ] **Beneficiaries on file for every account that allows one.** Retirement accounts, life insurance and many bank and brokerage accounts pass by beneficiary form, not by your will. A form filled out in 2009 still controls. See the [Beneficiary Designation Audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).
- [ ] **Durable financial power of attorney.** Without one, a family may need a court-ordered guardianship or conservatorship to pay your bills if you lose capacity. That court process is slower and more public than signing a document now.
- [ ] **Health care directive and a named health care agent.** If you have not named someone, hospitals generally turn to a default family order set by state law. That order does not always match who you would choose.
- [ ] **HIPAA release.** Often built into the health care directive. It lets your agent get medical information.
- [ ] **A list of where things are:** accounts, documents, and how to reach your passwords or password manager.
- [ ] **A will, or a trust plus a "pour-over" will.** Even people who rely mostly on beneficiary forms use a will as a safety net for anything left out.
- [ ] **Digital access plan.** Name a legacy contact with Apple or set an Inactive Account Manager with Google. Both are free and take minutes. Many states have adopted the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, and your online-tool settings generally take priority over your will.

## Section 2: Roughly ages 18 to 35, no children

- [ ] **Power of attorney and health care directive.** For many people at this stage, incapacity is far likelier than death, so this pair often comes first.
- [ ] **Beneficiaries on your 401(k), IRA and any life insurance through work.** Employer plans default in ways you may not expect. If you are married, federal law generally makes your spouse the beneficiary of a 401(k) unless the spouse signs a waiver (ERISA, 29 U.S.C. 1055).
- [ ] **Simple will**, mainly to name who gets your belongings, name an executor and cover pets.
- [ ] **If unmarried but in a long-term partnership:** most states' default inheritance rules do not recognize an unmarried partner. If you want your partner to inherit, it takes documents or beneficiary designations.
- [ ] **Student loans and co-signers.** Federal student loans are generally discharged at death, but private and co-signed loans can leave a parent liable. Check your loan terms.
- [ ] **Digital assets.** Crypto, brokerage apps and online business accounts have no branch office to call. Record access methods securely.

Can usually wait: a revocable trust and estate tax planning.

## Section 3: Married or partnered, with minor children

This is the stage where a plan matters most, because the cost of a gap falls on children.

- [ ] **Name a guardian and a backup guardian** in your will. If no guardian is named and both parents die, a court chooses. See the [guardian worksheet](/checklists/choosing-a-guardian-worksheet).
- [ ] **Avoid leaving assets outright to a minor.** A minor generally cannot hold title to significant assets. Without a plan, a court-supervised guardianship of the estate or a custodial account can result, and the child may receive everything at the age set by state law, commonly 18 or 21. A trust lets you choose ages and conditions.
- [ ] **Name a trustee or custodian** separate from, or in addition to, the guardian. The best person to raise your children is not always the best person to manage money.
- [ ] **Life insurance sized to replace income.** A rule of thumb many financial planners cite is 10 to 12 times annual income, adjusted for debts, childcare and college. It is an estimate, not a rule. A licensed insurance professional can help, and so can the [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs).
- [ ] **Beneficiary forms for minors.** Many families name a trust or a custodian under UTMA rather than "my children," or an insurer may hold funds until a court appoints a guardian of the estate.
- [ ] **Revocable living trust.** Worth considering if you own a home, hold assets of any real size, want privacy, or want to avoid a court process for your children's inheritance.
- [ ] **Powers of attorney and directives** naming each other first and an adult as backup.
- [ ] **A review after each new child, move, home purchase or job change.**

## Section 4: Roughly ages 50 to 65

- [ ] **Re-check beneficiaries.** Kids grow up, marriages end, and the rules for inherited retirement accounts have changed. Most non-spouse heirs must now empty an inherited IRA within 10 years under the SECURE Act, which affects beneficiary and trust language. See IRS Publication 590-B.
- [ ] **Fund your trust** if you have one. An unsigned deed or an account never retitled is a common reason a trust fails to avoid probate. See the [Trust Funding Checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist).
- [ ] **Long-term care plan.** Costs vary widely by state and type of care, so get actual local quotes. Medicaid has a 60-month look-back on transfers, so gifts made late can cause penalties. See the [Medicaid look-back tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date).
- [ ] **Estate tax check.** The federal exemption is $15 million per person for 2026<!-- verify -->, set by Public Law 119-21 and indexed for inflation after that. Most families are well under it. However, 12 states and DC levy their own estate tax, and 5 states (Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey and Pennsylvania) levy an inheritance tax, often at much lower thresholds. Thresholds change often, so ask an attorney licensed in your state for the current number.
- [ ] **Business owners:** a succession plan, a buy-sell agreement and key-person insurance.
- [ ] **Blended families:** decide in writing how a surviving spouse and children from a prior marriage share. Beneficiary forms and a will can contradict each other here.
- [ ] **Adult children with special circumstances.** A child on SSI or Medicaid can lose benefits from an outright inheritance, and a special needs trust may be part of the answer.
- [ ] **Talk to your executor and successor trustee.** Surprise appointments cause delay.

## Section 5: Roughly ages 65 and over

- [ ] **Everything in Sections 1 and 4**, reviewed in the last 3 to 5 years or after any major event.
- [ ] **A successor trustee and executor who are still able and willing**, plus a second backup.
- [ ] **A letter of instruction** covering funeral wishes, where documents are and who to call first. It is not a legal document. It is a practical gift to your family.
- [ ] **Pre-need arrangements.** If you pre-pay a funeral home, read the contract. The FTC's Funeral Rule gives you the right to itemized pricing.
- [ ] **Medicaid and VA benefit planning** if long-term care is a realistic risk. An elder law attorney can advise before any assets move.
- [ ] **Charitable giving.** Qualified charitable distributions from an IRA (available at age 70 1/2 and over), donor-advised funds and charitable beneficiary designations are often simpler than a bequest in a will. Confirm details with your tax adviser.
- [ ] **Safe deposit box.** Tell someone it exists and make sure your agents can reach it. In some states a box can be sealed at death until a court order.
- [ ] **Reverse mortgage.** If you have one, the loan generally becomes due after death. Heirs have limited time to sell or refinance, so make sure they know.

## Section 6: Life events that call for a review

| Event | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Marriage | Many states revoke or limit existing wills on marriage. Beneficiary forms do not update themselves. |
| Divorce | Many states automatically revoke an ex-spouse's role in a will or revocable trust, but often not on retirement accounts governed by federal law. Update every form. |
| Birth or adoption | Guardianship, trust terms, insurance. |
| Death of a named person | A deceased agent, beneficiary or executor leaves a gap. |
| Move to a new state | Documents often stay valid, but formalities, community property status and tax exposure may differ. |
| Buying or selling a home | Deed titling and trust funding. |
| Starting or selling a business | Succession, entity ownership, buy-sell. |
| Serious illness | Move incapacity documents to the top of the list. |
| Inheritance or windfall | Estate tax exposure and titling. |

## Section 7: The nine documents in one table

| Document | What it does | Who most often uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Last will and testament | Names executor and guardians; distributes probate assets | Nearly everyone |
| Revocable living trust | Avoids probate for funded assets; plans for incapacity | Homeowners, parents, multi-state owners, privacy seekers |
| Pour-over will | Catches assets not in the trust | Anyone with a trust |
| Durable financial POA | Agent manages money if you cannot | Nearly everyone |
| Health care directive / proxy | Medical wishes and agent | Nearly everyone |
| HIPAA authorization | Medical information access | Nearly everyone |
| Beneficiary designations | Controls many accounts outside the will | Anyone with accounts |
| Letter of instruction | Practical roadmap (non-binding) | Nearly everyone |
| Special-purpose trusts (special needs, life insurance, QTIP) | Solve specific problems | Only when the situation applies |

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have minor children, or a child with a disability or on public benefits.
- You own a home, a business, or property in more than one state.
- You have a blended family or an unmarried partner you want to protect.
- Your estate may be near your state's estate tax threshold. Rules vary by state, and an attorney licensed in your state can tell you the current number.
- A document was signed in another state or more than five years ago.
