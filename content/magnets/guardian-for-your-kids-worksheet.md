**How to use this worksheet.** Do Steps 1 and 2 alone, then compare answers with your co-parent. Score candidates together in Step 3, and talk through Step 4 over a quiet evening. A court has the final say on guardianship, and the law differs by state, so take the finished worksheet to an attorney licensed in your state.

## Why this matters

If both parents die or cannot care for a minor child, a court decides who has legal custody. A guardian named in a parent's will is not binding on a judge, but courts generally give it significant weight and follow it unless it is not in the child's best interests. A typical rule, found in Uniform Probate Code section 5-202, lets a parent appoint a guardian by will.

If no one is nominated, relatives may compete, a court decides after a hearing, and the child waits in an uncertain situation. Naming a guardian is one of the few jobs only a will can do. Beneficiary forms, trusts and insurance cannot do it.

There are two roles, and they can be held by two different people.

| Role | What they do | Typical document |
|---|---|---|
| **Guardian of the person** | Raises the child: home, school, health care, values | Will |
| **Guardian of the estate, trustee or custodian** | Manages the child's money and pays for the child's needs | Trust (often preferred), UTMA custodian or court-supervised guardian of the estate |

Many parents split the roles when the best caregiver is not the best money manager, or to create checks and balances. If you split them, say how the two people will communicate.

## Step 1: Brainstorm (5 minutes, alone)

List everyone you would trust, without filtering. Include family, friends, godparents and couples. Skip who you think you "should" pick.

- **Candidate 1:** ______________________
- **Candidate 2:** ______________________
- **Candidate 3:** ______________________
- **Candidate 4:** ______________________
- **Candidate 5:** ______________________

## Step 2: What matters most to you

Rank these from 1 (most important) to 8 (least). Do it alone, have your co-parent do the same, then compare.

| Factor | My rank | Co-parent's rank |

| Financial stability (not wealth, but not under severe strain) | | |

Common trade-offs: grandparents may love the children dearly but be in their 70s while the children are young. A sibling may match your values but live across the country with a new baby. A close friend may be perfect for the kids but a poor fit for family politics. There is no perfect answer, only a better-informed choice.

## Step 4: Questions to ask yourselves

1. Would the guardian raise the kids in their home, or ask the kids to move into yours? Is that fair to the kids?
2. If your first choice cannot serve, who is next? Name at least one backup.
3. What if the guardian divorces, becomes ill or moves abroad? Do you want them to be able to name a successor?
4. Do you want to name a couple? What happens if they separate? Many wills name an individual and say what happens if that person is married to someone else.
5. Is there anyone you specifically do not want to have custody? A will can say so, though a court can still consider anyone.
6. Does your guardian understand your approach to schooling, medical care, faith and discipline?
7. Who covers the guardian's added costs: a larger home, a larger car, education? That is where a trust and life insurance come in.

## Step 5: The conversation with the person you want to ask

Here is a script you can adapt.

> "We are working on our will, and we have to decide who would care for [child's name] if something happened to us. You are the person we trust most for that. We want to ask you honestly: is that something you could take on? There is no pressure, and if the answer is no, we want to know. We will also arrange money and insurance so that this is not a financial burden."

Things to cover: their honest yes or no, any health or life changes coming, what they would want to know about the child's routines and medical needs, whether their spouse or partner is on board, and how you will keep the conversation current. An annual check-in works well. Ask whether they would also be willing to manage the money, or whether you should name someone else for that.

## Step 6: Plan the money

A guardian needs resources, and a child needs protection. A will alone can lead to a court-supervised process, and a young adult may receive a full inheritance at 18 or 21. Options to discuss:

- [ ] **Life insurance** to replace both parents' income and fund the child's care. Many planners cite 10 to 12 times income as a starting heuristic, but needs vary with debts, number of children and future costs. It is an estimate, not a rule. A licensed advisor can help, and so can the [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator).
- [ ] **A trust for the children,** created in your will (a testamentary trust) or as a living trust. A trust lets you set ages and purposes, such as portions at set ages or at milestones, and names a trustee to manage money meanwhile.
- [ ] **Name the trust, not the children, as beneficiary** of life insurance and retirement accounts, if a trust fits your family. A minor generally cannot receive large sums directly, and a court-appointed conservator may be needed otherwise.
- [ ] **A UTMA or UGMA custodian** is an alternative for smaller sums, usually ending between 18 and 25 depending on the state.
- [ ] **Interim guidance.** Ask about a short temporary-guardian authorization for emergencies such as a parent's hospitalization abroad, and a caregiver authorization (some states have a statutory form) for relatives or sitters who need to approve medical care.
- [ ] **Pets.** Name a caretaker too. Many states allow pet trusts.

## Step 7: Write a letter to the guardian (optional)

This letter is non-binding and private. A few paragraphs help a great deal. Prompts:

- **Our child's routine** (sleep, food, comfort items, what scares them, what makes them laugh): ______________________
- **School, doctors, medications, allergies, therapists, activities and friends to keep:** ______________________
- **Our values** (faith, honesty, kindness, education): ______________________
- **Family traditions and relatives we want them to stay connected to:** ______________________
- **Where documents, accounts and insurance policies are:** ______________________
- **What we trust you to decide on your own:** ______________________
- **A note of thanks:** ______________________

Update the letter every year, perhaps around a birthday.

## Step 8: Put it into legal documents

| Item | Document | Done? |
|---|---|---|
| Name guardian and backup | Will (each parent) | |
| Name a guardian of the estate or trustee | Will and/or trust | |
| Provide a trust for the children's money | Living trust or testamentary trust | |
| Update beneficiaries (insurance, retirement) | Beneficiary forms | |
| Temporary caregiver authorization | State form or attorney | |
| Medical authorization for the guardian | HIPAA authorization | |
| Letter of instruction and letter to guardian | Non-binding letters | |
| Powers of attorney and health care directives | Separate documents | |

Both parents should sign their own wills and make sure they say the same thing. If you are divorced or unmarried, the other legal parent generally has custody rights if you die, unless a court finds that parent unfit or that parent's rights were terminated. Naming a guardian in your will does not override a surviving parent in most states. If the other parent is not a safe choice, talk to an attorney, since extra steps may be needed.

## Special situations

- **Blended families.** Be explicit about stepchildren, who are not legal children unless adopted. A stepchild generally has no automatic inheritance rights and no automatic standing for guardianship.
- **A child with special needs.** Name a guardian who understands benefits and daily care. A special needs trust helps an inheritance avoid disqualifying the child from SSI or Medicaid. Plan for adulthood too, since adult children with disabilities may need court-appointed guardianship or supported decision-making.
- **Single parents.** Naming a guardian matters even more. Some also name a second trusted adult to make immediate decisions if the guardian lives far away.
- **International families.** Documents may be recognized differently abroad. Ask an attorney with cross-border experience.
- **LGBTQ+ parents.** A legally recognized parent-child relationship, through adoption or a court judgment, is the strongest protection. A will can nominate a guardian but cannot create parentage. An attorney experienced in family formation can advise.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have minor children and no will.
- You are unmarried or divorced, or one or both parents have children from other relationships.
- A child has special needs.
- A parent living outside the home might contest your choice.
- You want a trust to manage the child's money.
- You want the guardian nomination executed correctly. It is simple to draft but must meet your state's signing rules.
