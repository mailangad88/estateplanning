**How to use this guide.** Read it before a parent starts paying you, or a sibling, for care. Then use the checklist and the template outline at the end to prepare for a meeting with an attorney. Rules for these agreements differ by state and by program, and mistakes can be costly, so treat this as an introduction.

## What a personal care agreement is

A personal care agreement, sometimes called a caregiver agreement or a family caregiver contract, is a written contract in which a parent agrees to pay a family member for care services. The family member agrees to provide specific care for a set rate.

Families often provide care for free out of love. That is understandable. The trouble is that informal arrangements can look, to a state Medicaid agency or to a sibling, like money disappearing from the parent's account without a clear reason. A written agreement shows that payments were for real services at a fair price.

## Why it matters

There are three common reasons families put this in writing.

1. **Medicaid.** To qualify for Medicaid long-term care, applicants generally face a **look-back period**, 60 months in most states (California differs). Transfers made for less than fair value during that period can cause a **penalty period** of ineligibility. A parent who gives a child money and calls it a gift may be penalized. A parent who pays for services actually provided, at a fair market rate, under a written agreement, is often in a stronger position. State agencies review these closely, and rules vary.
2. **Fairness.** One child often does most of the caregiving. Payment for that work, set in advance, can reduce resentment and the feeling that the parent's savings are being drained.
3. **Records.** If a parent later lacks capacity, or if siblings question the arrangement, a clear contract and payment history help everyone.

The [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) can help you see how timing works.

## What usually goes wrong

- **Payment for past care.** Many state Medicaid programs treat payment for care already given as a gift, not payment for services. An agreement generally works best when it is signed before care is paid, and covers services going forward.
- **Rates that are too high.** Pay above what a local agency would charge can look like a disguised gift.
- **Lump-sum prepayment.** Paying years of care in advance is often scrutinized. Some states disallow it or treat unused prepayment as a transfer.
- **No records.** Without a log of hours and tasks, it is hard to show the services were real.
- **Mixing money.** Paying from the parent's account to a caregiver's personal account without a record, or paying in cash, makes it harder to prove what happened.

## What to put in the agreement

A clear agreement is specific and short. Most include the following.

- **The parties.** Names of the parent (or the parent's agent) and the caregiver.
- **Date and signatures.** Signed by both, ideally notarized.
- **Services covered.** Examples: help with bathing and dressing, meals, medication reminders, transport to appointments, light housekeeping, supervision, shopping, managing bills.
- **Services not covered.** Skilled nursing or medical tasks that need a licensed professional.
- **Hours or schedule.** How many hours per week, or "as needed" with a log.
- **Rate of pay.** An hourly rate, supported by local market rates for home care aides.
- **Payment method.** For example, a check or bank transfer on a set date, with a record.
- **Time records.** A log of dates, hours and tasks, signed or reviewed regularly.
- **Term and ending.** When it starts, how either side can end it, and what happens if the parent moves to a care facility or dies.
- **Expenses.** Who pays for supplies, gas or mileage, and how receipts are kept.
- **Capacity and authority.** Who signs for the parent, if the parent cannot. If a family member signs on behalf of a parent, the agent should have clear authority under a [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney), and some states limit self-dealing.

## Fair pay records

The goal of documentation is to show what services were provided, how much they were worth, and that the payment matched.

- [ ] Research the local hourly rate for home health aides or personal care workers. Keep a printout or notes with the date.
- [ ] Set the rate at or below that range.
- [ ] Keep a **care log** with the date, hours and tasks.
- [ ] Pay on a regular schedule, from the parent's account to the caregiver's, and keep statements.
- [ ] Keep copies of the signed agreement, all payment records and any doctor's note supporting the care need.
- [ ] Review the agreement at least once a year, and after a change in health.

A sample log line might read: "Tuesday, 9 to 1, helped with bathing, grocery shopping, lunch, took to cardiology appointment."

## Taxes in general terms

Payment for care is generally income to the caregiver.

- The caregiver may owe **income tax** and possibly self-employment tax on the pay.
- The parent, as the person paying, may be treated as a **household employer**. In some cases this means payroll tax filings, withholding and a "nanny tax" form. Thresholds change from year to year, so confirm current rules.
- Some states have **workers' compensation** requirements for in-home workers.
- The caregiver's income might affect their own benefits, such as health insurance subsidies.
- Parents may be able to deduct some care costs as medical expenses in certain situations.

None of this is simple. A tax professional can say what applies to your family. Many people skip this step, then face problems later. Plan for it now.

## Fairness with siblings

Money and care are among the most common sources of family conflict after a parent's decline or death. A few habits reduce trouble.

- **Tell everyone early.** Share the agreement, the rate and the reasons with siblings before payments begin, if the parent agrees.
- **Offer to share the records.** A sibling who can see the log is less likely to suspect something.
- **Do not treat pay as a replacement for an inheritance.** Make clear that the payment is for work. A separate decision about the will can be made separately.
- **Consider equalizing.** Some parents adjust the will to reflect other sacrifices. Speak with an attorney before changing estate documents.
- **Name a neutral person** to review the log, such as an accountant, a care manager or an attorney.

Siblings who are not paid may still help in other ways: visits, appointments, errands. Naming those roles reduces the feeling that one person carries everything.

## Template outline

Take this outline to an attorney who can customize it for your state.

1. **Title and date.** "Personal Care Agreement."
2. **Parties.** Parent (the "Care Recipient") and caregiver, with addresses.
3. **Purpose.** A short statement that the parent wishes to receive care at home and the caregiver agrees to provide it.
4. **Services.** A list, with room to update as needs change.
5. **Schedule.** Hours per week, flexible or fixed.
6. **Compensation.** Rate, payment dates, method, and how raises, if any, are set.
7. **Records.** The care log and how often it is signed.
8. **Taxes.** Who handles withholding and filings.
9. **Term.** Start date, how to end it, and what happens on a move to a facility or the parent's death. Payment for services after death is not generally included.
10. **Changes.** Written amendments only.
11. **Signatures and notary.** Both parties, plus an agent if applicable.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A parent may need Medicaid long-term care within the next five years.
- You are considering paying a caregiver for care already given.
- A parent has lost or may lose capacity and someone else would sign for them.
- Siblings disagree, or you expect a dispute.
- You are unsure about tax filings or payroll obligations.
- Your state has special rules, such as Medicaid programs that pay family caregivers directly.
