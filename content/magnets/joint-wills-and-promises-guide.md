## How to use this guide

"I leave it all to my spouse, who will take care of the kids" is the most common plan and the most common source of family fights. Once the first spouse dies, the survivor often controls everything. This guide explains which documents bind the survivor and which do not. Rules vary by state and by the exact words, so confirm with an attorney where you live.

## The three words people mix up

| Term | What it is | Binding on the survivor? |
|---|---|---|
| Joint will | One document signed by both spouses | Depends on the words and state law; some courts treat it as a contract |
| Mutual (reciprocal) wills | Two separate wills with matching terms | Not binding by themselves |
| Contract to make a will | A written agreement not to change the plan | Can be enforced by suing the estate, if proven |

A joint will is rare today and often causes confusion about whether the survivor may change it. Mutual wills look similar but do not stop the survivor from changing their own will. Only a contract to make a will, with clear words and consideration, is likely to bind the survivor. Courts require proof, often in writing. <!-- verify: Illinois treatment of joint wills and contracts to make a will -->

## What a surviving spouse can usually do

| Situation | What the survivor can do |
|---|---|
| Everything passed to the survivor outright | Spend it, give it away, rewrite the will, leave it to anyone |
| Mutual wills, no contract | Change their will at any time |
| Joint will with a contract | May be bound, can face a lawsuit if they break it |
| Joint trust, survivor is trustee and has the power to amend | Can change the trust unless the trust says it is locked |
| Separate trust, locked at first death | Cannot change the first spouse's trust |

Most trusts for couples say the plan becomes irrevocable at the first death. Others do not. Read the clause titled "amendment" or "irrevocability." If your spouse can change your share of the trust after you die, your children may be left out. See /compare/will-vs-trust.

## A promise is not a plan

"Dad told me the house would be mine" is a verbal promise. Courts are cautious about enforcing them. Many states have a statute of frauds that requires land promises to be written. A promise about an inheritance can sometimes support a claim against the estate, but proof is hard, deadlines are short and the outcome is uncertain. <!-- verify: Illinois deadline for claims against an estate -->

The same applies when one spouse leaves everything to the other "with the understanding" that it will pass to the children. That understanding binds no one. The survivor can remarry and leave everything to new relatives.

## Ways to make a promise real

- [ ] A trust that locks your share at your death
- [ ] A life estate or trust for the house with the children as remainder beneficiaries
- [ ] Life insurance with the children as beneficiaries
- [ ] A signed contract to make a will, prepared by an attorney
- [ ] Beneficiary forms that name the children directly
- [ ] A postnuptial or prenuptial agreement that records the deal

Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and insurance pass outside the will. See /compare/beneficiary-designation-vs-will.

## If your parent remarried and left everything to the new spouse

Start with facts. Do not assume wrongdoing.

1. Get a copy of the will or trust. A will on file with the court is public. A trust is private, but a beneficiary can usually ask for a copy.
2. Find out what passes outside the will: accounts with beneficiaries, joint property, the house.
3. Check whether your parent and the new spouse had a prenup.
4. Write down the dates: marriage, will, health history.
5. Ask whether the spouse has legal rights, such as a share of the estate, regardless of the will. See /free/illinois-spousal-rights-guide.
6. Talk with an attorney before you write angry emails.

| If this is true | Then |
|---|---|
| The will leaves everything to the new spouse | The new spouse generally takes it; children have no automatic claim |
| There is no will | Intestacy law sets shares. See /free/illinois-intestacy-chart |
| A trust holds the property | The trust terms control, not intestacy |
| Your parent had dementia when signing | A capacity or undue influence claim may exist. See /free/undue-influence-warning-signs-checklist |
| A promise was made | Hard to enforce without writing |

## A second spouse's will

Can a stepmother disinherit you? If she inherited outright, usually yes. She owns the property and can leave it to whoever she chooses. Stepchildren often have no automatic inheritance rights. If the property was in a trust that locked at your parent's death, she may only have the income and the remainder goes to the named beneficiaries.

## What happens when the second spouse dies

| What the first spouse did | What happens at the survivor's death |
|---|---|
| Left all outright | The survivor's will controls |
| Left in a trust with children as remainder | Children receive what is left |
| Left a life estate in the house | Children take the house |
| Used joint ownership | The survivor owns it all |

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to bind a surviving spouse
- Your parent died and the new spouse received everything
- A joint will or trust already exists and you are unsure what it means
- Someone claims a promise was made
- A deadline for challenging a will may be running (many states allow only a short period)
