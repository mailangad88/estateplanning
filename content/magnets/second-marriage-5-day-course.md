A second marriage can mean two sets of loyalties: a spouse and children from an earlier relationship. The law does not choose between them for you. This five-day course takes one decision each day. It is general education, not legal advice, and rules vary by state.

## Day 1: Your plan may already be deciding for you

Most people assume a will controls everything. It does not. Assets that pass by title or by beneficiary form skip the will. Jointly owned property goes to the surviving owner. Retirement accounts and life insurance go to whoever is named.

That matters in a second marriage. Suppose you own a home with your spouse as joint owners and you die first. Your spouse owns the home outright. If your spouse later dies, it goes where your spouse decides, which may not include your children.

If you die without a plan, state law usually gives a share to your spouse and a share to your children. The split depends on the state and on whether the children are also your spouse's children. Stepchildren usually receive nothing.

Four routes to look for:

- **Survivorship title:** joint tenancy or similar
- **Beneficiary form:** retirement accounts, insurance, payable-on-death accounts
- **Trust terms:** assets titled in a trust
- **Will:** everything else, after probate

**Today's task:** List your five largest assets and write how each one passes today. Use the [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) if it helps.

## Day 2: Beneficiary forms and the retirement plan rule

Beneficiary forms are the quickest fix and the most often wrong. After a divorce or death, old forms can still name a former spouse. After a remarriage, they can leave out a new spouse or children.

Employer plans such as a 401(k) usually give a spouse a legal right to the account. A different beneficiary generally needs the spouse's signed written waiver. A prenup alone often does not replace it. IRAs follow different rules, and community property states add their own.

Ask each plan for its current beneficiary on file. Then decide:

- Who should be the primary beneficiary?
- Who is next if that person has died?
- Should a trust, not a person, receive the funds?
- Is the money meant to support your spouse, your children or both?

A spouse who has been named can also change the beneficiary after your death, unless the money is locked into a trust. If protecting the children's share is a goal, a trust can be a better home. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

**Today's task:** Log into each account, find the beneficiary and write it next to the account name. Mark any that surprise you.

## Day 3: The home

For many couples the home is the largest asset and the hardest decision. Both goals are real. A surviving spouse needs a place to live. Children may expect to inherit what a parent worked for.

Common approaches:

- **Joint title:** the survivor owns it. Simple, but the children depend on the survivor's later wishes.
- **Trust:** the spouse may live there for life or a set time, and the home then goes to the children.
- **Right to stay:** a time-limited right, such as one or two years, then sale or purchase by the children.
- **Sale at death:** proceeds split by an agreed formula.

Think about who pays taxes, insurance and repairs while the spouse lives there. A plan that gives a right to live in the home without saying who pays can cause conflict. Ask how title and mortgage rules work in your state. Read about [joint ownership vs trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust).

**Today's task:** Write one sentence about what you want to happen to the home if you die first, and one for if your spouse does. Compare with your spouse.

## Day 4: Using a trust to be fair to everyone

A trust can hold money for a spouse during life and then pass what is left to children. This is sometimes called a spousal lifetime trust or a marital trust. The trustee follows the written rules, so the first spouse to die can decide the outcome.

Key choices:

- **Trustee:** a neutral person or professional often reduces friction
- **Income:** does the spouse receive all of it?
- **Principal:** can the spouse use principal, and under what standard?
- **Reports:** do children get statements?
- **Remainder:** who receives what is left, and in what shares?

A trust can feel cold. Many couples write a short letter explaining their reasons. That letter is not binding but it can ease hurt feelings.

Life insurance is another tool. A policy naming your children can balance a house that goes to your spouse.

Spouses in most states also have some legal claim against the estate. Rules vary, and a prenup or postnup may address it.

**Today's task:** Write the three words that describe what each of you wants most: for example "security for my spouse", "equal treatment for my kids".

## Day 5: Talk, write and sign

A good plan can still fail if children are surprised. You do not have to share numbers, but you can share the approach.

A few practical steps:

- Tell your spouse and children who will be in charge if you cannot act.
- Say whether your spouse will be housed and who will manage the money.
- Name a trustee and executor who can handle both sides fairly.
- Update powers of attorney and health care directives. A spouse often has priority for medical decisions, so name who you want.
- Put personal items with sentimental value in writing.

Then meet an attorney in your state to turn decisions into documents: wills or trusts, retitled assets, new beneficiary forms and powers of attorney. Review again after any major change. Learn more in the [blended family guide](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families).

**Today's task:** Pick one person in your family to talk with this month and write the first sentence you will say. Then book a time with an attorney through the [plan finder](/plan-finder).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You and your spouse each have children and substantial separate assets
- You want a spouse to receive income but protect the principal for children
- You own a home or business together
- A spouse would need to waive retirement plan rights
- You are unsure how your state treats a surviving spouse's claim
