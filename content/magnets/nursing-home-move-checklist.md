**How to use this checklist.** Work through it with your parent and siblings where you can. Not every item applies to assisted living versus a nursing home, since the rules and contracts differ. The most important item is the first: read the admission agreement slowly, and do not sign under time pressure. Rules vary by state, so ask an attorney licensed in your state about anything unclear.

## 1. Before anyone signs: the admission agreement

Facilities often hand over a thick packet on the day of admission. You can usually take it home, read it and ask for changes or explanations. Signing is stressful, so give yourself time.

- [ ] Get a **copy of the full agreement** before the move date.
- [ ] Read who is signing, and in what role. Is it the resident, the agent under a power of attorney, or a family member as a "responsible party"?
- [ ] Check whether you are signing **personally** or only as an agent. If the form says you personally promise to pay, stop and ask questions.
- [ ] Watch for **third-party payment promises**, sometimes called third-party guarantor or co-signer clauses. Federal rules generally bar nursing homes that take Medicare or Medicaid from requiring a third party to personally pay as a condition of admission. A family member may sign as the resident's agent without taking on personal liability. Assisted living is regulated mostly by states, so the rules may differ. Ask an attorney before signing anything that makes you personally responsible.
- [ ] Look for **arbitration clauses**. Many agreements ask residents to give up the right to sue in court. Federal rules generally do not allow nursing homes that take Medicare or Medicaid to require arbitration as a condition of admission, but assisted living differs. Ask whether it is optional.
- [ ] Ask what is included in the **monthly rate** and what costs extra: medication management, incontinence care, transport, hair, therapy.
- [ ] Ask how often and by how much **rates can rise**, and how much notice is given.
- [ ] Ask about **discharge rules**: when can the facility ask the resident to leave, and what appeals exist?
- [ ] Ask what happens when **private funds run out**. Does the facility accept Medicaid, and is there a limit on how many beds are Medicaid-covered?
- [ ] Ask about the **refund policy** if the resident moves out, is hospitalized or dies.
- [ ] Ask about **bed-hold policies** for hospital stays.
- [ ] Keep a **signed copy** of everything.

## 2. Legal documents the facility will ask for

Bring copies, not originals.

- [ ] **Financial power of attorney.** Lets someone handle money, pay bills and deal with the facility. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).
- [ ] **Healthcare power of attorney** or proxy.
- [ ] **Advance directive or living will.** The facility will ask about code status, hospitalization and feeding choices.
- [ ] **POLST or similar medical orders**, if your parent has one. Names and rules vary by state.
- [ ] **HIPAA authorizations** for family members.
- [ ] **Guardianship or conservatorship paperwork**, if one exists.
- [ ] **Photo ID, Social Security card, Medicare and insurance cards.**
- [ ] **Medication list and recent medical records.**

If your parent has no power of attorney and still has capacity, signing one is generally the next step. If capacity is in question, a court-ordered guardianship may be the only route, which is slower and costlier. Speak to an attorney soon.

## 3. Benefits, insurance and income

- [ ] Collect statements for **Social Security, pensions, annuities, and VA benefits**.
- [ ] Ask whether the parent may be eligible for **VA Aid and Attendance**, if they or a spouse served in the military.
- [ ] Review **Medicare coverage**. Medicare generally pays for skilled nursing only for a limited time after a qualifying hospital stay. It does not generally pay for long-term custodial care.
- [ ] Check for **long-term care insurance**, and call the insurer about the claim process and elimination period.
- [ ] Notify the **Social Security Administration** of a new address or payee changes, if applicable.
- [ ] Update address with **banks, insurers, pharmacies, doctors and the post office**.
- [ ] Review **supplemental or Medigap coverage** and Part D drug plans, which may change when care level changes.

## 4. The home and belongings

Decisions about the house can have legal and tax consequences. Take your time before selling or transferring it.

- [ ] Decide whether the home will be **kept, rented, sold or left empty**. Each option has costs: taxes, insurance, upkeep, and effects on Medicaid.
- [ ] Do not **transfer the house to a child** without advice. Gifts and transfers for less than fair value can trigger a Medicaid penalty period, and a transfer can create tax problems for the child. Rules vary by state.
- [ ] Check **homeowner's insurance.** Many policies limit coverage when a home is vacant.
- [ ] If a **spouse still lives in the home**, ask an attorney about protections for the community spouse.
- [ ] Ask about **estate recovery**. Some states may seek repayment from a Medicaid recipient's estate, including the home, after death. Rules vary.
- [ ] Walk through the home with your parent and list **sentimental items** before they are moved or sold. See the guide on [settling an estate](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step) for ideas on dividing items later.
- [ ] Label what moves into the facility. Many have limits on furniture and valuables.
- [ ] Take photos of items before moving, and keep a **list of what goes to the room**.
- [ ] Do not leave **jewelry, cash or original documents** in a facility room.
- [ ] Choose a place for **mail and bills**, and forward them.

## 5. Medicaid planning and timing

Long-term care is costly, and many families eventually look at Medicaid. Planning early can reduce difficult surprises. Planning too late can limit options.

- [ ] Learn the **look-back period**, generally 60 months for most states (California differs). Use the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date).
- [ ] Make a list of **transfers or gifts** your parent made in the past five years, including to charities and family.
- [ ] Ask whether any **exempt transfers** exist for your situation, such as transfers to a spouse or certain disabled children.
- [ ] Ask about the **asset and income limits** in your state, which vary.
- [ ] Ask whether a **personal care agreement** is suitable if a family member has been providing care. See the personal care agreement guide.
- [ ] Do not give assets away to "qualify faster" without advice. It often backfires.
- [ ] Learn how the facility handles a **transition from private pay to Medicaid**, and what notice it requires.

See [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## 6. Day-to-day contacts and information

- [ ] Make an **important contacts list** for doctors, pharmacies, the agent and family. See the [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list).
- [ ] Ask who the **point person** is at the facility: administrator, social worker, nurse.
- [ ] Ask how to attend **care plan meetings** and how often they happen.
- [ ] Ask how complaints are raised, and write down the contact for the **long-term care ombudsman** in your area. Every state has one.
- [ ] Set up a **shared calendar or group chat** so siblings are not repeating each other.
- [ ] Keep a **notebook** of conversations, dates and names.

## 7. For the person moving

- [ ] Ask your parent what matters: a favorite chair, photos, a radio, a routine.
- [ ] Ask about **visiting hours, outings and religious services**.
- [ ] Make sure your parent's voice stays at the center of decisions, to the extent possible.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The admission agreement asks you to sign personally or contains a clause you do not understand.
- Your parent has no power of attorney and capacity is declining.
- The family home, savings or a spouse's needs are involved in the decision.
- Medicaid may be needed within a few years, or a gift has already been made.
- Siblings disagree about the move or about how money is used.
