## How to use this guide

A power of attorney for property lets someone you trust pay bills, manage accounts and handle other money matters for you. Illinois publishes a statutory short form in the Illinois Power of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45). This guide walks through the form, then gives you a worksheet to fill in. It is education, not legal advice, and other states have their own rules.

## What the Illinois form does

The person who signs is the principal. The person named is the agent. The form lists fifteen kinds of authority, and you strike any you do not want. They cover real estate, banks, stocks and bonds, personal property, safe deposit boxes, insurance and annuities, retirement plans, Social Security and benefits, taxes, claims and litigation, commodities, running a business, borrowing, estate transactions and a catch-all for other property. Powers continue before and after you lose capacity, unless you say otherwise.

The form does not cover health decisions. That is a separate document. See the [Illinois health care power of attorney guide](/free/illinois-health-care-poa-guide).

## Walking through the form

| Paragraph | What it covers | Decision for you |
|---|---|---|
| Notice | A statutory warning about the breadth of the powers | Read it aloud with the person helping you |
| 1 | Names your agent and revokes earlier powers | The form says you may not name co-agents on it |
| 2 | Lets you strike powers or add limits | Which powers does your agent really need? |
| 3 | Lets you add powers, such as gifting | Do you want gifts or trust changes allowed? |
| 4 | Lets the agent delegate some powers | Comfortable with an agent hiring help? |
| 5 | Agent compensation | Paid, or unpaid except for expenses? |
| 6 | Effective date | Now, or only on a future event? |
| 7 | Termination | Should it end on a date? |
| 8 | Successor agents | Who steps in if the first agent cannot? |
| 9 | Guardian nomination | Who do you want named if a court appoints a guardian? |

By default, the agent cannot make gifts or change beneficiaries. Paragraph 3 is where you add those, so write it carefully, because broad gifting authority gives an agent room to move your money.

## Choosing an agent

The agent has real power over your money. A good agent is organized, honest, and willing to keep records. Questions to ask:

- Do they live nearby, or can they handle things by phone and online?
- Can they stay calm with banks, doctors' offices and family members?
- Would other relatives accept them, or is a fight likely?
- Would they follow your wishes even when it is awkward?
- Do they have a conflict, such as owing you money or needing your assets?

Choose successors too. If the first agent dies or cannot serve, a successor takes over without a court case.

- **First choice for agent:** ____________________
- **Why:** ____________________
- **First successor:** ____________________
- **Second successor:** ____________________

## Limits you can add

Ask yourself what you want the agent never to do, or to do only in a certain way:

- [ ] Not sell my home without a stated reason, or without telling my family
- [ ] Not make gifts, or gifts only to listed people in listed amounts, such as the annual exclusion of $19,000 per recipient for 2026
- [ ] Not change beneficiaries
- [ ] Keep my estate plan intact, including my trust
- [ ] Give a yearly summary to a named relative

Write each limit in plain words, then ask an attorney to place it in the form.

- **Powers I want to strike:** ____________________
- **Limits I want:** ____________________
- **Gifts allowed, if any:** ____________________

## Effective date: now or later

By default the form takes effect when you sign. Paragraph 6 lets you delay it until a future date or event. People often choose a delay to keep control while healthy. The tradeoff is practical. If the form needs proof of incapacity first, a bank may ask for a doctor's letter or other proof, which can slow the agent when time matters. Ask an attorney how to word a trigger so that it can be proved quickly.

## Signing rules

The Illinois form needs the principal's signature, at least one witness and a notary public (755 ILCS 45/3-3). A second witness is allowed. The notary cannot also sign as the witness.

The witness cannot be:

- The agent or a successor agent
- A blood, marriage or adoptive relative of the principal or the agent
- The principal's attending physician or mental health provider, or that person's relative
- The owner or operator of a health care facility where the principal lives, or that person's relative

- [ ] Choose a witness with no tie to the agent
- [ ] Book a notary
- [ ] Sign, witness and notarize on the same day
- [ ] Make several certified copies, since banks often keep one

Agent specimen signatures are optional. Many institutions like them.

## What your agent owes you

Under the Act, the agent must act in good faith, with due care, competence and diligence. The agent should follow your known wishes and your interests. The agent must keep records of receipts, disbursements and significant actions, and give copies to the principal and certain others on request. If records are not produced within 21 days of a request, the person asking can petition the court, and the court may award costs. An agent who violates the Act can be required to restore the property's value and reimburse the principal's fees (755 ILCS 45/2-7).

Tell your agent this ahead of time. Give them the [agent handbook](/free/power-of-attorney-agent-handbook).

## Common mistakes

- Naming a co-agent on the statutory form, which the form does not allow
- Using a witness who is a relative or the agent
- Skipping the notary
- Signing, then never telling banks, so no one will recognize the document
- Giving gifting power without a limit
- Never reviewing it after a divorce, death or move

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own real estate, a business or property in more than one state
- You want gifts, trust funding or Medicaid planning authority in the form
- Family members may disagree about who should serve
- You want to delay the effective date, and want it to work in a crisis
- You already have a form from another state or an old form
- You fear someone may misuse your money
