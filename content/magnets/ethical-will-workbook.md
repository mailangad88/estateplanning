## How to use this workbook

An ethical will, also called a legacy letter, is a personal document in which you share what mattered to you. It is not a legal will and does not distribute property. It has a long tradition in many cultures, and today it can be a letter, a recording or a notebook. Write in your own words. Short and honest beats long and polished. Set aside three sittings of twenty minutes, and do not edit as you go.

Keep this separate from your legal documents. A legal will is read by a court and stays on the public record in many places. A legacy letter is private. Because it is not a legal document, do not use it to give instructions about money or property. Put those in your will or trust.

## Part 1: Where you came from

Start with stories. Specific details make people feel they know you.

- **A person who shaped me, and one thing they taught me:** ______________________
- **A place that feels like home to me, and why:** ______________________
- **A hard time I got through, and what helped:** ______________________
- **A family story I want remembered:** ______________________
- **Something I am proud of that few people know:** ______________________

Sample opening: "When I was nine, my father taught me to fix a bicycle chain. I did not know it then, but I was learning to be patient."

## Part 2: What I believe

This is the center of the letter. Choose three or four values rather than listing twenty.

| Value | A time I lived it | A time I fell short |
|---|---|---|
| ______________ | ______________ | ______________ |
| ______________ | ______________ | ______________ |
| ______________ | ______________ | ______________ |

Fill in the prompts that fit:

- **What I learned about money, work and enough:** ______________________
- **What I learned about forgiveness:** ______________________
- **What I learned about faith, meaning or gratitude:** ______________________
- **What I would do differently:** ______________________

Sample line: "I have learned that you do not need to win every argument. Most of the time, you only need to stay in the room."

## Part 3: Words for each person

A general letter is a good start. Short notes to specific people are even better. Keep these loving and fair. Avoid comparing children, bringing up old grievances or using the letter to settle scores.

- **Name:** ______________________
- **A memory I treasure with you:** ______________________
- **What I admire in you:** ______________________
- **My hope for you:** ______________________

Copy this block for each person. For young grandchildren, a few sentences is plenty. They may read it again at 20 or 40.

Sample note: "You have a way of making people feel at ease. Please keep that. It is rarer than you think."

## Part 4: Hopes and requests

This part looks forward. Be careful to make wishes, not demands, and avoid anything that sounds like a condition on an inheritance.

- **What I hope for our family:** ______________________
- **Traditions I hope you keep, or feel free to change:** ______________________
- **How I hope you will treat each other after I am gone:** ______________________
- **A final word:** ______________________

Sample closing: "Whatever you decide, I trust you. I loved watching you become who you are."

## Tone check

- [ ] Does it sound like me, spoken aloud?
- [ ] Is it kind, even where it is honest?
- [ ] Have I avoided blame, secrets that could hurt others and surprises about who inherits?
- [ ] Have I left money details to my legal documents?
- [ ] Would I be comfortable if everyone in the family read every line?

If the letter touches painful topics, such as an estrangement, write it and then set it aside for a week.

## Formats

| Format | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Handwritten letter | Warmth and handwriting that families keep | Legibility, one copy only |
| Typed letter | Easy to edit and copy | Feels less personal to some |
| Audio or video | Voice and face, especially for children | Technology changes, so keep a backup |
| Memory book with photos | Grandchildren and younger relatives | Label the people and dates |

## When to share it

Some people share at a family gathering or on a milestone. Some give it as a gift now and some leave it with their documents to be opened later. Consider telling someone where it is kept, and add a line to your letter of instruction. Update it every few years, since your views will grow.

## A note about legal documents

A legacy letter does not replace a legal will, powers of attorney or beneficiary forms. In some places a court may read a letter and treat it as an attempt to change a will, which can lead to disputes. To keep the letter personal, avoid including any language about who gets what. If your wishes about property or care change, your attorney can update the legal documents.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to include wishes about property, and need them in a legal document
- Your letter mentions a gift or condition that could affect an inheritance
- You have a blended family and want to avoid a misunderstanding
- You have a person in your life who may feel left out by your plan
- Your legal documents are out of date
