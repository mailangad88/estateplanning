**How to use this handbook.** You are likely grieving and busy. Take it one section at a time. Nothing here has to be done today. This is general education, not legal advice. Trustee duties, deadlines and notice rules vary by state, and the trust document controls.

## What a successor trustee does

A successor trustee takes over a trust when the person who made it dies or can no longer serve. You manage the trust property and give it to the beneficiaries as the trust says. You are a fiduciary, which means you must act carefully and honestly for the beneficiaries, not for yourself.

Many trusts avoid probate, but that does not mean there is no work. There is often still a good deal to do, and you may be personally responsible for mistakes.

## First steps

- [ ] Find the trust document and every amendment.
- [ ] Confirm you are named as successor trustee.
- [ ] Get several certified copies of the death certificate.
- [ ] Locate the person's will, usually a pour-over will.
- [ ] Secure the home, vehicles and valuables.
- [ ] Forward mail.
- [ ] Keep paying essential bills like insurance, utilities and taxes.
- [ ] Do not distribute anything yet.
- [ ] Contact an attorney familiar with trust administration.
- [ ] Start your record log.

Read the whole trust before taking action. Look for who gets what, when, any conditions, and any special instructions.

## Many trusts split at death

A joint trust of a married couple may divide into parts at the first death. A trust may become irrevocable at death. That means it generally can no longer be changed. These details affect taxes and accounts, so ask an attorney how your trust works.

## Getting an EIN

While the person was alive, the trust usually used their Social Security number. After death, an irrevocable trust is generally treated as its own taxpayer and often needs its own Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS. The EIN is free from the IRS and is usually obtained online. Banks typically ask for it when you open a trust account. Whether you need one, and how taxes are filed, depends on the trust. Ask an accountant or attorney.

Also list debts, taxes, subscriptions and recurring bills. Gather bank statements, brokerage statements, deeds, vehicle titles and insurance policies. Assets outside the trust, such as accounts with a named beneficiary, usually pass directly and are not yours to distribute.

Some assets were meant to go into the trust but never did. Those may need a short probate. Rules vary by state.

## Notices to beneficiaries

Some states require a trustee to send formal notices to beneficiaries and heirs within a set time after the trust becomes irrevocable. Others do not. If your state requires it, the notice usually says the trust exists, who the trustee is, and how to ask for a copy of the trust. Missing this can cause delays or give beneficiaries extra time to challenge the trust. Ask an attorney in your state what applies and by when.

Even where no formal notice is required, many trustees keep beneficiaries informed. Good communication prevents many disputes.

## Managing the assets

- [ ] Open a trust bank account under the trust's name and EIN.
- [ ] Keep trust money separate from your own.
- [ ] Insure property, especially vacant homes.
- [ ] Keep investments safe and reasonable until distribution.
- [ ] Do not sell property without understanding the tax effects.
- [ ] Get appraisals of real estate and other valuable items.
- [ ] Pay valid debts and expenses.
- [ ] Ask the accountant about final income tax returns and any trust returns.

Selling real estate is common, and the trust document usually gives you power to do so. Many trustees get an appraisal first.

## Distributing

Before you distribute, make sure debts, taxes and expenses are paid or reserved. Many trustees wait several months. Rules and deadlines vary by state.

- [ ] Follow the trust's terms exactly.
- [ ] Prepare a written accounting, if required or helpful.
- [ ] Keep a reserve for late bills and taxes.
- [ ] Get written receipts from each beneficiary.
- [ ] Ask an attorney before final distribution, and whether releases make sense.

Some trusts keep property in trust for years, for example for young beneficiaries. In that case, you may continue as trustee.

Also keep a log of calls, letters and decisions. Write down why you made each major decision.

Trustee pay: many states let trustees take reasonable compensation. Check the trust and state law, and write down what you take.

## When to get professional help

A trustee can usually hire help and pay for it from the trust. Common helpers:

- **Attorney:** notices, interpreting the trust, disputes, real estate transfers.
- **Accountant:** tax returns, EIN questions, basis step-up documentation.
- **Appraiser:** real estate, jewelry, art.
- **Financial advisor:** investments while the trust is held.
- **Estate sale or cleanout company:** for a home.

## Task timeline

| Timeframe | Tasks | Done |
|---|---|---|
| First week | Death certificates, secure property, find documents, call attorney |  |
| First month | Read the trust, get EIN if needed, open trust account, start log, send required notices |  |
| Months 2 to 3 | Inventory, appraisals, insurance, notify banks and agencies |  |
| Months 3 to 6 | Pay debts and bills, file final taxes, sell property if needed |  |
| Months 6 to 12 | Prepare accounting, make distributions as the trust allows |  |
| After distribution | Keep records, file final trust return, close accounts |  |

Real timelines depend on the size of the trust, your state and whether disputes arise. Many trusts take a year or longer.

## Common mistakes

- Distributing too early.
- Mixing trust money with your own.
- Ignoring beneficiaries' questions.
- Selling assets without an appraisal.
- Skipping insurance on an empty house.
- Not asking for help.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A beneficiary disputes the trust or your actions.
- The trust is unclear or conflicts with a will.
- There is real estate in another state.
- The estate may owe estate taxes.
- You are not sure about notices and deadlines.
- A beneficiary has special needs or is a minor.
