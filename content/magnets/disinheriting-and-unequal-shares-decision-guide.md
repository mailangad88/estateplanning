**How to use this guide.** Read the table, then write down what you want and why. Rules differ by state, and the Illinois points below are general. A will that disinherits someone is often the one most likely to be challenged, so this is a topic worth a conversation with an attorney. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Who can be left out

| Person | Can you leave them out? | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Adult child | In most US states, yes | Louisiana has forced heirship for some children, and other countries do too |
| Minor child | Usually yes on paper | Support duties during life, and other assets may pass by beneficiary form |
| Spouse | Only partly | Most states protect a surviving spouse with a spousal or elective share |
| Unmarried partner | Yes, no automatic right | Nothing passes without a will or other plan |
| Grandchildren | Yes | They take a parent's share only if you say so, or by statute |

Forced heirship is a legal system, used in Louisiana and many civil-law countries, that reserves part of an estate for certain children or relatives. Most states, Illinois included, do not apply it to adult children.

## Spouse: the elective share

The elective or spousal share lets a surviving spouse reject the will and take a fixed fraction instead. In Illinois, a spouse who renounces the will generally takes one third of the estate if the person left descendants, or one half if not, under 755 ILCS 5/2-8. <!-- verify: Illinois renunciation fractions and filing deadline in 755 ILCS 5/2-8 -->

A spouse must act within a short window after the will is admitted. Missing it can end the right.

| Plan | Result |
|---|---|
| Leaves spouse out entirely | Spouse may renounce and take the share |
| Leaves spouse a life interest in a trust | Spouse may still choose the statutory share |
| Spouse signs a written waiver, such as a prenup or postnuptial agreement | The share may be waived. See [prenup and estate plan checklist](/free/prenup-and-estate-plan-checklist) |

Beneficiary forms and joint ownership can move assets to a spouse outside the will.

## How to disinherit on purpose

1. **Name the person.** A silent will can look like an accident.
2. **State the choice in the will.** For example: "I intentionally make no provision for my son, Mark."
3. **Cover other routes.** Review beneficiary forms, joint titles and transfer on death deeds. A will does not control those.
4. **Skip the reasons.** Reasons in a will can invite arguments.
5. **Think about the backup.** If the person is left out and dies first, do their children get anything? Say so.

Do not rely on silence. Leaving a child out of a will without mention can raise a claim that you forgot them. A will does not have to mention every child, but a clear statement shows intent.

## Unequal but fair?

You may leave one child more, or everything, to one child. Common reasons:

- A child provided years of care
- One child already received help during life
- One child has a disability and needs a different structure
- One child is financially secure, one is not
- A family business goes to the child who runs it

| Reason | Ways to even it out |
|---|---|
| Caregiving | Extra gift, or a written personal care agreement paid during life |
| Past help | List loans and gifts in the will and treat them as advances |
| Family business | Equalize with life insurance or other assets |
| Disability | A supplemental needs trust, not a smaller outright gift |

Write down the math. Add up each person's share in dollars at current values, then compare.

- **Reason for any difference:** ____________________

## Conditions on gifts

A will can attach a condition to a gift. The limits come from public policy and clarity.

| Condition | Usually allowed? |
|---|---|
| Finish a degree by a certain age | Often yes, if clearly written |
| Stay sober, with drug test | Often yes, but hard to administer |
| Never marry someone of a certain religion or race | Often struck down as against public policy |
| Marry a specific person | Courts frown on these |
| Live in a particular house | Can be allowed, with a backup if it fails |
| Reach age 30 before getting full funds | Common and easily enforced through a trust |

Most conditions work best inside a trust with a trustee who can judge them. A will alone leaves the executor to guess. Illinois courts read conditions narrowly, and vague conditions may fail. <!-- verify: Illinois approach to conditional gifts and restraints on marriage -->

A conditional will, which only takes effect if an event occurs, is a different idea and is rarely used.

## No-contest clauses

A no-contest clause says anyone who challenges the will loses their gift. It only bites if the person has something to lose. A child left $0 has nothing to forfeit.

- Courts in many states enforce them but read them narrowly. Some states limit them if a challenge was in good faith. <!-- verify: Illinois enforcement of no-contest clauses -->
- It works best when the gift is large enough to make a challenge a real risk.
- It is not a substitute for a clear, well-signed will.

## Making a will harder to challenge

- [ ] Sign with the attorney, with independent witnesses and a self-proving affidavit
- [ ] Do not let a beneficiary take part in drafting or attend the meeting
- [ ] Ask a doctor to note capacity if there is any health concern
- [ ] Keep notes on why you made the choices
- [ ] Avoid last-minute changes made while ill
- [ ] Tell the family in your own words (see [talking to adult children](/free/talking-to-adult-children-about-your-will-guide))
- [ ] Keep prior wills, so the history is clear

See also the [contesting a will basics guide](/free/contesting-a-will-basics-guide).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You plan to leave out a child or spouse
- You are in a second marriage
- A child receives government benefits
- You want conditions that need a trustee
- You are worried about a challenge
