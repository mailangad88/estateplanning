## How to use this guide

We are sorry you are dealing with this on top of a loss. Most executor and trustee problems start as silence, not theft. This guide sorts the problem, shows the step that usually comes first, and gives you a log so you can keep the facts straight. It is general education for Illinois, not legal advice. Other states use different rules and deadlines.

## Step 1: Sort the problem

| What you see | Usually means | First step |
|---|---|---|
| No calls back, no news for months | Slow or overwhelmed | Written request for status |
| Will never filed with the court | Possible delay or confusion | Check the clerk's records, then ask in writing |
| No inventory or accounting offered | Missing information | Written request, then ask for the court file |
| Bills unpaid, estate property neglected | Neglect | Written request, then talk to an attorney |
| Executor living in or using estate property | Self-dealing risk | Ask for the basis in writing |
| Money moving to the executor with no record | Possible misuse | Preserve evidence, talk to an attorney soon |
| Trustee does nothing after the grantor's death | Inaction | Written request for the trust terms and a timeline |

## Warning signs

Slow is common. Dishonest is rare. Treat each sign as a reason to ask a question, not as proof.

### Signs of delay

- [ ] No letters of office obtained after several months
- [ ] No notice sent to heirs or beneficiaries
- [ ] No estate bank account opened
- [ ] Bills, taxes or insurance on the house falling behind

### Signs of trouble

- [ ] Refuses to share the will, trust terms or an accounting
- [ ] Sells property quickly, to friends or to themselves, with no explanation
- [ ] Pays themselves large amounts with no record
- [ ] Mixes estate money with their own
- [ ] Hands out property before debts and claims are dealt with

## What you are entitled to ask for

An executor in Illinois must file the will with the circuit court clerk within 30 days of learning of the death (755 ILCS 5/6-1). <!-- verify: current wording of 6-1 deadline --> Once the estate is open, the file is a public court record. You can ask the clerk what has been filed. Heirs and legatees are entitled to notice of the case (755 ILCS 5/6-10). <!-- verify: notice timing under 6-10 -->

For trusts, Illinois follows the Trust Code (760 ILCS 3). A trustee has a duty to keep beneficiaries reasonably informed and to respond to requests (see 760 ILCS 3/813.1). <!-- verify: section number for duty to inform and account -->

## Step 2: Ask in writing

A calm letter often fixes the problem and also starts your paper trail. Copy this and fill it in.

> Dear ____________,
> I am a beneficiary of ____________'s estate (or trust). I would like to understand where things stand. Please send me: (1) the case number or confirmation that the will was filed, (2) a list of assets found so far, (3) an accounting of money received and paid, and (4) your expected timeline. Please reply by ____________. Thank you.

- **Date sent:** ____________________
- **How sent (email, certified mail):** ____________________
- **Reply by:** ____________________

## Step 3: Options when asking does not work

1. **Informal:** a family meeting, or a neutral third person. This sometimes works when the trouble is poor communication.
2. **Resignation:** many people agree to step aside when the role is too much. A trust may name a backup successor trustee.
3. **Removal without court (trusts only):** some trust documents let a majority of beneficiaries, or a named person, replace the trustee. Read the trust.
4. **Court petition:** a beneficiary or interested person can ask the circuit court to remove an executor or trustee. For an executor, the Probate Act addresses removal at 755 ILCS 5/23-2. <!-- verify: grounds listed in 23-2 --> For a trustee, see 760 ILCS 3/706. <!-- verify: Trust Code removal section -->

## What courts commonly look at

Reasons that are commonly raised in removal cases include:

- Misuse or waste of estate or trust property
- Conflict of interest that harms beneficiaries
- Failure to act, or failure to account
- Inability to serve because of illness or incapacity
- Persistent unfair treatment of one beneficiary

Disagreement alone is rarely enough. Courts want facts: dates, amounts and documents.

## Paying yourself

Executors and trustees are generally allowed reasonable pay. Reasonable depends on the work done and the document. Pay for a task not done, or pay taken without a record, is where trouble starts. Ask to see the basis for any payment.

## Where to report suspected theft

- The circuit court judge handling the case, through a petition from an attorney
- Local law enforcement, for clear theft or forgery
- The Illinois Attorney General's office, for charitable trust problems <!-- verify: AG role for trust complaints -->

## Common mistakes

- Waiting years with no written request
- Posting accusations on social media
- Assuming the will is already filed
- Moving out belongings or accounts yourself
- Missing the deadline to challenge something because of delay <!-- verify: Illinois limits for claims against fiduciaries -->

## When to talk to an attorney

- Money or property seems to be disappearing
- The executor or trustee will not share the will, trust or an accounting
- You are close to a deadline to act
- The executor is also the main beneficiary and the family is split
- A court filing or petition for removal is being considered
