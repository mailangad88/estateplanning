**How to use this planner.** Work through it in order over a few weeks. It is educational, not advice, and benefit rules vary by program, by state and over time. Always confirm current rules with an attorney who works in special needs planning and with the agency that runs the benefit.

## Step 1: Understand how benefits and inheritance interact

Many public benefits for people with disabilities are means-tested. That means eligibility depends on income and countable assets. Two commonly discussed programs:

- **Supplemental Security Income (SSI):** a federal cash benefit with strict asset limits, set by federal rules.
- **Medicaid:** health coverage, and in many states long-term support, with asset limits that rules vary on by state and by eligibility category.

A gift or inheritance left directly to a person on these benefits can put eligibility at risk. A common reason families plan is to leave money in a way that supplements benefits, without replacing them. The details, such as exact limits and what counts, change, so verify them with the agency and an attorney.

## Step 2: Choose how money is held

Two tools are often compared.

| | Supplemental needs trust (third-party) | ABLE account |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A trust managed by a trustee, funded by family, for the person's extra needs | A tax-advantaged savings account owned by the person with a disability |
| Who funds it | Parents, grandparents, others, often through a will or insurance | Anyone, within annual limits |
| Annual contribution limit | None set by the trust itself | Equals the annual gift exclusion<!-- verify -->, as of 2026, with other rules |
| Who controls spending | The trustee, within the trust terms | The account owner or an authorized person |
| Eligibility rules | Generally flexible | Disability must begin before a certain age, set by federal law |
| Effect on benefits | Designed to avoid counting as the person's assets if properly drafted | Balances up to a threshold are generally disregarded for SSI, rules differ above that |
| Payback at death | Third-party trusts usually have none | States may have a claim for Medicaid payback |
| Best for | Large or long-term money, ongoing management | Day-to-day spending, housing, transport, education |

Many families use both. A trust holds the larger inheritance. An ABLE account pays everyday costs. See [Special Needs Trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts) and [special needs trust vs. ABLE account](/compare/special-needs-trust-vs-able-account).

### First-party vs third-party trusts

A trust funded with the person's own money, such as a settlement or back pay, is a first-party trust and generally has a Medicaid payback requirement. A trust funded by someone else is third-party and usually does not. Mixing the two can cause problems, so keep them separate.

### Questions to ask

- Who will be the trustee, and who will take over?
- What can the trust pay for that benefits do not?
- How are distributions made, and who approves them?
- What happens to the money if the person dies?
- Does every family member who might leave money know not to leave it outright?

### Things to think about

- **Guardianship alternatives.** Some adults can use supported decision-making or limited powers of attorney. Which options exist varies by state.
- **Siblings.** Many families ask a sibling to serve. Ask early and be honest about the workload.
- **Professional trustees.** A bank or nonprofit trustee may outlast any individual.

## Step 4: Write a letter of intent

A letter of intent is not a legal document. It tells the next caregiver what you know. Keep it up to date and store it with your documents.

### Template

1. **Who they are.** Name, birthday, diagnosis, personality, the things that make them laugh and the things that frighten them.
2. **Daily routine.** Wake time, meals, medications, hygiene, bedtime, and the order in which things usually happen.
3. **Health.** Doctors, medicines, allergies, therapies, hospitals, insurance, equipment.
4. **Communication.** How they express yes, no, pain, joy or distress. Words, signs, devices.
5. **Behavior and safety.** Triggers, calming tools, safety concerns, what to do in an emergency.
6. **Education and work.** School, day program, job, goals, supports.
7. **Home and community.** Where they live, who helps, favorite places, friends.
8. **Money and benefits.** Programs, case workers, trust information, bank accounts.
9. **Legal documents.** Where they are kept and who the attorney is.
10. **Your hopes.** What a good life looks like for them.

### Worked example

A mother of a 22-year-old with autism writes: "Sam wakes at 6:30 and wants the same breakfast. He covers his ears when he is overwhelmed. Give him headphones and a quiet room before you ask a question. Tuesdays and Thursdays he goes to his day program. His speech therapist, Ms. Okafor, knows him best." This is the kind of detail a new caregiver cannot guess.

## Common mistakes

- Naming the person with the disability directly as a beneficiary.
- Using a generic trust form without special needs language.
- Assuming a guardian will also be a good money manager.
- Not telling relatives about the plan.
- Writing the letter of intent once and never updating it.
- Forgetting that benefit rules and trust rules vary by state.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your loved one receives SSI, Medicaid or waiver services.
- You want to set up a supplemental needs trust or are unsure about a first-party trust.
- A child is approaching age 18 and decisions about guardianship are coming.
- A relative wants to leave money to your loved one.
- Your loved one received a settlement or inheritance.
- You are comparing a trust with an ABLE account.
