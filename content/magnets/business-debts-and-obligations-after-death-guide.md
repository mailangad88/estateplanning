## How to use this guide

Business debts are not one thing. Some belong to the company, some to the owner, and some to both. Use the table to sort each debt, then follow the steps. This is general education, and rules vary by state and by the terms of each loan or lease.

## Who owes what

| Business form | Debt usually belongs to | Estate exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | The owner personally | The estate, because business and owner are the same |
| LLC or corporation | The company | Usually the company only, unless the owner signed personally |
| General partnership | The partners, sometimes personally | The estate can be liable for partnership debts |
| Any form, personal promise signed | The owner and the company | The estate, up to what the paper says |

A personal promise is a signature on a loan, lease or credit line that makes the owner answerable if the company does not pay. These are common with banks and landlords.

## Does an LLC shield the estate?

Often, yes, for ordinary company debts. The company is a separate legal person. The shield can fail if the owner mixed personal and company money, ignored formalities, or signed a personal promise. The shield does not cancel loans the owner personally backed.

A charging order is a creditor's tool against an owner's LLC interest. It lets a creditor collect distributions the LLC pays out, but typically not take over the company. How strong it is varies by state.

## The first weeks, step by step

1. **List every debt.** Loans, cards, leases, vendor accounts, taxes, payroll.
2. **Mark the signature.** For each, note whether the owner signed personally.
3. **Freeze new spending.** Only the person with authority should sign checks.
4. **Notify lenders and landlords** in writing, with a death certificate.
5. **Keep payroll and taxes current.** These can create personal exposure.
6. **Decide: run, sell or wind down.** Do this with an attorney and an accountant.

## Debts, one at a time

| Debt | What commonly happens |
|---|---|
| SBA loan | The lender reviews the file and any personal promise or collateral. Death often triggers a conversation, not automatic demand, but terms vary. Call the lender early |
| Equipment loan | Secured by the equipment. Keep paying, or surrender it |
| Commercial lease | Stays in force until it ends or is assigned. A personal promise may reach the estate |
| Vendor accounts | Usually unsecured. The company or estate is asked to pay, in order of priority |
| Payroll and sales taxes | Priority debts. Unpaid trust-fund taxes can reach individuals |
| Credit cards in the owner's name | Owed by the estate |

## Probate and the business

A sole proprietor's business assets generally pass through probate, unless held in a trust. In Illinois, claims against an estate have a deadline set by law, generally tied to notice and to two years from death. See 755 ILCS 5/18-12. <!-- verify: claims bar date rules in 755 ILCS 5/18-12 --> Other states differ.

An LLC interest or stock in the owner's name also goes through probate if there is no trust or other transfer. The executor can often keep the business running only with court authority or a will that grants it. Ask about "letters of office" and operating powers early.

## Bank accounts

An account titled in the owner's name is frozen at death. Accounts titled to a company can stay open if another authorized signer exists. If no one is on the account, the bank will usually want proof of the executor's appointment first.

- [ ] Name a second signer on every business account while you are healthy
- [ ] Keep a separate operating account from personal accounts
- [ ] Ask the bank what it needs to add a successor signer

## If the business is losing money

A business that is losing money at death is a real decision, not a default. Options include a fast sale, an orderly wind-down, or a short bridge while heirs decide. An executor who keeps a failing business running may take risks. Written advice from an attorney helps.

## What family should not sign quickly

- A new personal promise to a lender or landlord
- A "temporary" lease assumption without reading it
- Payment promises to vendors by phone
- Any statement that the family will "take over" the business

## When to talk to an attorney

- The owner signed personal promises
- The estate may owe more than it holds
- A lender or landlord is demanding payment
- Payroll or sales taxes are behind
- The business is the main asset of the estate
