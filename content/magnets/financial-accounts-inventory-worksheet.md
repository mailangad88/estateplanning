**How to use this worksheet.** For each account, write the institution, a nickname, the last four digits at most, the ownership type and the beneficiary. Never write full account numbers, logins or PINs here. If this paper is lost, it should tell a stranger very little. Include accounts with small balances: they are the ones most often forgotten.

## Know your ownership types first

How an account is titled usually decides who gets it, and it can override your will. Terms and rules vary by state.

| Ownership type | What it means | What happens at death |
|---|---|---|
| Individual, no beneficiary | Yours alone | Usually goes through probate under the will |
| Individual with payable-on-death (POD) or transfer-on-death (TOD) | Yours alone, with a named beneficiary | Passes to the beneficiary, outside probate |
| Joint with right of survivorship | Two or more owners | Passes to surviving owner, outside probate |
| Joint tenants in common | Shares are separate | Your share goes through the estate |
| Retirement account (IRA, 401(k)) | Beneficiary form on file | Passes by the form |
| Trust account | Titled to the trust | Follows the trust terms |
| Custodial (UTMA or UGMA) | Held for a minor | Moves to the child or a successor custodian, per state law |

Read more in [probate vs non-probate assets](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) and [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

## Keeping it useful

- Store it with your plan binder or document locator, and tell your executor where it is.
- Keep the real details, such as statements and login, behind a password manager.
- Update after each life event and at least once a year.
- Record the date you last confirmed each beneficiary with the institution, not only from memory.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming a will beats a beneficiary form. Generally it does not.
- Naming "my children" with no names or shares, then having a child die or marry.
- Listing joint accounts as "mine" when they pass to the co-owner.
- Forgetting accounts held in a former employer's plan or at a bank you left.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your accounts pass in ways that do not match your will.
- You want a trust to be a beneficiary of a retirement account.
- You are in a second marriage or have children from a prior relationship.
- You have a beneficiary with special needs, a creditor problem or addiction.
- You hold assets in more than one state or country.
- You are adding a child to an account to make things easier and want to understand risks.
