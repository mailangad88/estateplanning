**How to use this cheat sheet.** Skim the section that matches your question, or search the page for a word. Definitions are general and simplified. Legal meanings and rules vary by state, so ask an attorney licensed in your state before relying on any of them. For the site's full glossary, see [/glossary](/glossary).

## Wills

**Will.** A written document that says who gets your property, who serves as executor and, if you have minor children, who you want as guardian. It takes effect at death.

**Executor.** The person you name to carry out your will. Some states use the term "personal representative."

**Personal representative.** The formal court term in many states for an executor or an administrator.

**Administrator.** The person a court appoints to handle an estate when there is no will or no named executor available.

**Intestate.** Dying without a valid will. State law then decides who inherits, usually relatives in a set order.

**Codicil.** A signed amendment that changes part of an existing will without replacing all of it. Many attorneys prefer a new will for larger changes.

**Witness.** A person who watches you sign a will and confirms you seemed to understand what you were doing. Required number and qualifications vary by state.

**Self-proving affidavit.** A signed, notarized statement by the witnesses attached to a will. In many states it lets the court accept the will without calling witnesses later.

**Residuary estate.** Whatever is left after specific gifts and debts are handled. A will usually says who gets it.

**Bequest.** A gift of property made in a will. A "specific bequest" names an exact item. A "general bequest" is usually a set amount of money.

**Testamentary capacity.** The mental ability needed to make a valid will. It generally means you know who your family is, what you own and what the document does.

**Will contest.** A legal challenge to the validity of a will, often claiming undue influence or lack of capacity.

## Trusts

**Trust.** A legal arrangement where one person or institution holds property for the benefit of others, following written rules.

**Grantor.** The person who creates and funds a trust. Also called a settlor or trustor.

**Trustee.** The person or company that manages trust property and follows the trust's rules.

**Successor trustee.** The person named to take over when the first trustee dies, resigns or cannot serve.

**Beneficiary (of a trust).** A person or group who receives benefits from the trust, such as income or the final distribution.

**Revocable living trust.** A trust you can change or cancel while alive. Property in it usually avoids probate. See the [revocable living trust guide](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).

**Irrevocable trust.** A trust that usually cannot be changed or ended easily once signed. It may offer tax or asset protection benefits. See [irrevocable trusts explained](/guides/irrevocable-trusts-explained).

**Funding.** Moving ownership of assets into the trust's name. A trust that is signed but never funded may accomplish little. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust).

**Pour-over will.** A will that sends any assets left outside your trust into the trust at death. Those assets generally still go through probate.

**Special needs trust.** A trust designed to help a person with a disability without disqualifying them from certain public benefits. See [special needs trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts).

**Fiduciary duty.** The legal duty to act in another person's best interest. Trustees, executors and agents under a power of attorney all owe it.

## Probate

**Probate.** The court-supervised process of proving a will, paying debts and distributing property. Length and cost vary by state. See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).

**Probate estate.** Assets that must pass through probate because they have no beneficiary or joint owner and are not in a trust.

**Non-probate assets.** Property that passes outside probate, such as accounts with a named beneficiary, joint accounts with survivorship and trust property.

**Letters testamentary.** A court document that proves the executor's authority. Banks and others often ask to see it. Some states use other names.

**Small estate procedure.** A simplified process for estates below a state-set value. Thresholds and steps vary widely.

**Ancillary probate.** A second probate in another state where the person owned real estate.

**Creditor claim period.** A set time during which creditors may make claims against the estate. The length varies by state.

**Heir.** A person who inherits under state law when there is no will. In casual use, anyone who inherits.

**Per stirpes.** A way to divide a gift so that if a child dies first, their share goes to their own children.

**Per capita.** A way to divide a gift equally among those who are alive at the time.

## Incapacity

**Incapacity.** Being unable to make or communicate your own decisions about health or money. The legal test varies.

**Power of attorney.** A document that names an agent to act for you on money or legal matters. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

**Durable power of attorney.** One that stays in effect if you become incapacitated. Without "durable" language, some power of attorney documents end then.

**Springing power of attorney.** One that takes effect only when a defined event happens, usually a doctor's finding of incapacity.

**Agent (attorney-in-fact).** The person you name in a power of attorney. They need not be a lawyer.

**Principal.** The person who signs a power of attorney and is represented by the agent.

**Healthcare proxy.** Another name for the person you choose to make medical decisions for you. Terms vary by state.

**Healthcare power of attorney.** A document naming someone to make medical decisions if you cannot.

**Living will.** A written statement of the medical treatment you would or would not want near the end of life. See [healthcare directives](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).

**Advance directive.** An umbrella term for a living will, a healthcare power of attorney or both.

**HIPAA authorization.** A signed release that lets named people receive your medical information.

**POLST or MOLST.** A medical order, signed by a clinician, for people with serious illness. Names and use vary by state.

**Guardianship (of an adult) or conservatorship.** A court process that appoints someone to make decisions for a person found unable to do so. It is often costly and public.

**Guardian (of a minor).** The person you name in your will to raise your children if both parents die. A court must approve.

## Tax

**Estate tax.** A federal tax on the value of a large estate at death. The federal exemption is $15 million per person for 2026 <!-- verify -->, with later indexing for inflation. Some states have their own lower thresholds.

**Inheritance tax.** A tax on the person who receives an inheritance, charged by a small number of states.

**Gift tax.** A federal tax on large lifetime gifts, tied to the same overall exemption as the estate tax.

**Annual exclusion.** The amount you can give each person per year without filing a gift tax return. It was $19,000 per recipient for 2025 <!-- verify -->.

**Step-up in basis.** For many assets inherited at death, the tax cost basis resets to the value on the date of death, which can reduce capital gains tax when the heir sells.

**Basis.** Generally what you paid for an asset, used to figure taxable gain when it is sold.

**Portability.** A rule that allows a surviving spouse to use a deceased spouse's unused federal estate tax exemption, if the right return is filed.

**Marital deduction.** A rule allowing unlimited transfers between spouses, in many cases, without federal estate or gift tax.

## Beneficiaries and ownership

**Beneficiary.** A person or organization who receives property from a will, trust, account or policy.

**Primary beneficiary.** The first person named to receive an account or policy.

**Contingent beneficiary.** The backup who receives it if the primary has died or declines.

**Beneficiary designation.** A form on a retirement account, life insurance or annuity that names who gets it. It often overrides the will. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

**Payable-on-death (POD).** A bank account feature naming who gets the money at your death. See [TOD and POD](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death).

**Transfer-on-death (TOD).** A similar feature for investment accounts, vehicles and, in some states, real estate.

**Joint tenancy with right of survivorship.** Co-owned property that passes automatically to the surviving owner at death.

**Tenancy in common.** Co-owned property where each owner's share passes under their own will or by intestacy.

**Community property.** In some states, property earned during marriage is owned equally by both spouses. Rules vary.

**Disclaimer.** A written refusal to accept an inheritance, which sends it to the next person in line. It has strict timing rules.

**UTMA or UGMA account.** A custodial account that holds money for a minor until they reach an age set by state law. See [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors).

**ABLE account.** A tax-advantaged savings account for people with certain disabilities, with limits tied to the annual gift exclusion.

**Medicaid look-back.** A review of gifts and transfers made before applying for long-term care benefits. The federal period is 60 months, though California differs. See [Medicaid planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning).

## When to talk to an attorney

- A term in your document is unclear and you are not sure what it does
- You are unsure whether a gift will go by will, trust or beneficiary form
- Your documents were written in a different state
- You see a term here that seems to match a family concern you have
- You want a document reviewed rather than interpreted by a glossary
