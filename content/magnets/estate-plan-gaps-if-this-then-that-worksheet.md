## How to use this worksheet

Plans fail at the edges: a form never filed, a name not changed, an account not retitled. Read each row. If the "if" describes you, write the date you will fix it. Rules differ by state and by plan, so this describes common patterns, not a promise of any result.

## Trust gaps

| If this is true | Then this commonly happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You signed a trust but never retitled the house | The house is not in the trust and may need probate | Record a deed into the trust, see [house in trust deed worksheet](/free/house-in-trust-deed-worksheet) |
| You forgot one account | That account follows its own beneficiary form or goes through probate | Retitle or name the trust as beneficiary |
| You never funded the trust at all | The trust may be empty and a pour-over will sends assets through probate | Work through the [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist) |
| The assets are in your name, but the trust says they are trust property | Some states let a court confirm it, for example the California petition called a Heggstad petition | Ask an attorney in your state |
| You named a trust as IRA beneficiary | It can work, but the trust terms affect tax timing | Have the trust reviewed for retirement account rules |
| The trust was drafted but never signed | It may not exist | Sign it with the right formalities |
| Your will refers to a trust that was never created | The gift may fail or be interpreted by a court | Create the trust or rewrite the will |

A Heggstad petition is a California court procedure to confirm that property belongs in a trust even though it was never retitled. Other states have different tools. In Illinois, ask an attorney whether a court petition could fit your facts. <!-- verify: Illinois equivalent for confirming trust property -->

## Beneficiary form gaps

| If this is true | Then this commonly happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Your ex-spouse is still named | State law may cancel the designation after divorce for some assets, but many retirement plans follow the form | Update forms right after a divorce |
| Ex named on life insurance | The insurer often pays the person named on the form | Change the beneficiary now |
| Ex named on a 401(k) | Federal rules can override state law, so the ex may be paid | Complete the plan's form |
| Blank beneficiary box | The default in the contract applies, often your estate or your spouse | Name a primary and a backup |
| No beneficiary on a bank account | The account usually goes through probate | Add a POD beneficiary |
| No beneficiary on a 401(k) | Plan rules apply, commonly the spouse and then the estate | File a form |
| No beneficiary on an IRA | The custodian's default order applies, often the estate | File a form |
| No beneficiary on life insurance | The policy's default order applies, often the estate | File a form |
| Two forms on file that conflict | The company generally follows the latest valid signed form | Keep one clean copy |
| Form conflicts with your will | The form usually wins | Update the form, not the will |

See [beneficiary beats will](/guides/beneficiary-designations) and the [beneficiary designation audit](/free/beneficiary-designation-audit).

## Gap checklist

- [ ] Every retirement account has a primary and contingent beneficiary on file
- [ ] Every life insurance policy has a beneficiary I would pick today
- [ ] Every bank account is retitled, POD or intentionally in my will
- [ ] Any trust has been signed and has assets in it
- [ ] An ex is not named anywhere
- [ ] A trust named as beneficiary has been reviewed for retirement account rules
- [ ] My will, trust and forms point to the same people

## What cannot be fixed after a death

- A blank beneficiary box stays blank
- An ex-spouse named on a retirement account may still be paid
- A conflict between a form and a will is decided by the form in most cases
- A missing signature cannot be added by family

Some mistakes can be reduced by a disclaimer, a court petition or a settlement among beneficiaries, but all depend on facts. Ask an attorney.

## Common mistakes

- Updating a will but never touching beneficiary forms
- Naming a minor child directly as beneficiary
- Not keeping copies of forms
- Assuming a divorce decree updates every account

## When to talk to an attorney

- You found a gap and cannot tell how to fix it
- A trust is involved in retirement account planning
- An ex or a blended family is in the picture
- The account is large
- You have a gap and a person has already died
