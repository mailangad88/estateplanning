## How to use this planner

Fair and equal are not always the same. A child who has worked in the business for twenty years and a child who moved away may both feel wronged by an equal split. Fill in the page below, then test each option against it. This is general education, and tax and entity rules vary.

## Five ways to equalize

| Option | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Life insurance for the others | A policy owned outside the estate pays non-business children | Premiums, health, and who owns it |
| Business child buys out siblings | Over time, with a note secured by the shares | The business must produce the cash |
| Unequal shares | Business child receives voting shares, others non-voting | Non-voting holders still want a fair return |
| Other assets for others | Home, savings or land go to non-business children | Needs enough other assets |
| Fixed payment from the estate | Business child owes an equalizing amount | Can strain the business |

## Worked example

Maria owns a plumbing company worth about $900,000 and has $300,000 in other assets. Her son works in the business. Her daughter is a nurse and has no interest in it.

- Equal split of everything: the son gets $600,000 of business value, the daughter $600,000 mostly in business shares she does not want.
- Fair-to-both option: the son takes the company. A $600,000 life policy owned by a trust pays the daughter, and the $300,000 in other assets splits equally.

The numbers are only an illustration. A real plan needs a valuation and a tax review.

## Gift now or leave at death

| | Gift or sell now | Leave at death |
|---|---|---|
| Control | You can phase it in, keep a role | You keep full control until death |
| Income tax basis | A gift generally carries over your basis | Heirs commonly get a basis reset to value at death |
| Gift tax | Reportable if above the annual exclusion | None at the time |
| Child's creditors or divorce | Exposed once the child owns it | Exposed after death unless held in a trust |

Ask a tax attorney which fits. Federal gift tax filing and basis rules apply, and some states add their own. <!-- verify: confirm current federal annual exclusion before quoting a figure -->

## When a child does not want it

| If this happens | Common responses |
|---|---|
| No child wants the business | Sell during life, or to key employees or a competitor |
| A child wants it but is not ready | A hired manager and a trust that holds shares until the child is ready |
| A child says yes, then leaves | A buy-back right in the agreement |
| A non-family employee is the best choice | Sell or bring the employee in through a gradual buy-in |

Ask each child directly, before you write the plan. Many family plans fail because nobody asked.

## Protecting the business from a child's divorce

Shares left outright to a child may be exposed to a spouse in a divorce, depending on state law. A trust holding the shares, with the child as a beneficiary and a trustee voting them, often helps. Other protections include a postnuptial agreement. Rules vary by state.

## Telling the family

1. Meet when no one is upset, not at a holiday.
2. Say what you decided and why, in plain words.
3. Say what you did not decide yet.
4. Give each child time to ask questions privately.
5. Put the plan in writing and in your attorney's file.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The business is most of your estate
- A child works in it and others do not
- You want to gift shares now
- You want a trust to hold the shares
- Your children disagree about the plan
