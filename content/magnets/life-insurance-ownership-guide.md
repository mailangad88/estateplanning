**How to use this guide.** A life insurance policy has three roles: the insured, the owner and the beneficiary. Many people never decide the second one. This guide explains how to choose, then gives you a map to fill in for each policy. If you are still working out how much coverage to buy, use the [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) first. Rules vary by state and by insurer, and tax rules change.

## The three roles

| Role | What it means | Common default |
|---|---|---|
| Insured | The person whose death pays the benefit | You |
| Owner | The person who controls the policy: can name beneficiaries, borrow, cancel or transfer it | You |
| Beneficiary | The person or entity who receives the benefit | A spouse, with a backup |

Most people are all three, except for the beneficiary. That works well for a small family. It starts to cause trouble in four situations.

## Situation 1: You have minor children

An insurer will not pay a large amount to a child directly. If a minor is named, a court may appoint a conservator to hold the money, with court oversight and cost, and it ends when the child reaches the age of majority.

Better options:

- Name a trust as beneficiary, with a trustee and instructions for how the money is used. This is often the cleanest choice.
- Name an adult as custodian under a state gifts-to-minors law, if your state allows it on insurance.
- Name a spouse first and the trust second, so the trust only takes over if the spouse dies too.

## Situation 2: Your estate is named, or no one is

If no beneficiary survives, or if "my estate" is written on the form, the benefit generally goes into probate. That can delay payment and expose it to creditors. Name a backup on every policy.

## Situation 3: You own a policy on someone else's life, or two spouses own policies on each other

Ownership can matter for taxes and for who controls the money. Examples:

- A business that owns a policy on an owner for a buy-sell agreement.
- A parent who owns a policy on a child.
- A trust that owns a policy on its grantor.

In each, ask what happens if the owner dies before the insured. The policy then becomes part of the owner's estate unless a successor owner is named.

## Situation 4: Estate tax may apply

Life insurance paid to a named beneficiary is generally free of income tax. But if you own the policy at death, the death benefit is generally counted in your estate for federal estate tax. For 2026, the federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000 per person, and many families are well below it. A policy can push a larger estate over. Some states tax estates at much lower levels, so check where you live.

Ways to keep a policy out of the estate for tax purposes:

- **Give the policy to someone else, or to a trust.** Under the three-year rule in the tax code, if you transfer an existing policy and die within three years, the benefit is generally pulled back into your estate.
- **Have someone else buy the policy from the start.** A trust or an adult child can be the original owner, which can avoid the three-year rule. This is the idea behind an irrevocable life insurance trust.

## ILITs at a high level

An irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) is a trust that owns a policy on your life. You are not the owner, and you cannot change or revoke it. The trustee pays premiums using money you give the trust each year, and when you die, the trust receives the death benefit and distributes it under the trust terms.

What it can do:

- Keep the benefit out of your taxable estate when set up correctly.
- Control who receives the money, and when.
- Protect the proceeds from an heir's creditors or divorce.

What to weigh:

- It is irrevocable, so you give up control.
- Gifts to the trust may need notices to beneficiaries and a gift tax return.
- The trustee must follow a formal process every year.
- It adds legal and administration cost, which may not make sense below estate tax levels.

It is most often considered when the estate is near or over the federal or state exemption, or when you want to give heirs money in a protected form. This guide does not replace advice from an attorney and a tax adviser.

## Choose beneficiaries, step by step

1. **Pick the purpose.** Replace income, pay the mortgage, cover the cost of the estate, equalize shares between heirs or fund a trust.
2. **Pick the owner.** You, a spouse, a trust or a business.
3. **Pick the primary beneficiary.** A named person or trust, with percentages if more than one.
4. **Pick the backup.** Always name a contingent beneficiary.
5. **Match it to the plan.** Your will, trust and other beneficiary forms should work together.
6. **Confirm in writing.** Ask the insurer to send a confirmation.

## Quick checks

- [ ] No policy names a minor child directly.
- [ ] No policy names "my estate" by accident.
- [ ] Every policy has a backup beneficiary.
- [ ] A divorce, marriage or birth has been reflected on every form.
- [ ] Group life insurance from work is on the list, since it is easy to forget.
- [ ] Term policies last as long as the need.
- [ ] If the estate may be taxable, I have asked about ownership before the next premium.
- [ ] I told the people who will need to file the claim where the policy is.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your estate may be near the federal or a state estate tax threshold.
- You want life insurance to go into a trust, including an ILIT.
- You have a child with special needs who receives benefits.
- A business owns the policy, or a policy supports a buy-sell agreement.
- You are divorcing, or a decree requires you to keep a policy.
- You have an old policy and are unsure who owns it.
