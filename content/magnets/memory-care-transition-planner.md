**How to use this planner.** Memory care moves go better when the paperwork is done before the move, not during it. Work through the sections in order, write in the blanks, and share a copy with siblings. Other resources cover nursing home contracts and general dementia planning. This one is about the move itself: timing, people and the first week. Rules and costs vary by state and by community.

## Step 1: Decide whether it is time

Families often wait for a crisis. Look for patterns, not single bad days.

- [ ] Wandering, getting lost, or leaving doors open
- [ ] Missed medications or repeated medication errors
- [ ] Weight loss, spoiled food, or an unsafe kitchen
- [ ] Falls, burns or unexplained bruises
- [ ] Caregiver exhaustion, illness or injury
- [ ] Night waking that leaves the household unable to sleep
- [ ] Aggression or distress that the home cannot manage safely

Ask the doctor for a written assessment of the level of care needed. Some communities require a physician form before admission, and some will not accept residents who need certain kinds of nursing care.

- **Doctor who will complete the form:** ______________________
- **Date of the assessment:** ______________________

## Step 2: A timeline you can adjust

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| 8 to 12 weeks before | Tour at least three communities. Ask about costs, staffing and what ends a stay. |
| 6 to 8 weeks before | Confirm the power of attorney and health care directive are signed and accepted. Request a waitlist spot. |
| 4 to 6 weeks before | Review the admission agreement with an attorney. Arrange the doctor's form and a tuberculosis screening if required. |
| 2 to 4 weeks before | Plan the money: who pays what, and from which account. Label belongings. |
| 1 week before | Pack, set up the room, and brief the staff on routines. |
| Move day and first week | See Step 6. |

Real life will not follow a calendar. If a hospital stay or crisis forces the timing, use the list in Step 3 and do the rest afterward.

## Step 3: Documents to gather before move-in

- [ ] Photo ID, Medicare and any supplemental or Medicare Advantage card, Medicaid card if one exists
- [ ] Signed power of attorney for property, with copies for the community and the bank
- [ ] Signed health care power of attorney and living will
- [ ] POLST form if the doctor has completed one
- [ ] HIPAA release naming family members
- [ ] Current medication list from the pharmacy, with doses and times
- [ ] Doctor and clinic contact list
- [ ] Insurance and long-term care insurance policy numbers
- [ ] Veteran discharge papers, if relevant
- [ ] Guardianship papers, if a court has appointed someone

If the power of attorney is not signed and your parent may no longer have the capacity to sign, stop and call an attorney before the move. The capacity question depends on state law and on the day.

## Step 4: Talking with your parent

Honest conversation matters, but a long explanation can cause more distress. Some families find short, repeated, calm messages work better than one large talk.

- Start with how your parent feels, not with the decision.
- Use simple, true statements: "The doctor wants you somewhere with people around you all day."
- Avoid promises you cannot keep, such as "This is only for a few weeks."
- Avoid arguing about facts your parent cannot recall.
- Ask the care team how they would handle questions like "When am I going home?"

Write the phrase you plan to use:

- **What we will say about why:** ______________________
- **What we will say if they ask to go home:** ______________________
- **Who will be there on move day:** ______________________

## Step 5: Siblings and money

Agree on roles in writing before the move. Resentment often grows when one sibling does the work and others offer opinions.

- **Agent for property:** ______________________
- **Health care agent:** ______________________
- **Who handles the community's billing:** ______________________
- **Who visits on which days:** ______________________
- **How we share updates (group text, weekly call):** ______________________

On cost, ask each community for a written list of what the base rate covers and what is extra. Memory care is mostly paid privately. Medicare generally does not pay for custodial long-term care. Medicaid may help in some settings, but rules and waiting lists vary by state, and Medicaid looks back at asset transfers. Do not give away money or property to "qualify" without legal advice. See the [guide to Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) and the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date).

## Step 6: First-week checklist

- [ ] Bring familiar items: a favorite blanket, photos, a clock, a chair
- [ ] Label clothing with the resident's name
- [ ] Give staff a one-page life story: work, family, hobbies, music, foods, fears
- [ ] Share known triggers and what calms your parent
- [ ] Meet the nurse, the care manager and the person who handles billing
- [ ] Confirm who staff will call first in an emergency
- [ ] Ask when the care plan meeting happens
- [ ] Visit, but consider short visits at first, and ask staff what timing works
- [ ] Change the mailing address for bills and benefits where needed
- [ ] Arrange for the house: lock, insurance notice, mail, utilities

Expect a rough adjustment. Distress in the first weeks is common. Ask the team how they will track it and when they will call you.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The power of attorney or health care directive is not signed, and your parent's capacity is uncertain.
- The admission agreement asks you to sign personally or to waive rights.
- Your parent owns a home, and you are unsure how a move affects taxes, Medicaid or the title.
- Family members disagree and a guardianship may be on the table.
- You want to plan for care costs without triggering a Medicaid penalty.
