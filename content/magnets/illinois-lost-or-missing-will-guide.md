**How to use this guide.** If you are making a will, read the storage section and fill in the location table. If you are looking for a loved one's will, start with the search checklist. Rules vary by state and by court. The Illinois points below should be confirmed with a local attorney or the circuit court clerk. This is general education, not legal advice.

## The short version

An original, signed will is the document courts usually expect. A copy may still help, but it is harder to use. In Illinois, the person holding a will after a death has a duty to file it with the circuit court clerk. See 755 ILCS 5/6-1. <!-- verify: filing deadline in 755 ILCS 5/6-1 -->

| Situation | What usually happens |
|---|---|
| Original found | It is filed with the clerk in the county where the person lived and the court admits it if it was validly signed |
| Only a copy exists, maker's own records unclear | The court may presume the maker tore it up on purpose |
| Only a copy exists, evidence shows loss was accidental | The court may allow proof of the lost will, but the proof burden is high |
| No will, or will not allowed | Illinois intestacy rules decide who inherits |

## Search checklist for a missing original

Work through these in order. Keep a note of the date and who you asked.

- [ ] The home: desk, file cabinet, fireproof box, bedroom closet, with tax papers
- [ ] A safe deposit box (a bank may need a court order or a form to let a family member look)
- [ ] The attorney who drafted it, or any attorney named in old paperwork
- [ ] The circuit court clerk in the county where the person lived, in case it was deposited for safekeeping
- [ ] The named executor, who may hold a copy
- [ ] Family members and close friends
- [ ] The person's financial advisor, accountant and insurer
- [ ] Email and cloud storage for scans (a scan is a copy, not an original)
- [ ] Mail forwarded or stored at a PO box

## Copies, lost wills and what a court weighs

When the original cannot be found, a court asks what happened to it. If the maker had it last and it cannot be found, courts in many states presume the maker revoked it by destroying it. The family can try to answer that with evidence.

Evidence that can matter:

- A signed copy with the maker's and witnesses' signatures visible
- Testimony of the drafting attorney or witnesses about the signing
- Statements by the maker that the will was in place and not revoked
- Proof the will was lost in a move, fire or flood

Illinois sets its own proof rules for lost or destroyed wills. <!-- verify: Illinois statute and proof requirements for a lost will -->
Ask a probate attorney early. Waiting does not improve the evidence.

## Hidden, destroyed or forged wills

- A will is forged when someone signs the maker's name or writes terms the maker never approved.
- Hiding or destroying a will to change who inherits can carry criminal and civil consequences. <!-- verify: Illinois penalties for concealing or destroying a will -->
- Someone who suspects forgery or concealment can raise it in the probate case and ask the court to inquire.

Signs that deserve a closer look:

1. The signature differs from other documents the maker signed
2. Witnesses cannot be found or do not recall the signing
3. A new will appears right after a death or a change in who lives with the person
4. Pages have different typefaces or staples that look re-done

A will that was properly signed and witnessed is hard to fake convincingly. That is why signing with witnesses and a self-proving affidavit helps. See the [Illinois signing requirements cheat sheet](/free/illinois-signing-requirements-cheat-sheet).

## Storage options compared

| Place | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Home fireproof box | Quick access | Fire rating limits, family may not know the combination |
| Safe deposit box | Secure | Access after a death can be slow |
| Attorney's office | Professional storage, easy to locate | Firm may close or move, so keep a note |
| Circuit court clerk (safekeeping deposit) | Official custody | A deposit during life is a choice, not a requirement. <!-- verify: Illinois lodging of will with clerk, 755 ILCS 5/6-2 --> |
| Digital scan only | Handy backup | Not an original |

Illinois does not require you to register or file a will during your life. A will is filed with the court after death. You can choose to deposit it with the clerk for safekeeping.

## Privacy and copies

Once a will is filed with the clerk for probate, it becomes part of the court file. In general, court files are open to the public. That means "who can see my will" has two answers:

- **During your life:** only you and whoever you show it to
- **After death:** the court record is generally public, and heirs and beneficiaries are entitled to notice

If privacy matters, many people use a living trust for the private parts of the plan, since trusts are usually not filed. Read the [probate vs trust guide](/free/probate-vs-trust-guide).

Who gets a copy is your choice. Common choices are the executor, a backup executor and your attorney. You may not want everyone to hold a copy. A beneficiary who sees the will early can raise questions you have not prepared for.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The original is missing and a death has occurred
- You think a will was hidden, replaced or forged
- Only a copy or a scan exists
- You want to deposit a will with the court or keep it private
- Several wills exist and the dates are unclear
