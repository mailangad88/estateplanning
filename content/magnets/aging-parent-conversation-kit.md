**How to use this kit.** Read it first on your own. Then pick one script and one conversation. This is general education, not legal advice. Rules vary by state, and an attorney licensed in your parent's state should handle any legal documents.

## Start from respect

Your parent is an adult with the right to make their own choices, including ones you would not make. Your role is to ask and to listen. You cannot make them plan, and pushing usually backfires.

Lead with your own worry, not their failing. "I want to be able to help if something happens" lands better than "you are getting older".

## Scripts that open the door

Choose the one that fits your family.

- **The story opener:** "A friend's family had a hard time when his dad got sick and nothing was in writing. It made me think. Do you have things set up?"
- **The help opener:** "If you were in the hospital, who should the doctors call? I want to be sure I know."
- **The practical opener:** "Can we sit down and make a list of your accounts and who to call? It would make things easier for everyone."
- **The shared opener:** "I am updating my own papers. Could we look at yours too?"
- **The ask for a story:** "What worries you most about getting older?"

If they say "I'm fine", try: "I know. I would like to know your wishes so I can respect them." Then drop it for a few weeks.

## What to avoid

- Starting at a holiday or in front of the whole family.
- Asking about the size of the inheritance first.
- Talking about them with siblings behind their back.
- Arriving with forms already filled out.
- Bringing in a lawyer without asking.

## Document checklist for your parent

Share this list as a gentle prompt. Each item is something they may or may not have.

- [ ] Will or revocable living trust
- [ ] Financial power of attorney
- [ ] Healthcare power of attorney
- [ ] Living will (advance directive)
- [ ] HIPAA release
- [ ] Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance
- [ ] List of bank and investment accounts
- [ ] List of debts and monthly bills
- [ ] Insurance policies, including any long-term care policy
- [ ] Deed for the home and where it is kept
- [ ] Digital account list and a safe way to find passwords
- [ ] Funeral or burial wishes
- [ ] Names of their attorney, accountant and doctor

## What you can and cannot do without legal authority

Without a document, you generally have little power over your parent's affairs. Rules vary by state, but in general:

| You can usually | You usually cannot |
|---|---|
| Visit, help with groceries, rides and appointments | Access their bank accounts |
| Help sort mail if they invite you | Speak to their bank or doctor about private details without permission |
| Ask them to sign releases for you | Sign for them or make decisions on their behalf |
| Attend appointments with their consent | Sell or move their property |
| Offer to add yourself as a helper on a bill-pay | Change their documents |

If your parent has lost the ability to sign a power of attorney, a court process may be the only route. A court-ordered guardianship or conservatorship can be time-consuming and costly, which is why planning while a parent can sign matters. See the [power of attorney vs. guardianship comparison](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## Warning signs of financial exploitation

Older adults are often targeted by scammers and sometimes by people close to them. Common warning signs include:

- Unusual bank withdrawals or wire transfers.
- New "best friend" or caregiver who isolates the parent from family.
- Sudden changes to a will, power of attorney or account ownership.
- Unpaid bills for a parent who always paid on time.
- Missing belongings, checks or cards.
- Pressure to sign papers or "keep a secret".
- Calls about prizes, taxes, grandchildren in trouble or gift cards.
- A parent who seems afraid or confused about money.

If you suspect exploitation, consider reporting to your local adult protective services, the bank's fraud team or the police. An attorney can explain what else may be possible.

## Handling sibling tension

- Share information before it becomes a secret.
- Keep an expense log if you spend your own money for the parent.
- Agree on how out-of-pocket costs are reimbursed.
- Do not assume fair means equal. Roles may not be even.
- Bring in a neutral party, such as a mediator or elder law attorney, if you are stuck.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your parent wants to make or change documents.
- Your parent may need long-term care and you want to understand the options.
- There is a blended family, a family business or real estate in more than one state.
- You suspect exploitation or undue influence.
- Your parent can no longer sign documents.
- Siblings disagree about who should be in charge.
