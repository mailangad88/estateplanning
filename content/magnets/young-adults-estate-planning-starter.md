**How to use this guide.** Read it in one sitting, then do the first two sections this week. Estate planning at your age is mostly about incapacity and paperwork, not about large estates. A short list done now beats a perfect plan done never. Laws differ by state, so use this as a map and check the details where you live.

## Why planning at your age is not morbid

Most young adults will never need an estate plan for death. Many will, at some point, need it for something else: a car accident, a serious illness, a sudden hospital stay. Once you are 18, your parents generally have no automatic legal right to make medical choices for you or see your records. Hospitals and banks may turn to no one, or to a court.

That is why the first two documents below are about being alive and unable to speak, rather than about dying.

## Step 1: A healthcare proxy and directive

A **healthcare power of attorney** (also called a healthcare proxy) names the person who can make medical choices for you if you cannot. A **living will or advance directive** records your wishes about life-sustaining care.

- [ ] Choose a person who is reachable, calm under pressure and willing to follow your wishes even if they disagree.
- [ ] Choose a backup.
- [ ] Talk to both about what matters to you.
- [ ] Sign the form your state uses, with whatever witnesses or notary it requires.
- [ ] Add a **HIPAA authorization** so your agent can get your medical information.
- [ ] Give copies to your agent, your doctor and keep one where you can find it.

Married or partnered does not always mean covered. Without a document, a partner who is not legally recognized may have no say. Unmarried couples especially should not assume.

See [healthcare directives and living wills](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).

## Step 2: A financial power of attorney

A **durable power of attorney** lets someone you trust pay bills, handle taxes and deal with your accounts if you cannot. Without one, loved ones may need to ask a court to appoint a conservator or guardian, which takes time and money.

- **Who I would trust with my finances:** ______________________
- **Backup:** ______________________
- **Accounts they might need to reach:** ______________________

Some young adults wait on this one until they have assets or a partner. It still costs little and spares a family from a court filing. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

## Step 3: Beneficiaries on workplace accounts

For many people in their 20s and 30s, the biggest assets are not a house or investments. They are a 401(k), life insurance from work, and a bank account. These pass by **beneficiary form**, not by will.

- [ ] Log in to your 401(k) or similar plan and check the named beneficiary.
- [ ] Check your group life insurance and any accidental death coverage.
- [ ] Check your bank accounts for a payable-on-death designation.
- [ ] Name a **contingent beneficiary** as a backup on each.
- [ ] Update after any breakup, marriage, divorce or new child.

A common problem is an old form that still names a former partner or a parent who has since died. That money can go to the wrong person even if your will says otherwise. Spend ten minutes now. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) and the [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).

If you are married, some retirement plans require your spouse to be the beneficiary unless they sign a waiver. That rule is federal for many workplace plans.

## Step 4: Your digital life

A lot of what you own is online: photos, social media, email, cloud storage, cryptocurrency, payment apps, domain names and subscriptions. If something happens to you, family may not be able to log in.

- [ ] Use a **password manager** and tell one trusted person how to reach it in an emergency.
- [ ] Turn on the **legacy contact** features that some platforms and phone makers offer.
- [ ] Write down the accounts that hold money or irreplaceable files.
- [ ] If you hold cryptocurrency, record how access works, since lost keys often mean lost funds.

See [digital assets estate planning](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan) and the [digital assets inventory](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory).

## Step 5: A simple will, when it fits

A will is not required in your first week of adult life. A simple will starts to make sense when any of the following is true:

- You have a **partner** and want them provided for. Unmarried partners generally do not inherit automatically.
- You own a **pet** and want to name who cares for it.
- You have **savings or property** you want to go to specific people.
- You have **children** (then a guardian choice is a priority).
- You want to name an **executor** rather than leave the choice to a court.

Without a will, state law decides who inherits, usually a spouse, children or parents. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) and [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will).

Online forms can work for very simple situations, but state signing rules are strict. An attorney can say whether a form suits you. See [online will vs estate attorney](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney).

## Renters, student loans and debt

You do not need to be wealthy for these to matter.

- **Renters.** A lease may continue after a death, and the estate or a co-signer may be responsible. Tell your roommate or landlord where to find your documents. Renters insurance may also have a beneficiary or loss-of-use rule worth reading.
- **Student loans.** Rules differ. Federal student loans are generally discharged on death, and private lenders vary. A co-signer may remain responsible with some private loans. Check your loan documents.
- **Credit cards and other debt.** Debts are generally paid from the estate before heirs receive anything, and heirs generally do not owe an individual's debts personally, unless they co-signed or share the account. Rules vary by state.
- **Life insurance.** If someone depends on you or co-signed a loan, term life insurance is usually inexpensive when you are young. The [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) can give you a rough sense.

## What to revisit when life changes

Put a reminder on your calendar once a year, and also review after any of these:

| Life event | What to revisit |
|---|---|
| Marriage or a new partnership | Beneficiaries, healthcare agent, will, name changes. See [getting married](/life-events/getting-married) |
| First home | Title, how you hold it, life insurance. See [buying a home](/life-events/buying-a-home) |
| New baby | Guardian, will, beneficiaries, a trust for the child. See [new baby](/life-events/new-baby) |
| Divorce or separation | Beneficiaries, agents, will. See [divorce](/life-events/divorce) |
| Starting a business | Ownership, successor, personal liability. See [starting a business](/life-events/starting-a-business) |
| Moving states | Review documents for local rules. See [moving to a new state](/life-events/moving-to-a-new-state) |

## A one-hour starter plan

- [ ] Choose a healthcare agent and a backup (10 minutes).
- [ ] Check beneficiaries on your 401(k), life insurance and bank accounts (15 minutes).
- [ ] Make a short list of accounts and set up a password manager (20 minutes).
- [ ] Book time with an attorney if you have a partner, a child, a pet or a house (15 minutes to schedule).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are unmarried and have a long-term partner, especially if you share a home or accounts.
- You have a child, are expecting one or care for a dependent.
- You own real estate, a business, cryptocurrency or other assets that are not easy to transfer.
- You have a chronic illness, a risky job or a hobby with real danger.
- You live or hold assets in more than one state or country.
