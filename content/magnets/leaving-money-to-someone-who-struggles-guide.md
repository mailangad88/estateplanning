**How to use this guide.** Read it through once, then answer the questions in the worksheet near the end. This guide is educational and not individual advice. Trust law varies by state, and the details below, especially around creditors and what a trustee may do, are general patterns. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you how they apply.

## The problem, said kindly

Many families have someone who struggles. It may be a child with a gambling habit, a sibling who has never held onto money, or a grandchild in recovery. The person is not a villain. They are someone you love, and you are afraid that a lump sum would do harm.

The fear is reasonable. A large inheritance paid out all at once may be gone in months, taken by a creditor, a bad relationship or a relapse. But the reverse worry is also real: being treated as untrustworthy can hurt a person who is doing their best, and a plan that humiliates them may break the relationship after you are gone.

Good planning can hold both. It protects the money and respects the person.

## Start with honest questions

Before choosing a tool, write answers to these.

- **What exactly am I worried about?** (Spending, creditors, a spouse, addiction, poor judgment, vulnerability to scams, a disability.) ______________________
- **Is the problem steady, getting worse, or improving?** ______________________
- **Does this person know I am worried?** ______________________
- **What would help them most: income over time, help with housing, help with treatment, or a safety net they can fall back on?** ______________________
- **Do I want them to ever receive money outright, and if so, at what age or under what conditions?** ______________________

If the person has a disability and receives needs-based benefits, a different type of trust is used. See [special needs trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts).

## The main tools

### A plain outright inheritance

The simplest approach is to leave the money directly. It gives the person full control and also full exposure. A creditor, a divorcing spouse or a judgment can reach it. Many families choose this only for small amounts or for people who have shown they can handle money.

### A trust with a spendthrift clause

A spendthrift clause is a trust provision that generally keeps a beneficiary's creditors from reaching the money while it is still in the trust, and keeps the beneficiary from pledging or selling their future share. Most states recognize spendthrift clauses, with exceptions that vary, such as for child support or some government claims. It is not a promise that the money is untouchable.

A spendthrift clause is usually paired with a trustee who controls payments.

### A discretionary trust

In a discretionary trust, the trustee decides whether, when and how much to give the beneficiary. The trust may say the trustee can pay for health, education, housing and support, or can pay more broadly. Features that families commonly consider:

- Payments made directly to a landlord, school or treatment center instead of to the beneficiary
- A monthly allowance with extra money for needs
- Power to pause distributions during a relapse and restart them later
- The option to end the trust at an age, or never

The tradeoff is dependence. A beneficiary may feel infantilized, and a trustee with wide discretion carries a heavy load. Clear guidelines in the trust, or in a separate letter, help the trustee decide.

### An incentive trust

An incentive trust ties payments to conditions. Examples families sometimes use: matching a beneficiary's earnings, paying for tuition when they enroll, releasing money after a period of sobriety verified by testing, or paying a portion at a milestone age.

The tradeoffs are real:

- **Conditions can be hard to measure.** What does "sober" mean, who verifies it, and how often?
- **They can feel controlling,** or create an incentive to hide a relapse.
- **They can be too rigid.** A beneficiary who has a job loss through no fault of their own may be penalized.
- **A trustee who must police compliance** may damage the relationship.

Many attorneys suggest incentives that reward a positive behavior, such as work or education, over rules that punish a failure. Others favor a discretionary trust with a guiding letter, so the trustee can use judgment. Both approaches have supporters.

### Staged distributions

A trust can pay out in pieces: a third at 30, a third at 35, the rest at 40. This gives the person time to mature, and each stage is a chance to prove themselves. It works poorly for someone with a lifelong problem, because it hands over the full amount eventually. A common compromise is staged payments with the trustee's power to hold back if there is a current crisis.

### Lifetime trust, kept in place

Some families keep the trust going for the beneficiary's whole life, with income paid regularly. At the beneficiary's death, what is left passes to their children or other relatives. This offers the most protection and the least control for the beneficiary.

### Other tools to know about

- **Naming the trust, not the person, as beneficiary** on life insurance and retirement accounts. If you name the person directly, those accounts bypass your trust. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).
- **Retirement accounts and trusts** have special tax rules, which an attorney should handle.
- **Giving a house to the trust** to provide stable housing, rather than cash.

## Choosing a trustee

The trustee is the most important decision. They need to be fair, organized and able to say no without anger, and also able to say yes. Read [choosing a trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee) for the full picture. Points specific to this situation:

- **A sibling can be a poor choice.** They may be resented, or have their own conflicts. Some siblings do an excellent job, and some are relieved to be named co-trustee with a professional.
- **A professional or corporate trustee** is neutral and charges fees, which are a cost against the trust. They may be less flexible with small trusts.
- **A co-trustee arrangement** can pair a family member who knows the person with a professional who handles the money.
- **Name successors,** and consider giving someone the power to replace a trustee who is not working.
- **Decide whether the beneficiary can ever be a co-trustee,** typically only after a period of stability.
- **Trustee guidance.** Trustees often need permission to consult doctors or counselors and to see treatment records, subject to privacy law.

## A letter of wishes

A letter of wishes is a non-binding note to the trustee, and sometimes to the beneficiary, explaining your reasons and hopes. It is not a legal document, and a trustee follows the trust itself. It still matters because it tells the trustee how you would have used discretion.

Prompts:

- **What I love about this person:** ______________________
- **Why I set it up this way:** ______________________
- **What I would want the trustee to help with first (housing, health, treatment, education):** ______________________
- **Things I would not want the trustee to fund:** ______________________
- **What I hope the beneficiary will do with the money in a good year:** ______________________
- **What I would want to happen during a crisis:** ______________________
- **A message directly to the beneficiary:** ______________________

Write it in your own voice and avoid blame. A letter that reads like a lecture is likely to be resented.

## Fairness among siblings

This is often where families get hurt. Ideas to consider:

- **Equal is not always fair, and fair is not always equal.** A parent may leave the same total to each child while holding one child's share in trust. Say so plainly, to everyone, ideally in your lifetime.
- **Explain the trust is protection, not punishment.** Siblings who receive money outright may feel judged by implication, and the one in trust may feel singled out.
- **Do not reduce the share just because of the struggle,** unless you have decided to. A smaller share is a different choice from a protected share.
- **Consider what happens at the end.** If the person dies, who receives what is left?
- **Think about the sibling's role.** If one sibling is trustee, discuss the workload, and whether they should be paid.

## Talk about it, if you can

Many people never tell the beneficiary. Surprises cause fresh pain. If the person is in a good place, consider telling them the outline: you have set up a trust, you chose this trustee, and you want to hear their view. Some families hold a meeting with the attorney present. Others choose not to, for good reasons. Either is a choice you can make with the attorney's help.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving the money outright and hoping.** Good intentions are not a protection.
- **Forgetting to name the trust on beneficiary forms.** The bypass defeats the plan.
- **Writing conditions no trustee can measure.**
- **Choosing a trustee who dreads the job.**
- **Leaving the trustee with no guidance.**
- **Never updating.** A person's situation can improve or worsen, so review the plan after major changes. See [updating your plan](/guides/updating-your-estate-plan).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want a trust with a spendthrift clause, or any trust that ends later than age 18 to 25.
- The beneficiary has creditors, a pending divorce or a lawsuit.
- The beneficiary receives disability or other means-tested benefits.
- You are considering conditions tied to treatment, testing or work.
- A retirement account or life insurance policy will fund the trust.
- You want to name a sibling and a professional as co-trustees.
