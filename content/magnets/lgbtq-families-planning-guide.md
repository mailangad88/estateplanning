**How to use this guide.** Skim the headings and read the sections that match your situation. This guide is educational and not individual advice. Family law, parentage rules and the treatment of unmarried partners differ from state to state and have changed quickly in recent years. An attorney licensed in your state, ideally one with experience in LGBTQ+ family law, can tell you how the rules apply to you.

## Why documents matter

Legal systems are built around default assumptions: that your closest family are your spouse, children and blood relatives. When there are no documents, the law fills the gaps with those defaults. For LGBTQ+ people, whose closest people may be a partner, an unmarried co-parent or a circle of friends, the defaults may point to someone you would not choose.

Documents let you decide instead. A power of attorney, a health care directive, a will or trust, and written instructions about your body and funeral make your choices legally visible. They cost little compared with the harm of leaving the decisions to a stranger or a hostile relative.

## Married couples

Same-sex couples have had the constitutional right to marry nationwide since 2015. Marriage brings a bundle of protections, including inheritance rights under state law, the unlimited marital deduction for federal estate and gift tax purposes, the ability to roll over a spouse's retirement account, and recognition for hospital visitation and many medical decisions.

That is a strong foundation, but it does not do everything. Married couples still commonly sign:

- **Wills or trusts,** to say who gets what, and to name an executor and a guardian for children. The default rules may not match your wishes, especially in blended families.
- **Powers of attorney and health care directives,** so decisions are not left to a default list that may include relatives.
- **Beneficiary designations,** which override a will.

If you married after a long relationship, or have lived in a state where your marriage was recognized late, your plan may carry old assumptions. Review it.

## Parentage: protecting the parent-child relationship

This is the area where LGBTQ+ families are most often advised to take extra steps.

Many states now recognize a spouse as a legal parent of a child born during the marriage, and some states recognize both parents of a child born to a same-sex couple, including through a voluntary acknowledgment or a birth certificate. But rules vary widely, and a birth certificate is generally not the same as a court order. Attorneys commonly recommend that a non-biological or non-gestational parent, even one listed on the birth certificate, consider a second-parent adoption or a confirmatory adoption, or a court judgment of parentage.

Why the extra step is suggested:

- A court judgment or adoption decree is generally entitled to recognition in other states under the full faith and credit principle. A birth certificate or a state statute may not travel as well.
- If the parents separate, or one dies, a judgment gives the legal parent a stronger position.
- When traveling, moving or dealing with schools, hospitals and agencies, a decree is easy to prove.
- It protects against challenges by a biological relative or a former partner.

The steps and costs depend on your state and your family's situation. Families who use a sperm donor, a surrogate or a known donor face additional legal questions, such as whether a donor agreement is enforceable and who is a legal parent. An attorney who handles family formation can advise you before conception whenever possible.

A will can nominate a guardian for your children, but a will cannot create a parent-child relationship. See [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children).

## Unmarried partners

If you are not married, the law generally treats you as legal strangers for most purposes. Without documents, an unmarried partner typically has no right to inherit under intestacy rules, no automatic authority for medical decisions (though many states have a hierarchy that may include a partner), and may be shut out of decisions about your body and funeral. Unmarried partners also lack the unlimited marital deduction for estate tax, and an inherited home or account can be taxed or subject to claims in ways a spouse's would not.

Steps often considered:

- **A will or revocable trust** naming your partner as beneficiary.
- **A durable financial power of attorney** and **health care directive** naming your partner.
- **A cohabitation or partnership agreement** about who owns what, how a shared home is divided, and who pays expenses.
- **Joint ownership with survivorship** for a shared home or account, with care, since it has tax and creditor consequences.
- **Beneficiary designations** on life insurance and retirement accounts.
- **A registered domestic partnership or civil union,** where your state offers it, though the rights vary and are not recognized everywhere.
- **A conversation about marriage,** which is a personal choice, and one that carries a different set of rights.

Rules for unmarried couples vary a great deal by state, and an attorney can tell you which of these tools is worth the cost for you.

## Chosen family

Many LGBTQ+ people count friends, former partners and mentors as family. The law lets you name them.

- **Agents:** anyone you trust can be named agent under a power of attorney or health care directive.
- **Executor and trustee:** you can name a friend as executor or trustee. Consider also naming a backup and, if the estate is large, a professional co-trustee. See [choosing a trustee](/learn/trusts/choosing-a-trustee).
- **Beneficiaries:** you can leave property to anyone, by will, trust or beneficiary form.
- **Guardians:** you can nominate anyone to care for minor children, though a court makes the final decision.
- **Contact lists and instructions:** write down who should be told and who should have access to your home and accounts.

A friend who is not related to you may face skepticism from banks and hospitals. Give them copies of the documents, and keep a short letter explaining your relationship and wishes. A HIPAA release helps them speak to medical staff.

## When relatives may be unsupportive

Some people fear that estranged or disapproving relatives will step in after a death or during an illness. Steps that can reduce the risk:

- **Clear, properly signed documents.** A well-executed will, trust and powers of attorney are the best defense. Ask the attorney about extra witnesses or a notary, and about a signing process that records your capacity and independence.
- **Name agents so no relative's consent is needed.** Agents named in a health care directive generally take priority over relatives.
- **Disposition authority.** Many states let you name a person to control your remains in a signed document. Ask your attorney about your state's form, and see [funeral and burial wishes](/checklists/funeral-and-burial-wishes).
- **No-contest clauses.** Many states enforce a clause that reduces or removes a gift to a beneficiary who challenges the will without good cause. Others limit or refuse to enforce them. Whether a clause helps depends on whether the relative has anything to lose. A relative who gets nothing has no reason to hold back.
- **Trusts and non-probate transfers.** A revocable trust or beneficiary designation keeps assets out of the public probate file, where relatives may see and contest things. See [revocable living trust explained](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust).
- **A letter explaining your choices,** which can reduce hurt and sometimes disputes.
- **Safe storage and sharing.** Tell your agents where the documents are.

No plan makes a challenge impossible. The aim is to make your wishes clear and well documented.

## Funeral and disposition authority

If you have not named someone, state law usually gives the right to control your remains to a spouse, then adult children, then parents, then siblings, and so on. Unmarried partners and friends are often low on the list or absent. To avoid that:

- [ ] Name a person in a signed document, using your state's form if it has one.
- [ ] Write down your wishes: burial or cremation, service, music, location.
- [ ] Give copies to the person and to the funeral home if you have chosen one.
- [ ] Consider prepaying, with advice, since prepaid plans vary.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Wills or trusts that name chosen executors, trustees and beneficiaries
- [ ] Financial power of attorney
- [ ] Health care directive and HIPAA release
- [ ] Parentage steps: second-parent adoption, confirmatory adoption or judgment, as advised
- [ ] Guardian nomination for minor children
- [ ] Beneficiary designations
- [ ] Funeral and disposition authority
- [ ] Partnership or cohabitation agreement, if unmarried
- [ ] Documents shared with agents

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming marriage covers everything.**
- **Relying on a birth certificate alone** for parentage.
- **Leaving beneficiary forms with an old partner's or relative's name.**
- **Naming a single agent with no backup.**
- **Keeping documents where nobody can find them.**
- **Moving states without a review.**

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are having a child, or have a child and one parent is not biologically related.
- You are unmarried and own property together.
- You expect a relative may contest your plan.
- You moved from a state with different rules, or have property in more than one state.
- You want to name a friend as agent, executor or trustee.
- You hold a prior marriage's documents that need changing.
