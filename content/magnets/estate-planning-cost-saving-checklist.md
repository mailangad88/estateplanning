**How to use this checklist.** Legal work is priced by scope and complexity, either as a flat fee or by the hour. You cannot make a fair plan cheap by pushing the attorney, but you can make it simpler. Most savings come from preparation and clear decisions, not from bargaining. Fees vary widely by state and by firm, so use this to ask better questions, not to compare to a number.

## 1. Know what actually drives cost

- More documents, more fees. A will plus powers of attorney is smaller than a trust package.
- More complexity, more time: blended families, businesses, special needs, trusts for tax planning, out-of-state property.
- More meetings and revisions add hourly work under hourly billing.
- Unorganized information turns into time spent chasing it.

Use the [will or trust tool](/tools/will-or-trust) for a view of which structure fits your situation before the first call. A will can be right for many families, and a trust is not automatically better.

## 2. Prepare the paper

- [ ] Complete a list of accounts, ownership and beneficiaries (the [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory))
- [ ] Gather existing wills, trusts, powers of attorney and directives
- [ ] Copy deeds, mortgage statements and vehicle titles
- [ ] List life insurance policies and who is named
- [ ] Write down business details and who owns what
- [ ] List debts and co-signers
- [ ] Collect prior divorce decrees or prenuptial agreements

Ask the firm which items matter most for you. The [documents to gather before your consult](/checklists/documents-to-gather-before-your-consult) is a reasonable base.

## 3. Decide the people questions first

Attorney time is wasted when clients are still arguing with each other.

- [ ] Ask each person if they are willing before you name them
- [ ] Agree on shares with your spouse or partner in advance
- [ ] Decide at what ages children inherit, or whether they need a trust
- [ ] Write down specific gifts and who gets them
- [ ] Settle charitable gifts and amounts

The [guardian for your kids worksheet](/free/guardian-for-your-kids-worksheet) and [choosing an executor worksheet](/checklists/choosing-an-executor-worksheet) help.

## 4. Bundle where it makes sense

- [ ] Ask for a package: will or trust, powers of attorney, health care directive and HIPAA release together
- [ ] Do a couple's plan in one engagement. Coordinated documents are often more efficient than two separate ones
- [ ] Add a child or parent's powers of attorney in the same visit when possible
- [ ] Ask if a periodic review or update is included or discounted
- [ ] Ask whether a limited scope is possible, such as reviewing a plan you already have

## 5. Ask about fees clearly

- [ ] Is it flat or hourly, and what triggers extra charges?
- [ ] What is included: drafting, funding help, deed preparation, signing meeting, copies?
- [ ] What costs are separate: recording fees, notary, out-of-state deeds?
- [ ] How are changes after signing charged?
- [ ] Is there a written fee agreement?

See [questions to ask an estate planning attorney](/free/questions-to-ask-an-estate-planning-attorney).

## 6. Cost-cutting that works

- Do your own legwork on inventories, contact lists and a letter of instruction.
- Send complete information in one go, not in pieces.
- Use one email thread and a short list of questions per meeting.
- Keep beneficiary forms up to date yourself, and bring them to the review.
- Fund the trust yourself where the attorney gives clear instructions, such as retitling bank accounts.

## 7. Shortcuts that usually backfire

- **Skipping beneficiary forms.** A cheap will does not fix an old form naming an ex.
- **Signing without witnesses or a notary.** A document that fails formalities can cost much more to fix.
- **A trust that is never funded.** A paid trust with an empty title does little.
- **Adding a child to the deed to avoid probate.** It can create gift, tax and creditor problems. See [transfer on death and payable on death](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death).
- **Copying a form from the internet for a complex family.**
- **Waiting for a crisis.** Planning in a hospital is slower and often more expensive, and capacity may be questioned.

## 8. Compare the cost with the cost of not planning

Probate fees, court time, family disputes and tax mistakes cost money too. They vary by state and by estate. Use the [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) to see a rough range for your situation, then weigh planning costs against that. An online document service may be fine for a very simple situation. See [online will vs estate attorney](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are unsure which documents you need and want a recommendation with reasons.
- You have a blended family, a business, a disabled beneficiary or property in more than one state.
- You have already used a template and want a limited-scope review.
- A quoted fee is unclear and you want the scope explained in writing.
- You want to compare a will with a trust for your own situation.
