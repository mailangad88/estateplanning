**How to use this guide.** Read it alone first, then share it with the people you trust. Fill in the worksheets in pencil. Nothing here is a legal document or medical advice. Forms and rules for healthcare decisions vary by state, so bring your answers to an attorney or to your doctor.

## Why the conversation matters

Documents record choices. Conversations make the choices understood. A form that says "no extraordinary measures" means little if your family has never heard what you mean by it.

When there is no conversation and no paperwork, relatives and doctors often guess. Families can disagree, and guilt can last for years. A calm talk now can spare them that.

## Picking the moment

- Choose a quiet time, not a crisis, a holiday dinner or a hospital hallway.
- Say up front that this is about wishes, not about being sick.
- Keep the first talk short. One conversation can be thirty minutes. Come back to it.
- Invite the people who may be asked to decide, one at a time if a group feels heavy.
- Listen more than you speak. Questions are more useful than speeches.

## Conversation starters

Pick one that sounds like you.

- "I read something about planning for medical care, and it got me thinking. Can I ask what you would want?"
- "If you were ever very sick and could not speak for yourself, who would you want making decisions?"
- "What did you think about how [a relative or friend] was cared for at the end?"
- "What makes a good day for you? What would make a day not worth living through?"
- "I want to be sure I can honor your wishes. Can you tell me what you would want me to know?"
- "I have filled out some forms. Can I show you what I wrote and ask what you think?"

If someone changes the subject, let them. Try again another day with a smaller question.

## Values worksheet

There are no right answers. This worksheet is a place to put thoughts in order.

### What matters most

Rank these from 1 (most important) to 8.

- **Being at home:** ______
- **Being free of pain:** ______
- **Being awake and able to recognize loved ones:** ______
- **Living as long as possible:** ______
- **Not being a burden:** ______
- **Being independent:** ______
- **Having family nearby:** ______
- **Spiritual or religious practices:** ______

### Tradeoffs

Circle or write what you lean toward.

- **Longer life with more treatment vs. more comfort with less treatment:** ______________________
- **Hospital with every option vs. home with comfort-focused care:** ______________________
- **Living with serious limits vs. risk of not surviving a procedure:** ______________________
- **Machines to breathe or feed me for a short trial vs. none at all:** ______________________

### Finish these sentences

- **What I fear most about the end of life is:** ______________________
- **What I hope for most is:** ______________________
- **Something I want my family to know:** ______________________
- **Things that would be hard for me to live without:** ______________________

## Choosing a healthcare agent

A healthcare agent (also called a proxy or surrogate) makes medical decisions if you cannot. The best choice is not always the closest relative. Think about who can stay calm and speak up.

Candidate comparison:

| Question | Candidate 1 | Candidate 2 | Candidate 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name |  |  |  |
| Understands my values? |  |  |  |
| Can stay calm with doctors? |  |  |  |
| Willing to follow my wishes, even if they disagree? |  |  |  |
| Lives close enough, or can travel? |  |  |  |
| Can handle family pushback? |  |  |  |

Name a backup too. Ask each person before you name them. Rules about who can serve, such as limits on your doctor or care facility staff, vary by state.

## How the documents differ

| Document | What it does | Who it is for |
|---|---|---|
| Healthcare power of attorney | Names a person to make medical decisions if you cannot | Every adult |
| Living will (advance directive) | Records your wishes about treatment such as life support | Every adult who wants wishes written down |
| POLST or MOLST | A medical order, signed by a clinician, that doctors and emergency staff follow | Seriously ill or frail people near the end of life, not for healthy adults |

The name of the POLST form varies. Some states use MOLST, POST or other names, and not every state has the program. Ask your doctor whether it applies to you. The healthcare power of attorney and living will are often combined into one advance directive in many states.

A HIPAA release is a separate form that lets named people see your medical information. Many people sign one alongside their directive.

## Sharing copies

A form in a drawer cannot help. After signing:

- [ ] Give a copy to your healthcare agent and backup.
- [ ] Give a copy to your primary doctor and ask that it be added to your chart.
- [ ] Give a copy to any hospital you use, if they accept them.
- [ ] Keep one in a spot family knows, not a safe deposit box nobody can open.
- [ ] Tell close family where it is.
- [ ] Review it every few years, and after any big health or family change.

## Common snags

- Naming two agents who must agree, which can freeze decisions.
- Naming someone who lives abroad or cannot be reached.
- Writing instructions so specific they do not fit a real situation.
- Never telling the agent they were chosen.
- Moving to a new state and not checking the form still works. Many states honor out-of-state forms, but not all.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want your directive to reflect a religious or cultural practice.
- Family members disagree and you want your choices clear.
- You have a serious diagnosis and want to coordinate directives with your other documents.
- You have moved states or your documents are old.
- You have questions about who can legally serve as your agent.
