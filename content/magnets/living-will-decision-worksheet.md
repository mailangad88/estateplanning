**How to use this worksheet.** A living will is a written statement of your wishes about medical treatment if you cannot speak for yourself. Forms often ask for yes or no answers to technical choices. Most people cannot answer those well without thinking first. This worksheet helps you think first. It does not create a legal document. Write your answers in pencil, discuss them with your doctor and the person you trust to speak for you, then ask an attorney to put them in the form your state recognizes. Rules and form wording vary by state.

## Part 1: What matters most

There are no right answers. Circle or write what fits.

- **A good day looks like:** ______________________
- **I could not accept living with:** ______________________
- **I want to be able to:** recognize my family / communicate / feed myself / live at home / be free of serious pain / other: ______________________
- **Faith, culture or personal beliefs that shape my choices:** ______________________
- **My biggest fear about the end of life:** ______________________
- **What I would want my family to know:** ______________________

## Part 2: Treatments in plain words

Ask a doctor to explain any item you do not understand. For each, write whether you would want it, not want it, or want a trial period.

| Treatment | What it means | My view (yes / no / trial / unsure) |
|---|---|---|
| CPR | Chest compressions and shocks if the heart stops | |
| Breathing machine | A ventilator breathes for you through a tube | |
| Feeding tube | Nutrition and fluids delivered by a tube | |
| Dialysis | A machine cleans the blood if kidneys fail | |
| Antibiotics | Medicine for infections in the late stage of illness | |
| Hospital transfer | Going to the hospital versus staying where you live | |
| Comfort care | Treatment aimed at relief of pain and distress | |

A trial period means starting a treatment with an agreed time to see if it is helping, and stopping if it is not. Many people prefer that to a flat yes or no.

## Part 3: Scenarios

Medical conditions rarely match a form. Use these to test your answers. Write what you would want and why.

**Scenario A: A sudden stroke leaves you unable to speak, but you may recover some ability.** Would you want full treatment for a time to see how much you recover?

- **My answer:** ______________________

**Scenario B: Advanced dementia. You no longer recognize your family and cannot feed yourself.** Would you want treatment to extend life, such as antibiotics, hospital transfers or a feeding tube?

- **My answer:** ______________________

**Scenario C: A terminal illness, with a doctor expecting months, not years.** What is more important: more time, or comfort and being at home?

- **My answer:** ______________________

**Scenario D: Permanent unconsciousness.** Would you want treatment to continue indefinitely?

- **My answer:** ______________________

**Scenario E: A serious illness where recovery is uncertain but possible.** How long would you want doctors to try before reconsidering?

- **My answer:** ______________________

## Part 4: Questions for your doctor

Bring this list to an appointment. Doctors can describe what these treatments are like for people with your health conditions.

- [ ] What are the likely paths of my condition over the next year?
- [ ] What does CPR realistically achieve for someone with my health?
- [ ] What would life be like after a ventilator or a feeding tube?
- [ ] What treatments could help comfort and quality of life?
- [ ] What happens if I choose to stop a treatment later?
- [ ] Should I ask about a POLST form? (A POLST is a medical order for people with serious illness. It is different from a living will. In Illinois, signing one is voluntary and cannot be required for admission or treatment.)
- [ ] Who in your office can help me document my choices?
- [ ] Will you put my directive in my chart?

- **Doctor's name:** ______________________
- **Appointment date:** ______________________
- **Notes from the visit:** ______________________

## Part 5: Who speaks for you

A living will cannot cover every situation. A health care power of attorney names a person to apply your wishes to real events. See [living will versus health care power of attorney](/compare/living-will-vs-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

- **First choice:** ______________________
- **Backup:** ______________________
- [ ] I have told them what I wrote here.
- [ ] They know where the documents are.

## Part 6: Making it count

- [ ] Put your wishes into the form your state recognizes, with the signature and witness steps it requires.
- [ ] Give copies to your agent, doctor, hospital and family.
- [ ] Add the date you reviewed it.
- [ ] Review after any major diagnosis, hospital stay, divorce or death in the family, and every few years.
- [ ] If you change your mind, say so in writing and replace old copies.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing answers on a form without understanding the treatment.
- Writing wishes so rigid they do not fit the actual illness.
- Never telling the agent or family.
- Keeping the only copy in a drawer or safe deposit box.
- Forgetting the POLST or a hospital form that may need to match.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want your living will to meet your state's signing rules.
- You are making end-of-life choices while in the middle of a serious diagnosis.
- Your family disagrees about your wishes.
- You live in more than one state or have moved recently.
- A document you already signed conflicts with what you wrote here.
