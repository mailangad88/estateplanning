**How to use this guide.** Read it together, then fill in the inventory and questions near the end. This is an area where a small detail can change the tax result, so the guide stays general and gives you questions to bring. Please work with an attorney experienced in cross-border estate planning. Immigration status, tax residency and the laws of other countries all matter, and they are separate questions.

## Why a non-citizen spouse changes the picture

For most married couples, a US citizen spouse can leave unlimited assets to the other spouse without federal estate tax at that point. This is called the **unlimited marital deduction**. It defers the tax until the second spouse dies.

In general terms, that deduction does **not** apply to assets left outright to a spouse who is not a US citizen. The concern behind the rule is that a non-citizen spouse may leave the country with the assets, and the US could lose the chance to tax them later. Federal tax rules have specific exceptions and tools, and this guide describes them only in outline.

Three words get mixed up, so it helps to separate them.

| Term | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **Citizenship** | Whether the person is a US citizen |
| **Immigration status** | Visa, green card or other status that governs the right to live and work in the US |
| **Tax residency (domicile)** | Where a person is treated as living for estate and gift tax purposes, which may not match immigration status |

A green card holder is generally treated differently from a person on a temporary visa, and both differ from a citizen. Tax rules for US citizens, residents and non-residents also differ. Confirm your situation with an attorney.

## The marital deduction and the exemption

Every person has a federal estate tax exemption, set at $15 million per person for 2026 <!-- verify --> and indexed for inflation afterward. For many families, that exemption is high enough that no federal estate tax is owed. Still, the rules can matter for larger estates, and state estate or inheritance taxes may apply at lower amounts. See [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes) and the [estate tax estimator](/tools/estate-tax-estimator).

A few points to discuss:

- When the **first spouse dies** and leaves property to a non-citizen spouse, the marital deduction may not apply, and amounts above the exemption may be taxed right away.
- **Lifetime gifts** to a non-citizen spouse are also treated differently from gifts to a citizen spouse. The annual limit on tax-free gifts to a non-citizen spouse is set separately from the usual rule, and the figure changes over time, so ask your attorney for the current number.
- If the **non-citizen spouse later becomes a US citizen**, the rules can change. There are conditions about timing, so do not assume.
- If the **non-citizen spouse is the one who dies** and the citizen spouse inherits, the marital deduction generally applies in the usual way to property passing to a US citizen spouse. The non-citizen's own exposure depends on their tax residency and where assets are located.

## What a QDOT is

A **Qualified Domestic Trust**, often called a QDOT, is one common tool for this situation. In general terms, assets left to a non-citizen spouse are placed in a QDOT, and the trust qualifies for the marital deduction if it meets specific rules.

How it works, broadly:

- The trust is set up to hold the property for the surviving non-citizen spouse.
- At least one trustee must be a US citizen or a domestic corporation, and the trust has rules about collecting tax on principal distributions.
- The surviving spouse can generally receive income from the trust without special tax, while certain principal distributions may be taxed as though they were part of the first spouse's estate.
- Tax may also be due when the surviving spouse dies, or if the trust stops qualifying.
- Larger trusts have additional safeguards.

A QDOT can be created in a will, in a living trust, or sometimes by the surviving spouse after death, within a limited time. Ask the attorney what is available. A QDOT adds cost and administration, so it may not suit every couple. Planning before death is generally easier than after.

Other approaches a couple may discuss include using each spouse's exemption, life insurance held in a trust, adjusting how assets are titled, or obtaining citizenship. None is automatic, and each has trade-offs.

## Assets and family in other countries

Many couples with one non-citizen spouse also have property, bank accounts, business interests or relatives abroad. Each country has its own laws about inheritance, taxes and which court handles the estate.

Things to think about:

- **Where the assets are.** Real estate is usually governed by the laws of the country where it is located, even if you live elsewhere.
- **Forced heirship.** Some countries require that part of an estate go to children or other relatives regardless of the will. This may apply to assets there, and sometimes to citizens living abroad.
- **Foreign estate and inheritance taxes.** The US and the other country may both claim some right to tax. Tax treaties and credits may reduce double taxation, but only in some cases.
- **Reporting.** US persons with foreign accounts or assets may have reporting duties for those accounts. Penalties for missing a filing can be significant, so ask a tax professional.
- **Local formalities.** Some countries require a notary, specific witnesses or an official language for a will.
- **Banking access.** Foreign banks may require local paperwork before releasing funds to an heir or an agent.

## Wills in more than one country

Couples sometimes ask whether they need a separate will in each country. The answer depends on the assets and the laws involved, and the answer from one attorney may differ from another.

Common approaches:

- **One worldwide will,** if both countries will honor it. This is simpler but may not work with local rules.
- **Separate wills for each country,** each covering the property there. This can help local courts accept the document, but the wills must be drafted so that a later will does not accidentally revoke an earlier one in the other country.
- **A trust** that holds assets, where local law recognizes it. Not every country does.

Whichever approach you choose, have attorneys in both places coordinate so that the documents do not conflict. Ask also which law governs the interpretation of each will, and whether the will names an executor who can act in both places.

## Guardianship when family lives abroad

If you have minor children, the question of who would care for them is more complex when relatives live in another country.

- **Name a guardian in your will,** and a backup. A court makes the final decision, and the law differs by state and country.
- **Think about where the child would live.** Would the guardian move to the US, or would the child move abroad? What does each mean for school, language and immigration status?
- **Consider the child's own citizenship** and any travel or passport needs.
- **Ask about immigration consequences.** A guardian who is not a US resident might face visa or entry questions. Ask an immigration attorney.
- **Plan the money.** Funds may need to be held in the US for a long time, or moved abroad. A trust with a trustee in the US can help manage this.
- **Provide a temporary caregiver.** Many families name someone local who can step in immediately while a longer-term decision is made.

See [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children).

## Documents in more than one language

If family or fiduciaries read another language, plan for translation.

- [ ] Keep an **English original** that meets US formalities. The signed English version is usually the legal one.
- [ ] Prepare a **certified translation** for relatives, banks and courts abroad. Ask whether a translation must be notarized or apostilled.
- [ ] Ask about an **apostille** or legalization, which many countries require before a US document is accepted.
- [ ] Add a **plain-language summary** in each language that your agent, executor or guardian can read.
- [ ] Keep a **contact list** in both languages for banks, attorneys and agencies.
- [ ] Note which document controls if the versions differ.
- [ ] Make sure the **healthcare directive and power of attorney** are understood by the person who will use them, and that their wording works for medical staff.

## When to talk to an attorney

- One spouse is not a US citizen and the couple owns significant assets, whatever their size.
- You own real estate, accounts or a business in another country.
- You plan to move abroad, return to a home country or become a citizen.
- You have children and relatives in more than one country.
- You have an existing will from another country, or a will drafted without considering a non-citizen spouse.
- A spouse has died or is seriously ill. Some options have short deadlines.
