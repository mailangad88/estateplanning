A plan signed years ago may still be working, or it may point to people and assets that have changed. This five-day course walks you through a simple check, one step per day. It is general education, not legal advice, and rules vary by state.

## Day 1: Find your documents and read them again

Start by locating what you signed. Many people cannot say where the originals are, or what is in them.

Look for:

- A will, and any updates called codicils
- A revocable living trust, with any amendments
- Financial and health care powers of attorney
- A health care directive or living will
- Beneficiary forms on accounts and insurance
- Any letter of instruction

Check where originals are kept. A will sometimes needs the original, not a copy, so note whether yours is at home, with your attorney or in a safe deposit box. If you cannot find one, that is worth knowing.

Then read each document slowly. Write a one-paragraph summary in your own words: who gets what, who is in charge, and who raises minor children. If something is hard to follow, mark it with a question.

Pay attention to dates and the state where you signed. Moving states does not usually erase a valid will, but it can change how your documents work. The [updating your estate plan guide](/guides/updating-your-estate-plan) covers this in more detail.

Keep a short list of your attorney, your accountant and your financial advisor.

If you find a draft, a copy or an unsigned version, set it aside and mark it clearly, so a future reader does not mistake it for the real one. Old versions of a will are usually replaced by a newer one, but keep them until an attorney confirms. If you cannot locate the original, an attorney can explain what options exist in your state.

**Today's task:** Gather every document into one folder. Write the date signed and the state beside each, plus your one-paragraph summary of the will or trust.

## Day 2: Check the people

Your plan is made of people, and people change. Today you check every person named.

List each role and the name currently written down:

- **Executor or personal representative,** and the backups
- **Successor trustee,** if you have a trust
- **Guardian for minor children,** and the backup
- **Agent under your financial power of attorney**
- **Agent for health care decisions**
- **Beneficiaries** of your will or trust

For each person, ask a few questions:

- Are they alive, healthy and still willing to serve?
- Do they live nearby, or have they moved far away?
- Has your relationship changed because of a divorce, a falling out or a new marriage?
- Are they still capable of managing money and handling stress?
- Do they know they were chosen, and where your documents are?

Former spouses are a common surprise. Some states cancel a former spouse's role after divorce, and others do not cancel everything. Rules vary, so verify with an attorney licensed in your state.

Also note people who are missing: a new child, a stepchild or a grandchild may not be in your documents.

The [choosing an executor guide](/guides/choosing-an-executor) can help you rethink a role.

Many people find that their first choice years ago, such as a close friend or sibling, no longer makes sense. That is normal, and changing names is one of the most common updates. Think about backups too. A plan with only one name per role can fail if that person cannot serve, and a second or third name adds a safety net.

**Today's task:** Make a table of each role, who is named and a "still right? yes, no, unsure" column. Mark every unsure.

## Day 3: The beneficiary check

Beneficiary forms often override your will. A form that is out of date can send money to the wrong person, no matter what your documents say.

Find each account with a form:

- 401(k), 403(b), IRA and pension
- Life insurance, including group coverage at work
- Annuities
- Bank and brokerage accounts with payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designations

For each one, confirm directly with the company:

- **Primary beneficiary** and share of each person
- **Contingent beneficiary**
- **Whether a minor or a trust is named**
- **Date of the last update**

Warning signs include an ex-spouse, a deceased person, a missing contingent, or a form that does not match your plan. Percentages that do not add to 100 can also cause delays.

Do not change anything blindly. A beneficiary choice can affect taxes, a child receiving benefits, and a spouse's rights, which vary by state and plan type. The [beneficiary designation audit checklist](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit) walks through the details.

Do not forget group life insurance through an employer, which sometimes defaults to an old form. Also check accounts you rarely look at, such as an old IRA or a small policy from a former job. If a company cannot find your form, ask for a fresh one. Keep a copy of every confirmation with your estate documents.

Beneficiary choices also interact with your other documents. If your will leaves everything equally to your children but a form names only one, that child may receive far more than you intended. Seeing both on one page is the quickest way to notice a mismatch.

**Today's task:** Request a current beneficiary confirmation from each company. Add the results to a table, and highlight every row that does not match what you want today.

## Day 4: The asset and title check

A plan only controls what it covers. Over the years, you may have bought a home, opened accounts or started a business that your documents do not reflect.

List what you own today, and next to each item write how it is titled:

- Home and other real estate: sole name, joint, or in a trust
- Bank and brokerage accounts: individual, joint, payable on death, or trust
- Vehicles and valuables
- Business interests and partnership shares
- Digital assets and online accounts
- Anything you inherited recently

If you have a trust, check whether assets were ever moved into it. Assets left outside may go through probate, which is the court process for settling an estate. The [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) is a handy template.

Joint ownership deserves a second look. A jointly owned account usually passes to the co-owner and ignores your will. That can be fine, or it can create a surprise. See [joint ownership vs. trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust).

Also look at value. If your estate has grown, or you moved to another state, taxes may be worth a conversation. The federal estate tax exemption is $15 million per person for 2026 <!-- verify -->, and some states have lower thresholds.

If you find an asset that is not covered, such as a rental property or a small business, mention it to your attorney, since these can have special rules. Digital assets, such as online accounts, photos or cryptocurrency, are also easy to forget. Even a short list of where they are and how to reach them is useful to your executor.

**Today's task:** Fill in your asset list with how each item is titled. Mark anything that is new since you signed, or not clearly covered.

## Day 5: Life changes, and when to update

Today you pull it together. Look back at your notes and match them against common reasons to update a plan:

- Marriage, divorce or the death of a spouse
- A birth, adoption or a child reaching adulthood
- A move to a new state
- Buying or selling a home or business
- A big change in wealth or an inheritance
- A new diagnosis or a change in someone's health
- A named person who can no longer serve

A common rule of thumb is to review your plan every three to five years, and after any major event. Beneficiary forms and account titles are worth checking more often, such as once a year.

Make a short action list from this week: what is out of date, what is unclear, and what is simply fine. Many people find most of the plan still works, with a few fixes.

Small changes are sometimes done with an amendment. Larger changes often call for a new document. An attorney licensed in your state can tell you which fits. If you want to talk it through, you can book a consult through the [plan finder](/plan-finder), and there is no pressure. Your notes from this week are what make that meeting useful.

One more tip: tell the people named in your plan where your documents are, even if you do not share the details. Many families lose time looking for papers after a death. A short letter of instruction, with contacts and locations, can help. Keep it somewhere secure, and update it whenever you revise the plan itself.

**Today's task:** Write your action list with three columns: fix myself (such as a beneficiary form), ask an attorney, and no change. Set a calendar reminder for your next review.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You moved to a new state since signing
- You married, divorced or remarried
- A named executor, trustee or guardian can no longer serve
- Your estate has grown, or includes a business
- You are not sure what your documents say
