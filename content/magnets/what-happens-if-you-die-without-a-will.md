**How to use this guide.** Read "The short version" first, then find the family situation closest to yours. The rules are set by each state, so treat the examples as illustrations and ask an attorney licensed in your state for your exact answer.

## The short version

Dying without a valid will is called dying **intestate**. Property that would pass under a will goes instead to relatives chosen by your state's intestacy statute. A court appoints someone to manage the process, and a judge picks guardians for minor children if no one else can. None of this is a punishment. The default rules are a one-size-fits-all guess about what a typical person would want. If your family is not typical, the guess can be wrong.

A will is not the only way property passes. Many assets pass **outside** the will, and intestacy governs only what is left.

| Passes by... | Examples | Does intestacy control it? |
|---|---|---|
| Beneficiary designation | 401(k), IRA, life insurance, annuity | No. The named beneficiary gets it. |
| Joint ownership with survivorship | Joint bank accounts, a house held as joint tenants or tenants by the entirety | No. The survivor gets it. |
| Payable-on-death or transfer-on-death | POD bank account, TOD brokerage account, TOD deed (in states that allow them) | No. |
| Trust | Revocable living trust, if the asset was transferred into it | No. The trust document controls. |
| Probate estate | Property in your sole name with no beneficiary | **Yes.** This is what intestacy controls. |

## Who inherits: the typical pattern

State statutes differ in detail, but many follow the Uniform Probate Code (UPC) family-tree pattern. These are illustrations, not your state's answer.

**Married, no children.** Under the UPC pattern, if no parent survives, the spouse inherits everything. If a parent survives, the spouse often shares with the parents. Under one enacted version of the UPC, the spouse receives the first $300,000 plus three-fourths of the balance. Many states differ.

**Married, with children who are all also the surviving spouse's children.** In UPC states, the spouse generally takes a large share or all. Other states split between spouse and children even here, which can leave a surviving parent co-owning assets with minor children and under court oversight.

**Married, with children from a prior relationship.** This is where defaults bite hardest. Many states give the spouse a smaller share or split with the children, which may not match what the person wanted for either side.

**Unmarried partner.** In nearly every state, an unmarried partner is not an heir under intestacy, however long the relationship. Property may pass to children, parents, siblings or more distant relatives.

**Single, no children.** Typically parents come first, then siblings and their children, then grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins in a fixed order. If no heir can be found, property "escheats" to the state. That is rare.

**Children but no spouse.** Generally children share equally. A minor child's share often requires a court-supervised guardian of the estate or a custodial arrangement until the age set by state law, commonly 18 or 21.

**Stepchildren, foster children and unadopted children.** Generally not heirs unless adopted. A few states have narrow exceptions.

**Community property states** (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin): community property commonly passes to the surviving spouse, while separate property follows the state's intestacy order. A few other states allow opt-in community property trusts.

## What a court decides when you do not

1. **Who runs the estate.** Without a will you have not named an executor. The court appoints an "administrator" (some states say "personal representative") from a priority list, usually the surviving spouse, then adult children. If family members disagree, the court decides, which can take weeks or months.
2. **Whether a bond is required.** Many states require an administrator without a will to post a surety bond, an insurance policy against mismanagement. A will often waives it. The premium is an estate expense.
3. **Who guards your minor children.** If one parent survives, that parent generally has custody. If both parents die and no guardian is named, a judge picks based on the child's best interests, sometimes after a contested hearing. See the [guardian worksheet](/checklists/choosing-a-guardian-worksheet).
4. **How property is divided.** By the statute, not your wishes. Specific gifts, like the watch to your nephew or the house to your daughter, cannot be made.
5. **When young heirs receive their share.** With no trust, a young adult may receive a full share at 18 or 21.
6. **Cost and timing.** Probate is generally slower and costlier without a will, because the court must find and notify all heirs and may require more filings. Cost varies by state and estate, so be wary of any single national average.

## Four real-world scenarios

**Scenario 1: an unmarried couple, one owns the house.** Maria and Dev have lived together for 12 years. The house is in Maria's name only. Maria dies without a will, and her mother, an heir in most states, inherits the house. Dev, who helped pay the mortgage, has no automatic right to stay. A simple will or a TOD deed would have changed the result.

**Scenario 2: parents of two minors, no guardian named.** Both parents die in a car accident. Grandparents on each side petition to be guardian, and a court holds a hearing. Some families settle quickly. Others spend months and thousands of dollars. A will naming a guardian would have put the parents' own choice front and center.

**Scenario 3: a second marriage.** Tom's three adult children are from his first marriage. He remarried eight years ago and dies without a will. In some states his wife receives part and the children share the rest. In others she receives the whole. Neither result may be what Tom wanted, and either can spark conflict.

**Scenario 4: a 401(k) and a will conflict.** Priya's will leaves everything to her sister, but her 401(k) still names her ex-husband because she never updated the form. For most employer plans governed by federal law, the form controls regardless of the will (Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan, 555 U.S. 285 (2009)).

## Five fixes you can start this week

- [ ] **Write down who you would want as guardian and executor,** and ask each person if they are willing. It takes one call each.
- [ ] **Check every beneficiary form:** 401(k), IRA, life insurance and bank accounts with POD. See the [Beneficiary Designation Audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).
- [ ] **List what you own and where it is:** accounts, deed location, insurance policies. See the [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory).
- [ ] **Get a will, and ask about a revocable trust if you own a home.** Handwritten wills are valid in only some states and are contested more often. Many states also allow electronic wills with specific rules. Formal defects are less likely in an attorney-drafted will.
- [ ] **Sign a durable power of attorney and a health care directive.** They cover a more common event than death: being unable to act for yourself.

## Common myths

- *"I don't have much, so I don't need a will."* Size affects cost, not whether intestacy applies. Small estates may qualify for a streamlined process, but the same family-tree rules apply.
- *"My spouse gets everything automatically."* Not always. See the scenarios above.
- *"The state takes everything if I die without a will."* Escheat happens only when no relative can be found, and that is rare.
- *"A handwritten note is a will."* It might be, in some states and under some conditions. If it is not signed and witnessed under your state's law, a court may reject it.
- *"Our family gets along, so it will work out."* Maybe. A court still follows the statute whether or not the family agrees, and banks and title companies follow legal documents, not goodwill.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have minor children, or a child who receives public benefits.
- You own a home, a business, or property in more than one state.
- You are unmarried but in a committed partnership.
- You have children from more than one relationship.
- You are already dealing with an estate where the person died without a will. A probate attorney can explain your state's process and deadlines.
