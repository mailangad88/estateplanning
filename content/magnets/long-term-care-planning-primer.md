**How to use this primer.** Read it from top to bottom once. Then fill in the budget worksheet near the end. This is general education, not individual advice. Costs and rules vary widely by state and by facility, so use local numbers.

## Why plan

Long-term care means help with daily living: bathing, dressing, eating, moving around, taking medicine. It can last months or years. It is often the biggest unplanned expense in a family's later years.

Planning early gives more choices. Planning in a crisis usually means fewer.

## Care settings

| Setting | What it is | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|
| Home care | Aides or family help at home | Needs help with some daily tasks |
| Adult day program | Daytime supervision and activities | Gives family caregivers a break |
| Independent living | Housing for active seniors, few services | Mostly self-sufficient |
| Assisted living | Housing with help for daily tasks and meals | Needs regular help, not round-the-clock nursing |
| Memory care | Secured setting with dementia-trained staff | Dementia or serious memory loss |
| Nursing home | Round-the-clock nursing care | High medical or personal care needs |
| Continuing care community | Several levels on one campus | Wants to stay in one place as needs change |

Prices differ by region and by level of care. Ask each place for a written fee schedule, including what triggers extra charges.

## Who pays

### Medicare

Medicare is limited for long-term custodial care. It generally covers medical care, and may cover a short stay in a skilled nursing facility after a qualifying hospital stay, under conditions. It does not generally pay for ongoing help with daily living at home or in assisted living. Read the current rules at Medicare.gov or ask a counselor.

### Medicaid

Medicaid does pay for long-term care for people who qualify, but eligibility has strict asset and income rules, and they vary by state. Spouses living at home often have special protections. Most states look back at asset transfers for a period before an application. As of 2026, the federal look-back period is 60 months <!-- verify --> in most states, and California differs. Gifts or transfers made during the look-back period can cause a penalty period during which Medicaid will not pay. Use the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) for a general estimate.

Medicaid may also be able to recover costs from an estate after death in many states. Ask an elder law attorney about rules in your state.

### Long-term care insurance

A policy may pay a daily or monthly amount once you meet its benefit triggers, often needing help with a certain number of daily activities or having cognitive impairment. Key points to compare:

- Daily or monthly benefit amount
- Benefit period and total pool
- Waiting (elimination) period
- Inflation protection
- Home care coverage
- Premium increase history
- Hybrid life and long-term care policies, which combine benefits

Premiums are lowest when you apply younger and healthier. Insurers may decline applicants with certain health conditions.

### Private pay

Savings, retirement accounts, home equity, pensions and family help. Some families use reverse mortgages or sell a home. Veterans may have benefits through the VA, so ask a veterans service officer.

## Timing

| Stage | Things families often consider |
|---|---|
| Healthy, 50s to 60s | Learn costs, compare insurance, update documents |
| Early signs of need | Sign powers of attorney, talk about preferences, tour places |
| Needs help at home | Hire aides, check eligibility, track costs |
| Likely facility move | See an elder law attorney, collect financial records |
| Already in care | Ask about Medicaid eligibility and spouse protections |

The look-back rule is the main reason timing matters. Transfers made too close to an application can delay eligibility. Some transfers, like to a spouse or in some cases to a disabled child, may be treated differently. These details are state-specific, so ask an attorney before moving assets.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting until a crisis to look at costs.
- Giving assets to children without checking the look-back rule.
- Assuming Medicare covers custodial care.
- Not reading what a long-term care policy requires to start paying.
- Forgetting the healthy spouse's needs.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Someone may need nursing home care soon.
- You are thinking of transferring a home or other assets.
- You want to understand rules for a spouse at home.
- You own a home, business or farm.
- You want to compare trusts and other options for your state.
- You are helping a parent who has dementia.
