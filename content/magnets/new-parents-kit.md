**How to use this kit.** Work through it with your co-parent, a pencil and a cup of coffee. Nothing here is a legal document. It is a set of worksheets that help you decide what to put into one, and what a guardian or sitter would want to know. Rules about guardians and money for minors vary by state, so bring your answers to an attorney licensed where you live.

## Part 1: Guardian shortlist

A guardian is the person a court may appoint to raise your children if both parents cannot. Courts generally give weight to a parent's written choice, but the judge makes the final call. If no one is named, the court decides on its own.

Start by naming up to three candidates, then fill in the grid together.

| Question | Candidate 1 | Candidate 2 | Candidate 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name and relationship | | | |
| Shares our core values | | | |
| Healthy and likely to be able to parent for 15+ years | | | |
| Lives near family, school, community | | | |
| Space and finances to add a child (or more) | | | |
| Already knows and loves our child | | | |
| Has said yes when asked | | | |

Mark each box with a plus, a minus or a question mark. Do not add up the pluses and call it done. A candidate with fewer pluses but a clear yes may beat a "perfect" relative who has never been asked.

### Common mistakes when choosing

- Choosing by default, such as the oldest grandparent, without checking health and energy.
- Naming a couple, then not saying what happens if they divorce or one dies. Name the people, and say so.
- Skipping the backup. Name a second choice and, ideally, a third.
- Never asking. Some people are honored. Some quietly say no.
- Treating siblings as an afterthought. Many parents prefer to keep children together, but this is a choice to state out loud.

## Part 2: Money for your kids

Minors generally cannot manage significant money, and a guardian is not automatically in charge of it. Without a plan, a court may supervise the funds, and a child may receive a lump sum at the age of majority, which is 18 in most states. Many parents separate two jobs: someone to raise the children, and someone to manage the money.

### Tools families commonly consider

| Tool | General idea | Things to ask about |
|---|---|---|
| Custodial account (UTMA/UGMA) | Adult manages for the child, child gets it at a set age under state law | Age of release, who the custodian is, rules vary by state |
| Trust for children | A trustee holds money and follows your rules | Cost, who the trustee is, when and how funds are paid out |
| Direct beneficiary naming | Child named on a form | Often not recommended for minors without a plan, ask why |
| Guardian-managed funds | Guardian holds and spends | Court oversight, bond or reporting rules |

### Worked example

A couple with two children, ages 1 and 3, own a $400,000 term life policy through work and a $500,000 personal policy. They name a trust for the children as beneficiary. The trust says: pay for housing, health and school costs as needed, give each child a third at 25, a third at 30 and the rest at 35. They name the children's aunt as guardian and a family friend who is an accountant as trustee. The aunt can ask the trustee for funds, and the trustee keeps records.

Your split will look different. The point is that the roles are separate and the ages are chosen, not accidental.

## Part 3: Emergency info sheet for babysitters

Print this page, fill it out and put it on the fridge. Update it twice a year.

- **Child's name and birthday:** ______________________
- **Parent 1 cell:** ______________________
- **Parent 2 cell:** ______________________
- **Nearby adult who can come quickly:** ______________________
- **Address of the house (for a 911 call):** ______________________
- **Pediatrician name and phone:** ______________________
- **Allergies and medicines (dose and time):** ______________________
- **Insurance card location:** ______________________
- **Poison control number:** ______________________
- **Bedtime routine, comfort objects, feeding notes:** ______________________
- **Who may pick up the child, and who may not:** ______________________
- **House rules (screens, snacks, doors):** ______________________

Many sitters say the most useful items are the allergies and the "who can pick up" lines. Do not skip them.

## Part 4: Letter to the guardian, outline

A letter of wishes is generally not legally binding, but it gives a guardian a voice to follow. Write it by hand if you like. Keep each section to a paragraph.

1. **Thank you.** Say why you chose them, in one or two stories.
2. **Who our children are.** Personality, fears, what makes them laugh, what calms them.
3. **Daily life.** School, doctors, activities, friends, routines.
4. **Values and traditions.** Faith, holidays, languages, family stories.
5. **People to keep close.** Grandparents, cousins, godparents, family friends.
6. **Money.** Who the trustee is, where the documents are, and how to ask for funds.
7. **Hopes.** Education, travel, work, what you hope for them.
8. **Permission.** Tell them to make their own calls and to ask for help.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to name a guardian, or you have disagreements between co-parents about who it should be.
- You are thinking about a trust for your children and need to know how one works in your state.
- A child has a disability or may need long-term support.
- One parent has children from a prior relationship, or the parents are not married.
- You own a business, rental property or assets in more than one state.
- You are unsure whether your insurance and beneficiary forms work together with your will.
