**How to use this checklist.** A do-it-yourself will is not automatically a mistake. For some people with simple lives it is a sensible, low-cost start. The trouble comes from details a form does not ask about. Answer each question honestly, tick what applies, and read the score at the end. Rules for wills vary by state, so use this as a screen, not a ruling.

## Part 1: Signs a DIY will may be fine

You can tick most of these:

- [ ] I am single, or married with assets that are mostly joint
- [ ] My estate is modest and well below any estate tax threshold
- [ ] My heirs are adult, financially stable children or a spouse
- [ ] My beneficiary forms on retirement and life insurance are up to date
- [ ] I have no business, no rental property, no out-of-state real estate
- [ ] No one is likely to contest the will
- [ ] No one depends on public benefits or needs ongoing care from my estate
- [ ] I am not expecting a divorce, remarriage or a move to another state soon

## Part 2: Red flags in your family

Each tick is a reason to have an attorney look at it.

- [ ] I have minor children and need a guardian or a trust for them
- [ ] I am in a second marriage or have stepchildren
- [ ] I am unmarried and want a partner protected
- [ ] A beneficiary has a disability or receives means-tested benefits
- [ ] A beneficiary struggles with money, addiction or creditors
- [ ] I want to treat children unequally, or leave someone out on purpose
- [ ] A family member has threatened to challenge the will
- [ ] I am a non-citizen, or my spouse is

See [estate planning for blended families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families) and [special needs trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts).

## Part 3: Red flags in your property

- [ ] I own a home, and I am unsure how it is titled
- [ ] I own property in another state
- [ ] I own a business, a farm or a share of one
- [ ] I own rental property
- [ ] My retirement accounts are the largest asset
- [ ] My estate might be near a state or federal estate tax threshold
- [ ] I have valuable collections, intellectual property or foreign assets
- [ ] Most of my assets are in joint names or have beneficiaries, so the will controls little

If most assets pass outside the will, a will alone may not do what you think. See [probate vs non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## Part 4: Signing red flags

Many invalid wills fail on formalities, not content. Rules vary by state: how many witnesses, whether witnesses may be beneficiaries, whether a notary is needed for a self-proving affidavit, and whether electronic signing is accepted.

- [ ] I do not know my state's witness requirements
- [ ] Someone who inherits under the will plans to witness it
- [ ] I plan to sign alone, or without two adult witnesses present at the same time
- [ ] I printed a will and plan to handwrite changes on it
- [ ] I plan to sign electronically without checking that my state accepts it
- [ ] There is no self-proving affidavit, and witnesses may be hard to find later
- [ ] I am unsure where the signed original will be kept

Never change a signed will by writing or crossing out words on it. Use a proper amendment or a new will. See [what makes a will valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid).

## Part 5: Content red flags in the document itself

- [ ] The form did not ask about a backup executor or a backup guardian
- [ ] It does not say what happens if a beneficiary dies before me
- [ ] It gives property to minors outright, with no age or trust
- [ ] It uses vague words such as "my estate" or "my family" without naming people
- [ ] It lists items I no longer own or that pass another way
- [ ] It does not address debts, taxes or who pays them
- [ ] It does not mention digital assets or a pet
- [ ] I cannot explain what each clause does in plain words

## Part 6: Life changes that break a DIY will

- [ ] Marriage, divorce or separation since I wrote it
- [ ] Birth or adoption of a child
- [ ] A move to another state
- [ ] Death of the executor, guardian or main beneficiary
- [ ] A big change in assets, such as selling a home or inheriting money
- [ ] More than five years since I reviewed it

## Scoring

| Result | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Part 1 mostly ticked, Parts 2 to 6 almost empty | A simple online will may be reasonable. Follow the signing rules exactly, and review it every few years. |
| One or two ticks in Parts 2 to 6 | Consider a limited-scope attorney review of the will and your beneficiary forms. |
| Three or more ticks, or any tick in Part 2 about minor children or a disabled beneficiary | Talk to an attorney before relying on a form. |

## The middle path

You do not have to choose between a form and a full engagement. Many attorneys offer a flat-fee review of a will you wrote, or a limited-scope signing supervision. Ask. Bring the document and beneficiary forms. See [online will vs estate attorney](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney) and [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will).

## Questions to ask any online provider

- [ ] Are the forms tailored to my state, and updated when laws change?
- [ ] What formalities does the service say I must follow, and does it explain them?
- [ ] Can I talk to a licensed attorney in my state, and at what cost?
- [ ] What happens to my documents if the company closes?
- [ ] Is a trust, a pour-over will or a funded trust offered, and who helps with funding?

## When to talk to an attorney

- You ticked anything in Part 2, especially minor children or a disability.
- You own a business or real estate in another state.
- You already signed a DIY will and are unsure it is valid.
- Your estate may be taxable.
- You want to disinherit someone or expect a challenge.
- You want to be sure the will matches your beneficiary forms and how property is titled.

## Next step

Take your score and the will you have to a consult. [Book a consult](/plan-finder) and ask for a review that tells you what to keep and what to change.
