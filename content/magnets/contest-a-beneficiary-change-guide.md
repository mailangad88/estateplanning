## How to use this guide

We are sorry you are facing this on top of a loss. A change to a beneficiary form can feel unfair, but a form is hard to undo. Courts generally start from the idea that the owner meant what the form says. This guide shows what has to be shown to challenge one, so you can decide what to do next. It is general information, not legal advice.

## Start with the facts

- **Who** changed the form, and was it the owner or someone acting for them?
- **When**, compared with the date of any diagnosis, hospital stay or death?
- **What changed**: from whom, to whom, and how much money?
- **How**: online, by phone or on paper, and who helped?

## Four grounds that courts consider

| Ground | What has to be shown | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Lack of capacity | The owner could not understand what they were doing when they signed | Medical records near the signing date |
| Undue influence | Someone's pressure replaced the owner's own wishes | A caregiver who isolated the owner and benefited |
| Fraud or forgery | A false statement, or a signature that is not the owner's | Handwriting mismatch, tampered email |
| Lack of authority | Someone acted without the power they claimed | An agent changing a form their power of attorney did not cover |

Having dementia does not by itself cancel a form. The question is the owner's state of mind at the moment of signing. Some people with a diagnosis have clear periods.

In Illinois, courts may presume undue influence when the person who got the benefit was in a fiduciary relationship with the owner, such as an agent under a power of attorney, and the owner was dependent. The presumption can be answered with evidence. <!-- verify: Illinois undue influence presumption standard for non-will transfers --> Other states have their own tests.

## Who can change a form

| Person | Can change a beneficiary? |
|---|---|
| The owner, with capacity | Yes. This is the main rule |
| Agent under a power of attorney | Only if the document clearly allows it, and must act for the owner's interests |
| An agent naming themselves | Usually restricted unless the document says so clearly, and it is a common source of disputes |
| Court-appointed guardian | Generally needs court approval |
| A child named for convenience | A convenience account holder is generally not the owner of the funds, and the account is still the owner's |

A signature by an agent for their own benefit is a red flag in a dispute. Read the power of attorney carefully. A good one uses specific wording for gifts and beneficiary changes.

## Is notice required?

An owner can usually change a beneficiary without telling the old one. There is generally no duty to tell. That is why changes are discovered after death. Notarization or witnesses are usually not required for account beneficiary forms, though a few companies ask for them. A sloppy form can still be valid.

## A will does not override a form

If a will leaves everything to all the children equally but a bank form names one child, that child generally gets the account. Forms control the account. That is why a will cannot repair a beneficiary form. The exception is when the form is set aside by a court.

If a parent names one child as sole beneficiary so that child will share with the others, the others have no legal right to a share unless there is an enforceable agreement, a trust or a court finding. Many families see this go wrong. Writing the wishes into a trust is safer.

## Evidence checklist

- [ ] A copy of the form and the change history, requested from the company
- [ ] Dates of any diagnosis, hospital stays, medication changes
- [ ] Medical and care facility records (a court may need to order these)
- [ ] Emails, texts and call logs around the change date
- [ ] Names of witnesses, nurses, bank staff and the notary if any
- [ ] Earlier versions of the form and the will
- [ ] Bank statements showing unusual withdrawals

## Steps and timing

1. **Ask the company in writing** to hold payment because of a dispute. Companies sometimes pay the named person within weeks.
2. **Collect the records** above.
3. **Speak with the executor.** The estate may have its own claim.
4. **Ask an attorney in the owner's state about deadlines.** Time limits vary and can be short. Do not assume you have years.
5. **Consider mediation** before a court case.

Court cases are costly and slow, and a form is presumed valid. Many families settle through mediation, especially when all of them share the loss.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting until the money has been paid out and spent
- Accusing someone before collecting records
- Assuming a will will fix it
- Missing a deadline because the estate seemed quiet

## When to talk to an attorney

- The change happened close to the death, or after a major diagnosis
- The person who benefited was an agent, caregiver or new friend
- The money at stake is large relative to the estate
- The company says it will pay out soon
- You are not sure how much time you have to act
