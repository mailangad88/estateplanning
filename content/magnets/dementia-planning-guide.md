**How to use this guide.** Read it in pieces. If you are the person with the diagnosis, the first two sections matter most. If you are a family member, start with "Acting early" and the checklist. This guide is educational and cannot replace advice from a doctor or an attorney licensed in your state. Rules about capacity, guardianship and documents vary by state.

## A diagnosis is not the end of decision making

Dementia is a group of conditions that affect memory, judgment and reasoning, usually gradually. A diagnosis does not mean a person immediately loses the legal ability to make decisions. For a time, often a long time, many people can still understand what they are signing and say what they want.

That window is the reason to plan early. The documents in this guide let a person choose, while they still can, who will help and how. Without them, a family may need to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator, which is public, slow and expensive, and gives the court the choice.

## What "capacity" means

Capacity is the legal word for a person's ability to understand and make a particular decision. Three ideas help.

- **Capacity is specific to the task.** Someone may no longer be able to manage a complex investment portfolio but still be able to name a trusted agent, say who they want to make medical choices, or sign a simple will. Different documents set different standards.
- **Capacity can vary by the day or the hour.** Many people with dementia have better and worse times. Some families schedule signings for the person's best time of day.
- **Capacity is a legal judgment, not just a medical one.** Doctors describe what they observe. The question of whether a person can sign a given document is judged under state law, usually by the attorney at signing and, if challenged later, by a court.

In general terms, signing a will usually requires understanding that you are making a will, roughly what you own, who your close family are, and what the document does. A power of attorney often asks for understanding of what powers you are handing over and to whom. A contract or a trust amendment may be held to a higher standard. These are common patterns, not a rule for every state.

### How an attorney assesses it

An attorney will usually meet the person alone, ask open questions (not yes or no questions), and listen for understanding. They may ask the person to explain in their own words why they are choosing a particular agent. Some attorneys request a letter from the treating doctor, or schedule a signing soon after a good medical appointment, or arrange for a witness. An attorney can decline to proceed if they have doubts. That is a protection for the person, not an insult.

Family members who are named in the documents generally should not sit in on the private conversation. This protects against claims of pressure later.

## Acting early: the documents

The order below is common, though your attorney may advise differently.

### 1. Financial power of attorney

A [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) names an agent to handle money and property. Points to consider:

- A **durable** power of attorney keeps working if the person loses capacity. Ask the attorney to confirm that the document is durable under your state's rules.
- It can take effect right away or only upon a stated trigger, such as a doctor's letter. A trigger can create delay and argument, so many people prefer one that works immediately but names a trusted agent.
- Name a backup agent. Consider whether two agents must act together or can act alone.
- Banks sometimes balk at documents they do not recognize. Ask the attorney about banks' own forms and about the agent's authority to make gifts, change beneficiaries or create trusts. These are powers many people choose to limit.

### 2. Health care directive and agent

A [health care directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) names a person to make medical decisions when you cannot, and can record your wishes about life support, feeding tubes, hospice and where you want to be cared for. People with dementia often have specific wishes about the later stages. Writing them down, and talking them through with the agent, removes a heavy burden from family later.

Also consider a HIPAA release so the agent and family can speak to doctors.

### 3. Will or trust

A will names an executor and says who gets what. A [revocable living trust](/learn/trusts/revocable-living-trust) can add a layer of management: if the person can no longer manage assets, a successor trustee steps in without a court. Assets must actually be retitled into the trust for that to work, which is covered in [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust).

If a trust is used, a trustee can pay bills and care costs from trust assets. This is one reason some families prefer it, but it is not required, and a power of attorney can cover many of the same needs. An attorney can say which fits.

### 4. Beneficiary designations and titling

Accounts that pass by beneficiary form are not controlled by the will. Check them. Also check how the house and bank accounts are titled, and be wary of adding a child's name to an account as a shortcut, since it can expose the asset to that child's creditors or divorce, and cause unplanned tax or benefit problems.

## When capacity may already be in doubt

If the person is already confused, do not simply have them sign. Documents signed without capacity can be challenged, and the signing itself may hurt the person. Options an attorney may discuss include:

- Waiting for a lucid period and documenting it
- Using a smaller document, such as health care agent only
- Supported decision-making, where available, in which a person keeps the decision but receives help
- Guardianship or conservatorship as a last resort, since it removes rights and requires court oversight

## Financial exploitation: the quiet risk

Judgment and memory changes make a person a target for scams, high-pressure sales, and sometimes for people close to them. Warning signs can include:

- Unexplained withdrawals, new joint accounts or sudden changes to a will, power of attorney or beneficiaries
- Unpaid bills when money is plentiful
- A new "friend" or caregiver who is isolating the person or controlling access
- Gifts or loans the person cannot explain
- Mail piling up from sweepstakes or charities

Simple safeguards:

- [ ] Ask the bank about alerts for large transactions and about a trusted contact on the account
- [ ] Have a second person review statements monthly
- [ ] Set up automatic payment for regular bills
- [ ] Reduce unsolicited mail and calls, with help from the family
- [ ] Limit credit with a security freeze at the credit bureaus
- [ ] Choose an agent who accounts to someone else, and consider requiring an annual accounting
- [ ] Keep a written record of who has keys, passwords and access

If you suspect abuse, you can contact your area's adult protective services office. Banks, attorneys and doctors may also have reporting duties that vary by state.

## Driving, firearms and home safety

These are hard conversations. A general approach:

- **Driving.** Ask the doctor to discuss it, and consider a formal driving evaluation. Many people accept advice more readily from a doctor than from a child. Plan alternatives first: rides, delivery, a standing arrangement with a neighbor. Some states have licensing review processes that vary.
- **Firearms.** If there are guns in the home, ask the doctor about safe storage or removal, and plan for it together.
- **Stove, wandering, medications.** Practical tools include automatic shutoffs, door chimes, ID bracelets and pill organizers. Ask the doctor or a local aging agency what is available.
- **Living alone.** Revisit regularly. A plan can include check-in calls and a neighbor with a key.

## Care planning and cost

- Talk to the doctor about the likely stages, and ask about a social worker or care manager.
- Learn the care options: in-home help, adult day programs, assisted living, memory care, nursing home.
- Know what pays for care. Medicare does not generally cover long-term custodial care. Medicaid does for people who qualify, with rules on income and assets. Look-back rules apply to gifts, and the federal look-back period is 60 months<!-- verify --> (California differs). Read [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning) before anyone gives away assets.
- Check for long-term care insurance and veterans' benefits.
- Write down the person's routines, favorite foods, music, and what calms them. Future caregivers will be grateful.

## Caring for the caregiver

Caregivers get sick, tired and isolated. This is not weakness, and it is predictable.

- Ask for specific help. "Can you sit with Dad Tuesday from 2 to 5?" works better than "let me know if you need anything."
- Look for respite care, adult day programs and caregiver support groups, which many local aging agencies and national dementia organizations offer.
- Keep your own medical appointments.
- Learn about caregiver counseling and about leave rights at work, which vary.
- Decide in advance what you can and cannot do, and revisit it. Moving a loved one to professional care is not abandonment.
- Notice your own grief. It often begins long before a death.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting for a crisis.** The documents are simplest while the person can take part.
- **Signing at the hospital without an attorney.** It can be done, but hurried signings invite challenge.
- **Adding a child to the title of the house or accounts.** It can create tax, creditor and fairness problems.
- **Giving away assets to qualify for Medicaid** without advice. It may trigger a penalty period.
- **Leaving siblings out of the conversation.** Surprises later can cause lasting conflict.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You or a loved one has a diagnosis and no power of attorney or health care directive.
- You are not sure whether the person can still sign documents.
- A family member is pressuring the person or has taken control of money.
- You are thinking of guardianship or conservatorship.
- Long-term care costs are approaching and assets need protecting within the rules.
- A family member is a caregiver and wants to be paid, which is best set out in a written agreement.
