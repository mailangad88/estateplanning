## How to use this guide

If you own one to four rentals, you are small enough that no one has built a system for you. This guide covers the decisions that matter for a small owner: how to hold title, how leases and deposits survive your death, and who can act if you are ill. It is meant to add to our [rental property owner's guide](/free/rental-property-owners-guide), which covers out-of-state property and larger portfolios. Property, landlord-tenant and trust laws vary by state, so confirm local rules with an attorney.

## The four basic ways to hold a rental

| Method | How it passes at death | Strengths | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Your own name | Through your will and probate | Simple, no extra cost | Probate delays management. Probate in each state where you own |
| LLC owned by you | The LLC interest passes by will, trust or transfer designation | Can separate rental risk from your other assets. Operating agreement can say who manages | Lender consent, insurance, fees and annual filings. An LLC can lose its protection if run casually |
| Transfer on death deed (where allowed) | Directly to named person on death | Avoids probate for that property | Not available everywhere. No management during incapacity. No control over how the heir handles it |
| Revocable trust | Trustee manages and distributes without probate | Management during incapacity, privacy, one document for all properties | Deeds must be signed and recorded. Lender and insurance notices |

Our [comparison of transfer on death deeds and trusts](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust) gives more detail. A common approach for small landlords is an LLC for each property or a small group, with LLC interests held by a revocable trust. Another approach is a trust holding the deeds directly. Which one makes sense depends on your state, your lender, your insurance and your tax situation.

An LLC is not automatic protection. Mixing personal and rental money, missing filings or skipping insurance can undo the benefit.

## What happens to tenants and leases at death

Rules vary, but in general:

- A lease is usually a contract that continues. The tenant's right to stay does not typically end because the landlord died.
- Your estate, or the trust, steps into the landlord's role. Someone must collect rent, repair, and handle notices.
- Security deposits are usually owed to tenants. If you have not kept them separate, your estate may have trouble returning them.
- Insurance may require notice, and some policies limit coverage when a property is vacant or the named insured has died.
- Utilities, property taxes and mortgages keep going, even while an estate is not yet opened.

In many states, a court must appoint an executor before anyone has authority over a rental held in your own name, which can take weeks. This gap is the strongest reason small landlords use a trust or LLC with a successor manager.

## Property manager authority

If you use a manager, read the agreement.

- [ ] Does the agreement end at the owner's death, or continue with the estate or successor?
- [ ] Who can the manager take direction from if I cannot give it?
- [ ] What spending limit can the manager approve without asking?
- [ ] Does the manager hold deposits, and where?
- [ ] Is there a written list of vendors and keys?

If you manage yourself, name a back-up person now: a trusted relative, a friend in the business or a local manager who can step in. Put their name in your power of attorney or trust.

- **Back-up manager:** ______________________
- **Authority I want them to have (rent, repairs up to $____, evictions with my agent's approval):** ______________________

## Incapacity matters too

A financial power of attorney lets someone act if you are hurt. If properties are in a trust or LLC, name a successor trustee or manager. If properties are in your own name, make sure your power of attorney is broad enough to cover rental property, and ask your lender and insurer how they treat it. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

## Mortgages and lenders

Loans on rentals often include a due-on-sale clause. Federal law limits lenders from using it in some family transfers of owner-occupied homes, but those protections often do not apply to rentals. Before retitling a mortgaged property into a trust or LLC, read the loan and consider asking the lender in writing. An attorney can help you decide whether to ask first or proceed.

## Tax and basis basics

When someone inherits property, its tax basis is often reset to the value at the date of death, which can reduce capital gains if the heir sells. Gifting a property during life usually carries over your old basis. Depreciation you have claimed on the rental can also have tax effects. A tax professional should review any plan before you transfer property to heirs while you are alive.

## Splitting rentals between heirs

If you leave rentals to several children, decide how they will make decisions.

- Who manages day to day?
- How are repairs funded and reserves kept?
- What if one wants to sell and one wants to keep?
- Is there a buy-out formula?

Shared ownership with no rules often ends in conflict. A trust or LLC operating agreement can set voting rules, a manager and a buy-out method.

## Successor to-do list (leave this with your plan)

- [ ] Notify insurer and lender of changes
- [ ] Confirm the manager continues, or hire one
- [ ] Open or confirm an account for rent and deposits
- [ ] Notify tenants in writing of who to pay
- [ ] Review each lease for terms and renewals
- [ ] Pay taxes, mortgage and insurance on time
- [ ] Keep records for the tax return

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own rentals in more than one state
- You want to move rentals into an LLC or trust and have mortgages on them
- You are unsure whether a transfer on death deed is allowed or wise
- You plan to leave rentals to several heirs
- A tenant has an unusual lease, a purchase option or a long-term arrangement
- You are close to retirement and want a transition plan
