## How to use this guide

We are sorry for your loss. If you live in the home, the first fear is usually "Do I have to leave?" The answer depends on who owned the house and how, not on who lives there. Find your row in the table, then read the steps. This is general information about Illinois law. A short call with an Illinois attorney can confirm your situation.

## Who can stay: the quick table

| Your situation | Usually means | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You are the surviving spouse and a co-owner (joint tenancy, tenancy by the entirety) | The house is yours | Ownership passes to the survivor outside probate |
| You are the surviving spouse, home in the deceased's name alone | You can usually stay while the estate is open | A spouse has rights as an heir and may claim a spouse's award and homestead protections <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/15-1 spouse's award and 735 ILCS 5/12-901 homestead --> |
| You are an adult child living in a parent's home with no ownership | You may stay for a time until the executor acts | The house belongs to the estate and goes to heirs |
| You are one of several heirs | You share ownership with the others | See buyout and sale options below |
| You are a sibling with no ownership | The executor decides, usually a short time | You are not an heir unless the will says so |
| You are a tenant renting from the deceased | The lease usually continues until ended by the estate or its terms | Landlord's estate steps into the role |
| You are the deceased's renter and the deceased was the tenant | The lease belongs to the estate | See lease section |

## Step flow after a death

1. **Do not move out in a panic.** Moving does not help you and can hurt your claim.
2. **Find out who owns the house.** Check the deed at the county recorder's office.
3. **Find the will or trust.** See [Illinois probate checklist](/free/illinois-probate-checklist).
4. **Talk to the executor or trustee.** Ask in writing how long you can stay.
5. **Keep the house insured and maintained.** An empty house can lose its insurance.
6. **Keep paying the bills if you are able.** Keep receipts.

## Who owns it

| Ownership | What happens at death |
|---|---|
| Sole name, no will | Passes by Illinois intestacy to heirs |
| Sole name, with a will | Goes through probate to the person named |
| Joint tenancy with right of survivorship | The survivor owns it automatically |
| Land trust or living trust | The trustee follows the trust terms |
| Transfer on death instrument | The named beneficiary receives it, after recording <!-- verify: Illinois TOD instrument rules --> |

If the deed is still in the name of someone who died years ago, see [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).

## Who pays the bills during probate

The estate owns the house until it passes to the heirs. In practice:

- **Mortgage:** payments continue. Lenders generally cannot call the loan simply because the owner died when a close family member inherits. <!-- verify: Garn-St Germain Act protection for relatives --> See [mortgage after death guide](/free/mortgage-after-death-guide).
- **Property tax:** due on schedule. The estate pays, or the person living there advances it and asks to be reimbursed.
- **Utilities and insurance:** keep them on. A vacant home often needs special insurance.
- **Repairs:** the executor decides, using estate money if available.

Advances you pay may be reimbursed if you keep receipts, but do not count on it without asking in writing.

## Sale, buyout or rent

| Option | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| One heir buys out the rest | Agree on a value with an appraisal, then pay the others | Financing, and a written agreement |
| Sell and split | The executor lists the house and divides the proceeds | The court may need to approve a sale in some cases |
| Keep it jointly | All heirs share costs | Disagreements and who pays what |
| Rent it | Heirs agree to rent and divide the income | Landlord duties, taxes and insurance |
| Partition | A court orders a sale or division if heirs cannot agree | Slow and costly <!-- verify: Illinois partition statute 735 ILCS 5/17 --> |

One heir cannot normally sell the whole house alone. Everyone who owns a share has to sign. If the estate is still open, the executor controls the sale. An heir who refuses to sell may be forced into court by another heir.

## Rights of the surviving spouse

In Illinois, a surviving spouse has protections that may include a spouse's award from the estate and the right to renounce a will in favor of a share. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/2-8 renunciation share and 5/15-1 award --> That matters when a will leaves the house to someone else. Stepmothers and stepfathers are in the same position as any spouse. A stepparent who is the spouse of the owner has rights. A stepparent who is not married to the owner does not have the same.

See [Illinois spousal rights guide](/free/illinois-spousal-rights-guide).

## Rental and lease situations

- If your parent rented an apartment, the lease does not automatically end.
- The estate is responsible until the lease is ended, terminated or taken over.
- Landlords commonly negotiate an early end. Ask in writing.
- Remove belongings in an orderly way, and do not throw out items without the executor's say.

## Warning signs that you may be asked to leave

- [ ] A letter from an attorney or the executor
- [ ] The house is listed for sale
- [ ] Locks are changed or utilities cut
- [ ] A court filing with your name

If any appear, speak with an attorney quickly. In Illinois, removing an occupant normally requires a court process, not self-help. <!-- verify: Illinois eviction law for occupants -->

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are not on the deed and have lived in the home for years
- A will leaves the house to someone other than you
- Heirs disagree about selling
- You are a spouse and the house is in someone else's name
- You received a notice or court papers
