## How to use this guide

A revocable living trust is a document that holds your property while you are alive and passes it to your chosen people at your death, without probate. It is not right for everyone. This guide covers the Illinois points that matter most, then gives you a worksheet to judge whether a trust is worth pursuing. It is educational, not advice for your situation.

## What probate looks like in Illinois

Probate is a court process run in the circuit court of the county where the person lived. The will is filed with the clerk, heirs are notified, and creditors have a period to make claims. Illinois requires publication of notice to creditors in a newspaper once a week for three successive weeks, and sets a claim deadline at least six months from first publication. Under an independent administration, the executor acts without asking the court to approve each step. Even so, public filings and a six-month or longer wait are common.

Illinois also has a small estate affidavit that skips full probate when personal property is modest. On the current Cook County form for people who died on or after August 15, 2025, the limit is $150,000 of personal property, not counting vehicles. Real estate in one name does not fit that route. See the [Illinois small estate affidavit guide](/free/illinois-small-estate-affidavit-guide).

## What a trust changes

A trust avoids probate only for property that is actually in it. A trust that is signed but never funded does little. For most families, the question is real estate and a few large accounts.

| Question | Will only | Funded revocable trust |
|---|---|---|
| Court case after death | Usually yes, unless the estate is small | Usually no for trust assets |
| Privacy | Will is a public court record | Trust terms stay private |
| Incapacity | Needs a power of attorney | Successor trustee can step in |
| Control over timing | Limited | Detailed instructions possible |
| Upfront work | Lower | Higher: drafting and retitling |

## Putting an Illinois home in a trust

A home goes into a trust by deed. The owner signs a deed naming the trustee as the new owner, and the deed is recorded with the county. The words and form matter, so have an attorney draft it. Check three items before you sign.

- **Mortgage:** Federal law generally bars a lender from calling a loan due when residential property of fewer than five units moves into a trust where the borrower remains a beneficiary and keeps occupancy rights. Tell your lender anyway.
- **Title insurance:** Ask your title company whether the policy continues after the transfer.
- **Property tax exemptions:** Ask the county assessor whether homeowner exemptions continue when the home sits in a trust.

Do the same for each parcel. Property in another state may need its own deed under that state's rules.

## Other ways Illinois real estate can pass

- **Transfer on death instrument.** Illinois allows a recorded transfer on death instrument for real property. It must be recorded before the owner dies, and it can be revoked only by a recorded document. The people named still take the property subject to mortgages and creditor claims.
- **Land trust.** A land trust holds title to Illinois real estate, with the owner keeping the beneficial interest as personal property. See the [Illinois land trust guide](/free/illinois-land-trust-guide).
- **Joint tenancy.** Survivors take by right of survivorship, but the joint owner also gains rights now. Compare it with a trust in [joint ownership vs. trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust).

## What a trust does not do

- It does not shrink federal estate tax by itself. The federal exemption is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and Illinois has its own lower exclusion, covered in the [Illinois estate tax guide](/free/illinois-estate-tax-guide).
- It does not protect assets from a nursing home spend-down. Medicaid treats a revocable trust as the owner's.
- It does not change a surviving spouse's marital rights. Illinois courts have treated funded revocable trusts as valid against those rights unless the transfer was a sham. Ask an attorney about your facts.
- It does not replace a will. Most trust plans also include a pour-over will as a backstop.

## Costs

Trusts usually cost more to set up than a will, because of drafting and retitling. Fees vary by attorney and county, and by how many assets and trusts are involved. Compare a quote for the trust, the pour-over will, powers of attorney, deeds and recording fees. Ask what is included and who handles the deeds. The [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) can help you compare against the cost of probate.

More boxes in the first six lines suggest a trust deserves a conversation. A mostly beneficiary-designated estate with no real estate in one name may be fine with a will.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own real estate, especially more than one property.
- You are in a second marriage or have children from another relationship.
- You have a mortgage, a reverse mortgage or a home equity loan.
- Your estate may exceed the federal or Illinois estate tax thresholds.
- A family member receives government benefits.
