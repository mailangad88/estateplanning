## How to use this guide

Most families learn too late that Medicare pays for short recovery stays, not for years of daily help. This guide shows who pays for what, so you can see the gap before you are in it. Coverage and premiums change, and every policy reads differently. It is general education, not legal advice or insurance advice.

## Who pays for what

| Type of care | Medicare | Medigap | Long-term care policy | Medicaid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hospital stay | Yes | Fills cost sharing | No | Yes, if eligible |
| Rehab in a nursing facility after a hospital stay | Yes, for a limited time | Fills cost sharing | May also pay | Yes, if eligible |
| Ongoing nursing home care | No | No | Yes, per policy | Yes, if eligible |
| Assisted living | No | No | Often | Only through some state programs |
| Memory care | No | No | Often, if the policy covers it | Some state programs |
| Home health aide for bathing, meals | No, unless part of skilled care | No | Often | Waiver programs |
| Skilled nursing at home, short term | Yes, with a doctor's order | Fills cost sharing | May also pay | Yes |

## What Medicare covers

Medicare pays for medically needed skilled care, such as rehabilitation after a hospital stay. It does not pay for custodial care, which means help with bathing, dressing, eating and supervision.

The skilled nursing benefit is limited to 100 days per benefit period, with a daily copay after day 20, and generally requires a prior qualifying hospital stay. <!-- verify: current Medicare skilled nursing coverage rules and copay start day --> Many people never use all 100 days, because coverage stops when they stop improving.

Medicare Advantage plans must cover what Medicare covers, but extra benefits differ by plan. They generally do not pay for ongoing custodial care.

Medigap helps with Medicare's cost sharing. It pays nothing for care Medicare itself does not cover.

## Long-term care insurance

A long-term care policy pays when you cannot do a set number of daily activities, or have a severe cognitive problem such as dementia. The terms control everything.

### Policy terms to compare

- [ ] **Daily or monthly benefit amount**
- [ ] **Benefit period** (total length or total dollars)
- [ ] **Waiting or elimination period**, the days you pay yourself before benefits begin
- [ ] **Inflation protection**, which raises the benefit over time
- [ ] **Which settings are covered**: home, assisted living, memory care, nursing home
- [ ] **How you qualify**: how many activities of daily living, or cognitive impairment
- [ ] **Whether family caregivers can be paid**
- [ ] **What is excluded**, such as certain pre-existing conditions or care by a relative
- [ ] **Premium history**: has the insurer raised rates on this policy type before

Many policies cover dementia as a cognitive impairment. Many have waiting periods of several weeks to months. Many offer inflation riders, and the rider adds cost. Read the exclusions, which can include care in a setting the policy does not list.

### Traditional versus hybrid

| | Traditional policy | Hybrid life or annuity policy |
|---|---|---|
| Premium | Can rise over time | Often fixed |
| If you never need care | Premiums are gone | A death benefit or cash value may remain |
| Cost | Usually lower per dollar of care | Usually higher |
| Underwriting | Health review | Health review |

A hybrid trades a lower chance of regret for a higher cost. Neither is better in every case.

### If the insurer raises premiums

Insurers can ask state regulators to approve rate increases. When that happens, a policyholder commonly has choices: pay more, reduce the benefit, or let the policy lapse. Ask about reduced paid-up options before you drop it, so you keep something.

## When Medicaid steps in

Medicaid pays for nursing home care once income and assets fall within the state's limits, and for some home and assisted living services through waiver programs. See the Medicaid sorter on this site for what counts.

## Common mistakes

- [ ] Assuming Medicare will cover a long stay
- [ ] Buying a policy without reading the elimination period and exclusions
- [ ] Dropping a policy after a rate increase without asking about reduced options
- [ ] Waiting until a diagnosis, when most policies are no longer available

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are deciding whether to buy a policy or self-fund
- You are about to apply for Medicaid
- A claim was denied or reduced
- A parent's coverage is unclear
