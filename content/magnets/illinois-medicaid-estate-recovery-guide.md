## How to use this guide

Estate recovery is the state's right to be repaid from a Medicaid recipient's estate after death. It is not a lien on a living person's rights to stay home. This guide covers the Illinois approach, which comes from the Public Aid Code at 305 ILCS 5/5-13 and federal law at 42 U.S.C. 1396p. <!-- verify: confirm 305 ILCS 5/5-13 is the current estate recovery section --> Other states differ, sometimes a lot. It is general education, not legal advice.

## The basic rule

Federal law requires every state to try to recover long-term care costs paid for people age 55 and older, and for people of any age who were permanently institutionalized. The state can only recover from what is in the **estate**. How broad "estate" is differs by state, and states may choose to include non-probate assets.

Illinois defines the reachable estate broadly, so do not assume that avoiding probate avoids a claim. <!-- verify: how Illinois HFS defines estate for recovery, including non-probate transfers -->

## Who is protected

| Situation | Recovery |
|---|---|
| Surviving spouse alive | Waits until the spouse dies |
| Child under 21 | Not pursued |
| Child who is blind or permanently disabled | Not pursued |
| Hardship to heirs | A waiver may be requested |
| Home of a caregiver child | Special federal protections can apply if rules are met |
| Small claims that cost more to collect than they yield | May be waived by the agency |

The surviving spouse rule is a delay, not a cancellation. The claim can come back after the second death against what is left.

## What the state can reach

| Asset | Usually reachable? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| House in the recipient's name | Yes | Often the largest item |
| Bank account in the recipient's name | Yes | Probate or not |
| Joint account | Possibly | Depends on the state's definition of estate and whose money it was |
| Life estate deed | Possibly | Illinois law lets the state reach more than the probate estate in some cases |
| House in a revocable trust | Yes | A revocable trust is generally treated as still yours |
| Irrevocable trust set up long ago | Generally no | Transfers inside the look-back are a separate problem |
| Retirement account with a beneficiary | Possibly | Depends on whether the state counts non-probate transfers |
| Life insurance with a named beneficiary | Possibly | Same question |
| Pension or Social Security paid after death | No for the monthly benefit | Money already in the bank in the person's name can be reachable |

## If this, then that

| If this is true | Then check this |
|---|---|
| Spouse still lives in the home | Recovery is deferred. Ask about the spouse's own plan and any later claim. |
| Home was deeded to a child two years ago | A transfer penalty may apply. This is a look-back issue, not recovery. |
| You have a life estate in the home | Ask whether Illinois treats the life estate interest as part of your estate. |
| Your child lives with you and cared for you | A caregiver child exemption may apply to a transfer in life. |
| Only a bank account is left | A claim can still be made, but small amounts may be waived. |
| A claim letter arrives | Do not pay or ignore it. Follow the steps below. |

## Common questions in order

### Does the state take the house?

The state does not seize a living person's home. After death, the home can be part of a claim if it is in the estate and no spouse or protected child is living there. Families often sell or keep the home and pay the claim.

### Are children personally liable?

No. A child is not responsible for a parent's nursing home bill just because they are the child. The state's claim is against the estate. If the child signed the admission agreement as a guarantor, that is a separate promise. See the nursing home admission checklist on this site. <!-- verify: Illinois filial responsibility statute 750 ILCS 65/15 is rarely used against adult children for nursing care -->

### Can I leave something to my children?

You can leave property by will or beneficiary designation, but the state's claim gets paid first from the estate. There is no fixed amount that is always safe. Life planning done early, such as an irrevocable trust, works differently. See the Medicaid guide.

## What to do when a claim letter arrives

1. Read the date and the amount. Note the deadline to respond.
2. Ask for an itemized list of what Medicaid paid.
3. Collect the death certificate, the will, and an asset list.
4. Check whether a spouse, minor or disabled child is protected.
5. Ask whether a hardship waiver applies.
6. Keep the estate open until the claim is settled. Distributing early can put the executor at risk.
7. Talk to an Illinois attorney before sending money.

## Common mistakes

- [ ] Assuming a will or a deed to a child automatically avoids recovery
- [ ] Letting the surviving spouse sign over assets without checking
- [ ] Paying heirs before the claim is resolved
- [ ] Ignoring a claim letter

## When to talk to an attorney

- A claim letter has arrived
- A recipient is still alive and owns a home
- A surviving spouse or disabled child lives in the home
- You want to know if a hardship waiver applies
- A life estate or an old deed is involved
