**How to use this planner.** Work through the five decisions in order and fill in the worksheet at the end. A children's trust holds money for your kids if you die while they are young. Without one, a court may appoint a conservator to manage a minor's money, and many states then pay the child outright at age 18 or 21. Rules vary by state.

## Why a trust and not just a guardian

A guardian cares for the child. A trustee manages the money. They can be the same person or two different people. Splitting the roles lets a loving relative focus on parenting while a careful money manager handles accounts. It also adds a check against waste. Use the [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) to estimate how much your family may need.

## Decision 1: When should children receive money?

There are three common approaches.

| Approach | Example | Good for | Concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| One lump sum | All at 25 | Smaller amounts, mature family | A young adult may receive a large sum at once |
| Staged ages | One third each at 25, 30, 35 | Larger amounts | Requires the trustee to stay involved longer |
| Trustee discretion | Trustee decides until a set age | Uncertain circumstances | Needs a trustee you trust with judgment |

Think about the real child, not an ideal one. Ask yourself:

- Is my child careful or impulsive with money today?
- Is there a health, learning or addiction issue to protect?
- Do I want the trust to end at a milestone such as finishing school?
- Would a lifetime trust, with the child as a beneficiary but not the owner, protect them from creditors or divorce? (See the [spendthrift and incentive guide](/free/spendthrift-and-incentive-trust-guide).)

## Decision 2: Equal shares or one pooled trust?

A pooled trust (sometimes called a family pot) keeps all assets together until the youngest reaches a set age, then splits into equal shares. It lets the trustee pay for the greatest need, such as a child's medical bills, rather than strictly equal amounts. Separate trusts treat each child the same from the start.

## Decision 4: What can trust money pay for?

Most trusts allow a "health, education, maintenance and support" standard. You can add priorities. Tick the ones you want.

- [ ] Housing and daily living costs for the child and their caregiver
- [ ] Extra costs of raising your child in the guardian's home, such as a larger car or an additional room
- [ ] Travel and experiences
- [ ] A first home or a start-up business
- [ ] A matching payment if the child works

Sample wording to give your attorney:

> The trustee may pay for my children's health, education, maintenance and support. The trustee should consider other resources available to the child, and should favor education and health expenses. The trustee may pay the guardian directly for reasonable expenses of caring for my children.

## Decision 5: Where does the money come from?

- **Life insurance:** a term policy is a common way to fund a children's trust. Name the trust, not the child, as beneficiary. A minor cannot receive proceeds directly.
- **Retirement accounts:** naming a trust as beneficiary is possible, but the tax rules are technical. Ask before you do it.
- **Cash and investments:** can be held in a living trust, or passed to the children's trust by a will.
- **Gifts from grandparents:** can be directed into the trust, avoiding a direct gift to a minor.

Annual gifts can also be placed in the trust, within the $19,000 annual exclusion per person in 2026.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a child directly as beneficiary of insurance.
- Setting distribution at 18 by default.
- Naming the guardian as trustee without a check on their spending.
- Forgetting to name a backup trustee.
- Never reviewing the trust as the children grow.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have a child with a disability or a child who receives government benefits.
- You are in a blended family or share custody with an ex-partner.
- You own a business or a large retirement account.
- You are considering a lifetime trust with spendthrift protection.
- The guardian and the trustee will be different people who may disagree.
- You are not sure how a trust interacts with your state's rules for minors.
