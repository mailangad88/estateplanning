**How to use this scorecard.** Answer each question with a 0, 1 or 2. Score **0** if the answer is no or you do not know. Score **1** if it is partly done, informal or out of date. Score **2** if it is done, in writing and current within the last few years. Be honest. A low score is useful information, not a grade. If you have partners, each partner should score separately, then compare. Differences in scores often show where the real conversation needs to happen.

This worksheet is educational and is not advice for your business. Business law varies by state, and each business is different.

3. **Does it cover disability and divorce, not only death?** Score: ____
4. **Was it reviewed or updated in the last three years?** Score: ____
5. **Does your operating agreement (LLC) or shareholder agreement say what happens to an owner's interest at death, including transfer limits and who may vote?** Score: ____

## What your score may mean

| Score | What it may suggest |
|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | Little is in place. A sudden event would likely leave your family and your business improvising. This is common, and it is fixable. |
| 11 to 22 | Some pieces exist, but there are gaps. Look for places where documents disagree, such as a will that conflicts with a buy-sell. |
| 23 to 30 | Most of the structure is there. Focus on updating values, funding and training. |
| 31 to 36 | You are well prepared. Keep it current with a yearly review. |

## What each gap usually means

**No buy-sell (questions 1 to 4).** Without an agreement, an owner's share generally passes through the estate. Heirs who know nothing about the business may become co-owners with you or your partner. Or a family may be pushed to sell fast. A buy-sell lets everyone agree in advance on who buys, at what price.

**No funding (questions 12 and 13).** A buy-sell with no money behind it can promise a purchase that nobody can afford. Many owners use life insurance to fund a death buyout. The ownership structure of the policies matters, since mismatched structures can cause surprising tax and legal results. Ask an attorney and a tax professional.

**No valuation method (question 2 or 11).** Disagreement about price is a common reason buy-sells fail. A clear formula or a regularly updated number lowers the risk of a dispute.

**No named successor (questions 6 to 8).** A hope is not a plan. Successors need time to learn and to build trust with staff and customers.

**Missing documentation (questions 15 and 16).** A successor who cannot find the operating agreement, the lease or the bank logins loses days, and days can matter.

**Operating agreement silent on death (question 5).** In many states, default rules apply when the agreement does not address something. Those defaults may not match your wishes.

**Personal plan out of sync (questions 17 and 18).** Your will, trust and powers of attorney should work with the business agreements, not against them.

### A simple 90-day rhythm

- **Days 1 to 30:** Gather documents, find out what exists, list key people and meet with your partners.
- **Days 31 to 60:** Meet an attorney and a tax or insurance advisor in your state. Get a value estimate. Ask for a draft buy-sell or amendment.
- **Days 61 to 90:** Sign and fund agreements, update your personal documents, brief your successor and set a yearly review date.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have no buy-sell, or you have one that is older than you can remember.
- You are in a partnership or LLC with other owners and no written agreement.
- Your business owns real estate, licenses or regulated assets.
- A family member wants to take over and others disagree.
- You are considering selling in the next few years.
- Your will or trust does not mention the business.
