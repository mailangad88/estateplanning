## How to use this worksheet

In a blended family, the way an asset is titled often decides who gets it, no matter what the will says. A house held jointly goes to the surviving spouse. An IRA goes to whoever is on the form. This worksheet maps each asset and how it passes. Fill it in together. Be honest with each other, because surprises hurt later. Rules vary by state, so use it as preparation for an attorney visit.

Mark an asset "mine only" if it was owned before the marriage or received as a gift or inheritance and kept separate. Mixing it with joint money can change its status in some states.

## Step 3: Check how each one passes today

There are four common routes.

| Route | Examples | Who decides |
|---|---|---|
| Survivorship | Joint tenancy, tenancy by the entirety | The title: the survivor gets it |
| Beneficiary form | Retirement accounts, insurance, payable-on-death accounts | The form |
| Trust terms | Assets titled in a trust | The trust |
| Will | Everything else in your name alone | The will, after probate |

For each asset above, write the route here.

- **Assets passing to the spouse automatically:** ______________________
- **Assets passing to children from forms:** ______________________
- **Assets passing by will or trust:** ______________________

## Step 4: Find the conflicts

Look for these common problems.

- [ ] **Joint home:** The surviving spouse owns it outright and may leave it to their own children, so your children could receive nothing.
- [ ] **Everything to spouse:** A "to my spouse, then to my children" plan in a will does not bind the spouse unless a trust or agreement enforces it.
- [ ] **Old beneficiary forms:** An ex-spouse or a deceased person is still named.
- [ ] **Retirement plan rights:** A spouse often has legal rights to a 401(k), and a waiver is needed to name someone else.
- [ ] **Spousal share laws:** Many states let a surviving spouse claim a set share of the estate even against the will. Rules vary.
- [ ] **Unequal children:** One child receives a house while others get cash of lower value.
- [ ] **Stepchildren omitted:** They usually inherit nothing without a will or trust naming them.
- [ ] **Home occupancy:** The surviving spouse needs housing, but the home is meant for the children.

Mark which apply and circle the top three.

## Step 5: Decide what you each want

Answer separately first, then compare.

- **If I die first, my spouse should get:** ______________________
- **If I die first, my children should get:** ______________________
- **Can my spouse live in the home? For how long?** ______________________
- **Who pays the home costs during that time?** ______________________
- **Who should manage the money if there is a trust?** ______________________
- **When my spouse dies, what happens to what is left?** ______________________
- **Should the children be told now?** ______________________

## Step 6: Match tools to goals

| Goal | Tools to ask about |
|---|---|
| Provide for a spouse and protect children's share | Trust with the spouse as lifetime beneficiary and children as final beneficiaries |
| Let the spouse stay in the home | Life estate, right of occupancy or trust terms |
| Keep separate property separate | Separate titling, a prenup or postnup |
| Provide cash to children now | Life insurance, beneficiary forms |
| Reduce fights | Neutral trustee, written explanations, family meeting |
| Include stepchildren | Specific gifts in a will or trust |

A neutral trustee, such as a professional or a trusted friend, can reduce tension when a spouse and children have different interests. See [choosing a trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee) and [estate planning for blended families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families).

## Step 7: Next actions

- [ ] Get current account statements and deeds
- [ ] Update beneficiary forms to match the plan
- [ ] Write down personal items that have promised recipients
- [ ] Schedule a joint meeting with an attorney
- [ ] Decide whether each spouse should have separate counsel on shared contracts

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your plan treats a spouse and children very differently
- A home or business is involved
- You want to restrict how a trust is used after the first death
- You are asking a spouse to waive rights
- You live in or own property in more than one state
- A child has special needs or a past estrangement
