**How to use this worksheet.** Fill in Part 1 alone before the meeting. Use Part 2 as the agenda. Complete Part 3 together, and keep Part 4 in the same folder as your documents. This is a conversation guide, not legal advice. Your attorney can explain how your documents work in your state.

## Why a meeting helps

Many family disputes after a death come from surprise. A child learns for the first time that a sibling is executor, or that a house is going to be sold. Talking in advance does not mean everyone must agree. It means the people named know what to expect, and you hear concerns while you can still adjust.

## Part 1: Before the meeting

### Decide your purpose

Pick one. Mixed goals make for messy meetings.

- [ ] Tell them who is named in key roles (executor, trustee, agents)
- [ ] Tell them where documents and information are kept
- [ ] Explain my wishes for healthcare and end-of-life care
- [ ] Explain how my assets are likely to be divided and why
- [ ] Ask who would be willing to serve in a role

### Decide what to share

| Topic | Share fully | Share roles only | Keep private for now |

There is no right answer. Some families share numbers. Others share only who does what and where papers are. Consider what the group can hear calmly.

## Part 2: The agenda (about 60 minutes)

| Time | Topic | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 min | Why we are meeting. State your purpose in two sentences. | |
| 5 to 15 min | Roles. Say who is named, and why you chose them. | |
| 15 to 25 min | Where things are. Documents, accounts, passwords, advisors. | |
| 25 to 40 min | Wishes. Health care, home, keepsakes, anything personal. | |
| 40 to 55 min | Questions. One person speaks at a time. | |
| 55 to 60 min | Next steps. Who does what, by when. | |

### Opening lines you can adapt

- "I am not changing anything today. I want you to know where things are."
- "I chose you because you are organized. If you would rather not, I need to know now."
- "I will not debate how I decided. I do want to hear what worries you."

### Handling hard moments

- **A sibling feels slighted.** Acknowledge it. Explain that a role is a job, not a ranking of love.
- **Someone asks about money.** It is fine to say "I will share that later" or to refer to the attorney.
- **Raised voices.** Take a ten-minute break. Resume or reschedule.
- **A relative wants to negotiate.** Say you will consider it, and do not decide in the room.

### Worked example

A widowed parent holds a meeting with her three adult children. She names her oldest as executor, her youngest as healthcare agent and the middle child as financial agent. She says the roles are about who lives nearby and who is comfortable with spreadsheets. She tells them the will is in a fire-safe at home and her attorney has a copy. She does not share dollar amounts. All three leave with a one-page sheet. A month later the middle child asks to swap roles with her brother. She considers it and talks to her attorney before deciding.

## Part 4: After the meeting

- [ ] Send a short written summary to everyone who attended

- [ ] Call your attorney if someone raised a concern that might change the plan
- [ ] Put the sheet where your executor can find it
- [ ] Schedule a check-in in a year, or sooner after a major change

## Common mistakes

- Holding the meeting during a holiday, an illness or a funeral.
- Inviting people who are not named in a role, then turning it into an audit.
- Saying "we will split everything equally" without checking what the documents say.
- Promising a keepsake verbally. Write it down, because verbal gifts are not generally enforceable.
- Never revisiting the plan after the meeting.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A family member is likely to object, or there is a history of conflict.
- You want to change who is named after the meeting.
- You are thinking about unequal shares among children.
- You are unsure whether sharing details could affect a benefit someone receives.
- A family member has a disability, addiction or financial difficulty that affects how you leave assets.
