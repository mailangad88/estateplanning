**How to use this guide.** Pick 8 to 10 questions that matter most to you for a first call, and ask the same ones of each attorney. Use the scorecard at the end to compare. A good attorney will not be offended, and a defensive answer is information. These questions apply to any attorney, including ours.

## Part 1: Credentials and focus

**1. How much of your practice is estate planning?**
Good answer: a clear majority, with trusts and probate named specifically. Estate planning has state-specific traps. Red flag: "I do a little of everything" for a complex plan, such as a blended family, business, special needs or multi-state property.

**2. Are you licensed in my state and in good standing?**
Good answer: yes, with a bar number you can check. Every state bar has a public lookup, and the American Bar Association lists state bar sites. Check discipline records.

**3. Do you hold any board certification or specialization in estate planning, trusts or elder law?**
Good answer: either a certification or an honest "no, but this is my main work." A few states certify lawyers in estate planning and probate law, and the National Elder Law Foundation certifies elder law attorneys. In many states certification does not exist, so its absence does not make a lawyer poor. Under ABA Model Rule 7.2(c), a lawyer may claim to be certified in a field only if an approved or accredited organization certified them, and must name it clearly. Red flag: claims of being "the best" or "top" with no verifiable basis.

**4. Who will actually do the work: you, an associate or a paralegal?**
Good answer: the attorney reviews and signs off on everything, and staff help with logistics. Red flag: you never speak to the lawyer.

**5. Have you handled situations like mine?** Describe yours briefly: blended family, small business, a child with disabilities, out-of-state property, a large retirement account or a recent diagnosis.
Good answer: specific, with general non-confidential examples. Red flag: "everyone's the same."

## Part 2: Process and recommendations

**6. What would you recommend for me, and what are the alternatives?**
Good answer: reasoned trade-offs among a will, a trust and beneficiary designations. Red flag: one product pitched to everyone ("everybody needs a trust"). Many families do well with a will plus good beneficiary forms, and others need a trust. Ask for the reasoning.

**7. What does the process look like, and how long will it take?**
Good answer: a clear timeline of intake, drafting, review meeting and signing. For a complete plan it is commonly measured in weeks. Ask what to bring.

**8. Will you review my existing documents and beneficiary forms?**
Good answer: yes, and tell me whether they still work. Red flag: pressure to scrap everything.

**9. What should I do after signing, such as funding the trust and updating beneficiaries?**
Good answer: a written funding list and an offer to help or check the work. Many trusts fail because assets were never moved into them.

**10. What happens at the signing meeting?**
Good answer: the attorney explains each document, and witnesses and a notary are provided as the state requires. Red flag: documents mailed with no explanation.

**11. How do you keep my documents safe, and will you hold the originals?**
Good answer: a clear policy, secure storage and a copy for you. Know where the originals are.

**12. How will we communicate, and how fast do you respond?**
Good answer: specific, for example "within one business day."

## Part 3: Fees

**13. How do you charge: flat fee, hourly or something else?**
Good answer: a flat fee for planning packages is common and gives certainty. Hourly is more common for probate or complex matters. Ask what triggers more fees. Red flag: vague answers. Under Model Rule 1.5(b), the basis or rate of the fee must be communicated, preferably in writing.

**14. What exactly is included in the fee?**
Check for: will, trust, pour-over will, power of attorney, health care directive, HIPAA release, deed preparation and recording, funding help, review meeting, signing meeting, copies and updates.

**15. What is not included, and what could it cost?** Ask about recording fees, notary, out-of-state deeds, additional trusts and amendments.

**16. Do you offer a free or low-cost first consultation? What will I leave with?**
Good answer: yes, with an honest summary of recommendation, scope and price. Be cautious of "free" meetings that are sales presentations for financial products.

**17. What do you charge for updates or questions after signing?**
Good answer: a clear policy, such as periodic check-ins or reduced fees.

**18. Do you or the firm receive commission or compensation for recommending insurance, annuities or investment products?**
Good answer: no, or full disclosure. Red flag: the attorney is paid by a product seller. Model Rules 1.8(f) and 5.4 limit the influence of third-party payment, and conflicts must be disclosed.

**19. If I am handling an estate, how will you charge, and what can I do myself?**
Many states let executors do a lot of the paperwork. Good answer: a menu of options, such as full service, limited scope or a flat fee for specific steps. Ask whether the fee is set by state statute (a few states do this) or is negotiable.

## Part 4: State law and special situations

**20. How does my state's law affect my plan?** Ask about intestacy, probate thresholds, estate tax, community property, homestead, Medicaid rules and spousal rights.
Good answer: specifics, with citations when asked. Red flag: generic answers that could apply anywhere.

**21. Will my documents work if I move or own property in another state?**
Good answer: documents are generally recognized across states, with notes on formalities and an offer to review after a move. For real estate elsewhere, ask whether local counsel or a deed in that state is needed.

**22. How should we handle my specific concern?** Examples: a child who is not good with money, a child on benefits, a second marriage, a family business, long-term care costs, or a family member who may challenge the plan.
Good answer: concrete tools, such as trust structures, no-contest clauses where allowed and buy-sell agreements, with trade-offs.

**23. Do I need to worry about estate or inheritance tax?**
Good answer: the federal exemption is $15 million per person for 2026<!-- verify -->. Many families are far below it, but some states tax at much lower levels. A good lawyer says whether you are near or above the line and what to do about it.

## Part 5: Relationship and ethics

**24. What happens if you retire or the firm closes?**
Good answer: a succession plan and a way to retrieve originals.

**25. Can I see a sample engagement letter?**
Good answer: yes. It should describe scope, fee and your responsibilities.

## Red flags at a glance

- Promises about results ("this will definitely avoid probate") or claims of being "the best."
- Pushing a living trust, or any product, without asking about your family and assets.
- Urgency tactics: "this offer expires today," or fear stories as the main selling method.
- Seminars that pitch annuities, insurance or "asset protection" packages and treat the documents as a loss leader.
- No written fee agreement, or unclear fees.
- Nobody explains the documents at signing.
- Out-of-state "document preparation" with no local attorney review.
- Reluctance to name the attorney who will be responsible.

## Online document service or an attorney?

Online document services can be reasonable for very simple situations: a single person, modest assets, no minor children and no complex beneficiaries. They are cheaper. The drawbacks are no advice tailored to you, no help funding a trust, and signing mistakes that can invalidate a document.

The more moving parts your life has, such as children, a home, a second marriage, a business or a family member with special needs, the more an attorney's review is worth. Many attorneys offer limited-scope or flat-fee reviews of an existing do-it-yourself plan. See [online will vs estate attorney](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have minor children, or a home or business.
- You have a blended family, or a family member with a disability or on public benefits.
- You own property in more than one state.
- Your estate may be taxable, or you are not sure.
- You have an existing plan you are not sure still works.
