## How to use this guide

Illinois law gives parents three separate tools for naming someone to care for a child. Each covers a different situation. Read all three, fill in the worksheet at the end, then have an attorney check that your documents meet the statutes. This guide is educational, not advice for your family.

## Three tools, three situations

| Tool | When it applies | Court approval first? | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will nomination | After the death of the parents | Yes, a court appoints | Until the child is an adult, or the court ends it |
| Standby guardian designation | When a parent dies or can no longer make day-to-day decisions | Yes, a petition must be filed and approved | Ongoing, once appointed |
| Short-term guardian appointment | A planned or sudden absence, such as surgery or deployment | No | Up to 365 days under the statute, with a longer military rule |

## Naming a guardian in your will

A parent can designate a guardian in any writing, including a will. Illinois requires two or more credible witnesses, at least 18 years old, and neither can be the person you name. A will signed with the usual will formalities carries prima facie validity. See the [Illinois signing requirements cheat sheet](/free/illinois-signing-requirements-cheat-sheet).

Your choice is not final. The court confirms an appointment only if it serves the child's interests. Your nomination carries real weight, but the judge decides. Naming a successor guardian in the same document protects against your first choice being unable to serve.

## Standby guardian designation

The standby guardian statute (755 ILCS 5/11-5.3) lets a parent name, in writing, someone to be appointed if the parent dies or can no longer carry out day-to-day child care. The writing can be a will. The statute offers a form. Signing the form does not appoint anyone. A petition must be filed and the court must approve the appointment.

The court cannot appoint a standby guardian if another living parent, whose whereabouts are known, is willing and able to make and carry out day-to-day decisions. That parent can consent in writing, or may fail to object after notice. Think through this if you share custody.

Once appointed, the guardian takes an oath and posts a bond, and the court supervises.

## Short-term guardian appointment

The short-term guardian statute (755 ILCS 5/11-5.4) lets a parent appoint someone in writing without court approval. The document must be dated and identify you, the child and the person appointed. You sign before two credible witnesses who are 18 or older and are not the appointee. The appointee also signs, though not at the same time.

The appointment can start right away, on a later date, or when an event happens, such as hospital admission. It lasts up to 365 days from the date it takes effect unless ended sooner. Military members have a longer rule that runs with active duty. You can change or revoke it at any time by telling the appointee or anyone else.

If the other parent is living, known and willing to provide care, the appointment generally needs that parent's written consent.

## Worksheet: who would care for my children

- **Child or children (names, birth dates):** ____________________
- **First choice for a short absence (days to weeks):** ____________________
- **First choice for a long absence (up to a year):** ____________________
- **First choice if I die:** ____________________
- **Backup if the first choice cannot serve:** ____________________
- **Where the guardian lives, and does this match where the children go to school:** ____________________
- **Who the guardian can call for money to raise the children:** ____________________
- **Who manages the children's inheritance, and is it the same person:** ____________________

## Before you sign

- [ ] Ask each person if they will accept, and what they would need to say yes
- [ ] Tell the other parent, and agree on the same names where possible
- [ ] Choose two adult witnesses who are not the guardian
- [ ] Keep the signed original where the guardian can find it
- [ ] Give the short-term appointee a signed copy, plus insurance and medical information
- [ ] Review the choices after a move, a divorce, a new child or a change in anyone's health

## When to talk to an attorney

- You and the other parent disagree on who should be named, or you are separated or divorced.
- The other parent is not in the child's life, or you are worried about their involvement.
- You want to name different people for care and for money.
- Your child has special needs or receives benefits.
- You are a member of the military, or you travel often for work.
