## How to use this guide

Most gifts never produce a tax bill. Even so, some need a report to the IRS, and some carry hidden income tax consequences for the person who receives them. Read the sections, fill in the gift log and take questions to your attorney or tax adviser.

## What counts as a gift

A gift is any transfer where you do not receive full value in return. That includes cash, a deed to a house, adding a child to a bank account in a way that gives them ownership, forgiving a loan, and selling something for far less than it is worth. The donor, not the recipient, is generally responsible for any gift tax.

## The annual exclusion

For 2026 you can give up to $19,000 to each person without any reporting or use of your lifetime exemption. The limit is per recipient, so ten recipients means $190,000 in total. A married couple can combine to give $38,000 per recipient.

Gifts that do not count against anything:

- **Tuition paid directly to a school.** Pay the school, not the student.
- **Medical costs paid directly to a provider.**
- **Gifts to a spouse who is a U.S. citizen.** The marital deduction is generally unlimited. Different rules apply if your spouse is not a citizen.
- **Gifts to qualifying charities.**

## When Form 709 is required

You generally file Form 709, the gift tax return, if you give more than the annual exclusion to any one person in a year. You also file it to split gifts with a spouse, even if each of your gifts is under twice the limit. The return is due with the same deadline as your income tax return for that year.

Filing does not mean you owe tax. The amount above the annual exclusion simply reduces your lifetime exemption, which for 2026 is $15,000,000 per person. Gifts and the estate tax share the same exemption, so a taxable gift made today lowers what is available at death.

## Gift splitting

Spouses can agree to treat a gift from one as made half by each. This lets a gift of $38,000 to one child fall within both annual exclusions even when only one spouse's account funded it. Both spouses must consent on a Form 709, and the rules have conditions, so ask before relying on it.

## Gifts of property: the basis trap

When you give property, the recipient generally takes your cost basis. If you paid $50,000 for stock now worth $250,000 and give it away, the recipient's basis is generally still $50,000. A later sale triggers tax on the $200,000 gain.

Compare that with leaving the stock at death. Inherited property generally receives a basis equal to its value at death. See the [step-up in basis guide](/free/step-up-in-basis-guide) for the comparison.

| Situation | Usually better as a lifetime gift | Usually better to hold until death |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | Yes | Not needed |
| Property that has lost value | Ask first, special basis rules apply | Ask first |
| Property that has grown a lot | Rarely | Often |
| Estate likely to be taxable | Often, to use exemption | Less so |

## Gifts that cause other problems

- **Medicaid.** Gifts can trigger a look-back penalty on long-term care benefits. Use the [Medicaid look-back tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) before giving large amounts.
- **Minors.** Large gifts to a child need a custodian or trust. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).
- **Creditors and divorce.** A gift to an adult child may be reachable by that child's creditors or in a divorce.
- **Your own security.** Do not give away money you may need for care later.

## Before-you-give checklist

- [ ] I know the recipient's tax basis consequences if I am giving property.
- [ ] I have kept proof of value (appraisal, statement or closing document).
- [ ] I have totaled all gifts to this person for the calendar year.
- [ ] I have filed or scheduled a Form 709 if any gift went over the limit.
- [ ] I have left enough for my own care.
- [ ] I have told the recipient in writing whether the transfer is a gift or a loan.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You plan to give a home, business interest or large sum.
- You want to forgive a family loan.
- You are considering gifts to qualify for Medicaid.
- Your spouse is not a U.S. citizen.
- You want to give through a trust rather than outright.
