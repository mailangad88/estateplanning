**How to use this guide.** Guardianship (called conservatorship in some states) is a court process that gives someone legal authority over another adult. It can protect a person who is at risk. It also takes away rights, so many states and courts expect families to consider less restrictive options first. Read the problem table, then the options that fit. The terms and rules vary by state.

## What guardianship is, in short

A court decides that an adult cannot make some or all decisions, and appoints a guardian, often a relative. Depending on the state, there may be a guardian of the person (health, living situation) and one of the estate or property (money). The process typically involves a petition, notice to family, a medical report, a hearing and ongoing reports to the court. It is public, costs money and can take months. A person under guardianship can lose the right to decide where to live, sign contracts or manage money.

The comparison page on [power of attorney versus guardianship](/learn/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship) shows the main differences.

## Start with the problem, not the tool

| The problem | Options to look at |
|---|---|
| Bills, accounts and taxes are not getting handled | Power of attorney for property; joint account with care; trust; bill pay and alerts |
| Medical decisions or hospital stays | Health care power of attorney; surrogate law; HIPAA release |
| Social Security or VA benefits are mismanaged | Representative payee or VA fiduciary |
| The person with a disability needs help but still decides | Supported decision-making agreement, where the state recognizes one |
| Large assets or inheritance for someone who needs help | Trust, possibly a special needs trust |
| Someone is exploiting the person | Protective services, an attorney, and sometimes a court order |

## Option 1: Powers of attorney

A power of attorney lets a person (the principal) name an agent. It must be signed while the principal understands what they are signing. It is the most common alternative because it is quick, private and low cost. A financial power of attorney covers money and property. A health care power of attorney covers medical decisions. See the [powers of attorney guide](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

Limits:

- It must be signed before capacity is lost.
- Banks may be slow to accept it.
- An agent can misuse it, so choose with care and consider a backup and record keeping.

## Option 2: Surrogate decision-making laws

Many states have laws that say who can make medical decisions when there is no health care agent. In Illinois, for example, the Health Care Surrogate Act lists, in order, a guardian, spouse, adult child, parent, adult sibling, adult grandchild, close friend and others. These laws help with medical choices, not money.

## Option 3: Representative payee and VA fiduciary

Social Security can appoint a representative payee to manage benefits when a beneficiary cannot manage them. Social Security looks first to family or friends. The payee must use the money for the person's needs, keep records and may have to complete an annual report. This is a narrow tool. It covers only the benefits, and a power of attorney does not replace it. The VA has a similar fiduciary program for VA benefits.

- [ ] Ask Social Security whether the person needs a payee.
- [ ] Ask the doctor to document why.
- [ ] Keep a separate ledger of how benefits are spent.

## Option 4: Supported decision-making

Supported decision-making lets an adult, often one with an intellectual or developmental disability, choose trusted people to help understand information and make choices, while the adult stays the decision maker. A number of states have laws that recognize agreements of this kind, and others do not. Ask a local disability rights organization or an attorney whether your state does. Even where no statute exists, informal support can work well, such as a family member joining medical visits with permission.

## Option 5: Trusts

A revocable or irrevocable trust can hold money, with a trustee paying bills and managing investments. A person who is able to sign can set up a trust before capacity fails. A third party, such as a parent, can fund a trust for an adult child with a disability. A special needs trust can protect eligibility for means-tested benefits. See the [special needs trusts guide](/guides/special-needs-trusts). Trusts do not make medical decisions.

## Option 6: Joint accounts, automatic payments and bank tools

Simple tools can solve narrow problems. Some are riskier than they look.

- **Joint accounts:** convenient, but the money becomes available to the joint owner and may be exposed to that person's creditors or divorce. It may also change who inherits.
- **Convenience signers or agent access:** some banks offer limited access for paying bills.
- **Automatic bill pay and alerts:** can help.
- **Trusted contacts:** brokerage firms often allow a person to name one.

## Option 7: Care management and informal help

Geriatric care managers, adult day programs, home care, meal delivery and a family calendar can reduce risk without any legal change. Some of these may not be covered by insurance.

## When guardianship is the right answer

Alternatives do not work when:

- There is no power of attorney and the person can no longer sign one.
- An institution will not release the person's money or information and no other path exists.
- The person is in danger from self-neglect or exploitation and refuses help.
- Family members are in serious conflict about care.
- A court order is needed to stop a harmful relationship or a transaction.

Even then, many states allow limited guardianship, which covers only the areas of need, or temporary and emergency orders. Ask an attorney whether a limited order is available.

## Checklist before anyone files

- [ ] Is there a signed power of attorney? Has it been tried?
- [ ] Has the doctor described what the person can and cannot do?
- [ ] Have we asked the person what they want, and listened?
- [ ] Is there a narrower option, such as a payee or limited order?
- [ ] Do we understand the cost, the court's reports and the loss of rights?
- [ ] Who will be guardian, and is that person ready for ongoing reports?
- [ ] Do the siblings agree, and what will we do if one objects?

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are being asked to petition for guardianship, or someone has filed against your family member.
- The person cannot sign a power of attorney and you are not sure whether the capacity is gone.
- A bank, hospital or agency will not accept your authority.
- You suspect financial abuse.
- A child with a disability is turning 18 and you want to keep helping without full guardianship.
