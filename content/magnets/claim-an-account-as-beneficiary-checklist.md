## How to use this checklist

We are sorry for your loss. There is no deadline to get everything done in a week. Work through the steps in order, write down each call, and keep every letter. This is general information, and each company has its own process.

## Step 1: Find out if you are named

1. **Ask the family or the executor** which accounts exist.
2. **Look through mail, statements and tax forms.** A 1099 or a yearly statement names the company.
3. **Check the person's email** for policy notices, if you are authorized to see it.
4. **Contact the employer's benefits office** for a life insurance policy or 401(k).
5. **Search unclaimed property.** Your state treasurer and the National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators run lookups.
6. **Search for lost policies.** The National Association of Insurance Commissioners offers a policy locator.

A company usually will not tell a stranger who the beneficiary is. If you are the executor, you can ask for the information. If you are not sure you are named, ask for written confirmation.

## Step 2: Gather documents

- [ ] Certified copies of the death certificate (order several)
- [ ] Your government photo ID
- [ ] The policy or account number, or the owner's Social Security number
- [ ] The company's claim form, completed and signed
- [ ] Your own Social Security number or taxpayer ID
- [ ] Your contact and payment details
- [ ] For a retirement account: your choice of rollover, lump sum or inherited account

Some institutions need a notarized or signature-verified forms for larger amounts.

## Step 3: File the claim

| Account | Where to file | Typical extras |
|---|---|---|
| Life insurance | Insurer's claims department | Claim form, death certificate |
| 401(k) or pension | Plan administrator, via the employer | Beneficiary proof, rollover election |
| IRA | Custodian (the bank or brokerage) | Inherited IRA paperwork |
| Bank POD account | The bank branch | Death certificate, ID |
| Brokerage TOD account | The brokerage | Transfer paperwork |
| Annuity | Insurer | Claim form, election of payout |

## How long it takes

Life insurance often pays within a few weeks after the insurer receives a complete claim, but a claim made soon after the policy began, or involving a questionable cause of death, can take longer. Insurers typically owe interest if payment is delayed. Retirement accounts and brokerage accounts vary. Keep a call log and ask for a reference number.

## Taxes on what you receive

| Type | Usual federal income tax treatment |
|---|---|
| Life insurance death benefit, paid in a lump sum | Generally not income to you |
| Interest paid on the life insurance after death | Generally taxable as interest |
| Life insurance paid in installments | The principal part is not taxed, the interest part is |
| Traditional IRA or 401(k) | Withdrawals taxed as ordinary income |
| Roth IRA | Often tax-free if rules are met |
| Bank POD and brokerage TOD | The money itself is not income; later earnings may be |
| Annuity | The growth part is generally taxable |

States differ. A few states charge an inheritance tax to some beneficiaries. Illinois does not charge an inheritance tax. <!-- verify: Illinois has estate tax but no inheritance tax --> See the [inherited IRA guide](/free/inherited-ira-10-year-rule-guide) for retirement account deadlines.

## Debts

A beneficiary does not usually owe the owner's debts, including credit cards, just because they were named. The estate pays valid debts from estate property. If the estate runs out, creditors generally go unpaid. Do not pay a credit card from your own funds, and do not sign anything that says you are responsible. Be careful if you co-signed or shared an account, because those debts can be yours.

If you suspect the estate is insolvent, tell the executor and ask whether the estate is paying creditors in the legal priority order. A named life insurance payout or a retirement account is usually not the first thing creditors can reach. Ask an attorney in the state where the person lived.

## Refusing or disclaiming

You can say no. A disclaimer is a written refusal that must follow legal rules and generally must be signed before you accept benefits or interest. The share then usually goes to the contingent beneficiary, or to the estate if none is named. A federal qualified disclaimer has a deadline of nine months after death. <!-- verify: nine-month deadline, 26 USC 2518 --> Disclaiming a retirement account works the same way, and you cannot choose who gets it.

## If the paperwork has a problem

- **Misspelled name:** send a notarized statement, ID and the death certificate. The company can often fix it. If not, a court order may be needed.
- **Lost form:** ask for a copy. Companies must keep records. Without it, the default rules apply
- **Two people disagree about a split:** the company may hold the money until they agree or a court rules.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A claim is denied or delayed with no reason
- Two people claim the same account
- The estate has more debt than assets
- A trust, a minor or a person with a disability is the beneficiary
- You suspect the form was changed under pressure
