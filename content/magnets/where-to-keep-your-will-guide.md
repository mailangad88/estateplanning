**How to use this guide.** Pick the storage option that matches your situation, then fill in the locator card. The right place is one that is safe from fire, theft and loss, and also easy for your executor to open soon after your death. Many wills are signed correctly and then lost.

## Why the original matters

Courts usually want the original signed will. A copy can sometimes be used, but a lost original raises a presumption in many states that you destroyed it on purpose, and your family may have to prove otherwise. That means extra time, court hearings and cost. Some states require whoever holds a will to deliver it to the court promptly after a death. In Illinois, the person who has the will must file it with the clerk of the circuit court within 30 days after learning of the death.

## Option 1: Home safe or file cabinet

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| Access | Immediate for you; depends on your family knowing the code or key |
| Cost | Low to moderate |
| Risk | Fire, flood, theft, or a safe nobody can open |
| Fits | People who keep a clear paper trail and tell their executor |

Use a fireproof and waterproof safe if you can. Never keep the only key or code in the safe. Put the code in a sealed envelope with your attorney or in a password manager your executor can reach.

## Option 2: Attorney's office or vault

Many firms store originals for clients, often for free or a small fee, in fireproof storage with a log.

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| Access | The attorney can deliver to the executor or file with the court |
| Cost | Often none or modest; ask |
| Risk | Firm closes, retires or moves |
| Fits | Anyone who wants one trusted custodian |

Ask these questions.

- **What happens if the firm closes or the attorney retires?** ______
- **Who gets the original if I ask for it back?** ______
- **Will you tell my executor where it is?** ______
- **Is there a written receipt?** ______

## Option 3: County or court deposit

Some states and counties let you deposit a will with the court or a county office for safekeeping. Availability, fees and procedures vary. Ask the clerk of the court where you live whether this is offered, whether the will can be withdrawn, and how your executor will be told it is there.

## Option 4: Safe deposit box

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| Access | Often restricted after death until the bank has court paperwork |
| Cost | Annual rental fee |
| Risk | Executor cannot get in quickly; box may be sealed |
| Fits | People who add a co-renter or confirm state rules first |

Banks may freeze access to a box when they learn of a death. Many states have a process that allows a family member or executor to open it to look for a will, often with a bank officer present, but the process takes time. Do not make the box the only place where the will lives. If you use one, add your executor as an authorized person or co-renter so they can open it, and check the bank's rules.

## What to avoid

- A drawer with other papers, with no mention to anyone.
- A relative's house far away.
- Stapling or clipping other documents to the will.
- Giving the only original to a beneficiary.
- Storing copies and calling one "the will" in conflicting places.

## Keep copies, but label them

Make a scan or paper copy marked "Copy. Original is at ____." This helps your executor see what the will says and where to find the original. Do not sign the copy.

## Locator card

Cut this out or keep it with important papers. Give a copy to your executor.

- **My name:** ______
- **Date my will was signed:** ______
- **Original is at (place or firm):** ______
- **Address and phone:** ______
- **Key, code or contact needed:** ______
- **Attorney who prepared it:** ______
- **Executor name and phone:** ______
- **Alternate executor:** ______
- **Other documents stored with it (trust, powers of attorney, health care directive):** ______
- **Last checked on:** ______

## If you cannot find the original

1. Search likely places again, including old files and bank boxes.
2. Call the attorney who drafted it and ask whether they kept the original.
3. Ask the court clerk whether it was deposited.
4. If it is gone, talk to an attorney before you do anything else. In some states a copy can be admitted with proof, but a new will is usually safer while you are alive.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You cannot find the original of your own will.
- You have wills in more than one state.
- You want your attorney to hold the original and want terms in writing.
- You have a trust and want its documents stored with your will.
- Your executor lives out of state and may need quick access.
- You think someone else holds your will.
