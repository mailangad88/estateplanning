## How to use this checklist

We are sorry for your loss. Thieves read obituaries and look for people who can no longer notice a new credit card in their name. A deceased person's Social Security number is valuable to them, and fraud can go unseen for months because nobody is checking the credit report. The steps below are short and mostly free. Do them in order, keep a log, and act on warning signs quickly. Where rules vary by company or state, ask.

## Step 1: Limit what you publish

- [ ] In the obituary, leave out the full birth date, home address and mother's maiden name
- [ ] Do not list the home address together with the funeral time, since an empty house is a target
- [ ] Ask a neighbour to watch the house during the service
- [ ] Do not post photos of documents, such as the death certificate

## Step 2: Order death certificates and use them wisely

- [ ] Order more certified copies than you think you will use, since many agencies and companies ask for them
- [ ] Send copies, not originals, unless an agency says otherwise
- [ ] Record each place you send one

## Step 3: Tell the credit bureaus

There are three national credit bureaus: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. A report can be flagged to show that the person is deceased, which makes it harder to open new credit.

- [ ] Send each bureau a letter with a copy of the death certificate
- [ ] State your relationship and, if you are the executor, include proof of authority
- [ ] Ask the bureau to place a "deceased" alert or flag on the file
- [ ] Ask for a copy of the credit report and read it for accounts you do not recognise
- [ ] Check the report again after a few months

Sample wording:

"I am writing to report the death of [full name], Social Security number ending in [last four], date of birth [date]. I am the [relationship / executor]. A copy of the death certificate is enclosed. Please place a deceased notice on the credit file, and send me a copy of the report. My contact details are below."

## Step 4: Notify the Social Security Administration

- [ ] The funeral home often reports the death to SSA. Ask whether they did
- [ ] If not, report it yourself by phone or at a local office
- [ ] Do not spend any benefit payment issued after the date of death. SSA may take it back
- [ ] Ask about survivor benefits for a spouse or child

## Step 5: Tell the IRS

Tax identity theft is common after a death. A thief may file a return and claim a refund using the person's number.

- [ ] File the final income tax return for the year of death, and mark it as deceased
- [ ] The executor can file IRS Form 56 to tell the IRS who is acting for the estate
- [ ] If a return was already filed in the person's name, or you receive an IRS notice you do not understand, respond to the notice and ask an attorney or tax professional
- [ ] If the IRS notice mentions identity theft, ask about Form 14039, the identity theft affidavit

Keep copies of everything you send.

## Step 6: Close or freeze accounts

- [ ] Notify banks, card issuers and lenders of the death. Ask each to mark the account as deceased
- [ ] Cancel credit cards after paying or documenting the balances. See [what happens to debt when someone dies](/blog/what-happens-to-debt-when-someone-dies)
- [ ] Stop automatic payments that should not continue
- [ ] Remove the deceased as an authorized user on any account you hold
- [ ] Close or memorialize online accounts. See [digital accounts after death](/free/digital-accounts-after-death-checklist)

Do not remove or pay accounts from your own money without a note of why. Keep estate and personal funds apart.

## Step 7: Stop junk and risky mail

- [ ] Forward the person's mail to the executor through the U.S. Postal Service, with proof of authority
- [ ] Opt out of prescreened credit offers at OptOutPrescreen.com, which is the official consumer site
- [ ] Register the number with the Deceased Do Not Contact list from the Data and Marketing Association if you want to reduce marketing mail
- [ ] Cancel magazines and catalogues

## Step 8: Watch for warning signs

- [ ] Bills or collection calls for accounts you do not recognise
- [ ] IRS or state tax notices about unfiled or unusual returns
- [ ] A credit report showing new accounts after the date of death
- [ ] Mail about benefits, loans or cards you did not know about
- [ ] Medical bills for care after the death
- [ ] A refused claim because someone has already filed

## If you find fraud

1. Write down the date and what you found.
2. Report it to the company in writing, and ask for the account to be closed and the fraud documented.
3. Report to the Federal Trade Commission at IdentityTheft.gov and ask for a recovery plan.
4. File a police report and keep a copy.
5. Tell the executor and the estate attorney.
6. Ask the credit bureaus to add notes to the file.

Do not pay a debt created by fraud. Dispute it in writing.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A creditor claims the family or estate owes money for fraudulent accounts
- The IRS says a return was already filed for the person
- Fraud involves property, a home loan or a business
- You are not sure whether you have authority to deal with a company
- A relative or caregiver is suspected of misusing the identity
- Collectors continue to contact you after you have disputed a debt
