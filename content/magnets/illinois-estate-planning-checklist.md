**How to use this checklist.** Read the document list, mark what you already have, and circle what is missing. Check the signing table before you sign anything, because Illinois rules differ by document. Fill in the last page and bring it to an Illinois attorney. This is educational, not advice for your situation.

## The Illinois document set

Most Illinois plans draw from the same eight documents. You may not need all of them.

- [ ] **Will.** Names who receives your property, who serves as executor and, if you have minor children, who you want as guardian. Illinois does not recognize handwritten wills that lack witnesses.
- [ ] **Statutory short form power of attorney for property.** Lets an agent you choose pay bills and manage accounts if you cannot. Illinois publishes a statutory form in the Illinois Power of Attorney Act (755 ILCS 45).
- [ ] **Power of attorney for health care.** Names the person who speaks with doctors for you. Illinois also has a statutory form for this document.
- [ ] **Living will.** States your wishes about life-prolonging treatment under the Illinois Living Will Act (755 ILCS 35).
- [ ] **HIPAA authorization.** Lets your agent and other people you name see medical records. Many Illinois health care forms include this language, so check yours.
- [ ] **Transfer on death instrument for a home.** A recorded document that passes your residence to a named person at death, without probate. It comes from the Residential Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument Act (755 ILCS 27).
- [ ] **Guardian nomination for minor children.** Usually placed in the will. A parent may also name a guardian in another signed writing with two witnesses.
- [ ] **Revocable living trust, if needed.** Worth discussing if you own real estate in more than one state, want privacy, or want to manage assets for a child or a person with a disability. A trust works only for property you retitle into it.

## Illinois signing rules at a glance

Rules below come from the Illinois statutes named in the Sources section. A mistake in signing can undo a document, so confirm the details with an attorney.

| Document | Witnesses | Notary | Special point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will | 2 or more credible witnesses who see you sign | Not required | A witness who is also a beneficiary can lose part or all of the gift. Use people who receive nothing. |
| Power of attorney for property (statutory form) | At least 1 witness | Required | Not effective without both. The witness cannot be your agent or a relative of you or your agent. |
| Power of attorney for health care (statutory form) | 1 witness, age 18 or older | Not required | The witness cannot be your agent, a relative, or your physician or a relative of your physician. |
| Living will | 2 witnesses, age 18 or older | Not required | Avoid heirs, beneficiaries and anyone paying for your care.<!-- verify --> |
| HIPAA authorization | Not required by federal law | Not required | Often signed along with the health care power of attorney. |
| Transfer on death instrument | 2 or more credible witnesses | Required | Must be recorded with the county recorder before you die. |
| Guardian nomination | 2 or more credible witnesses, age 18 or older | Not required | Neither witness can be the person named as guardian. |
| Revocable trust | Not required by statute<!-- verify --> | Common practice | A deed is needed to move a home into the trust. |

## What happens in Illinois if you do nothing

If you die without a will, Illinois law decides who inherits. Under the intestacy statute (755 ILCS 5/2-1) the result is fixed by family tree, not by your wishes.

| Your family | Who receives your probate property |
|---|---|
| Spouse and children | One-half to your spouse, one-half to your children |
| Spouse, no children or other descendants | All to your spouse |
| Children, no spouse | All to your children |
| No spouse or descendants | Parents, brothers and sisters share, and a surviving parent takes a double share |
| No close relatives | Grandparents and their families, then more distant kin |

Three things follow from this table.

- **An unmarried partner receives nothing.** The statute lists spouses and blood relatives only.
- **Stepchildren and friends are left out.** So are charities.
- **Your spouse may share with your children.** In a blended family, the children from an earlier marriage take half, and your spouse may not hold the house outright.

Beneficiary forms, joint accounts and a recorded transfer on death instrument pass outside the will, so they still follow their own paperwork. Without a power of attorney or health care power of attorney, your family may need a court to appoint a guardian to act for you if you lose capacity.

## Illinois numbers worth knowing

- **Estate tax.** Illinois has its own estate tax with a $4,000,000 exclusion that is not portable between spouses. Federal law is separate. The federal exclusion is $15 million per person as of 2026.<!-- verify -->
- **Inheritance tax.** Illinois does not have one.
- **Small estate affidavit.** For deaths on or after August 15, 2025, up to $150,000 of personal property can pass by affidavit, with vehicles not counted. It cannot transfer Illinois real estate.
- **Spouse's rights.** A spouse can renounce a will and take one-third of the estate if you leave descendants, or one-half if you do not.
- **Creditor claims.** Claims against an estate are barred two years after death.

## Common mistakes

- Using a will witness who is also named in the will.
- Signing a property power of attorney with a witness and no notary.
- Signing a transfer on death instrument but never recording it.
- Leaving an old beneficiary form on file after a divorce.
- Keeping the only original in a place nobody can reach.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own a home or other real estate and want to use a transfer on death instrument or a trust.
- You have minor children, a child with a disability, or a blended family.
- Your estate may be near the Illinois $4,000,000 estate tax exclusion.
- You own property in another state.
- You signed documents in another state or more than five years ago.
- A parent or partner needs these documents now and may be losing capacity.
