**How to use this worksheet.** Gather your policies first, then fill in each section. It takes about an hour. It does not tell you what to buy. It helps you see what you have, who gets it, and what gaps to raise with a licensed insurance professional and an attorney. Rules vary by state.

## Why a review is worth the hour

Life insurance pays according to the beneficiary form on file with the insurer, not according to your will. If the form is out of date, the money goes to the wrong person, sometimes an ex-spouse, a deceased parent or no one. If a minor is named, a court may need to appoint someone to manage the money. If group coverage ended when you left a job, the policy you counted on may not exist.

A review catches these before they matter. Do it after any major life event, and at least every few years.

### The main types

- **Term life.** Covers a set number of years, such as 10, 20 or 30. It usually has no cash value. If you outlive the term, coverage ends. Some policies can be converted to permanent coverage, so ask the insurer.
- **Whole life.** Lifelong coverage with level premiums and a cash value that grows by a schedule set by the insurer.
- **Universal and variable policies.** Permanent coverage with more flexible premiums and, in some cases, investment choices. They need regular review, because a policy can lapse if the cash value runs low.
- **Group life through work.** Often one or two times salary, sometimes more with extra purchase. It is usually cheap, but it generally ends when the job ends, or soon after. Some plans allow a conversion or portability option for a limited time. Ask HR for the deadline, which can be short.
- **Credit and mortgage insurance.** Pays a specific loan. Often has a declining benefit.

## Step 2: Beneficiary check

Go through each policy and check every item.

- [ ] **The primary beneficiary is who I intend today.** Divorce, remarriage, death and births all change that.
- [ ] **There is a contingent beneficiary.** If the primary dies first and no contingent is named, the money may go to the estate, and then through probate and creditor claims.
- [ ] **Shares add up to 100 percent** for each tier.
- [ ] **Names are exact,** including relationship, date of birth and contact details, so the insurer can find the person.
- [ ] **The ex-spouse question is settled.** Some states revoke an ex-spouse as beneficiary on divorce, but not all, and not for every kind of policy. Employer plans governed by federal law may follow the form on file regardless of state rules. Update the form after a divorce.
- [ ] **The owner is who I expect.** For some policies, the owner is not the insured, and the owner has control over beneficiary changes.
- [ ] **No minor is named directly.** See below.
- [ ] **I have a copy of the confirmation** from the insurer showing the current designation.

### Never naming a minor child directly

An insurer generally will not pay a large sum to a child under 18. A court may appoint a conservator to hold the money, with filings, bonds and fees, and the child may receive the whole amount at 18 or 21. Rules vary by state. See [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors).

Options families consider:

| Option | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Name a trust as beneficiary** | A trust holds and manages the money under rules you write, with a trustee | Needs a drafted trust and a trusted trustee |
| **Name a custodian under UTMA or UGMA** | Insurer pays an adult "as custodian for [child] under the [state] Transfers to Minors Act" | Simple, but the child gets control at a fixed age set by state law |
| **Name a surviving parent** | The other parent receives the money for the children | Works for many families, but the money becomes the parent's, with no protection for the child |
| **Name the estate** | Money flows through your will | Probate delay, public record and creditor exposure |

### Naming a trust

If you name a trust, use the exact name and date of the trust, as in: "The [Last Name] Family Trust dated [date], [Trustee name], Trustee." Do not guess. Ask your attorney for the wording. A revocable living trust or a standalone life insurance trust can both receive the money. Make sure the trust is signed before you name it. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) and [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

## Step 3: Group coverage and job changes

- **Your group policy may end when you leave.** Some plans end on the last day of work, others at the end of the month.
- **Conversion and portability.** Ask whether you can convert to an individual policy or continue group coverage at your own cost. Deadlines are often 30 to 60 days, though this varies by plan.
- **New job waiting periods.** A new employer's plan may not start on day one.
- **Health changes.** A new individual policy may be harder or costlier to obtain after a diagnosis, which is why some people keep a personal policy outside work.
- **Beneficiary on the new plan.** A new employer plan starts with a blank form. Fill it in.

## Common mistakes

- Not naming a contingent beneficiary.
- Naming a minor child, or "my children" with no plan for who holds the money.
- Forgetting that group coverage ends with the job.
- Assuming the will controls the policy.
- Letting a term policy expire when a dependent still relies on the income.
- Not telling the beneficiary a policy exists or where to find it.
- Naming a trust that does not exist yet, or using the wrong date or name.
- Overlooking an old policy bought by a parent or an ex-spouse.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want a trust to receive policy proceeds.
- You have a child with special needs who receives benefits. A direct payment could affect eligibility, and a special needs trust may be considered.
- You are divorced, remarried or have children from more than one relationship.
- Your estate is large enough that policy ownership might matter for estate tax. In 2026, the federal exemption is $15 million per person <!-- verify -->, and some states tax at lower levels.
- You co-own a business and have a buy-sell agreement funded by insurance.
- A beneficiary has a creditor, a pending divorce or other risk.
