**How to use this guide.** Most surprises after a death come from things a parent left unsaid, not from the will itself. Go through Part 1 on your own, then decide how much to tell using Part 2. Part 4 gives scripts, and Part 5 helps you write a short letter. This is general information, not legal advice, and a will does not have to be shared with anyone while you are alive.

## Part 1: Get clear before you speak

Settle these on your own first. Unclear thinking becomes unclear conversation.

- **Who will be my executor, and why:** ______________________
- **Who has the health care power of attorney, and the money power of attorney:** ______________________
- **Will my children each receive an equal share?** Yes / No
- **If not, what is the reason in one sentence per child:** ______________________
- **What would I most like my children to understand about my choices:** ______________________
- **What would I want to protect them from fighting about:** ______________________

Then ask whether the plan itself should change. A plan you are nervous to explain is worth a second look. Sometimes the answer is a better plan, and sometimes it is a better explanation. Both are fine.

## Part 2: The disclosure ladder

You can share at three levels. Pick one per child, per topic.

| Level | What you share | Good when |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Roles only | Who is executor, who holds powers of attorney, where documents are | Always a good idea. This is what children need to act in an emergency. |
| 2. Roles and principles | Add how you decided: equal, or unequal and why, with no numbers | You expect questions about fairness |
| 3. Full details | Add amounts and specific gifts, perhaps by sharing the will | Children are mature, trust each other, and you have no secrets to guard |

Why not always tell everything? Because a plan can change. Telling a child "the house is yours" and then changing the plan causes more pain than silence. If you share numbers, add: "This is the plan today, and I may change it."

What tends to go wrong with secrecy: a child who learns of an unequal gift only at the reading feels it as a verdict on their worth. What tends to go wrong with full disclosure: a child may count on money, or compare with a sibling in a harsh way. Level 2 is where many parents settle.

## Part 3: Explaining unequal inheritances

Reasons parents give for unequal shares, with the framing that tends to work:

- **One child provided years of care.** "You gave me time I cannot repay. This is a way to say thank you." Consider a written care agreement while care is happening, rather than a bigger share later.
- **One child needs more help,** such as a disability or serious illness. "I want each of you to have what you need, which is not always the same as the same amount." A special needs trust may be needed, so talk to an attorney.
- **One child already received help,** such as a down payment or tuition. "I counted what I gave earlier." Write it down so it is clear.
- **One child will take over a business or farm.** Put in writing how the other children are treated, and say so before a death.
- **One child has debts, addiction or a rocky marriage.** Often a trust, instead of an outright gift, is the answer, as discussed in [leaving money to someone who struggles](/free/leaving-money-to-someone-who-struggles-guide).
- **A child is estranged.** Say plainly in your will whether the omission is intended, because silence invites a court challenge.

A good explanation has three parts: the decision, the reason, and what you hope for the relationship. "I split the house equally and gave Jordan the lake cabin because Jordan has cared for it for years. I hope you will both feel loved by both." Short and kind beats long and defensive.

## Part 4: Scripts for the hard questions

**"How much is there?"** "That is private for now. What matters is who will help and where the documents are."

**"Why does my sister get more?"** "I tried to be fair, not identical. Here is my reason. I understand if it hurts, and I am open to talking about it."

**"Are you leaving things to your new spouse?"** (blended family) "I have set it up so my spouse is cared for, and you each receive something too. Here is the outline." See the guide to [estate planning for blended families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families).

**"Why did you pick him as executor?"** "It is a job, not a prize. I chose the person who is organized and available, and I want the rest of you to help."

**"Are you giving away everything to charity?"** "I am giving some. Here is the portion that goes to family."

**"I do not want to talk about this."** "I know. I am asking for twenty minutes so you do not have to guess later."

**"Will you be fair after you die if I am not nice to you now?"** (a child is angry) "My plan is not a reward for behavior. I still hope we can repair what is wrong."

Tips for any answer:

- Say what you mean and then stop. Do not try to win agreement.
- Do not promise a gift outside your documents. Promises you cannot keep become family legends.
- Do not discuss it when someone is angry, sick or has been drinking.

## Part 5: A letter to each child

Writing a personal letter lets you say what the will cannot. Keep it with your will and tell your executor where it is.

- **Dear (name):** ______________________
- **One thing I admire about you:** ______________________
- **A memory I treasure:** ______________________
- **Why I made the choices I did about you in my plan:** ______________________
- **Something I hope for you and your siblings:** ______________________
- **What I would like you to do with a particular item or gift, as a wish and not a rule:** ______________________

Letters are not a substitute for legal documents, and anything that sounds like an instruction may confuse the will. Ask your attorney before putting directions in a letter.

## Checklist before and after the conversation

- [ ] Documents are signed and current before I talk.
- [ ] I decided my disclosure level for each topic.
- [ ] I told the people I named as executor and agents before the others.
- [ ] I followed up by phone or in writing within a week.
- [ ] I told my attorney if anything said changed my mind.
- [ ] I put a review date in my calendar.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You plan an unequal division and want it to hold up if challenged.
- You are leaving out a child, or a child is estranged.
- You have a blended family, or a child with a disability or on public benefits.
- A child may take over a business, a farm or a family home.
- You worry that a child could pressure you, or contest your will.
