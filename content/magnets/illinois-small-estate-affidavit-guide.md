## How to use this guide

We are sorry you are dealing with this. Illinois has a shortcut for small estates called the small estate affidavit. It lets a family collect a loved one's personal property without opening a court case. This guide explains who qualifies and walks through the steps. Fill in the lines as you go. It is general education, not legal advice, and a short call with an Illinois attorney can confirm the route for your family.

## Who qualifies

The affidavit is set out in Illinois law at 755 ILCS 5/25-1. For a death on or after August 15, 2025, the gross value of the personal property is $150,000 or less. Before that date the limit was $100,000. <!-- verify: effective date of P.A. 104-0346 -->

In general, all of these need to be true:

- [ ] The total personal property, counted as the worksheet below explains, is $150,000 or less
- [ ] No court case is open or planned for the estate
- [ ] There is no Illinois real estate that needs to change hands
- [ ] You can list the heirs, or the people named in a will, and who gets what
- [ ] Funeral costs and valid debts are paid or can be listed in order of priority
- [ ] A certified death certificate is available

A will does not rule out the affidavit. If there is a will, it still needs to be filed with the circuit court clerk in the county where the person lived.

## What counts toward the limit

The law counts tangible and intangible personal property that belongs to the estate and passes through it. In plain terms, that means things like:

- Bank and credit union accounts in the person's name alone
- Stocks, bonds and brokerage accounts with no beneficiary
- Furniture, jewelry, tools and collectibles
- Money owed to the person, such as a final paycheck or a tax refund

## What does not count

- **Motor vehicles registered with the Secretary of State.** Cars and trucks are left out of the $150,000 count. The statute lists other vehicle types as well, such as motor homes and commercial vehicles.
- **Accounts with a named beneficiary or payable on death designation.** These pass by the form, not through the estate.
- **Joint accounts with a surviving owner.** The survivor generally takes these directly.
- **Life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary.**
- **Property in a living trust.**

Real estate is a different matter. The affidavit covers personal property only. A house held in the person's sole name usually needs probate or another recorded transfer.

## The affidavit steps

1. **Get the form.** Illinois courts publish a standardized Small Estate Affidavit approved by the Illinois Supreme Court for use statewide. Find it at illinoiscourts.gov under approved forms, or ask the circuit court clerk in the county where the person lived. <!-- verify: exact statewide form name and number -->
2. **Gather proof.** Get certified copies of the death certificate. Collect statements for each account.
3. **List heirs and the people who inherit.** Include names, addresses and relationships.
4. **List debts.** Funeral expenses and valid claims must be paid or listed in priority order.
5. **Complete the sworn statements.** The form asks you to state that the facts are true and to accept responsibility if they are not.
6. **Sign before a notary.** The person who signs is the affiant, and signs under oath in front of a notary public.
7. **Deliver it.** Give a copy to each bank or company holding property. They release it as the affidavit directs.
8. **Keep records.** Note what you collected, what you paid and what each heir received.

## Who signs

The affiant signs. That is usually the executor named in a will, a spouse or an adult child who is taking responsibility. The affiant swears the facts are true. Under the law, the affiant also agrees to repay creditors, heirs and legatees for losses caused by relying on false statements. Take that seriously and be sure of your numbers.

## What banks typically ask for

Every institution has its own rules, and some are slower than others. Plan to bring:

- [ ] The notarized affidavit, original and copies
- [ ] A certified death certificate for each institution
- [ ] Your photo ID
- [ ] A copy of the will, if there is one
- [ ] The account number or a recent statement
- [ ] Their own claim form, if they ask for one

Ask the bank what they need before you visit. Tell them the affidavit is under 755 ILCS 5/25-1.

## When you need probate instead

Consider opening a probate case if any of these apply:

- The personal property is above $150,000
- Illinois real estate must be sold or transferred
- Heirs disagree, or the will is being questioned
- Debts are large, or valid claims may exceed the assets
- An institution refuses the affidavit
- You are unsure about the heirs

See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works) and the [illinois probate checklist](/free/illinois-probate-checklist).

## When to talk to an attorney

- The total is close to the limit or you are unsure how to value something
- The person owned a home or land in Illinois
- A bank turns down the affidavit
- There may be more debt than money
- Family members disagree about who gets what
