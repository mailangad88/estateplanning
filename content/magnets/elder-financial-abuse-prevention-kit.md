**How to use this kit.** Part one is for the whole family: warning signs and common scams. Part two covers safeguards you can set up this week. Part three is what to do if you suspect something. Part four is a printable checklist. Approach your relative with respect. Most people who are targeted are careful, capable adults, and anyone can be fooled by a skilled criminal.

## Why this happens

Older adults often have savings, a home and regular income. They may also be lonely, grieving, newly dependent on others or less familiar with technology. Criminals know this. Exploitation can also come from within the family or from a paid caregiver, which makes it harder to talk about.

Being targeted is not a sign of foolishness. Scams are built by people who do this for a living, and they use urgency, fear and affection on purpose.

## Warning signs

No single sign proves anything. A cluster of changes deserves a gentle conversation.

**Money signs**

- Unusual withdrawals, wire transfers or gift card purchases.
- Large checks written to a new "friend," caregiver or business.
- Unpaid bills for a person who has always paid on time.
- Missing cash, jewelry or belongings.
- Changes to a will, power of attorney, beneficiary form or bank account that surprise the family.
- New joint accounts, or someone newly added to the title of a home.
- Signatures that look unlike the person's own.

**Behavior signs**

- Secrecy about a new relationship, a prize, an investment or a "emergency."
- Fear, anxiety or confusion about money.
- A new person who controls contact, answers the phone or discourages visits.
- Isolation from family and friends.
- Sudden requests to keep something private.

**Practical signs**

- Stacks of unopened mail, or a flood of mail about sweepstakes and charities.
- Frequent calls from unknown numbers.
- Remote-access software installed on a computer.
- Utilities shut off, or a decline in home care despite funds being available.

Cognitive decline can look like some of these signs and can also make a person more vulnerable. If you suspect memory problems, a medical check is a kind step. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) for planning while capacity remains.

## Common scams

### Impostor scams

The caller claims to be from a government agency, a bank, a utility, tech support or a well-known company. They say there is a problem: unpaid taxes, a frozen account, a virus, a suspicious charge. They demand payment or access now.

Features to watch for:

- **Urgency.** "Act today or you will be arrested."
- **Unusual payment methods.** Gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency or cash sent by courier.
- **Instructions to stay on the phone** and tell no one, including bank staff.
- **A request to move money to a "safe account."** Real banks and agencies do not do this.

Government agencies generally do not call to demand immediate payment by gift card. If in doubt, hang up and call the number on a statement or the agency's official site.

### Romance scams

A person met online builds a close relationship, often over weeks or months, then begins asking for money: a plane ticket, a medical bill, a customs fee, a business setback. The person often cannot meet in person or appear on video.

Warning signs include declarations of love very quickly, stories of overseas work, requests for secrecy and requests for money, however small at first.

### "Grandparent" and family emergency scams

A caller says, "Grandma, it's me," and claims to be a grandchild in trouble: a car accident, an arrest, a hospital bill. Another person may pose as a lawyer or police officer. They ask for money right away and for secrecy. Voice cloning technology can make a call sound like a real relative.

A simple protection: **agree on a family code word** that anyone in real trouble would know. If the caller cannot say it, hang up and call the relative directly on a number you already have.

### Other common patterns

- **Sweepstakes and lottery scams.** You are told you have won but must pay fees to claim.
- **Home repair and contractor fraud.** High-pressure offers, large deposits up front, work left unfinished.
- **Investment pitches.** Promises of high returns, pressure to move retirement funds, unregistered sellers.
- **Fake charities.** Especially after disasters.
- **Phishing emails and texts.** Links that lead to fake log-in pages.
- **Misuse by an agent.** A relative or caregiver who has access to accounts uses the money for themselves.

## Safeguards you can set up this week

Technology and paperwork can reduce risk without taking away independence.

### At the bank and brokerage

- [ ] Ask about a **trusted contact person** on each account. Many financial firms allow the customer to name someone the firm can reach if they suspect exploitation or cannot reach the customer. This person generally cannot make transactions.
- [ ] Ask about **view-only access** so a family member can see statements without moving money.
- [ ] Set up **transaction alerts** for large withdrawals, new payees and low balances, sent to the customer and, if desired, to a family member.
- [ ] Ask about **limits** on wire transfers or daily withdrawals.
- [ ] Ask whether the institution offers a **senior-specific account or fraud team.**
- [ ] Use **direct deposit** for income, and automatic payment for regular bills.

### On the phone and online

- [ ] Add numbers to the **National Do Not Call Registry**, and use call-blocking tools.
- [ ] Do not answer unknown numbers. Let voicemail take it.
- [ ] Use **strong, unique passwords** and a password manager.
- [ ] Turn on **two-factor authentication** for email and financial accounts, and make sure a trusted person can help if a code is needed.
- [ ] Place a **credit freeze** with the three credit bureaus. It is free.
- [ ] Never give remote access to a computer to someone who called you.
- [ ] Opt out of pre-approved credit offers.

### At home

- [ ] Use a **locked mailbox** or have mail forwarded to a family member for sorting.
- [ ] Keep checkbooks, cards and important papers locked up.
- [ ] Keep a **list of every caregiver and service provider** who enters the home.
- [ ] Invite family and friends to visit often and at different times.

### In legal documents

- [ ] A **durable power of attorney** can be a safeguard, because the agent has a legal duty to act for the principal. It can also be a risk if the wrong person is chosen. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).
- [ ] Ask about naming **two agents who must act together**, or an agent with a **co-agent or monitor** who receives account statements.
- [ ] Add duties such as keeping records and giving yearly accountings.
- [ ] Consider a **trust** with an independent trustee for larger assets. See [choosing a trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee).

## Choosing honest agents

An agent, trustee or caregiver will have access to money and trust. Questions to ask about any candidate:

- Do they treat money and records carefully, and keep personal and shared funds separate?
- Are they open about their own finances and willing to share records?
- Do they have debts, gambling, addiction or legal trouble that create pressure?
- Do they respect the person's wishes and encourage independence?
- Would they accept a second person reviewing the accounts?

Ask a prospective paid caregiver for references and a background check through a licensed agency. Do not give a caregiver access to bank cards or accounts. Choose an agent who is not dependent on the person's money.

## If you suspect abuse

Take it seriously and act in steps that fit the situation.

**In an emergency, call 911.** This includes immediate danger, physical harm, threats or someone being held against their will.

**Otherwise:**

1. **Stay calm and respectful.** Shame often keeps people silent. Say, "A lot of smart people have been fooled by this."
2. **Stop any ongoing payment** if you are able and authorized. Call the bank right away. Fast action sometimes helps recover money, especially for wires.
3. **Preserve evidence.** Save texts, emails, receipts, letters and statements. Do not confront a suspect in a way that could cause harm or destroy evidence.
4. **Report to Adult Protective Services (APS).** APS exists in every state, though the name and process vary. You can find your local office through the national Eldercare Locator or your state's website. Reports can often be made anonymously.
5. **Report to the police.** A police report may help with banks and legal action.
6. **Report scams** to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and internet crimes to the FBI's IC3.
7. **Contact the long-term care ombudsman** if the person lives in a facility.
8. **Contact an attorney** about protective steps, such as revoking a power of attorney, freezing accounts, or pursuing recovery. Deadlines may apply.

## Printable family safety checklist

- [ ] We have a **family code word** for emergency calls.
- [ ] A trusted contact is named on each bank and brokerage account.
- [ ] Alerts go to at least two people.
- [ ] A credit freeze is in place.
- [ ] Do Not Call and call-blocking are set up.
- [ ] Passwords are managed, and two-factor access is working.
- [ ] A durable power of attorney and healthcare directive exist and have been reviewed in the last few years.
- [ ] We have discussed who is allowed to access accounts, and who is not.
- [ ] Mail is sorted by a trusted person.
- [ ] We know the number for **Adult Protective Services** in our county: ______________________
- [ ] A relative visits or calls on a regular schedule.
- [ ] The person has agreed to talk to family before any large purchase, loan or gift.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You suspect that an agent, caregiver or relative has misused funds or a power of attorney.
- A will, trust, deed or beneficiary form was changed in a way that seems out of character.
- Your relative has memory loss and decisions are being made without proper authority.
- You want to set up a power of attorney or trust with built-in safeguards.
- Large sums have been transferred and you want to understand recovery options and deadlines.
