**How to use this template.** Read Part 1 first, because whether a memo is legally binding depends on your state and on how your will is written. Then fill in Part 2 by hand or on a computer, sign and date it, and keep it with your will. Take a draft to your attorney so the will can point to it in the right way. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Part 1: How a memorandum works

Wills often say "I leave my tangible personal property to my children in equal shares." That works until the children want the same watch. A memorandum is a separate list that names who gets which item. Tangible personal property means things you can touch and move: jewelry, furniture, art, books, tools, clothing, collectibles, family photographs. It does not mean money, accounts, real estate or vehicles titled with a state agency. Those usually need different handling.

Why use one:

- You can change the list without redoing your will.
- The will stays short, and the list stays private until it is needed.
- Small gifts get recorded while your wishes are fresh, which cuts down on arguments.

The catch is legal force. Some states have a statute that lets a will refer to a separate signed list. The Uniform Probate Code has such a rule, and a number of states have adopted a version of it. Other states do not, and a list there may be treated as a wish rather than an order unless the will was written to incorporate it. Rules vary by state. In Illinois, for example, an attorney will normally look at how the will refers to the list before relying on it. Ask your attorney three things:

- Does my state enforce a separate memo?
- What words must my will use to refer to it?
- Does the memo need witnesses or a notary?

If the answer is that the memo is not binding, it is still worth writing. Executors and family usually follow it, and it shows what you wanted.

## Part 2: Your memorandum

Fill in this heading once.

- **Name of person making this memo:** ______________________
- **Date of my will it goes with:** ______________________
- **Date of this memo:** ______________________
- **City and state where I live:** ______________________

Sample opening paragraph: "I, [full name], make this memorandum to identify who should receive certain items of my tangible personal property under my will dated [date]. If I make a newer memorandum, the one with the latest date controls. Items not listed here pass as my will directs."

### Item list

Describe each item so a stranger could pick it out of a room. "Mom's gold ring" is weak. "Gold ring with a small blue sapphire, kept in the top drawer of my dresser" is strong.

### Gifts of whole categories

- **My books and papers go to:** ______________________
- **My tools and workshop go to:** ______________________
- **My clothing and personal effects go to:** ______________________
- **My photographs and albums go to:** ______________________ (consider a copy for each sibling, not one set)
- **Any item I list that I no longer own at my death:** gift fails; no replacement is owed.

### What to leave off the list

- **Items with a beneficiary form or title.** Vehicles, bank accounts and life insurance pass by title or form, not by this memo.
- **Items owned jointly with someone else.** You cannot give away their share.
- **Items worth a lot of money.** Rare art, valuable jewelry or a collection may belong in the will itself or in a trust, with an appraisal. Your attorney can advise.
- **Pets.** They need separate planning, since they are treated as property but need care.
- **Firearms.** Transfer rules differ by state and by type of firearm. Ask your attorney before naming a recipient.
- **Digital items.** Photos in the cloud and online accounts belong in a digital assets inventory.

### Wishes that are not gifts

You can add non-binding notes, kept clearly separate from the list.

- **If nobody wants an item, please:** donate / sell and split proceeds / ask the family first (circle one)
- **Items I would like kept in the family:** ______________________
- **Items I would like donated to (name of charity or museum):** ______________________

Sample wording: "These are requests rather than directions. I hope my family will treat them kindly."

## Part 3: Signing and keeping it

- [ ] Date the memo and sign it at the bottom, using the same name as on your will.
- [ ] Ask your attorney whether witnesses or a notary are needed in your state. Having two adult witnesses who are not named in the memo is a safe habit even where not required.
- [ ] Number the pages ("Page 2 of 3") and initial each one so nothing can be added later.
- [ ] Make one copy, and tell your executor where the original is.
- [ ] Keep it with your will, not in a safe deposit box that may be sealed after death.
- [ ] Review it every time something big changes: a move, a death, a divorce, a new grandchild, or a gift you made while alive.

Sample signature block:

- **Signed:** ______________________
- **Printed name:** ______________________
- **Date:** ______________________
- **Witness 1 name and date:** ______________________
- **Witness 2 name and date:** ______________________

## Common mistakes

1. **Vague descriptions.** "My jewelry" or "the good dishes" gives people room to disagree. Describe, or take photographs and attach them.
2. **Making gifts conditional.** "To Jo, if she visits more" invites a dispute. Keep gifts plain.
3. **Forgetting a backup.** If your first choice dies first, the item falls back to the will's general gift, often to everyone in shares.
4. **Writing more than one version.** Two undated lists cause fights. Date every one, destroy old versions and say which controls.
5. **Never telling anyone.** A short talk, like the one in the [family meeting worksheet](/free/family-meeting-worksheet), prevents surprise.
6. **Treating the memo as a safe place for secrets.** It may be read in court if there is a dispute. Do not write anything you would not want a judge to see.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are not sure whether your state enforces a separate list, or what your will needs to say about it.
- Your items are valuable, or include a collection, firearms, heirloom jewelry or art.
- Family members disagree about who should receive what, or about your capacity to decide.
- You have a blended family, and want to be sure items reach your children and not only your spouse.
- Your will predates a move, a marriage or a divorce.
