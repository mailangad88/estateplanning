**How to use this worksheet.** Print three copies of Part 2 and fill in one for each community, on the tour and again from the written price sheet. Do Part 1 first so you know what to look for. Visit at least twice at different times, once unannounced if you can, such as at a mealtime. Compare in Part 5. Terms and licensing differ by state, and the name "assisted living" is not the same everywhere. For skilled nursing, see the [nursing home move checklist](/free/nursing-home-move-checklist). This worksheet is for assisted living and memory care. This is general information, not legal advice.

- **Medical needs (diabetes, wound care, oxygen, injections):** ______________________
- **What matters to them (privacy, garden, pets, faith, food, being near family):** ______________________
- **Likely changes in the next two years:** ______________________
- **Monthly budget, and how long it must last:** ______________________
- **Income and resources paying for it (Social Security, pension, savings, long-term care insurance, home sale, VA):** ______________________

Medicare generally does not pay for ongoing custodial care such as help with daily living in assisted living. Some Medicaid programs help in some states, often through a waiver with limited spots. Check with the state agency. Use the [Medicaid savings runway tool](/tools/medicaid-savings-runway) to see how long savings might last.

Two things to confirm in writing: how the community decides a resident's care level, and how often fees increase. Ask for the last three years of rate increases.

### Contract questions

- [ ] How is the care level assessed, and who decides? Can we see the assessment form?
- [ ] What happens if my parent runs out of money? Does the community accept Medicaid, and how long must someone pay privately first?
- [ ] Under what conditions can the community ask my parent to leave? How much notice is required?
- [ ] What is the refund policy if my parent leaves or dies? How fast is the refund paid?
- [ ] Who is the responsible party, and does the contract make family members guarantors? An adult child should read carefully before signing as a guarantor, since federal rules limit nursing homes asking this, and assisted living rules are state by state.
- [ ] Is there an arbitration clause, which would send disputes to private arbitration instead of court?
- [ ] What happens if care needs rise beyond what the community provides?
- [ ] Who pays for hospital or rehab stays, and is the room held?
- [ ] Is the resident's room a private lease, and are there tenant protections?
- [ ] Who can see my parent's records, and how are complaints handled?

### What to notice on a tour

- [ ] Are residents dressed, clean and engaged, or parked in front of a television?
- [ ] Do staff greet residents by name and speak kindly?
- [ ] Is there a smell of urine, or heavy air freshener?
- [ ] Are call lights answered quickly?
- [ ] Do residents seem to know each other?
- [ ] Is there outdoor space, and do people use it?
- [ ] Would my parent want to live here?

## Part 3: Check outside sources

- [ ] Search the state licensing agency's database for inspection reports and complaints. In Illinois, assisted living and shared housing establishments are licensed by the state's Department of Public Health under the Assisted Living and Shared Housing Act. Other states use different agencies.
- [ ] Call the local long-term care ombudsman. Ombudsmen advocate for residents and can tell you what they hear about a community.
- [ ] Ask current families how they feel, without the staff nearby.
- [ ] Read the full contract at home, not at the sales desk.

## Part 4: Move-in legal paperwork

- [ ] A power of attorney for health and for property is signed before capacity declines.
- [ ] The community has copies of the health care directive and any POLST form.
- [ ] A HIPAA release names the people who may hear updates.
- [ ] The person signing the contract knows whether they sign as agent or as an individual.
- [ ] Beneficiary designations, a will and a trust have been reviewed, and nobody has been added to bank accounts as a quick fix. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are asked to sign as a guarantor, or the contract has an arbitration clause or broad discharge terms you do not understand.
- Your parent may need Medicaid soon, and you need to plan how to pay without losing eligibility.
- Your parent has not signed powers of attorney, or may no longer have capacity.
- A spouse remains at home and needs protection from running out of money.
- You are thinking of selling the home or gifting assets to pay for care.
