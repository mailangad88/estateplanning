**How to use this guide.** Keep it next to a draft will and mark each gift with the label it earns. Most will problems come from a gift that fails and a will with no backup. Words and rules vary by state, and Illinois points are noted where they matter. This is general education, not legal advice.

## The four kinds of gift

| Type | What it is | Example | Common trouble |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specific bequest | One identified item | "My grandmother's ring to my niece Ana" | The item is sold before death |
| General bequest | A sum of money or a set amount, not tied to an item | "$10,000 to my brother" | Not enough cash left to pay it |
| Demonstrative bequest | A sum paid from a named source | "$5,000 from my Fifth Third savings account" | The account is closed |
| Residuary gift | Everything left after the other gifts, debts and costs | "The rest to my two children equally" | Left out entirely |

A real estate gift is often called a devise and a gift of personal property a bequest. Many wills use "bequest" for both.

## The residuary clause does the heavy lifting

The residuary clause names who gets everything not given away elsewhere. That includes gifts that fail and property you buy after signing.

Without one, leftover property can pass under the intestacy rules, as if you had no will for that part. In Illinois, see 755 ILCS 5/2-1 for the order of heirs, and the guide on what happens if you die without a will.

## When a gift fails: lapse and ademption

### Lapse: the beneficiary dies first

A gift to a person who dies before you is a lapsed gift. Without a rule to catch it, the gift falls into the residue, or if the residue also lapsed, to your heirs.

An anti-lapse statute is a state law that redirects some lapsed gifts, usually to the dead beneficiary's children. Illinois has one at 755 ILCS 5/4-11, aimed at a gift to a descendant of the testator, and it can be overridden by the will's own words. <!-- verify: current text and scope of 755 ILCS 5/4-11 -->

Other states differ in who is covered. Do not count on the statute. Write the backup yourself.

**Worked example.** Dana leaves $20,000 to her friend Rosa. Rosa dies two years before Dana. The will has no backup. The $20,000 does not go to Rosa's children. It falls into Dana's residue. If Dana had written "to Rosa, or if she does not survive me, to the Humane Society," her wish would hold.

### Ademption: the item is gone

Ademption happens when a specifically named item is not in the estate at death. The gift simply ends. The beneficiary usually does not get cash instead.

**Worked example.** Marcus leaves "my 2015 Honda" to his son. Marcus sells it and buys a new car. At death the Honda is gone, so the son's gift is adeemed. Better wording: "my car at the time of my death, or if none, $4,000."

### Abatement: not enough to go around

Abatement is the order in which gifts shrink when debts, taxes and costs use up the estate. A common default order is the residue first, then general gifts, then specific gifts. Your will can set a different order. If a certain person must be paid in full, say so.

## Per stirpes and per capita

These words decide how a share splits when a child has died.

| Method | How it works | Three children, one died leaving two kids |
|---|---|---|
| Per stirpes | Each family branch gets an equal share; a dead child's share goes down to that child's children | Two living children get one third each; the two grandchildren split one third |
| Per capita (equal shares among those alive) | Living people at the same level share equally | Pool is divided among the two living children only, or among all living descendants, depending on the version used |

Per capita has more than one version, so ask the attorney which one the will uses. Draw the family tree for your own case before you choose.

## Survival clauses and shared accidents

A survivorship clause says a beneficiary must outlive you by a set time to inherit. It avoids a gift passing through someone who survives you by hours and then through their will, and it helps when spouses die close together.

A common disaster clause deals with the same case: if two people die in one event, who is treated as dying first. Without a clause, state law applies. Illinois has a simultaneous death statute that generally treats each person as having survived the other for his or her own property when order cannot be shown. <!-- verify: Illinois Simultaneous Death Act citation and rule -->

| Situation | Likely effect with a survival clause | Likely effect without one |
|---|---|---|
| Spouses die in the same crash, order unknown | Each estate passes as if the other died first | State statute decides, rules differ |
| Spouse survives you by one day | Spouse must meet the survival period or the backup takes | Spouse inherits, then their will controls |

Ask your attorney what period is common in your state and whether it suits blended families and second marriages.

## The child born after the will

A child born or adopted after a will is signed, and not mentioned, is sometimes called a pretermitted heir. Many states give that child a share. In Illinois see 755 ILCS 5/4-10. <!-- verify: Illinois treatment of after-born children -->

Rather than rely on it, update the will after each birth, and say in the will whether future children are included.

## Grandchildren when a parent dies first

If your will gives a share to "my children" and one died before you, the answer depends on wording and state law. Grandchildren may take their parent's share by the anti-lapse statute, by a "my descendants per stirpes" phrase, or not at all. Write the instruction instead of letting a statute guess.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a specific item with no backup if it is sold
- No residuary clause, or one that names only one person
- Using "per stirpes" without understanding it
- Leaving large cash gifts that the estate cannot cover after debts
- Never updating after births, deaths or divorce

## When to talk to an attorney

- A gift would pass to a beneficiary who has a disability or receives benefits
- You are in a second marriage or have stepchildren
- You own a business or land that may be sold before death
- A beneficiary is a minor or lives abroad
- Spouses travel together and want a shared-accident plan
