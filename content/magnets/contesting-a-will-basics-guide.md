## How to use this guide

A will contest is a court case that asks a judge to decide whether a will, or part of it, should be set aside. The topic matters to two kinds of readers: heirs who think something went wrong, and people making a plan who want it to stand. Each has a section below. Rules differ by state, so this guide explains the shape of the issue, not the rule where you live. Use it to ask better questions of an attorney.

## What a contest is, and what it is not

A contest challenges the validity of the will itself. It is not the same as being unhappy with it. Courts generally respect a person's right to leave property as they choose, including leaving a child less than another or nothing. Disagreement about fairness is not a legal ground by itself.

A contest is also different from:

- A **claim against the estate**, such as a creditor's bill
- A **dispute over the executor's actions**, which is handled differently
- A **question about what a clause means**, which is a construction request
- A **challenge to a beneficiary designation or a trust**, which follows different rules

## Common grounds

| Ground | What it means | What helps show it |
|---|---|---|
| Lack of capacity | At signing, the person did not understand what they owned, who their family was, or what the will did | Medical records, witness accounts, how the person acted at the time |
| Undue influence | Someone pressured the person so that the will reflected the pressure instead of their wishes | A caregiver or relative who controlled access, got a big gift and helped arrange the will |
| Improper signing | The will was not signed or witnessed as the state requires | Witness testimony, the document itself |
| Fraud or forgery | The person was tricked about what they signed, or the signature is not theirs | Handwriting evidence, how the will came to be signed |
| Revoked or replaced | A later will or a deliberate act, such as destroying the will, ended the earlier one | The later document and proof of intent |

Learn the signing rules at [what makes a will valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid). Proof is the hard part, and suspicion is not proof.

## Who may challenge

Usually only an interested person can bring a contest, meaning someone who would gain if the will fell. That often includes a person named in an earlier will and an heir who would inherit under state law without a will. A person who gains nothing either way generally cannot. ## Deadlines

Time limits are one of the most common reasons contests fail. In many states the window opens when the will is admitted to probate and can be short, sometimes a few months. Some states require notice to heirs, and the clock may start from that notice. Rules vary and exceptions exist. Do not guess.

- [ ] Find out whether a probate case has been opened and when the will was admitted
- [ ] Ask the court clerk or an attorney what the deadline is in that state and county
- [ ] Write down the date you learned of the death and of the will
- [ ] Do not wait to gather evidence

- **Court and case number:** ____________________
- **Date will admitted:** ____________________
- **Contest deadline (confirmed by whom):** ____________________

If you are in Illinois, the Probate Act of 1975 sets the rules for contesting a will. Confirm the current deadline with the clerk or an attorney licensed there.

## Evidence an attorney will ask about

- [ ] The will and any earlier wills or drafts
- [ ] Medical and care records near the signing date
- [ ] Names of the witnesses and the lawyer who prepared the will, if any
- [ ] Who was present when it was signed
- [ ] Who took the person to appointments or controlled their money
- [ ] Letters, emails and notes that show the person's wishes
- [ ] Changes in the will that seem out of character

## Costs, risks and alternatives

Contests are expensive, slow and can damage family relationships. Public court files can reveal private matters. A contest may fail even when feelings are strong, and a no-contest clause may put a gift at risk. Ask these before you start:

1. What is the realistic chance, and what does the evidence show?
2. What would it cost, and who pays?
3. How long might it take?
4. Could mediation or a settlement give a similar result? See [when heirs disagree](/free/when-heirs-disagree-guide).

## No-contest clauses

A no-contest clause, sometimes called an in terrorem clause, says that a beneficiary who challenges the will loses their gift. Some states enforce them, some enforce them only in part, and some enforce them only when there was no good cause to challenge. These clauses only matter to someone who has something to lose. A person left nothing has nothing at risk.

## Making your own plan harder to challenge

You cannot stop anyone from filing a case. You can make a challenge less likely and less likely to succeed.

- [ ] Use an attorney who can supervise the signing and keep the file
- [ ] Follow your state's signing and witness rules exactly
- [ ] Choose witnesses who do not benefit under the will
- [ ] Keep the original in a safe place and record where it is
- [ ] If capacity could be questioned because of age or illness, ask the attorney about documenting your understanding at signing, for example with a note from a doctor
- [ ] Do not let anyone who benefits from the will be present when you discuss it with your attorney
- [ ] Explain unequal gifts in a letter or a family meeting. See the [family meeting worksheet](/free/family-meeting-worksheet)
- [ ] Ask whether a [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained) fits, since trusts follow different challenge rules, although they can be challenged too
- [ ] Consider a no-contest clause only after asking how your state treats it
- [ ] Update your plan after major life events. See [updating your estate plan](/guides/updating-your-estate-plan)

## When to talk to an attorney

- You think a will was signed under pressure or when the person lacked capacity
- A deadline to file may be close
- You were left out of a new will or your share was cut
- You are making a plan that treats family members unequally
- The person making the will has dementia or a serious illness
- You have been named in a will with a no-contest clause
