## How to use this guide

A deed is the document that records who owns your home. The way names appear on it often matters more than what your will says. Read this before closing if you can, since changing title later may cost more. This guide is educational. Terms, rules and taxes vary by state, so ask an attorney licensed where the home sits.

## Common ways to hold title

| Title type | Who it fits | At death |
|---|---|---|
| Sole ownership | One owner | The home goes by will or intestacy, usually through probate |
| Joint tenancy with right of survivorship | Two or more owners, often unmarried or family | The survivor takes the share, outside of probate |
| Tenancy by the entirety (in states that offer it) | Married couples only | The survivor takes the home, and creditors of just one spouse may have limited reach |
| Tenants in common | Co-owners, often unrelated | Each owner's share passes by will or intestacy |
| Community property (in states that use it), sometimes with survivorship | Married couples | Depends on the form used and on state law |
| Revocable living trust | Anyone | The trustee follows the trust terms, without probate |

The names of these forms differ, and some are not available everywhere. Your closing agent or attorney can tell you which your state recognizes.

## What happens at death under each form

### Sole ownership

The home is a probate asset unless something else moves it. Your executor handles the process. Read [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works) to see why people look for ways around it.

### Joint tenancy with survivorship

When one owner dies, the other becomes the full owner, usually after recording a death certificate. The will does not control the home. That can be convenient for a couple, but it is a problem if you meant the home to go to your children from an earlier marriage.

### Tenancy by the entirety

This is a form used only by married couples in some states. It generally passes to the survivor and may protect the home from a debt owed by only one spouse. Rules differ widely.

### Tenants in common

Each owner holds a share that does not pass automatically. If you die without a will, your share goes to your heirs, not necessarily to your co-owner. Co-owners who are unmarried partners or friends should discuss this before closing.

### Community property

In a community property state, a home bought during marriage is often treated as owned by both spouses. How it passes depends on how title is written and what state law says. Many couples add survivorship language.

## Transfer-on-death deeds

Many states allow a transfer-on-death (TOD) deed, sometimes called a beneficiary deed. You sign and record it while alive, naming who gets the home at your death. You keep ownership and control, and you can usually change your mind.

Points to check in your state:

- [ ] Does the state allow TOD deeds?
- [ ] What must be recorded, and when?
- [ ] What happens if the named person dies first?
- [ ] How does it interact with a mortgage, Medicaid planning or a divorce?
- [ ] What happens if you later put the home in a trust?

A TOD deed passes the home outside probate, but it does not handle incapacity, and a mistake in the wording can create a title problem. See [transfer-on-death deed versus a trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust).

## Putting a home in a trust

A revocable living trust can own your home. You usually stay in charge as trustee, and the home passes to your chosen people without probate. This can also help with incapacity, because a successor trustee can manage the property.

Questions to ask:

- Will the trust be funded by a new deed? (A trust that is not funded with the deed does not hold the home.)
- Will your lender need to be notified? Federal rules often protect transfers into a revocable trust for a home you live in, but ask your lender and attorney.
- Does your homeowner's insurer need to add the trust as an insured?
- Will a title insurance policy continue to cover you?
- Does your state reassess property taxes on a transfer to a trust? Many states exclude transfers to a revocable trust, but rules vary.

See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) for the full process.

## Mortgage and insurance considerations

- **Mortgage:** Most mortgages have a due-on-sale clause. Some transfers are protected by law, and others are not. Always ask the lender before you change names on a deed.
- **Co-borrowers:** Being on the loan and being on the deed are separate things. A person can owe the debt without owning the home, and the reverse.
- **Homeowner's insurance:** The named insured should match the owners. A mismatch can create trouble at a claim.
- **Title insurance:** Ask whether a deed change could affect coverage.
- **Life insurance or mortgage protection:** Many families look at coverage that would pay off the loan if an owner dies.
- **Property tax exemptions:** Homestead and senior exemptions may be tied to the owner's name.

## Common deed mistakes

- **Adding a child to the deed.** This is a frequent shortcut, and it can backfire. The child becomes a co-owner immediately. A child's divorce, debt, lawsuit or death may affect the home. You may also create gift tax filing issues, and the child may lose the tax basis step-up that heirs usually receive when property passes at death. Ask an attorney before doing this.
- **Adding a new partner or friend without an agreement.** That person becomes an owner, and you may not be able to sell without them.
- **Signing a quitclaim deed from a template.** Forms differ by state and may omit legal wording.
- **Forgetting to record.** A deed that is signed but not recorded may not protect the new owner.
- **Never funding the trust.** The trust exists, but the home is still in your name.
- **Assuming the will controls.** Joint title and TOD deeds can pass the home regardless of the will.
- **Ignoring a former spouse on title.** A divorce decree does not always change the deed.

## A closing-day checklist

- [ ] Ask who will appear on the deed and why
- [ ] Ask what form of ownership the deed uses
- [ ] Ask what happens to the home if one owner dies, with and without a will
- [ ] Ask whether a trust or TOD deed is available
- [ ] Confirm the mortgage lender's rules on later transfers
- [ ] Confirm the homeowner's insurance names every owner
- [ ] Store the recorded deed and title policy with your other estate documents

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are buying with a partner, a friend or a relative
- You have children from a prior relationship
- You own property in more than one state
- You are thinking about adding a child, moving the home to a trust or recording a TOD deed
- A family member may need Medicaid or long-term care in the future
- You are not sure whether your state offers survivorship forms
