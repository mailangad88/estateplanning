**How to use this guide.** Work through the timeline, fill in the blanks, and bring the pages to your installation legal assistance office or a civilian attorney. Benefit rules change, so check current rules with official sources. Law varies by state, and the state you claim as home, the state you are stationed in and the state where family lives can all matter.

## Why deployment changes the planning

A deployment adds three problems at once. You may be hard to reach. Someone at home may need legal authority to act for you. And the risks that planning exists for are on your mind. A plan that works when everyone is present may fail when one spouse is thousands of miles away with limited communication.

The good news: most of what is needed is paperwork, and military families often have free help available.

## Free help: military legal assistance

Each branch has legal assistance offices at most installations. Services generally include wills, powers of attorney, advance health care directives and notary services, usually at no cost to eligible service members and often to dependents. Hours and eligibility vary by location, and appointments can fill up before a mobilization, so book early. A legal assistance attorney may be licensed in a different state than the one you consider home, which is a fair question to ask. For complex matters such as trusts, a special needs plan, or a business, they may refer you to a civilian attorney.

## Deployment readiness timeline

| When | Task |
|---|---|
| **90 or more days before** | Gather documents. Book a legal assistance appointment. Talk with your spouse or partner about guardians, money and decisions. |
| **60 days before** | Sign a will and powers of attorney. Review beneficiary forms for life insurance, retirement and bank accounts. |
| **45 days before** | Finish the family care plan if you are a single parent or both parents serve. Brief the people named. |
| **30 days before** | Make copies and give them to the agents and caregivers. Set up online access for the spouse. Arrange pet and home care. |
| **14 days before** | Run a "what if" drill with your family: who they call, where papers are, how bills get paid. |
| **Before you leave** | Confirm contact methods and a way to reach your unit's family readiness support. |
| **After you return** | Review and update. Revoke special powers of attorney that are no longer needed. |

## Core documents

### Will

A will names who receives your property, who serves as executor, and who is guardian for minor children. Some states have special rules for wills signed by service members, and some recognize military testamentary documents. Many service members do not have a will beyond what the military provides on forms, so a signed will is worth the hour. A will does not control assets with a named beneficiary, such as life insurance and retirement accounts. See [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will).

### Powers of attorney

A power of attorney lets a trusted person act for you. Two types commonly come up:

- **General power of attorney.** Broad authority over finances, property and legal matters. It is powerful, so choose the agent carefully. It may expire on a date, on your return or on revocation.
- **Special (limited) power of attorney.** Authority for one task, such as selling a vehicle, signing a lease, handling a bank account or managing a household move. Many businesses prefer specific powers and will not honor a general one, so a series of special powers can work better in practice.

Ask the legal assistance office which kinds they draft, how long they last, and which businesses in your area accept them. Banks, car dealers and landlords may have their own preferences. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

## Beneficiary forms

Many service member benefits pass by form, not by will. Check each one.

- **Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance (SGLI).** The beneficiary is named on a military form, and the form controls who gets the benefit. Check current rules with your personnel office or the Department of Veterans Affairs on who may be named, the available coverage amounts, and special rules for spouses and minors. Naming a minor child directly can cause delays or court involvement, so ask about alternatives.
- **Death gratuity and other benefits.** These often follow a separate form. Ask your personnel office.
- **Thrift Savings Plan and other retirement accounts.** Each has its own beneficiary form.
- **Civilian accounts, bank accounts and vehicles.** Check each one, and consider payable on death designations. See [transfer on death and payable on death](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death).

Review all forms after marriage, divorce, birth, adoption or a death in the family. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) and the [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).

## Family care plans

Many branches require a written family care plan for single parents, dual-military couples and others with dependents who cannot care for themselves. The plan names who will care for the dependents if the service member is deployed or otherwise unavailable. Check your branch's regulations and your unit's requirement.

A strong plan covers:

- [ ] **A short-term caregiver** who can arrive within hours.
- [ ] **A long-term caregiver** if the absence lasts months.
- [ ] **A backup** for each.
- [ ] **Medical authority** for the caregiver to approve care and to access school records.
- [ ] **Money:** a way to pay for the child's care, such as an allotment or a joint account.
- [ ] **A travel and documents folder:** birth certificates, passports, insurance cards, immunization records, school contact details.
- [ ] **A conversation** with the caregiver about routines, schools, medicines and discipline.
- [ ] **Transportation** if the caregiver lives elsewhere.

## Guardianship when both parents serve, or a single parent serves

A family care plan covers deployment. A guardianship nomination in a will covers death or long-term incapacity. They are different documents, and both matter.

- **Single parent.** The other legal parent generally has custody rights if you die, unless a court rules otherwise. If the other parent is not a safe option, talk to an attorney about how to document your wishes. Name a guardian and a backup in your will.
- **Both parents serve.** Name the same guardian in both wills, and a backup. If one parent is killed or injured, the other may be unavailable too. Consider whether the guardian lives near the children's school and family.
- **Out-of-state caregivers.** Check how your state treats caregiver authorizations and whether the guardian's state will recognize your documents.
- **Money.** A guardian needs funds. Consider a trust for the children and a trustee separate from the guardian. See [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children) and [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors).

## Digital and pet plans

- **Digital accounts.** List email, banking, phone, cloud storage, social media and subscription accounts. Share a way for the spouse or agent to get in, such as a password manager with emergency access. Do not put passwords in a will. See [digital assets estate planning](/guides/digital-assets-estate-planning) and the [digital assets inventory](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory).
- **Phone and email access.** Check whether the carrier or email provider allows a trusted contact.
- **Pets.** Name a caretaker, a backup, a vet and a budget. Include medications and routines. Some states allow pet trusts. Ask whether the caretaker will board or rehome pets and for how long.

## Survivor benefits in general terms

Military families may be eligible for several types of survivor support, which can include life insurance, a death gratuity, Survivor Benefit Plan annuities, Dependency and Indemnity Compensation, health coverage, housing and education benefits. Eligibility, amounts and deadlines depend on service, status and circumstances, and they change. Ask your personnel office and the Department of Veterans Affairs for current rules. Make sure your spouse knows who to call and where the papers are.

Put these in one folder or binder:

- [ ] Marriage certificate, children's birth certificates and Social Security cards
- [ ] DD Form 214 or service records, when available
- [ ] Orders and unit contacts
- [ ] Insurance policies and beneficiary forms
- [ ] Bank, retirement and loan statements

## Common mistakes

- Signing a general power of attorney without understanding how broad it is.
- Not checking that a bank or business will accept the document.
- Forgetting to update beneficiary forms after a life change.
- Assuming a family care plan replaces a will.
- Naming a guardian who has not agreed.
- Leaving the spouse with no access to accounts.
- Never revoking a power of attorney after the need ends.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have a child with special needs.
- You own a business or rental property.
- You have children from more than one relationship, or an unfinished divorce or custody matter.
- You own property in more than one state or hold assets abroad.
- You want a trust for your children or for a spouse.
- You have questions about which state's law governs your will.
