**How to use this guide.** Read the five sections, fill in the lines, and finish with the 90-day plan. You do not need to be wealthy for any of this. Plans at this age are about who is in charge, who is protected and what happens to the people and pets who depend on you. Rules vary by state, so treat the examples as general.

## 1. A first will, even with modest assets

A will names who inherits, who serves as executor and, if you have children under 18, who you want as guardian. Without one, state law decides, and the default may not match your wishes, especially if you are not married. See [what happens if you die without a will](/guides/what-happens-if-you-die-without-a-will).

- **Executor (first choice):** ____________________
- **Executor (backup):** ____________________
- **Guardian for minor children (first choice):** ____________________
- **Guardian (backup):** ____________________
- **Who gets what if I die with no spouse or children:** ____________________

Common mistake: signing an online template without witnesses or a notary where your state requires them. See [what makes a will valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid).

## 2. Beneficiary forms do more work than your will

Your 401(k), IRA, life insurance and many bank accounts pass by the form on file, not by your will. Millennials often have an old employer plan that still names a parent or an ex.

- [ ] Log in to each retirement account and read the named beneficiary
- [ ] Name a backup (contingent) beneficiary on every account
- [ ] If a child is a beneficiary, ask about a trust or custodian rather than naming the child directly
- [ ] Check group life insurance through work, which is often small and easy to forget
- [ ] Confirm your partner is named where you want them to be

Details are in [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

## 3. Student loans and other debt at death

Debt does not usually pass to your family as a personal obligation, but it is generally paid from your estate before heirs receive anything. Federal student loans are generally discharged when the borrower dies, with a death certificate sent to the loan servicer. Private lenders set their own rules, and a co-signer may still be responsible. Parent PLUS loans have their own treatment, so check the servicer.

Co-signers and joint borrowers are the people most exposed. If a spouse co-signed your private loan, term life insurance sized to that debt is worth pricing. The [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) gives a starting figure.

## 4. Digital life and pets

Your phone, email and cloud photos are often the real keys to your accounts. Write down where your password manager lives and who can open it. Many services let you name a legacy contact.

- **Password manager and how my agent gets in:** ____________________
- **Phone passcode held by:** ____________________
- **Accounts with money in them (payment apps, crypto, brokerage):** ____________________
- **Photos and files I want kept:** ____________________

Pets are property under the law, so a gift of "my dog" with no plan can leave a pet in a shelter. Name a caretaker, leave a small sum, and write down vet, food and medication details. Many states allow a pet trust. Start with the [digital assets inventory](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory) and, if you have animals, the [pet care plan](/free/pet-care-plan).

## 5. Partner rights, married or not

A spouse has default rights in most states. An unmarried partner generally has none: no automatic inheritance, no automatic say in medical care, and sometimes no right to stay in a shared home owned by you alone.

- [ ] Sign a health care power of attorney naming your partner
- [ ] Sign a property power of attorney in case you cannot manage money
- [ ] Add a HIPAA release so providers can talk to them
- [ ] Decide how the home is titled and whether that matches your wishes
- [ ] Name your partner on beneficiary forms if you intend them to inherit

See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and, for unmarried couples, the [unmarried couples planning guide](/free/unmarried-couples-planning-guide).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have children under 18 or are expecting a child.
- You own a home, a rental or a share of a business.
- You are unmarried and want your partner protected.
- You have a child, relative or partner with a disability or on public benefits.
- You have a side business, crypto or other assets that are hard to transfer.
- You own property or have moved between states.
