**How to use this guide.** Read it once through, then fill in the worksheet near the end. Nothing here has to be decided this week. Retirement account rules are technical, change over time, and depend on your own tax situation. Treat this as a map for your conversations with the plan and with a tax professional.

## Start by doing very little

You may be receiving calls, letters or emails about money you did not expect. The first and most useful step is usually to pause. An inherited retirement account is not an emergency, and the account company can hold it while you learn your options.

The most common costly mistake is to withdraw the whole balance quickly. Money taken out of a traditional IRA or 401(k) generally counts as taxable income in the year you take it. A large withdrawal can push you into a higher bracket than you would otherwise reach. Once cash is out, it generally cannot be put back into an inherited account.

Also avoid retitling the account in your own name or rolling it into your own IRA unless you are a surviving spouse who has chosen that path. A non-spouse beneficiary who does this can lose the ability to use inherited-account rules at all.

## First, gather the facts

Before choosing anything, collect these.

- **The death certificate.** The plan or account company will ask for certified copies.
- **The latest statement.** It shows the balance and whether the money is in a traditional or Roth account.
- **The beneficiary form.** The named beneficiary on file, not the will, generally decides who receives the account. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).
- **Your relationship to the person who died.** Spouse, child, sibling, friend, trust or charity. This sets your options.
- **Whether the person had started required withdrawals.** Their age at death affects the yearly withdrawal rules for some beneficiaries.

## If you were the spouse

A surviving spouse generally has more choices than anyone else. Common options, in general terms, include the following.

1. **Treat the account as your own.** You roll it into your own IRA, or name yourself the owner. Your own age and withdrawal timeline then apply. This often fits a younger spouse who does not need the money soon.
2. **Keep it as an inherited account.** You stay a beneficiary. Some surviving spouses choose this when they are younger than the age where penalty-free withdrawals usually begin and want access now.
3. **Take a lump sum.** Possible, but taxable if the account is traditional.
4. **Disclaim part or all of it.** See below.

The right choice turns on your age, your other income, whether you need cash now, and whether you plan to remarry or move. A tax professional can compare the paths with real numbers.

## If you were not the spouse

Non-spouse beneficiaries are treated differently. Under current federal rules, many non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited account by the end of the tenth year after the year of death. This comes from the SECURE Act and later changes. Confirm the details with a tax professional, because the rules are technical and have been updated.

Within that window, the timing is often yours to choose, though in some situations yearly withdrawals are also required. Depending on the account and the date of death, you might withdraw evenly, wait and take more later, or take a large amount in a low-income year. There is no single best plan. A common thought is to spread withdrawals across years in which your income is lower, rather than taking everything in year ten.

### Eligible designated beneficiaries

Some beneficiaries fall in a category federal rules call "eligible designated beneficiaries." Generally, these may use a longer payout period than ten years. In general terms the category includes:

- A surviving spouse.
- A minor child of the person who died (until reaching the age of majority, after which the ten-year clock generally begins).
- A beneficiary who is disabled or chronically ill under the tax definitions.
- A beneficiary not more than ten years younger than the person who died, such as a sibling or friend close in age.

If you think you fit one of these, ask the plan and a tax professional how to document it. The rules for what counts are specific.

### Trusts, estates and charities

If the account named a trust, an estate, or a charity, the rules change again. A trust can be a deliberate choice, for example to protect a young beneficiary or someone with special needs, but its terms must work with the tax rules. A charity pays no income tax on the money, which is why some people name charities for retirement accounts and leave other assets to family.

## Taxes in plain terms

How the withdrawals are taxed depends on the type of account.

| Account type | How inherited withdrawals are generally taxed |
|---|---|
| Traditional IRA or 401(k) | Taxed as ordinary income when you withdraw |
| Roth IRA or Roth 401(k) | Generally not taxed if the account met the five-year holding test, though a ten-year emptying rule can still apply |
| Inherited account with after-tax contributions | Part may be tax-free; the plan and tax return track this |

A few more points, again stated generally:

- Inherited accounts are usually not subject to the 10 percent early-withdrawal penalty that can apply to the original owner, but confirm for your situation.
- Withdrawals are not income you can earn extra tax credits against. They count in the year you take them and can affect things tied to income, such as some health insurance subsidies or Medicare premiums.
- Missing a required yearly withdrawal can trigger a penalty. If you miss one, a tax professional can often help correct it.
- A state may tax withdrawals differently from the federal government. Rules vary by state.

For other asset types, see [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes), since inherited retirement accounts do not get the same stepped-up basis that many other inherited assets receive.

## Roth versus traditional

A Roth account can feel like a gift, because qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free. Even so, many non-spouse beneficiaries must still empty it within ten years. Because withdrawals are untaxed, some people let the Roth money grow for the full ten years and take it out last. A traditional account asks for more planning, because every dollar adds to your taxable income.

If you inherited both, a common approach is to draw on the traditional account first in lower-income years and leave the Roth to grow. Whether that suits you depends on your own finances.

## Disclaiming

You are generally allowed to refuse an inheritance by signing a qualified disclaimer. If you do, you are treated as if you had died before the account owner, and the money passes to the next beneficiary on the form. You cannot choose who gets it, and you cannot accept any of the disclaimed amount first.

Disclaiming can make sense when you do not need the money and the next person in line would be a better fit, such as a child in a lower tax bracket. Strict deadlines apply, often nine months from the date of death, and rules vary by state. Do not withdraw anything before disclaiming. Ask an attorney before you sign.

Checklist before you act:

- [ ] I have a certified death certificate and a recent statement.
- [ ] I know whether the account is traditional or Roth.
- [ ] I have not retitled the account or rolled it into my own IRA (unless I am a spouse who chose to).
- [ ] I have asked the plan for its deadlines, in writing.
- [ ] I have a plan for taxes on any withdrawal before I make it.
- [ ] I have spoken with a tax professional about the ten-year window.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The account named a trust, an estate or more than one beneficiary, or the form is missing or unclear.
- You are considering a disclaimer, or a family member disagrees about who should receive the money.
- The account is large enough that taxes are a significant concern.
- The beneficiary is a minor, has a disability, or receives needs-based benefits.
- The original owner died with a pending divorce, a recent remarriage or a conflict between the will and the beneficiary form.
