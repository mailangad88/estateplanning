## How to use this guide

Illinois lets an owner sign a recorded document that names who receives a home at the owner's death, without probate for that home. The law calls it a transfer on death instrument (TODI). Read each section, fill in the worksheet and bring it to an attorney. Rules in other states differ, and this guide is education, not advice for your situation. The statute is the Illinois Residential Real Property Transfer on Death Instrument Act, 755 ILCS 27.

## What it does

While you are alive, a TODI changes almost nothing. The beneficiary has no ownership, and you can still sell, mortgage or lease the home. Your lender, your creditors and your lienholders are not affected, and neither is a beneficiary's eligibility for public assistance (755 ILCS 27/60). At your death, title passes to the beneficiary named in the recorded instrument.

It is not a will. The law treats it as a nontestamentary transfer, so it does not go through probate as a will (755 ILCS 27/30). It also does not need the beneficiary to accept it, receive a copy or pay anything (755 ILCS 27/50).

## Who it fits

The Act covers residential real estate, which it defines as:

- A building with one to four residential units
- A single condominium unit
- An agricultural tract of 40 acres or less with a single-family home

A larger farm, commercial building or land with several uses may not qualify. Ask before you assume.

| Often a good fit | Often worth a second look |
|---|---|
| One home, one or two clear beneficiaries | Many beneficiaries who may disagree |
| Owner wants to avoid probate for the home | A beneficiary who is a minor or has a disability |
| Owner expects to keep the home until death | The home is the main asset in a blended family |
| Simple estate with other items covered by beneficiary forms | Large debts, or a possible need for long-term care |

Beneficiaries of a TODI face the same claims, costs, and funeral expenses as the beneficiaries of a revocable trust. The Act apportions liability among properties by their net values (755 ILCS 27/85). So avoiding probate does not mean avoiding debts. See [transfer on death deed versus trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust).

## Signing: the requirements

An instrument that skips a requirement is void. The statute asks for the following (755 ILCS 27/40 and 27/45):

- [ ] It has the elements of a properly recordable deed, including a legal description and the owner's name
- [ ] It states that the transfer happens on the owner's death
- [ ] The owner signs, or someone signs in the owner's presence at the owner's direction
- [ ] At least two credible witnesses sign
- [ ] The witnesses attest that the owner signed in their presence, as a free and voluntary act, and that they believed the owner was of sound mind
- [ ] The owner and the witnesses acknowledge their signatures before a notary public

Do not use a beneficiary or a beneficiary's spouse as a witness. The statute can void that beneficiary's interest unless enough other credible witnesses attest. Choose adults with no stake in the property.

The capacity standard is the one for making a will. A person acting under a power of attorney cannot sign or revoke a TODI for the owner, even if the power of attorney is broad (755 ILCS 27/35). That makes timing important if capacity is in question.

## Recording before death

The instrument must be recorded in the county where the property sits before the owner dies. If it is not, it does not work. Plan to record promptly after signing.

- [ ] Ask the county recorder about its recording fee and format rules
- [ ] Record the original instrument, and keep the recorded copy
- [ ] Note the document number and recording date
- [ ] Tell the beneficiary where the copy is kept, even though notice is not required

## Naming beneficiaries

The Act lets you transfer to one or more beneficiaries in any form of ownership valid under Illinois law (755 ILCS 27/20). That includes shares, successive interests and limited interests. Because it is flexible, the wording matters. Questions to settle:

- **Primary beneficiary or beneficiaries:** ____________________
- **Shares, if more than one (for example, 50 and 50):** ____________________
- **Backup if a beneficiary dies first:** ____________________
- **Do any beneficiaries need a trust or a guardian for money (a minor, a person with a disability)?** ____________________

A beneficiary can disclaim, which means refuse the property, as the Disclaimer Under Nontestamentary Instrument Act allows (755 ILCS 27/80). Name a backup, and ask an attorney what happens if a beneficiary dies first or refuses the property.

## Joint owners

If you own the home with someone else, either owner can sign a TODI. When all joint owners sign, revoking it takes agreement from the survivors, but the last surviving owner can revoke alone. If fewer than all owners sign, the instrument takes effect only according to who dies last (755 ILCS 27/70). It does not change a joint tenancy. Ask an attorney how it should fit with your deed.

## Changing or revoking it

An Illinois TODI is revocable, even if the paper says it is not (755 ILCS 27/25). It can be revoked only by:

1. A later recorded TODI that expressly revokes it or conflicts with it, or
2. A recorded instrument that revokes it

The revoking paper must be signed, witnessed and notarized with the same formalities, dated after the first, and recorded before death. These do not work: writing "void" on the instrument, an unrecorded paper, or a line in your will (755 ILCS 27/55).

- [ ] Review the instrument after a marriage, divorce, birth, death or a sale
- [ ] Check it against your will and beneficiary forms
- [ ] Record any change before anything else

## After the owner dies

The beneficiary can record a notice of death affidavit with the county recorder. It lists the beneficiaries, the property, the recording information of the TODI and the owner's death date. Filing is optional, and title passes without it (755 ILCS 27/75). It is still the easiest way to show the title record has changed.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Property is not a one-to-four unit home, a condo or a small farm
- You own the home jointly or with a spouse and the title wording is unclear
- A beneficiary is a minor, has a disability or receives public benefits
- Medicaid or long-term care planning may be in your future
- Someone may question your capacity or pressure you to sign
- You want to know how a TODI compares with a revocable trust
