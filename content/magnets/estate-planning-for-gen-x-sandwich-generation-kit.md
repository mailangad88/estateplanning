## How to use this kit

If you are raising kids and helping a parent at the same time, you are probably the person everyone calls. You are also the person whose own plan is most likely to be out of date. This kit gives you a map of who depends on you, a single checklist, and ways to protect yourself so the system does not collapse if you get sick. It complements our [aging parent conversation kit](/free/aging-parent-conversation-kit) and [caregiver planning guide](/free/caregiver-planning-guide). Laws vary by state, so use this to prepare for an attorney.

## Step 1: Map who depends on you

| Person | What they need from me | Hours per week | Money per month | Backup if I cannot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Child 1 | | | | |
| Child 2 | | | | |
| Parent or parent-in-law 1 | | | | |
| Parent or parent-in-law 2 | | | | |
| Spouse or partner | | | | |
| Other (sibling, adult child with needs) | | | | |

- **Total hours per week I give to others:** ______________________
- **Total money per month I contribute:** ______________________
- **What I have stopped doing for myself:** ______________________

Look at the totals honestly. Many caregivers underestimate both. This number is the basis for conversations with siblings and for deciding what you can keep doing.

## Step 2: Your own plan first

You cannot hold up two generations if your own paperwork is empty. Check these first.

- [ ] Will with a guardian and backup for minor children
- [ ] Trust or trustee for children's money, not a lump sum at 18
- [ ] Financial power of attorney, naming a person who is not already overloaded
- [ ] Health care power of attorney and HIPAA release
- [ ] Life insurance sized for children and for any parent who depends on you. Try the [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs)
- [ ] Disability insurance, if your income supports others
- [ ] Beneficiary forms matched to the plan

If a parent depends on your income, say so in your documents. A parent who is receiving means-tested benefits can lose them after an outright gift or inheritance, so a trust may fit better. Ask an attorney.

## Step 3: Your parents' documents

| Document | Parent 1 | Parent 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Will or trust, current | | |
| Financial power of attorney, with an agent who is willing | | |
| Health care power of attorney and living will | | |
| HIPAA release naming me | | |
| Beneficiary forms up to date | | |

| Insurance (health, long-term care, life) | | |

If a parent has memory problems, move quickly. A parent can generally sign only while able to understand the document. See our [capacity and signing guide](/free/capacity-and-signing-guide).

## Step 4: Money limits

Helping a parent with money can quietly drain your own retirement and children's college fund. Decide on limits before a crisis.

- **Most I can give each month without hurting my own plan:** ______________________
- **What I will pay for (medical bills, home help, rent) and what I will not:** ______________________
- **How I will record the money I give, loan or spend:** ______________________
- **Whether I will ask to be repaid from my parent's estate:** ______________________

If you spend a parent's money on their care, keep receipts. If you are paid for care, a written personal care agreement can protect both of you. See [personal care agreements](/free/personal-care-agreement-guide). Gifts and transfers can affect Medicaid eligibility, so ask before moving a parent's money. See [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning).

## Step 5: Time limits and work

- [ ] Ask about family leave and flexible schedules at work. Rules and eligibility vary
- [ ] Learn what local agencies on aging, adult day programs and respite care exist
- [ ] Ask your parent's doctor about a care plan and who coordinates it
- [ ] Keep one shared calendar for appointments, school and medications
- [ ] Decide what you can pass to a paid helper, a sibling or a neighbor

## Step 6: Split the load with siblings

Brothers and sisters often disagree about who does what. A short meeting with a written outcome prevents resentment.

- **Who is the main contact for doctors:** ______________________
- **Who handles bills and paperwork:** ______________________
- **Who visits or provides daily care:** ______________________
- **Who pays what and who keeps receipts:** ______________________
- **Who lives far away and can do tasks by phone or online:** ______________________
- **How often we meet or update each other:** ______________________
- **How we will decide when we disagree:** ______________________

Our [family meeting worksheet](/free/family-meeting-worksheet) gives a structure.

## Step 7: What if you are the one who is hurt

If you are the caregiver and something happens, do others know what you do? Write a short "if I am out" sheet.

- **Who steps in for my children:** ______________________
- **Who steps in for my parent's care:** ______________________
- **Where parents' medications, appointments and contacts are:** ______________________
- **Passwords and bills I handle:** ______________________
- **Who has a copy of this sheet:** ______________________

Add the sheet to your [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list).

## Combined checklist

- [ ] My own will, powers of attorney and guardian are signed
- [ ] Each parent has documents, or a plan to sign them
- [ ] A list of accounts and bills for each parent exists
- [ ] Monthly spending limits are written
- [ ] Siblings have met and agreed who does what
- [ ] An "if I am out" sheet exists
- [ ] Insurance is reviewed
- [ ] A review date is set for six months from now

## When to talk to an attorney

- A parent needs long-term care and you are asking how to pay for it
- A parent has memory loss and no powers of attorney
- A parent wants to give you money or property, or add you to accounts
- Siblings disagree about a parent's care or money
- You support a parent or a child with special needs and want a trust
- You want your own documents reviewed with your children and your parents in mind
