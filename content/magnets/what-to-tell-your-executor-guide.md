**How to use this guide.** Naming an executor in a will is step one. Step two is making the job possible. Fill in the sections below, store the sheet where your executor can find it, and then have a short conversation. This is a briefing, not a legal document. Your will controls where it conflicts.

## Start with the conversation

Ask before you assume. Someone named as executor is allowed to say no, and a "yes" is better when it is informed. Sample wording:

> "I would like you to be my executor. That means handling my paperwork after I die: filing the will, paying bills from my estate, and distributing what I have left. It takes time, often many months. I will write down where everything is. Can you do it? If not, I will ask someone else and I will not be upset."

Then name a backup, and tell the backup too.

## Section 5: My wishes

- **Burial, cremation or other:** ______________________
- **Service preferences:** ______________________
- **Prepaid arrangements with:** ______________________
- **Organ donation status:** ______________________
- **Pets and who will care for them:** ______________________
- **Items with a specific recipient but no mention in the will:** ______________________

A will is not always read until days or weeks after death, so funeral wishes should be written elsewhere. Use the [letter of instruction outline](/checklists/letter-of-instruction-outline). Such a letter is generally not binding unless the law says so, so state anything binding in the will.

## Section 6: Digital access

Do not put passwords in your will. A will becomes a public court record in many states. Instead:

- [ ] Use a password manager and tell your executor where the master password is stored (for example, in a sealed envelope or safe).
- [ ] Turn on legacy contact or inactive account features, where offered.
- [ ] List devices, phone passcode and two-factor methods.
- [ ] List online accounts that handle money, such as banks, payment apps and crypto wallets.
- [ ] Note which email account receives resets for other accounts.

Rules about access to digital accounts vary by state and by company. See [digital assets inventory](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory).

## What to say about money

Executors face questions about fairness. If you plan to divide things unequally, or leave a gift to a friend, explain why in a letter or conversation. A short explanation reduces conflict. Sample wording:

> "I left more to Sam because he has been caring for me. I love you both equally. Please do not read it as anything else."

## Practical limits of the job

Your executor generally must follow the will and state law, not your verbal wishes. They may need court approval for some steps, and can usually be reimbursed for estate expenses. Many states permit a fee for the work. Read the [choosing an executor guide](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor) for the duties in more detail.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have not yet signed a will or named an executor.
- You want your executor to have access to a trust or business.
- You own property in more than one state.
- You expect disagreement among heirs.
- You are unsure whether your executor can serve if they live far away.
