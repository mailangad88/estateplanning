**How to use this checklist.** Gather your annuity contracts and the latest statements. Fill in one table per annuity, then work through the checks. Contract terms differ a great deal between companies and states, so the contract and the insurer's written answers outrank any general rule here.

## Why annuities need their own review

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company. It usually passes by the beneficiary form on file, not by your will. If the form is blank, out of date, or names an estate, the result can be a slower, more expensive path for your family. Annuities also carry tax and fee rules that bank accounts do not.

## Step 2: Check the beneficiary designation

- [ ] I have a copy of the current beneficiary form from the insurer, not just my memory of it.
- [ ] Primary beneficiaries are named with full legal names and percentages that add up to 100.
- [ ] A contingent beneficiary is named in case a primary dies first.
- [ ] No beneficiary is a minor child without a plan (see [Leaving Money to Minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors)).
- [ ] No beneficiary receives benefits who may lose eligibility for means-tested programs, unless a special needs trust is named.
- [ ] If I named a trust, the trust is current and the trustee has the insurer's required wording.
- [ ] The form matches my wishes after any marriage, divorce, birth or death.

Naming "my estate" can send the annuity through probate and may shorten the time heirs have to take the money out. Ask the insurer what the contract provides before choosing that.

## Step 3: Understand likely taxes for heirs

Rules differ by contract type, so confirm each point with a tax professional.

- Gains in a non-qualified annuity are generally taxed as ordinary income when withdrawn. Your heirs generally do not get a step-up in basis on those gains the way they might on stocks or a house.
- An annuity inside an IRA or plan follows retirement account rules for the heir, including payout deadlines. See [Do Retirement Accounts Go Through Probate?](/blog/do-retirement-accounts-go-through-probate).
- The IRS lists distributions to a beneficiary or estate after the owner's death as an exception to the 10 percent additional tax on early withdrawals. Ordinary income tax can still apply.
- A surviving spouse often has the option to continue the contract in their own name. Other beneficiaries usually do not.
- Larger estates may also face estate tax. The federal exemption for 2026 is $15,000,000 per person, and some states set their own lower limits.

## Step 4: Check fees and surrender charges

- [ ] I know the length of my surrender charge schedule and the current charge percentage.
- [ ] I know whether a death benefit is paid without surrender charges.
- [ ] I know the annual fees on any riders (income, long-term care, enhanced death benefit).
- [ ] I know the free withdrawal amount each year, if any.
- [ ] I have asked whether a 1035 exchange to a different annuity would restart a surrender period.

A rider that sounds helpful can lower the value passed to heirs. Some income riders pay a stream while you are alive and leave little afterward. Write down what each rider does at death.

## Step 5: Check the payout choice

Annuitizing (turning the balance into income payments) can change what heirs receive.

| Payout option | What heirs may get |
|---|---|
| Life only | Often nothing after the annuitant dies |
| Life with period certain | Payments for the remaining fixed period go to the beneficiary |
| Joint and survivor | Payments continue to the second person, often at a reduced amount |
| Lump sum | The remaining value, taxed as described above |

- [ ] If I annuitize, I have compared a payout that leaves something for family against income for me.
- [ ] Both spouses understand which option is chosen and why.

- [ ] A trusted person knows each contract exists and where the insurer's phone number is.
- [ ] The annuity appears on my asset list (see the [Asset and Account Inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory)).
- [ ] I will re-check the form after any major life event.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your beneficiary is a trust, a minor, a person with a disability or someone with creditor problems.
- An annuity is a large part of your estate, or you own several.
- You are divorced, remarried or have children from more than one relationship.
- You are considering naming your estate, or moving an annuity into a trust.
- The insurer's forms will not fit what you want to do.
