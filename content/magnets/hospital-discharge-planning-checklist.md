**How to use this checklist.** Discharge can be announced quickly, sometimes within a day. Use this list as soon as you hear that a parent may be leaving the hospital. Tick items as you go and write answers in the blanks. It is general education about Medicare in the US. Plans, state rules and hospitals differ, so confirm details with the discharge planner.

## 1. First, find out who can act

Hospitals deal with the patient first. If your parent can decide, they decide. If not, the hospital looks for a legal decision maker.

- [ ] Ask your parent whether a health care power of attorney exists, and where it is.
- [ ] Give the hospital a copy for the chart.
- [ ] If there is no agent, ask the hospital how it identifies a surrogate. Many states have a statute listing who may decide. In Illinois, the Health Care Surrogate Act lists a guardian, then a spouse, then adult children, and so on.
- [ ] Ask your parent to sign a HIPAA release naming you, if they can.
- [ ] Bring the power of attorney for property if bills or accounts need action.

- **Health care agent:** ______________________
- **Phone:** ______________________
- **Hospital patient advocate or social worker:** ______________________

## 2. Questions for the discharge planner

Ask these in a calm moment, and write the answers.

- [ ] What is the expected discharge date, and what could change it?
- [ ] What care will my parent need in the first two weeks: therapy, wound care, injections, oxygen, help with bathing?
- [ ] Is the care medical (skilled) or help with daily living (custodial)? The difference matters for coverage.
- [ ] Which options are realistic: inpatient rehab, a skilled nursing facility, home health, or home with family help?
- [ ] Can you give us a list of providers, and can we choose?
- [ ] Which medications are new, stopped or changed, and why?
- [ ] What warning signs should send us back to the doctor or emergency room?
- [ ] Who do we call after hours?
- [ ] What equipment is needed: walker, hospital bed, commode, ramp?

- **Discharge date given:** ______________________
- **Recommended setting:** ______________________
- **Reason:** ______________________

## 3. Inpatient or observation: ask early

A hospital stay can be an inpatient admission or "observation," which is outpatient status even if your parent sleeps in a bed. Medicare's coverage of a skilled nursing facility depends on a qualifying inpatient stay. Time spent in observation or the emergency room does not count toward it.

- [ ] Ask: "Is my parent admitted as an inpatient, or in observation?"
- [ ] Write down the date and time of each status.
- [ ] If the status looks wrong, ask the care team and the hospital's patient advocate to review it.

- **Status today:** ______________________
- **Date admitted as inpatient:** ______________________

## 4. What Original Medicare may pay for rehab

For 2026, Medicare says a skilled nursing facility stay may be covered after a medically necessary inpatient hospital stay of at least three days in a row. After that, coverage generally looks like this:

| Days in the facility | What the person pays |
|---|---|
| 1 to 20 | $0 daily, after the Part A deductible if not already met |
| 21 to 100 | $217 per day coinsurance |
| Day 101 and later | All costs |

Medicare covers skilled care that is medically needed, such as rehabilitation or skilled nursing. It does not generally pay for custodial long-term care, such as help with bathing and dressing when no skilled need exists. Some Medicare Advantage plans and some accountable care organizations handle the three-day rule differently, and Advantage plans may require approval first. Call the plan.

- [ ] Ask whether your parent has Original Medicare or an Advantage plan.
- [ ] Ask whether the plan needs to approve the facility before transfer.
- [ ] Ask whether the facility is Medicare certified and has a bed.
- [ ] Ask what the supplemental policy pays toward the daily coinsurance.

## 5. Compare the settings

| Option | Good fit if | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Inpatient rehab | Intensive therapy several hours a day is needed | How many hours daily? Who decides length of stay? |
| Skilled nursing facility | Daily skilled care and therapy, but not hospital level | Therapy hours, staffing, cost after coverage ends |
| Home health | Needs periodic nursing or therapy and has help at home | Who is at home overnight? How many visits per week? |
| Home with family or paid help | Needs help but not skilled care | Who pays? What if the caregiver becomes ill? |

## 6. Your right to ask questions

Hospitals must give discharge planning and notices of patient rights. If you disagree with the discharge, ask for the written notice and ask how to appeal. There are deadlines, often short, so ask the same day.

- [ ] Ask for the written notice of discharge rights.
- [ ] Ask who to call to request a review.
- [ ] Write down who you spoke to and when.

## 7. Paperwork to have ready

- [ ] Insurance cards: Medicare, supplement or Advantage, prescription plan
- [ ] Photo ID
- [ ] Medication list
- [ ] Power of attorney for health care and for property
- [ ] Contact list for doctors and pharmacy
- [ ] Veteran papers, if relevant
- [ ] A notebook with dates, names and instructions

## 8. The first 72 hours at the next place

- [ ] Confirm medications arrived and match the discharge list.
- [ ] Ask to attend the first care plan meeting.
- [ ] Ask for the therapy schedule and the goal for going home.
- [ ] Keep asking, every week, how long Medicare is expected to cover care.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your parent cannot decide and no one holds a signed power of attorney.
- Family members disagree about who decides.
- A facility asks you to sign personally for payment or to accept arbitration.
- Long-term care costs may continue and you want to plan around Medicaid.
- A decision about life-sustaining treatment is coming up.
