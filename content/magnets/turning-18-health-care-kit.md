**How to use this kit.** Read the first two sections yourself, then sit down with your young adult for the conversation in Part 3. The documents belong to them, not to you. They choose the agents, they sign, and they can change their minds. Rules vary by state, so have an attorney licensed in your state confirm the forms before anyone signs.

## What changes on the 18th birthday

In most states, a person is a legal adult at 18. The law then treats your child like any other adult, even if they still live at home, use your insurance and have never paid a bill. You stop being the default decision maker.

In practice, that can look like this:

- A doctor or hospital may decline to discuss a diagnosis, test results or treatment with you, because of privacy rules such as HIPAA.
- If your young adult is unconscious or unable to decide, hospitals often look for a person with legal authority, such as a named health care agent. A parent with no paperwork may be asked to wait, or may be treated as next of kin depending on state law. Rules vary by state.
- A college registrar, financial aid office or bursar may not release grades, billing or records to you without consent. FERPA is the federal privacy law for education records.
- Banks will not let you see or manage an account that is in your child's name alone.
- If a serious accident leaves your child unable to manage money or sign papers, a court-supervised guardianship may be the only route without documents. That takes time and money, and it is public.

None of this means something is likely to go wrong. It means a short set of signed papers can fill a gap that appears overnight.

## The four documents

| Document | What it does | Who is named | Takes effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Health care power of attorney** (also called health care proxy, agent or surrogate) | Lets someone make medical decisions if your young adult cannot | A health care agent, plus a backup | Usually only when a doctor finds they cannot decide |
| **HIPAA authorization** | Lets doctors and insurers share medical information with named people | Anyone your young adult lists | When signed, often in effect right away |
| **Financial (durable) power of attorney** | Lets someone pay bills, handle bank accounts, deal with the school and taxes | An agent, plus a backup | Right away, or only on incapacity, depending on how it is written |
| **Living will or advance directive** (optional) | Records wishes about life support and end of life care | Not needed, but many forms combine this with the proxy | When needed |

Some states combine the health care proxy and living will in one form. Some states offer a statutory form that is free to download. A few schools hand out their own forms, which may or may not meet your state's rules. See [healthcare directives and living wills](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) and [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) for background.

### Choices your young adult makes

- [ ] **Agent for health decisions.** A parent is a common choice. A sibling, partner or older relative also works. It is the young adult's decision.
- [ ] **Backup agent.** Name one in case the first is unreachable. Parents who are divorced often pick one parent as agent and the other as backup, or vice versa.
- [ ] **HIPAA list.** Name everyone who may receive medical information, including both parents, even if only one is the agent.
- [ ] **Financial agent powers.** Decide whether the agent can act right away (a "durable" power) or only if the young adult is incapacitated (a "springing" power). A springing power can be slow to prove, and rules vary by state.
- [ ] **Limits.** A young adult can limit the financial power to specific tasks, such as managing a single bank account and dealing with the school.
- [ ] **Organ donation and mental health wishes.** Some state forms ask about both. These are personal and worth a separate talk.

## Part 3: How to talk to your young adult

The framing matters. A young adult who hears "I want control" says no. A young adult who hears "I want to make sure someone can help you" often says yes. Try this script.

> "When you turn 18, the hospital will not be allowed to talk to me, even if you are hurt and I am on the way. I am not asking to be in charge of your life. These forms let you pick who can speak for you, and you can cancel them any time. Can we spend an hour this weekend going through them together?"

Tips for the conversation:

- Offer to share the forms with them in advance, so they are not asked to sign something on the spot.
- Let them choose. If they prefer a different person as agent, say thank you and respect it.
- Explain the difference between a health agent (medical choices) and a financial agent (money and paperwork).
- Say what you will not do. For example: "I will not read your records unless there is an emergency or you ask me to."
- Keep it short. If it turns into a debate, stop and try again in a week.

Write down what you agree on:

- **Who is the primary health care agent:** ______________________
- **Who is the backup:** ______________________
- **Who may receive medical information:** ______________________
- **Who is the financial agent:** ______________________
- **What the agent may do, and what they should not do without asking:** ______________________
- **Date we will meet to sign:** ______________________

## Part 4: Signing and storing the documents

Signing rules differ by state. Many states require two adult witnesses or a notary for a health care proxy, and some do not allow certain people (such as the agent) to act as a witness. A financial power of attorney often needs a notary. If the young adult goes to school in a different state, ask whether they should sign in the home state, the school state or both. Many states honor out-of-state documents that were valid where signed, but a hospital may still ask questions, which is why some families sign a second set.

After signing:

- [ ] Give each agent a copy, including the backup.
- [ ] Give a copy to the primary care doctor and the campus health center, if they will accept it.
- [ ] Keep the original in a fireproof place at home, not a safe deposit box that no one can open at night.
- [ ] Save a scan in a shared, password-protected folder that both the young adult and the agent can open.
- [ ] Put the health forms on the young adult's phone (see below).

### Storing copies on a phone

An emergency does not wait for you to find a file cabinet. A few simple steps help:

1. **Photo or PDF.** Save the signed health care proxy and HIPAA release in a "Medical" album or folder.
2. **Lock screen medical ID.** Most smartphones allow an emergency card that can be seen without unlocking. Add allergies, conditions, medications and emergency contacts.
3. **Share with the agent.** Send the PDF to the agent and ask them to save it too.
4. **Cloud copy.** Store a copy in a cloud folder that the agent can access, with a strong password and two-step sign-in.
5. **Keep it current.** If the agent changes, replace the file, and delete the old one.

## Studying abroad or away from home

- Check whether your insurance covers care outside the country, and whether it includes medical evacuation. Many plans do not.
- Bring a copy of the HIPAA release and health care proxy. Foreign hospitals may not honor a US form, but it still tells family who to call.
- Ask the program whether they require their own consent or emergency contact form.
- Confirm that the financial agent can pay bills from a home bank account while the student is away, and that the bank will accept the power of attorney. Some banks prefer their own form.
- Share time zones and the best way to reach each other in an emergency.
- Register with the home country's travel registration program if there is one.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting until the 18th birthday.** Some families sign in the weeks before, so the documents are ready the day the law changes. Check your state's rule on when an adult can sign, since the signing person must be an adult.
- **Naming only one parent for the HIPAA release.** Both parents often want access to information.
- **Not telling the agent.** The agent should know they are named and where the documents are.
- **Assuming the school form is enough.** A campus form may only cover that campus.
- **Never updating.** A new partner, a move or a falling out are all reasons to review.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your young adult has a disability, a chronic condition or needs ongoing care. Other planning, such as a supported decision-making arrangement or guardianship, may be worth discussing.
- Your young adult has significant assets, a trust or an inheritance.
- Parents are divorced, and there is disagreement about who should be the agent.
- Your young adult will live abroad for a long period.
- You are unsure whether your state's form meets the rules for out-of-state use.
