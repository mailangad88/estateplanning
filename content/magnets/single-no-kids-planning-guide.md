## How to use this guide

If you have no spouse or children, there is no default person who steps in. That makes planning more useful, not less. Read each section, fill in the lines and bring your answers to an attorney. This guide is educational, and rules vary by state.

## Who decides if you cannot

Without documents, your family may have no legal authority to act for you, and state rules for who is allowed vary. Often a court gets involved. Three documents prevent that gap:

- A **financial power of attorney** lets a person pay bills, manage accounts and deal with agencies if you are unable.
- A **health care power of attorney** (sometimes called a health care proxy) names who speaks with doctors for you.
- A **living will or directive** states your wishes about life support and other treatment.

If you have none of these, a family member may need to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator. That process can be slow, public and costly. Read more about [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and [health care directives](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).

## Choosing agents without a spouse or child

You can pick anyone you trust who is an adult and willing. Common choices are a sibling, an adult niece or nephew, a close friend, a cousin or a professional such as a trust company or fiduciary. Rules about who may serve vary by state.

Test each candidate with these questions:

- Do they live near me, or can they travel?
- Are they organized with money and paperwork?
- Can they stay calm and make choices I would stand behind?
- Do they know my wishes, and would they follow them even if they disagree?
- Are they likely to outlive me and be healthy in 20 years?

## Who inherits

With no spouse or kids, you have wide freedom. Common choices are siblings, nieces and nephews, friends, godchildren, a favorite teacher, a faith community or a charity. Many people split their estate among several of these.

Points to keep in mind:

- Percentages adjust as your assets change, while fixed dollar amounts may leave later gifts underfunded. Many people use percentages for the bulk and fixed gifts for specific people.
- A gift to a young niece or nephew generally should not go straight to them. Ask about a trust or custodian arrangement (see [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors)).
- Friends and charities can be named in a will, on beneficiary forms and on transfer-on-death or payable-on-death accounts.
- If you name a charity, use its exact legal name and tax ID number to avoid confusion.
- If anyone you name receives government benefits, an inheritance may affect eligibility. Ask an attorney about a special needs trust.

## Beneficiary forms control the biggest assets

Retirement accounts and life insurance pay whoever is on the form, not whoever is in your will. Many singles still have a parent, an old partner or nobody listed. If nobody is named, the money may go to your estate and through probate.

- [ ] List every account with a beneficiary form
- [ ] Name a primary and a backup on each
- [ ] Confirm names are spelled exactly as they appear on ID
- [ ] Check again after major life changes

Use the [beneficiary designation guide](/guides/beneficiary-designations) for the full list.

## What intestacy might do

If you die without a valid will, your state's intestacy law decides who inherits. For a single person without children, the order often runs to parents first, then siblings and their descendants, then more distant relatives. If no relatives are found, property may go to the state. The order and the shares vary widely.

That default may not match what you want. A close friend, a partner you never married or a charity would typically get nothing. A sibling you rarely speak with might. The guide on [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) walks through this in more depth.

Ask yourself:

- **If state law decided, who would get my property?** ______________________
- **Is that who I want?** ______________________

## Pets

Pets are property under the law, and most states do not allow you to leave money directly to an animal. Many states do allow a pet trust. Others may allow a gift to a caretaker with a stated wish.

- [ ] Name a primary caretaker and a backup, and ask them first
- [ ] Decide how much money to leave for care, and who controls it
- [ ] Write down veterinarian name, diet, medications, habits and microchip number
- [ ] Add a line to your will or trust about the pet
- [ ] Put an emergency note in your wallet and on your fridge

- **Pet caretaker:** ______________________
- **Backup caretaker:** ______________________
- **Funds set aside for care:** ______________________

## Digital life

If you live alone, no one else may know how to get into your phone, email or cloud accounts. Many companies have their own rules about who may access an account after death, and federal and state law can limit access.

- [ ] List email, banking, social media, cloud storage and subscription accounts
- [ ] Set up a password manager and tell your agent where the master access sits
- [ ] Use legacy contact or inactive account features where the service has them
- [ ] Decide what you want deleted, preserved or shared
- [ ] Keep a short list of recurring subscriptions so bills can be cancelled

See [digital assets and estate planning](/learn/digital-assets/digital-assets-in-your-estate-plan).

## A starter plan

Many single adults without kids put these pieces in place:

- [ ] A will naming an executor and your beneficiaries
- [ ] Financial and health care powers of attorney, with backups
- [ ] A living will or directive
- [ ] Updated beneficiary forms
- [ ] A letter of instruction listing accounts, contacts and wishes
- [ ] A review every few years or after big changes

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own a home, a business or property in more than one state
- You want to leave money to a minor, a person with a disability or a charity in a structured way
- You want a pet trust or need help with one in your state
- You expect a family member to challenge your choices
- You want a trust to avoid probate or keep gifts private
- You want help choosing between a person and a professional as agent
