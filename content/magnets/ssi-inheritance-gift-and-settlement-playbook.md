## How to use this playbook

Money that lands in the wrong hands can pause SSI and Medicaid within weeks. This playbook shows the order of steps, who should act and what to write down. It is general education. Rules vary by state and the details turn on the person's benefits, so a call to an attorney before the money moves is worth making.

## What happens if the money arrives outright

SSI is limited by a resource cap (see /free/ssi-rules-cheat-sheet). An inheritance is generally income in the month received and a resource after that. A large check can mean no SSI for the month and a resource problem after. Medicaid usually follows, since eligibility is often tied to SSI or has its own asset test. <!-- verify: SSI treatment of inheritance as income then resource -->

There is no safe amount that an SSI recipient can simply "inherit." Small amounts may fit under the limit, but any money held in the person's name is counted. The question is how much room they have left in the month.

## The reporting clock

An SSI recipient or representative payee must report an inheritance. Social Security's rule is to report changes promptly, generally by the 10th day of the month after the change. <!-- verify: SSI reporting deadline and method --> Late reports can create an overpayment that SSA asks the person to repay, and can lead to penalties.

- [ ] Write the date the person first learned of the inheritance
- [ ] Write the date funds arrive
- [ ] Report in writing, keep a copy, ask for a receipt
- [ ] Keep notes on any call: date, name, what was said

## If SSI already overpaid

SSA will send a notice with the amount and reason. Options usually include paying it back, asking for a waiver, or requesting reconsideration. Deadlines are short. <!-- verify: appeal and waiver deadlines --> Do not ignore the letter. Ask for help from an attorney or a local disability rights group.

## Decision table: how should the money be held?

| Situation | Common route | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Relative leaves money in a will to the person | Redirect to a third-party special needs trust, if the will allows | If the will names the person outright, the money may go to them |
| Relative has not yet died | Ask them to change the will or beneficiary form | See /guides/beneficiary-designations |
| Person is already on SSI and has a small inheritance | Spend on non-resource items, or use an ABLE account within limits | Check the annual limit and timing |
| Gift of cash from a friend | Gifts of cash can count as income | Ask the giver to pay a vendor instead |
| Lawsuit settlement | A first-party special needs trust or pooled trust, set up under rules for the person's age | Under 65 at set-up is a key rule <!-- verify: age 65 rule --> |
| Person is over 65 and receives a settlement | A pooled trust may be an option, depending on state | A state-specific question |
| Money came from the person's own funds | First-party trust with Medicaid payback | Different from a trust funded by others |

## Gifts

A gift of cash to a person on SSI generally counts as income in the month it arrives. Gifts of things the person needs, paid to a store or provider, are usually treated differently. A gift of shelter, such as paying rent, can reduce SSI under the in-kind rules. See /free/special-needs-trust-spending-guide.

Give relatives a short script:

> Thank you for wanting to help. Please do not give money directly. If you want to help, we can send you a short form to give to the trust or pay a bill directly.

## A special needs trust can hold an inheritance

A properly drafted **third-party** special needs trust can be named in a will or beneficiary form. The inheritance goes into the trust, not to the person. The trustee then pays for supplemental needs. If the person already inherited, a **first-party** trust may still protect the money if it is set up in time, but it must repay the state Medicaid agency at death. Ask an attorney which route fits.

## Small inheritances

Small does not mean safe. A small amount might fit under the limit one month and cause trouble the next. Families sometimes choose to spend the money in ways SSA does not count, such as paying for needed items or prepaying a funeral. Some use an ABLE account. See /free/able-account-planner. Each choice has limits.

## Step flow before the check arrives

1. Find out who is handing out the money and when
2. Tell no one to wire or write a check to the person yet
3. Confirm what benefits the person receives (SSI, SSDI, Medicaid, housing aid)
4. Ask an attorney about a trust, an ABLE account or spending
5. Report to SSA and the state Medicaid agency in writing
6. Keep receipts and notes for every dollar spent

## When to talk to an attorney

- A will or beneficiary form leaves money outright to someone on SSI
- A settlement is pending
- You received an overpayment notice
- You are unsure whether to use a trust or an ABLE account
- Medicaid has sent a notice about assets
