## How to use these letters

Beneficiaries often feel left in the dark, and silence can turn grief into suspicion. Regular, plain letters protect relationships. Fill in the brackets, adjust the tone to the family, and keep a copy. Rules vary by state about what notice must be given and when, so check your state's requirements before you rely on a template. Copies of these letters can also be useful if questions come up later.

Send the same news to all beneficiaries at the same time. That avoids a sense that someone got a head start.

## What beneficiaries usually want to know

- [ ] That the person who died has been properly honored and the estate is being handled
- [ ] Who is in charge and how to reach them
- [ ] What the will or trust says about them, at least as it affects them
- [ ] A rough timeline, with a note that things take longer than people hope
- [ ] Whether anything is needed from them

Be careful not to promise amounts or dates you cannot control. Say "I expect" and "I will update you if that changes".

## Letter 1: Initial notice

> [Date]
>
> Dear [name],
>
> I am writing to let you know that I have been named [executor / successor trustee] of [full name]'s [estate / trust]. [He / She / They] died on [date]. I am sorry for your loss, and I will keep you informed.
>
> You are named as a beneficiary. [A copy of the will or trust, or the portion that applies to you, is enclosed / I will send it once it is filed with the court.] I have contacted [an attorney, if applicable: name] to help with the legal steps.
>
> What happens next: I will gather the assets, notify creditors, pay what is owed and then distribute what remains according to the document. This often takes many months. I will send updates about every [three] months, or sooner if something significant happens.
>
> There is nothing you need to do now. Please keep your address and email current with me.
>
> My contact information: [phone, email, mailing address]
>
> Sincerely,
> [Name]

Note what you cannot share. Some states require beneficiaries to be given a copy of the will or notice of the proceedings. Your attorney can tell you what applies.

## Letter 2: Status update

Send this on a regular schedule. Even "no change" is useful news.

> [Date]
>
> Dear [name],
>
> This is an update on the [estate / trust] of [full name].
>
> **Done since my last letter:**
> - [Example: Opened an estate account and collected the bank balances]
> - [Example: Sent notice to known creditors]
> - [Example: Filed the inventory with the court]
>
> **Still in progress:**
> - [Example: Selling the house, listed on (date)]
> - [Example: Waiting for the claims period to end on (date)]
> - [Example: Preparing the final income tax return]
>
> **Expected next steps and timing:** [Describe in one or two lines. Use "I expect" rather than a promise.]
>
> **Questions or concerns:** Please reply to me directly.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Name]

Include in your own file a short list of what has been paid out of the estate, such as funeral costs, taxes and legal fees. Many states require an accounting. Be ready to share it if asked.

## Letter 3: Distribution letter

Before sending, confirm that debts, taxes and expenses are paid or reserved, that any required waiting period has passed and that the court or your attorney has signed off if needed. Distributing too early can leave you personally responsible if a bill turns up.

> [Date]
>
> Dear [name],
>
> I am pleased to tell you that the [estate / trust] is ready to make its [partial / final] distribution.
>
> **Your share:** [description of the asset or amount]
> **Delivered by:** [check / transfer / transfer of title / hand delivery]
> **Expected on or about:** [date]
>
> Enclosed is a summary of how the amount was reached, including [income, expenses and any advances]. Please review it and let me know about any questions before [date].
>
> Please sign and return the enclosed receipt. If you would like to talk it over, call me.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Name]

Tax note: inherited assets may have special tax treatment, and some retirement accounts have timing rules. Suggest that beneficiaries talk to their own tax adviser.

## Letter 4: Receipt and release form

A receipt shows that a beneficiary received what the letter says. A release goes further and says the beneficiary will not make a claim. Do not pressure anyone to sign a release. Some families use only a receipt, and some states or attorneys use a release only after an accounting.

> **Receipt**
>
> I, [name], acknowledge that I received [description of asset or amount] on [date] from [executor / trustee] of the [estate / trust] of [full name].
>
> Signature: ______________________   Date: ______________________
>
> [Optional release language, reviewed by an attorney: "I have reviewed the accounting provided and release the [executor / trustee] from further claims related to this distribution."]

## Timing guide

| Stage | Typical letter | When |
|---|---|---|
| Soon after appointment | Letter 1 | Within a few weeks |
| While the estate is open | Letter 2 | Every few months |
| After debts and taxes are settled | Letter 3 | When ready |
| With each distribution | Letter 4 | Same day |

## Tricky situations

- **A beneficiary is upset:** Reply in writing, calmly and with facts. Avoid arguing by text.
- **A beneficiary cannot be found:** Keep a record of every attempt to reach them.
- **A beneficiary is a minor:** Ask an attorney how to deliver the share.
- **Someone asks for an early advance:** Say no until debts and taxes are settled.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your state requires a formal accounting or court approval
- A beneficiary threatens to challenge the will or trust
- You are asked to sign or give a release
- The estate may owe more tax or debt than expected
- You are unsure whether it is safe to make a distribution
