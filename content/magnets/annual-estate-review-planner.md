**How to use this planner.** Pick a date each year, such as a birthday or the first weekend of January. Block 60 minutes. Print these pages, fill them in and file them with your plan. Next year, start from the log at the end.

## Why a yearly review helps

A plan that was right when signed can drift out of date without anyone noticing. Children are born, accounts move, a named executor relocates, a marriage ends. Each change can leave a document pointing to the wrong person.

A short, regular check catches most of these. It also keeps your papers and the people who need them easy to find. The shorter [Annual Estate Plan Review checklist](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review) is a good companion.

## Triggers that call for an earlier review

Do not wait for the calendar if any of these happen. Tick the ones that applied this year.

- [ ] Marriage, divorce or separation
- [ ] Birth or adoption of a child or grandchild
- [ ] Death of a spouse, child, executor, trustee or guardian
- [ ] A named person moved, became ill or no longer fits the role
- [ ] You moved to another state or bought property elsewhere
- [ ] You bought or sold a home or business
- [ ] A large inheritance, sale or other change in assets
- [ ] Retirement, a new job or a major change in income
- [ ] A serious diagnosis for you or a family member
- [ ] A child turned 18, or a child developed special needs
- [ ] A change in tax law that may affect your estate
- [ ] An account was opened, closed or rolled over

Rules on how life events affect a will vary by state. For example, some states treat divorce as cancelling gifts to a former spouse, and others do not in every case. An attorney licensed in your state can explain what applies.

If a person is no longer the right choice, note it now. Changing a role usually requires a signed amendment or new document. A note on this page does not change anything legally.

A rough habit many people use is to have an attorney look over documents every three to five years, or sooner after a trigger. Some institutions are less willing to honor a power of attorney that is many years old, so ask your bank how it treats older ones.

If you have a trust, also check that new accounts and property were actually retitled to it. See the [funding your trust guide](/guides/funding-your-trust).

- [ ] Executor and backup have current phone and email
- [ ] Executor knows where the originals and the letter of instruction are kept
- [ ] Safe, safe deposit box and key locations are noted
- [ ] Digital account list and password manager access are up to date
- [ ] Insurance agent, advisor and attorney contact details are current
- [ ] Emergency contacts on phones and wallet cards are current

## A decade-by-decade note

This is general guidance on what people often think about at each stage. It is not advice for any one person.

- **20s and 30s:** Name beneficiaries, sign a healthcare directive and power of attorney, and write a basic will if you have anyone or anything to protect. With children, name a guardian.
- **40s:** Review guardian choices as children grow. Check life insurance against debts and income. Retitle assets as they change.
- **50s:** Consider long-term care options, retirement account beneficiaries and whether children are ready for roles you named them in.
- **60s:** Review retirement withdrawals, Social Security timing and any gifting plans. Check that agents are still able and willing.
- **70s and beyond:** Review capacity planning, long-term care funding and who has access to what. Keep one up-to-date packet where family can find it. See the [Medicaid and long-term care guide](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## After the review

- [ ] Make a list of changes that need an attorney
- [ ] Contact beneficiary-form holders for any updates
- [ ] Update copies held by your executor
- [ ] Set a calendar reminder for next year, or use the [plan review reminder](/tools/plan-review-reminder)

## When to talk to an attorney

- A trigger event may change who should inherit or serve in a role
- You moved to another state
- A beneficiary is a minor, has special needs or has creditor or divorce risk
- Your assets grew enough that estate or inheritance taxes may be a question
- You want to amend a will or trust, not just note a change
