## How to use this worksheet

Sit down with a pen and your operating agreement, partnership agreement or shareholder agreement. Fill in each table for your own business. If a line has no answer in the paperwork, that is a gap, and gaps are where disputes start. This is general education, and rules vary by state and by entity type.

## What happens by default

Without an agreement, state law fills in. The result is often not what the owners would have chosen.

| Entity | Common default when an owner dies | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| General partnership | The partnership may dissolve or the partner is treated as leaving, and the estate is owed the value of the share | Partnership agreement, state partnership act |
| LLC | The member is usually dissociated. The estate often keeps economic rights (a share of profit) but not a vote | Operating agreement, state LLC act |
| S or C corporation | Shares pass through the owner's will, trust or intestacy like any other property. Heirs become shareholders | Bylaws, shareholder agreement |
| Sole owner of a single-member LLC | The membership interest goes through the estate unless it is in a trust | Operating agreement, trust |

In Illinois, LLC rules sit in the Limited Liability Company Act, 805 ILCS 180, and partnerships in the Uniform Partnership Act (2002), 805 ILCS 206. <!-- verify: current Illinois citations and the default treatment of a deceased member --> Other states differ, so check yours.

## Do heirs become members?

Often no, or not fully. Many operating agreements say a transferee gets only a share of profits and distributions, not management rights, unless the other members vote them in.

- [ ] My agreement says whether a deceased member's heirs can become full members
- [ ] My agreement says who votes on admitting them
- [ ] My agreement says what happens if they are not admitted

If you have no written agreement, write one. A spouse or sibling who inherits a voting share can change how the business runs overnight.

## Rights of first refusal and buyout terms

A right of first refusal says: before an owner (or an heir) sells to an outsider, the other owners get to match the offer. It is the usual answer to "what if my partner's heirs want to sell to a stranger?"

## Three ways to structure the buyout

1. **Cross-purchase.** Each owner buys the deceased owner's share personally, often with a policy on each other.
2. **Entity redemption.** The company buys back the share, usually with a policy it owns.
3. **Hybrid or wait-and-see.** The choice is made after the death, based on tax and cash.

Tax treatment differs between these. Ask a business or tax attorney before choosing. See the [buy-sell agreement checklist](/free/buy-sell-agreement-checklist) for the full list of terms.

## If you have no agreement yet

If you have no written plan, the table below shows common choices.

| If this is true | Then owners often do this |
|---|---|
| Owners want the survivors to keep control | A buy-sell with a mandatory purchase at death |
| An owner wants the spouse to receive value, not a vote | The spouse gets the sale price, not the share |
| Owners are siblings and want the family to stay in | A right of first refusal plus a transfer to bloodline only |
| Cash is tight | Installment payments over several years with a secured note |
| No one can afford premiums | Term policies on each owner, reviewed every few years |

## A partner I would not choose

You cannot control who a partner's spouse is. You can control what the spouse receives. Options owners commonly use:

- Spouse receives cash from a buyout, not ownership
- Spouse keeps profit rights only, with no vote
- A trust holds the share and a named trustee votes it
- Spouse has a short window to decide to keep or sell

When an agreement and a will conflict, the agreement usually controls the business interest. Your will cannot give away what the agreement says you cannot give.

- [ ] Who talks to banks, landlords and customers?
- [ ] Do owners' wills and trusts match the agreement?
- [ ] Is each owner's spouse aware of the plan?

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have no written agreement
- The agreement is more than a few years old
- An owner is ill or has recently been diagnosed
- A spouse or sibling is involved in a way that could cause conflict
- You are comparing cross-purchase and redemption structures
