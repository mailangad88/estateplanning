This is a five-day email course for parents. Each lesson takes about ten minutes and ends with one small task. It is general education, not legal advice, and rules vary by state.

## Day 1: Who would raise your kids? Start with this one name

If both parents died or could not care for the children, a court would decide who raises them. Without a named guardian, a judge chooses, often after family members disagree. A guardian named in a will gives the court a clear statement of your wishes. Courts usually give it serious weight, though the judge still decides what is best for the child.

Many parents avoid this question because every answer feels imperfect. That is normal. The goal is a good choice, not a perfect one.

Parents commonly weigh a few things:

- Values and parenting style that feel close to yours
- Age, health and energy for raising children
- Location, and whether the children would have to move
- Whether the person has room, time and willingness in their own life
- How the person would get along with your children's other relatives

Then talk to the person. Naming someone who has never been asked can lead to a surprise and a refusal at the worst time. Also name a backup, because the first choice may not be able to serve.

The guardian of the person is separate from whoever manages the money. Many families name two different people on purpose, so one role checks the other. Day two covers the money side.

A common mistake is choosing a guardian based on guilt or family pressure. Another is assuming grandparents are the only option, when their age or health may change over eighteen years. Revisit the choice as your children grow, since a good fit for a baby may not be the best fit for a teenager. Keep the choice private until you have asked the person.

**Today's task:** Write down up to three possible guardians and one backup. Beside each name, jot two reasons and one concern. Share the list with your co-parent tonight and compare.

## Day 2: Money for your kids: how much, and who holds it

Children cannot legally manage large sums. If a minor inherits money outright, a court may set up a supervised account, and in many states the child can take the full amount at 18. Few parents want that.

Common options include a trust for the children, a custodial account under your state's uniform transfers law, or naming a trustee to hold insurance proceeds. Each has different costs, controls and ages of release. The guide on [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors) compares them.

To get a rough number, think about what your children would need if your income stopped:

- Housing, food and everyday costs until they are grown
- Child care and the cost of a caregiver adjusting their work
- Health insurance and medical costs
- Education, such as college or training
- A cushion for the guardian, who may need a bigger car or home

The [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) turns these into an estimate. It is a starting point for discussion, not a prediction.

Choose a trustee carefully. This person can be your guardian, but separating the roles is common. Name a backup here too.

A frequent mistake is leaving everything to a spouse and stopping there. If both parents died together, the money could land with the children by default. Another is naming the guardian as the only person who touches the money, with no oversight. Even a simple written plan, such as a trust with a clear trustee, can spare a court from stepping in.

**Today's task:** Use the guardian fund calculator, or list the five costs above with a rough yearly figure for each. Write down who you would trust to manage the money and who would be the backup.

## Day 3: Life insurance basics for parents

Life insurance is the most common way parents fund the gap if income disappears. It pays a set amount to the people you name when you die. Compared with savings alone, it can create a large sum quickly.

There are two broad types. Term insurance covers a set number of years, such as 20 or 30, and is usually the more affordable choice for young families. Permanent insurance lasts a lifetime and builds cash value, but costs more. Which fits depends on your goals and budget, and a licensed insurance professional can explain the details.

A common starting point is to add up the money your family would need, subtract savings and other resources, and see what gap remains. The [life insurance needs tool](/tools/life-insurance-needs) walks through this.

Some points parents often miss:

- A stay-at-home parent provides work that would cost money to replace, so coverage on both parents is common.
- Coverage through work may end if you change jobs, and may be smaller than the gap.
- Naming a minor child as beneficiary can send proceeds to a court-supervised account. A trust is often named instead.
- The policy only pays what it says to whoever is named on the form, not what your will says.

One more point: coverage amounts that feel large today can shrink in real terms over twenty years. Many parents also forget to update the policy after a second child. Comparing quotes from more than one insurer is common, and so is asking an independent agent. Avoid cancelling an existing policy until a new one is in force.

**Today's task:** Find out what life insurance you and your co-parent already have. Write down the insurer, the amount, the type and who is currently named as beneficiary.

## Day 4: Check your beneficiary forms

Retirement accounts, life insurance and many bank accounts pass by beneficiary form, not by will. These forms often control more money than the will does. A form filled out years ago may name an ex, a parent, or nobody at all.

Read more in the [beneficiary designations guide](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

For each account, log in or call and confirm:

- **Primary beneficiary:** the person or trust who is first in line
- **Contingent beneficiary:** who receives it if the primary cannot
- **Whether a minor is named:** if so, who would manage the money
- **When the form was last updated**

Some warning signs: no contingent is named, the beneficiary has died, or the form lists "my children" without naming a trust. A named beneficiary can also affect a child who receives government benefits, so those families often involve an attorney first.

Changing a form is usually free and takes a few minutes. Spouses sometimes have legal rights in employer retirement plans, and rules vary by state, so ask before changing one.

Another common gap is a form that names one child, such as a first child, and not a later one. Some parents also name a spouse who then becomes the only owner, with no backup if both die. Keep a copy of each confirmation once you check it. Saving the confirmation email gives you proof of what was on file.

**Today's task:** Make a simple table with every account that has a beneficiary form. Add columns for current primary, contingent, and "needs updating." Do not change anything yet, just note the gaps.

## Day 5: The emergency sheet and your next step

If you were rushed to the hospital, a caregiver would need answers fast. Today you build a one-page sheet that holds them. Put a copy where a trusted adult can find it, and tell them where it is.

Include:

- Children's full names, birthdates, allergies, medications and doctors
- Pediatrician, school or child care contact and pickup rules
- Names and phone numbers of your guardian and backup
- Where your will and documents are kept
- How to reach your attorney, insurer and employer
- Accounts, bills and who to call about them
- Passwords or how to reach a password manager, stored securely

You now have a guardian shortlist, a rough money number, your insurance facts, a beneficiary table and an emergency sheet. That is most of what an attorney would ask for in a first meeting.

Documents turn those notes into something that works. A will names the guardian, and a trust can hold money for your children. Both need to be signed correctly under your state's rules.

If you would like help, you can book a consult through the [plan finder](/plan-finder). There is no pressure to do so, and your notes are useful whichever route you take.

Do not worry about making the sheet perfect. A rough version in a shared note beats a polished one that never gets made. Update it when a child changes schools, starts a medication or when a phone number changes. Review it every time clocks change, or on each child's birthday, so the habit sticks.

**Today's task:** Fill in the emergency sheet, print one copy, and tell your guardian or a trusted adult where it is.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have a child with special needs or who receives benefits
- You and your co-parent disagree about who should be guardian
- You are not married, or have children from a prior relationship
- You own a business, or your estate may be large
- You live in more than one state or have family abroad
