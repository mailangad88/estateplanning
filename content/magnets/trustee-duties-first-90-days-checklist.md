**How to use this checklist.** If you are reading this, someone close to you has died, and you have been asked to carry on their wishes. You do not have to do everything at once. Work through the sections in order, tick items off, and write dates beside them. This list covers a living trust that became irrevocable at death. Your trust document and state law control, so read the trust first.

## Week 1: Steady the ground

- [ ] Get several certified copies of the death certificate.
- [ ] Locate the trust document, every amendment, and the pour-over will if there is one.
- [ ] Read the trust, especially the sections on trustee powers, distributions, and who the beneficiaries are.
- [ ] Confirm you are the named successor and whether co-trustees must act together.
- [ ] Sign any acceptance of trusteeship the document calls for.
- [ ] Secure property: lock the house, forward mail, keep vehicles insured, and photograph valuables.
- [ ] Do not give anything away or make distributions yet.
- [ ] Start a trustee log with a notebook or spreadsheet. Record each call, date and decision.

## Weeks 1 to 3: Tell the right people

- [ ] List the beneficiaries with names, addresses and relationships.
- [ ] Check your state's law on notice. Some states require written notice to beneficiaries within a set number of days. Illinois, for example, requires a trustee to notify qualified beneficiaries within 90 days of accepting the role or of the trust becoming irrevocable.
- [ ] Send a letter that gives the trust's name, your name and contact details, and the beneficiaries' right to request a copy of the trust terms.
- [ ] Notify Social Security if the funeral home has not (they often report it).
- [ ] Notify banks, brokers, insurers, pension plans and credit bureaus as needed.
- [ ] Notify an attorney if the trust or any asset is complex.

Sample notice wording:

> I have become the successor trustee of the [Name] Trust following [Name]'s death on [date]. My contact details are [address, phone, email]. You are a beneficiary. You may ask me for a copy of the trust terms and for information about how the trust is being administered.

## Weeks 2 to 4: Get an EIN and set up accounts

During life the trust likely used the grantor's Social Security number. After death it is a separate taxpayer.

- [ ] Apply for an Employer Identification Number (EIN) for the trust through the IRS. The online application is free.
- [ ] File Form 56 (Notice Concerning Fiduciary Relationship) with the IRS if advised.
- [ ] Open a trust checking account in the trust's name and EIN.
- [ ] Move trust cash into that account. Do not use your personal account.
- [ ] Re-title brokerage accounts into the trust's name, as the institutions direct.
- [ ] Keep every deposit and payment documented.

- [ ] Order an appraisal for real estate and any unusual items.
- [ ] Ask custodians for date-of-death statements.
- [ ] Identify assets outside the trust such as retirement accounts and life insurance, with their own beneficiaries. They are not trust assets unless the trust is named.
- [ ] Look for assets that were never moved into the trust. These may need probate or a small-estate process.
- [ ] Gather debts, bills, loans, taxes and funeral costs.

## Weeks 4 to 12: Pay bills and protect value

- [ ] Pay valid expenses from trust funds. Keep receipts.
- [ ] Keep the house insured, taxed and maintained. Do not leave property uninsured.
- [ ] Do not sell assets until you have the inventory and the trust's instructions.
- [ ] Invest cash prudently in insured accounts while you plan.
- [ ] Do not lend trust money to yourself or a beneficiary.
- [ ] Keep trust funds separate from your own money at all times.

## Taxes in the first 90 days

- [ ] Find a tax preparer who handles trust and estate returns.
- [ ] Ask about the final personal income tax return for the person who died.
- [ ] Ask about the trust's income tax return (Form 1041) and its due date.
- [ ] Ask whether an estate-tax return is needed. Most estates fall below the 2026 federal exemption of $15,000,000 per person, but some states have their own taxes at lower levels.
- [ ] Consider with your tax preparer whether to treat the trust as part of the estate for income tax purposes (Form 8855 election).

## Be gentle with yourself

Most trustees are family members doing this while grieving. It is fine to say "I need to check on that and get back to you." Keep emails short and factual. Write things down. If a relative is angry, you do not have to argue. Pass questions along in writing, and ask an attorney before you answer a demand.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The trust is unclear, or beneficiaries disagree about what it says.
- A beneficiary asks for money, a copy of the trust, or an accounting and you are unsure how to respond.
- There is real estate, a business, or property in another state.
- The estate may owe estate tax or you see large unpaid debts.
- You are also a beneficiary and want to be sure you are not in a conflict.
- You want to resign, or want a co-trustee or professional trustee to help.
