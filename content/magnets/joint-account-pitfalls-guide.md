**How to use this guide.** Many parents add an adult child to a bank account "to make things easier." It does make bill paying easier. It also changes who owns the money, who can reach it and who gets it. Read the comparison table, then answer the questions at the end. Bank rules and state law vary, so ask your bank and an attorney before you sign anything.

## Two different goals that get mixed up

People add a child to an account for one of two reasons.

1. **Help.** The child can pay bills, deposit checks or find money if the parent is ill.
2. **Inheritance.** The child should receive what is left when the parent dies.

A joint account does both at once, and often more than the parent meant. The other tools separate the goals.

## What a joint account does

With a typical joint account with right of survivorship, both people are owners. Either can withdraw any amount. At the first death, the survivor owns the account outright, and it does not go through a will.

That is useful for spouses. For a parent and a child it creates problems.

## Five risks of adding a child as joint owner

**1. The child's creditors can reach the money.** If the child is sued, divorces or owes taxes, a creditor may be able to claim funds in an account the child co-owns. Rules differ by state, but the risk exists.

**2. The money may not be shared the way you planned.** At your death, the joint owner usually takes the whole account. If you have three children and added one, the others may receive nothing from it, even if your will splits everything equally. Some states presume the joint owner holds the money for the estate when there is evidence it was only for convenience, but this often needs a court fight.

**3. The child can take it all while you are alive.** The bank generally will not question a co-owner's withdrawal. A trusting arrangement can be abused. Financial exploitation by a family member is common enough that banks and regulators warn about it.

**4. Tax and benefit effects.** Adding a child can be treated as a gift in some situations, and a large withdrawal by the child for personal use may be too. If you may need Medicaid long-term care help later, the account is still counted as yours, and moves can affect eligibility. The account may also be reported on the child's financial aid forms.

**5. It can derail a plan that already exists.** Your will or trust may say one thing and the account says another. Accounts outside the plan often cause the bigger surprise.

## The alternatives

| Tool | What the child can do now | Who owns the money | What happens at death | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Joint account with survivorship | Everything | Both | Survivor takes it | Creditors, unequal shares, misuse |
| Convenience account or signer (bank-specific form) | Pay bills, deposit, withdraw as agreed | Parent only | Goes by will, trust or POD | Bank may not offer it or may not honor it after death |
| Agent under a financial power of attorney | Act for the parent under the document's terms | Parent only | Authority ends at death | Needs a trusted agent. Some banks ask for their own form |
| Payable-on-death (POD) beneficiary | Nothing while alive | Parent only | Pays to the named person without probate | Must name backups and keep it updated |
| Revocable trust account | Trustee can manage | Trust | Passes by trust | Needs a trust and the account retitled |
| Ordinary account plus will | Nothing | Parent only | Probate, in most cases | Delay and cost |

For many families the combination of a power of attorney, a POD designation and a will works better than a joint account, because it separates control from inheritance. See the [guide to POD and TOD](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death).

Checklist:

- [ ] If the only goal is help, I asked the bank about a convenience signer or an account agent.
- [ ] I signed a durable power of attorney naming the child, and the bank has a copy.
- [ ] I added a POD beneficiary, and a backup, to the account.
- [ ] If I keep a joint account anyway, I told my other children and wrote down why.
- [ ] I asked the bank how it treats the account at the first death.
- [ ] My will or trust matches what the accounts do.
- [ ] I review the list every year.

## If you already added a child

Do not panic. Many families have done this and nothing went wrong. Still, look at the facts.

- [ ] Find the account agreement. It says whether it is joint with survivorship.
- [ ] Ask the bank whether the child's name can be removed or changed to an agent role. Often both owners must sign.
- [ ] If the money was meant for all heirs equally, write a note of intent now and share it with the family. This can help if there is a dispute.
- [ ] If the child has creditor or divorce risk, ask an attorney about the options before there is a claim.
- [ ] Do not move money in a rush if Medicaid may be coming. Timing matters.

## Script for the bank

> "I would like my daughter to be able to pay my bills, but I want to keep ownership. What forms do you offer for an agent or convenience signer, and what do you need to add a payable-on-death beneficiary and a backup?"

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are about to add a child, a friend or a new partner to a large account.
- You have several children and want an equal result.
- You may need Medicaid long-term care in the next five years.
- A child has debts, a divorce or a business and you worry about creditors.
- Someone is already using your account in a way that worries you.
- You want a trust to hold bank and brokerage accounts together.
