**How to use this guide.** Read the problems first, then the options, then fill in the worksheet with your family. This guide is educational and not individual advice. Property, trust, LLC and tax rules vary by state, and a cabin in another state brings that state's rules into the picture. An attorney can tell you how they apply to your property.

## Why the cabin causes trouble

A family cabin is usually worth more in memories than in dollars, and that is what makes disputes painful. The usual causes are not villains. They are ordinary pressures that nobody planned for.

- **Different distances and different budgets.** One heir lives an hour away and uses it every weekend. Another lives across the country and visits twice a year. A third is short on cash and cannot pay a share of the roof.
- **Different ideas of use.** One family wants quiet, another wants a house full of guests. Someone wants to rent it out. Someone else would rather sell.
- **Unequal sweat.** The heir who paints, mows and fixes the dock may feel owed. The others may not know what is being done.
- **Spouses and in-laws.** When an heir divorces or dies, their share can move to a person who has no history with the place.
- **Money stalls.** Property tax, insurance, repairs and utilities are due whether anyone is using the place or not.
- **No exit.** An heir who wants out may have no way to leave except to force a sale.

In many states, any co-owner of real estate can ask a court to force a sale, called a partition. A cabin that cannot be shared fairly can end up sold at auction in a court case. Plans exist to avoid that.

## Co-ownership by default

If the cabin is left to several children in a will, or by a deed to several people, they usually become co-owners with equal rights to use the whole property. Depending on how title is held, a co-owner may be able to sell their share to an outsider, or their share may pass to their own heirs, or to their spouse in a divorce. Without a written agreement, there is no rulebook on who pays, who uses and what happens next. Rules vary by state.

## Options

### Leave it to one heir

One child inherits and others receive other assets of similar value. This is simple and avoids shared ownership, but it only works when there are other assets to balance and when the siblings agree. Life insurance can help balance. The chosen heir should be able to afford the upkeep.

### Sell and divide

Selling can be the kindest answer when the family cannot share, no heir can afford the place, or the property is a burden. Some families sell and set aside part of the proceeds for a joint trip each year. It is a legitimate choice, not a failure.

### A trust

A revocable living trust can hold title to the cabin during your life and after your death. The trust can name a trustee, set rules for use and costs, and say what happens if someone wants out. See [revocable living trust explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained). A trust can also keep the property out of probate. If the property is in another state, that is a significant benefit (see below).

A trust can last for a set period, such as the life of the youngest child, or until the cabin is sold. Some families create a separate trust just for the property, with a cash fund to pay for upkeep.

### An LLC

A limited liability company can own the cabin. Family members hold membership interests, and an operating agreement sets the rules. In general terms, an LLC can:

- Provide a place to write use, cost and buyout rules
- Allow shares to transfer without deeds changing each time
- Offer some liability protection, depending on structure and state law
- Make it easier to give shares over time during life

An LLC also has costs: filing fees, annual reports, possible tax issues, insurance and lender or property tax consequences in some states. Some states may reassess property tax upon a change in ownership, though many have exceptions for family transfers, and rules vary a great deal. Ask about this before transferring anything. An LLC that is not run properly may not give the protection people expect.

### Other tools

- **Transfer on death deed.** In states that allow it, a TOD deed can pass the cabin without probate. It does not set rules for co-owners. See [transfer on death deed vs trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust).
- **Life estate.** A parent keeps use for life and the heirs own the remainder. It has tax and control consequences.
- **A written family agreement,** without a trust or entity, which is easy but only as strong as the family's good faith.

## Use schedules

A schedule can be as simple as a calendar. Approaches that families use:

- **Rotation.** Each branch of the family gets a turn, with the order rotating each year.
- **Draft.** Each branch picks weeks in order, reversing each year.
- **Prime weeks.** Treat summer holidays, hunting season or ski weeks as special, and assign them by rotation.
- **Open weeks.** Weeks nobody has claimed are available to anyone first-come.
- **Guest rules.** Who may bring friends, and how many.
- **Check-out standards.** Cleaning, stocking supplies, leaving things as found.
- **Rentals.** Whether the property can be rented, who handles it, and where income goes. Rentals can change taxes, insurance and the character of the place.

Put the schedule in writing, and say how to resolve a conflict.

## Cost sharing

A fund is more reliable than occasional requests. Components:

- **Annual budget** covering property tax, insurance, utilities, routine maintenance, and a reserve for the roof, septic, dock or heating.
- **How costs are split.** Equal by branch, by ownership share, or by use. Equal by branch is simple. Use-based costs are fairer when use varies widely.
- **A reserve fund** with a target amount.
- **Special assessments** for big repairs, and when a vote is needed.
- **What happens if someone cannot pay.** Options include a loan from the fund, reduced use, or an interest charge that accrues against their share.
- **Sweat equity.** Whether work is credited, and at what rate.
- **Who keeps the books,** and how often they report.

## Buyout terms

An agreement that lets someone leave with dignity reduces the chance of a forced sale.

- **Offer to the family first.** The other owners or the entity get the first chance.
- **Price.** An appraisal by an agreed method, perhaps with a discount for a minority share or for a short-notice sale. Choose a method and put it in writing.
- **Payment.** Lump sum or installment note with interest.
- **Timing.** A window to accept and to close, and a limit on how often buyouts can occur.
- **Outside sales.** Whether an owner may sell to an outsider, and under what conditions.
- **Death, divorce and bankruptcy.** Specify what happens to a share when an owner dies, divorces or goes bankrupt.
- **Spouses.** Whether a spouse can inherit or hold a share, and whether a prenuptial agreement is expected.

## Out-of-state property and ancillary probate

If you live in one state and the cabin is in another, your estate may need a second, separate probate in the cabin's state, called ancillary probate. It means another court, another set of fees and often another attorney. It can slow things down and add cost. Rules vary by state.

Ways families often address this:

- **Put the cabin in a revocable living trust or an LLC.** If the trust owns the property, it generally avoids probate in both states. This only works if the deed is actually changed.
- **Use a transfer on death deed** if the cabin's state allows it.
- **Check with a local attorney.** Local rules on deeds, recording, taxes and transfers can surprise out-of-state owners.

Out-of-state heirs may also face state-level estate or inheritance taxes where the property sits. See [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving it to all the children equally with no rules.**
- **Skipping the money.** The cabin may need cash to survive.
- **Assuming every heir wants it.** Ask each one privately.
- **Not checking property tax effects** of a transfer.
- **Failing to change the deed** after creating a trust or LLC.
- **Ignoring spouses.**
- **Waiting for a death** to begin the conversation.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The property is in a different state from where you live.
- You are considering an LLC or trust to hold title.
- Heirs disagree about whether to keep or sell.
- There is a mortgage, a lien or a shared title with someone outside the family.
- You plan to rent the property.
- You want to give shares to children during your life, and want to understand gift and tax effects.
