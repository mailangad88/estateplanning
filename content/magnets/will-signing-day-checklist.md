**How to use this checklist.** Read it a few days before the appointment, finish the "before" items, and carry it with you. Signing rules vary by state, so your attorney's instructions come first. In Illinois, for example, a will generally needs your signature and two credible adult witnesses who watch you sign, and a notarized affidavit can make it easier to prove later.

## A week before

- [ ] Read the draft all the way through, slowly. Mark anything unclear.
- [ ] Check every spelling: your name, your spouse, children, executor, guardian, charities.
- [ ] Check the order of who inherits if the first person named has died.
- [ ] Confirm your executor and any alternates have agreed to serve.
- [ ] Confirm the guardian for minor children, if named, and the alternates.
- [ ] Write out questions for the attorney (space below).
- [ ] Ask the office who the witnesses will be and whether a notary is included.
- [ ] Ask about the cost of any extra copies, certified copies or storage.

## What to bring

- [ ] Government photo ID that is current and not expired
- [ ] The draft documents you were sent, with your notes
- [ ] A list of account numbers or beneficiary forms to update afterward
- [ ] Reading glasses and a pen the office approves (many offices provide pens)
- [ ] If a trust is part of your plan, the deed and account details for funding
- [ ] A way to pay any remaining balance, if one is due at signing
- [ ] Your spouse or partner, if the documents involve both of you

Leave at home: anyone who might pressure you, and any beneficiary who would be acting as a witness. Witnesses should not be people who inherit under the will.

## Who can be a witness

Rules vary by state. Typical features are:

| Point | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Number of witnesses | Two is common |
| Age | Adults |
| Interest | Witnesses should not receive anything under the will |
| Presence | They watch you sign, or you acknowledge your signature to them |
| Notary | Often used for a self-proving affidavit, not always required to make the will valid |

Most firms use staff members so the witnesses are neutral and available later if anyone questions the will.

## What happens in the room

1. **Identity and capacity.** The attorney confirms who you are and asks you simple questions: what the document is, who your family is, what you own in general terms. This is routine and protects your will.
2. **Walk-through.** The attorney reviews the key choices. Ask about anything you do not remember.
3. **Last edits.** Small fixes are normal. Do not sign a page with an error you intend to correct by hand.
4. **Declaration.** You say the document is your will and you sign it. Initial pages if the attorney asks.
5. **Witnesses sign.** They sign in your presence and in each other's presence, if your state requires it.
6. **Notary step.** A notary completes the self-proving affidavit, if used.
7. **Other documents.** Powers of attorney and health care directives may follow, each with their own signing rules.

Do not take pages out of the stapled document or separate them. Missing pages can raise doubts about whether the will is complete.

## Before you leave

- [ ] You have the signed original or a clear record of where it is kept.
- [ ] You have a copy of each signed document.
- [ ] You know which documents need follow-up, such as a trust deed or beneficiary forms.
- [ ] You have a list of who needs to know where the documents are.
- [ ] You know what your attorney's office will do to keep the file.

## After signing

- [ ] Store the original in a safe, fireproof place or with your attorney. See the [document locator](/free/estate-plan-document-locator).
- [ ] Tell your executor where the original is and how to reach the attorney.
- [ ] Update beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and insurance.
- [ ] Revisit [the plan review schedule](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review) and set a reminder.
- [ ] Do not write on, staple to or mark the signed will.

## Common mistakes

- Signing before reading the final version because the meeting feels rushed.
- Having a family member who is a beneficiary act as a witness.
- Making pen corrections on the signed pages.
- Leaving the original in an unlabeled drawer.
- Forgetting beneficiary forms that override the will.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have not seen a final draft at least a few days before signing.
- Something in the draft does not match what you discussed.
- You are in poor health, in a hospital, or cannot attend an office.
- A family member has pressed you to make particular changes.
- You own property in more than one state.
- You signed a document elsewhere and are unsure if it is valid.
