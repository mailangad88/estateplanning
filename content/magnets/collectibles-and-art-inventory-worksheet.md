**How to use this worksheet.** Work through one room or one collection at a time. Take photos as you go. Keep the finished worksheet with your estate documents and give your executor a copy. Fill in the table once, then update it when you buy, sell or re-appraise.

## Why collections need their own record

Collections often matter most to the owner and least to everyone else. Heirs may not know which items are valuable, which have stories, or whether a "family painting" is a print or an original. Without a record, items get sold for little, split unevenly, or disputed. A good record answers four questions: what is it, what is it worth, where is it, who should get it.

For each item, also gather:

- [ ] Clear photos from several angles, including marks, signatures and labels
- [ ] Receipts, invoices or gallery paperwork
- [ ] Certificates of authenticity
- [ ] Provenance: who owned it before you, how you acquired it
- [ ] Repair, restoration and cleaning history
- [ ] Loan, display or exhibition records

## Part 2: Appraisals

Values change, so an old number can mislead. Appraisals serve different purposes, and a figure for one purpose may not fit another.

| Purpose | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Insurance replacement | Cost to replace the item |
| Fair market value | What a willing buyer would pay a willing seller |
| Estate or tax | Fair market value at the date of death |
| Donation | Fair market value, with IRS documentation for large gifts |

Choose a qualified appraiser who is independent and not paid by a percentage of the value. Professional appraisal groups keep directories. Avoid anyone who offers to buy the piece after appraising it.

## Part 3: Insurance and storage

Standard homeowner policies often cap coverage for jewelry, art, and collectibles. A rider or separate policy may be needed.

- [ ] Reviewed coverage limits with my insurer
- [ ] Insured items listed with current appraisals
- [ ] Policy covers items in storage, on loan and in transit
- [ ] Photos and inventory kept off site or in the cloud
- [ ] Items are stored away from sunlight, damp and heat
- [ ] Valuables are in a safe, safe deposit box, or controlled storage
- [ ] Executor knows how to reach the insurer

Note: a safe deposit box may be sealed on death in some states until the court or bank allows access, so tell your executor where the key is.

## Part 4: Who gets what

Equal value is not the same as equal feelings. Decide how you want to handle each item.

- **Items to a specific person by name:** ______________________
- **Items to be shared or rotated:** ______________________
- **Items to be sold, with proceeds divided:** ______________________
- **Items to be donated:** ______________________
- **Charity or museum to approach first:** ______________________

Options for dividing items:

- **Specific gifts.** Your will or trust names a recipient for an item. Clear, but update it when things change.
- **A personal property memorandum.** A separate list referred to in the will. Some states recognize it, others do not. Ask an attorney.
- **A draft system.** Heirs take turns picking, with a rule for disputes. Good for items of mixed value.
- **Sale and split.** Appropriate when nobody wants the items.

Explain unequal gifts in a letter. A short note, such as why a child with a love of the subject received a painting, helps. See the [letter of instruction outline](/checklists/letter-of-instruction-outline).

## Part 5: Selling or donating

If your heirs choose to sell, options include:

| Option | Fits | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Auction house | Higher-value art, antiques, jewelry | Seller fees and reserve prices |
| Dealer or gallery | Niche collections | Commission and consignment terms |
| Online marketplaces | Mid-value items | Fraud, shipping and insurance |
| Estate sale company | Whole households | Fees, and who handles valuables |
| Donation | Items with museum or charity interest | Documentation and appraisal rules |

Get more than one opinion on value before selling any significant item. Do not accept a first offer from someone who appears right after a death.

## Part 6: Taxes and gifts, in brief

Most families will not owe federal estate tax, because the 2026 exemption is $15,000,000 per person. Some states tax estates at lower levels. Heirs who sell inherited items may owe capital gains tax only on the gain above the value at the date of death, which is generally why a date-of-death appraisal helps. Lifetime gifts may need a gift tax return if they exceed the annual exclusion of $19,000 per recipient. Special valuation rules can apply to collections. Ask a tax professional.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your collection is a significant share of your estate.
- You want to leave items unequally, or tie them to a condition.
- You want to donate a collection to a museum or charity.
- Items are jointly owned, held in an entity, or located in another state.
- Your estate may owe estate tax.
