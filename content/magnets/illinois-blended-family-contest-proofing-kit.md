## How to use this kit

No plan can stop someone from filing a claim. A good plan makes a claim weak, expensive to bring and unnecessary. This kit covers who can challenge a plan in Illinois, the spouse and child rules that apply no matter what your will says, and ways to cut the risk. Illinois law is cited so your attorney can check it. Other states differ.

## Who can contest in Illinois

An "interested person" can file to contest a will, which usually means someone who would gain if the will fails. In Illinois that is typically heirs and people named in an earlier will. The deadline is six months after the will is admitted to probate. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/8-1 six month deadline -->

| Person | Can contest? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Adult child from a first marriage | Yes, as an heir | Would inherit if the will failed |
| Stepchild | Usually no, unless named in an earlier will or adopted | Stepchildren are not heirs by default |
| Second spouse | Yes, and has the renunciation right | Heir and surviving spouse |
| Ex-spouse | Generally no, divorce ends rights | See divorce rules |
| Charity named earlier | Possibly | Interest under the old will |

Grounds usually include lack of capacity, undue influence, fraud or improper signing. See /free/contesting-a-will-basics-guide and /free/capacity-and-signing-guide. Suing a second spouse is possible, but it is expensive and the ground must be real.

## The spouse's share

Illinois lets a surviving spouse **renounce** the will and take a statutory share. The share is one third of the estate if the decedent left descendants, and one half if not. The spouse must file within seven months after the will is admitted. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/2-8 percentages and deadline --> The right can be waived in writing, such as in a prenuptial agreement.

For your plan, that means:

| If this | Then |
|---|---|
| You leave your spouse less than the statutory share | Your spouse may renounce and take more |
| You leave your spouse a trust only | Ask how the renunciation rule treats it |
| Spouse signed a waiver | The waiver may block the claim |
| Assets pass outside the will, like joint property | Treatment differs; ask an attorney |

See /free/illinois-spousal-rights-guide.

## Children born after the will

Illinois protects a child born or adopted after the will is signed. If the will does not provide for the child, and the testator did not intend to omit them, the child may take a share as if there were no will. The statute is 755 ILCS 5/4-10. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/4-10 terms and exceptions --> A child like this is sometimes called a pretermitted heir.

That also applies to a child you did not know about. A genetic test or paternity proof after death can add a new heir. A clear statement that you intend not to leave more to unnamed children can help, but ask your attorney how it works with the statute.

## No-contest clauses

A no-contest clause says anyone who sues and loses forfeits what they were left. Illinois courts generally enforce them but read them narrowly. <!-- verify: Illinois enforceability of in terrorem clauses --> They work only if the person has something to lose. A child left nothing risks nothing. A small gift paired with a clause can give some deterrence. Do not rely on it alone.

## Ten ways to lower the risk

- [ ] Sign with proper formalities. See /free/illinois-signing-requirements-cheat-sheet
- [ ] Use a trust for assets that should lock at your death
- [ ] Name children and stepchildren explicitly, even when leaving nothing
- [ ] Put the reason in a letter, not in the will
- [ ] Keep an attorney's notes on capacity if health is a concern
- [ ] Avoid last-minute changes in favor of a caregiver
- [ ] Use an independent trustee or executor
- [ ] Match beneficiary forms to the plan
- [ ] Review after marriage, birth or death
- [ ] Tell the family

## Letter of wishes outline

A letter of wishes is a non-binding explanation to your family and trustee. It states why, not what.

1. Who you are naming and why
2. What you want your spouse to have
3. What you want each child and stepchild to have
4. How you want disputes handled
5. Items that matter more for memory than for money
6. A word for the people who did not get what they hoped

- **To my spouse:** ______________________
- **To my children:** ______________________
- **To my stepchildren:** ______________________
- **If you disagree:** ______________________

## Talking to the family

Say it before you sign if you can. Silence breeds suspicion.

1. Choose a calm day with a neutral setting
2. Start with your goal, not the numbers
3. Explain who will serve as trustee and why
4. Invite questions, but do not take a vote
5. Follow up in writing
6. Tell your spouse first

If a conversation could hurt more than help, write the letter and hand it to your attorney. See /free/talking-to-adult-children-about-your-will-guide.

## Common blended family mistakes

- Leaving everything to a spouse and trusting them to pass it on
- Naming a spouse as sole trustee over the children's inheritance
- Forgetting an old beneficiary form that names an ex
- Joint titling that overrides the will
- A will that does not mention a child
- No update after remarriage
- Unequal gifts with no explanation
- Letting the plan sit for ten years

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have a spouse, children and stepchildren and a house
- A child might sue or threaten to
- You want to leave a child less or nothing
- You or a parent may have a capacity issue
- You are unsure whether your spouse can renounce
