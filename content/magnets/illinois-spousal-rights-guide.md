## How to use this guide

Illinois gives a surviving spouse several protections that a will cannot simply switch off. This guide explains each one, then walks through a worksheet for couples in a second marriage. It is educational, not advice for your situation. Other states use different rules, so it applies to Illinois residents and Illinois property only.

## The four Illinois protections at a glance

| Protection | What it gives the spouse | Time limit |
|---|---|---|
| Intestate share | A set share if there is no will | Applies automatically |
| Renunciation of the will | A fixed share of the estate instead of what the will gives | 7 months after the will is admitted to probate |
| Spouse's award | A support payment from the estate | Claimed in the probate case |
| Homestead | A protected interest in the family home | Continues while the spouse lives there |

## If there is no will

Under the Illinois intestacy statute, a surviving spouse takes the whole estate when the person who died left no descendants. If there are children or other descendants, the spouse takes one half and the descendants share the other half. This matters in second marriages. A spouse's half is no longer protected for the first family once it passes to the spouse. Read more about [what happens if you die without a will](/guides/what-happens-if-you-die-without-a-will).

## Renouncing the will

A spouse who is unhappy with a will can file a written renunciation with the court. The share is one third of the entire estate if the person left a descendant, and one half if there is no descendant. The spouse must file within seven months after the will is admitted to probate. The court can extend that time in limited cases, such as pending litigation that affects the share.

Filing the renunciation bars any claim under the will. Gifts the will made to the spouse are treated as if the spouse had died first, so they go to the other beneficiaries.

## The spouse's award

Illinois lets a court award the surviving spouse a sum for support out of the estate. The statute sets a floor of $20,000, plus at least $10,000 for each minor child of the person who died who lived with the spouse. The award ranks near the top of the claims against an estate, ahead of most creditors. It applies to a deceased Illinois resident's estate that is administered in Illinois.

## Homestead

Illinois protects a limited amount of equity in a home. The statute sets an exemption of $15,000 per person, or a proportionate share of $30,000 when more than one owner shares the home. The protection continues after death for a surviving spouse who stays in the home, and for dependent children until the youngest turns 18. It is a modest shield against creditors, not a right to own the house.

## Trusts, beneficiary forms and joint property

Many people ask whether a revocable trust lets them leave a spouse less. Illinois courts have held that a transfer to a revocable trust is generally valid against a spouse's marital rights unless it is tantamount to a fraud, meaning no real intent to make a present gift. In a leading Illinois Supreme Court case, keeping control and a power to revoke were not enough on their own to defeat the trust. The case turned on its facts, so do not rely on a general rule.

Newer law adds a wrinkle for real estate. Illinois now allows a recorded transfer on death instrument for real property. That law gives a surviving spouse the same one third or one half share, claimed within seven months of death.

Beneficiary forms, joint accounts and joint property pass by their own terms. Whether a spouse can reach them depends on how and when they were set up. Ask before assuming.

## Second-marriage worksheet

Answer these together, and again with your own attorney.

- **My children from before this marriage:** ____________________
- **What I want my spouse to have, and for how long:** ____________________
- **What I want to be sure reaches my children:** ____________________
- **Who will live in our home, and who pays for it:** ____________________
- **Do we have a signed marital agreement? Does it waive these rights?** ____________________
- **Who manages a trust if one is used, and is it someone both sides trust?** ____________________

Common approaches include a trust that supports the spouse for life and then passes to the children, a clear home arrangement, and a written agreement each spouse signs after seeing their own attorney. A spouse may be able to waive these rights in writing. A waiver signed without full disclosure can be challenged, so details matter.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming a will controls everything, when the renunciation share can override it.
- Naming a new spouse on an old retirement account by habit, or leaving an ex-spouse on one.
- Leaving the home to children while the spouse still lives there, with no plan for who pays the taxes.
- Changing a plan after a diagnosis or a fight without checking the spouse's rights.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are in a second marriage or have children from a prior relationship.
- You want to leave a spouse less than the statutory shares, or leave property in trust for them.
- A marital agreement exists, or you are considering one.
- A spouse has died, and you want to know whether to renounce the will. The seven-month window is short.
- Your estate includes a business, farm or real estate in more than one state.
