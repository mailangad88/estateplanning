## How to use this guide

Many immigrant families have bank accounts, land or relatives in more than one country, and children who were born here. Planning in that setting raises questions that a standard checklist skips. This guide helps you list what you have, understand the main places where citizenship and borders matter, and prepare documents that relatives can actually use. Rules differ by country and by state, so use it to prepare for a conversation with an attorney. If your family needs a lawyer in another country, say so early. A U.S. lawyer usually cannot advise on foreign law.

## Why this matters

A plan that works in one place may not work in another. A U.S. will may not control land overseas. A foreign will may not meet U.S. formalities. A guardian in another country may face visa limits or court hurdles. Taxes on gifts, inheritance and foreign accounts can apply to some families. None of this is a reason to wait. It is a reason to write things down.

## Step 2: Assets abroad

Property in another country is usually governed by that country's laws, even if you live in the U.S. Some countries have "forced heirship" rules that reserve parts of an estate for certain family members. Some require local procedures such as a local will, a notary's deed or a court certificate. Local rules may also affect bank accounts and shares.

- [ ] Ask a lawyer in that country what happens to your property at your death
- [ ] Ask whether a local will is advisable, and whether it can be coordinated with a U.S. will so the two do not cancel each other
- [ ] Gather title deeds, account details and identification numbers
- [ ] Record who in the family has access to each foreign account
- [ ] Ask whether a local power of attorney is needed for a relative to act

Two U.S. tax points to ask about. U.S. persons with foreign financial accounts may need to file a Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts when the combined balance goes over $10,000 at any time in the year. A U.S. person who receives a large gift or inheritance from a foreign person may need to file an information form with the IRS once the total goes over $100,000 in a year. Penalties for missing these forms can be high, so ask a tax professional. See the sources below for the current rules.

## Step 3: Heirs who are not U.S. citizens

If a spouse is not a U.S. citizen, the usual tax break for transfers between spouses at death may not apply in the same way. Special trusts can allow a deferral. If a child or other heir lives abroad, consider:

- Whether the heir can receive money from a U.S. estate easily, and what tax their country may apply
- Whether an heir's own benefits, such as means-tested benefits, could be affected
- Whether a trust is a better way to deliver money than an outright gift
- Whether your executor will be able to locate and communicate with the heir

A non-citizen who is not a U.S. resident can face a much lower estate tax exemption on U.S. property than a citizen. Check current IRS guidance or ask an attorney if you or your spouse are in that situation. Also read our guide on [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes). For couples, our [non-citizen spouse guide](/free/non-citizen-spouse-planning-guide) goes deeper.

## Step 4: Children and guardians

Your children may be U.S. citizens, and you may want your own parents or siblings to raise them if you cannot. That can be hard if the guardian lives abroad.

- [ ] Name a guardian, and a backup who lives in the U.S.
- [ ] Ask whether the guardian could travel or live in the U.S., and what visas that needs
- [ ] Consider how the children's schooling, language and citizenship would change
- [ ] Name someone in the U.S. who can take the children in the first weeks
- [ ] Keep passports, birth certificates and immigration papers in one place the guardian can find
- [ ] Decide who holds the children's money, and keep that role in the U.S. if possible

Courts usually look at the child's interests. Your written choice and reasons help, but a court makes the final decision. Read about [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children).

## Step 5: Language and documents

A document that your family cannot read will not help them. Courts and banks generally want documents in English, and foreign authorities often want local-language versions.

- [ ] Sign the U.S. documents in English
- [ ] Ask your attorney whether a translation, a certified translation or a bilingual version is wise
- [ ] Keep translations with the original and label them "translation, not a legal document"
- [ ] Ask whether foreign documents will need an apostille or consular authentication to be used abroad
- [ ] Keep certified copies of birth, marriage, death and naturalization records
- [ ] Do not sign anything you cannot read, in any language

## Step 6: Immigration status and planning

Your status does not stop you from making a will, naming a guardian or signing powers of attorney. It can affect some tax rules and benefits. If your own immigration case is pending, tell your attorney early, and speak with an immigration attorney before large transfers abroad. Keep an emergency plan for the possibility that you are detained or have to travel suddenly.

- [ ] Power of attorney for finances in case you are away
- [ ] Standby guardian or caregiver authorization for children
- [ ] List of who to call, including an immigration attorney
- [ ] Copies of key documents stored with a trusted person

## Step 7: Talk with your family

Many families avoid this conversation. Try a short, calm one.

- [ ] Explain who you chose as guardian and why
- [ ] Say where documents are kept
- [ ] Explain what you want done with property abroad
- [ ] Ask what relatives abroad would need to act
- [ ] Agree on who is the family contact for lawyers in each country

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own land, accounts or a business in another country
- A spouse, child or heir is not a U.S. citizen or lives abroad
- You want a guardian who lives abroad
- You need foreign documents recognized in the U.S., or U.S. documents recognized abroad
- You may owe U.S. reports on foreign accounts or inheritances
- You or a relative has a pending immigration matter
