**How to use this guide.** Read the three handoff options, pick one, then complete the sheet and the test. A password manager is the one place that holds the keys to everything else, so the handoff deserves care. The goal is that the right person can get in at the right time and no one else can.

## What goes wrong without a plan

Most password managers are built so that even the company cannot open your vault. That is good security. It also means that if you die or become incapacitated and no one has the master password or an emergency option, the vault may be unreachable. Your family could be locked out of email, banking, photos and subscriptions all at once.

## Option 1: Built-in emergency access

Many managers let you name a trusted contact. They request access, you or the system waits a delay period you set, and then they can see or take over the vault. Check your provider's help pages for the details, as features and plan levels vary.

- [ ] I confirmed my plan includes emergency access.
- [ ] I chose a contact who is reliable and is not likely to lose their own account.
- [ ] I set a waiting period that gives me time to deny a mistaken request.
- [ ] I decided whether the contact gets view-only access or full takeover.
- [ ] My contact has accepted and knows how to use it.

Good fit: people who want the vault to stay private during life and open only on request.

## Option 2: Sealed instruction sheet

A paper sheet stored with your important papers or in a safe deposit box. It holds the master password and any two-factor recovery codes, or tells the reader where to find them.

- [ ] The sheet is in a sealed envelope marked with the name of the person who may open it.
- [ ] It sits in a place that person can reach, such as a home safe.
- [ ] I will replace it whenever the master password changes.

Weigh the trade-offs. A paper sheet is simple and works without a computer. It is also a single point of theft. Do not tape it to a monitor or leave it in a desk drawer.

## Option 3: Shared family vault

A shared vault holds the accounts your family needs, such as utilities, streaming and a joint bank account. The rest of your vault stays private. This suits couples who both need daily access.

- [ ] Shared items are listed and reviewed twice a year.
- [ ] Each person has their own master password.

## What to put on the sealed sheet

- **Name of password manager and website:** ____________________
- **Account email:** ____________________
- **Master password location (not the password itself, unless sealed):** ____________________
- **Two-factor method (app, hardware key, text):** ____________________
- **Where recovery codes or backup keys are kept:** ____________________
- **Primary phone number used for codes:** ____________________
- **Who may open this envelope and when:** ____________________
- **Date written:** ____________________

If your manager uses a secret key plus a master password, the sheet needs both, so check how yours works. If a hardware security key is used, say where it is kept.

## What never goes in a will or trust

A will often becomes public once it is filed with a court. A trust is private but is read by trustees and sometimes beneficiaries. Keep these out of both:

- Master passwords and PINs
- Seed phrases or private keys for crypto
- Full account numbers and security answers
- Anything that would let a stranger who reads the document take money

Instead, give your executor authority. Sample wording to discuss with your attorney: "My executor may access my digital accounts, devices and password manager, and may use any credentials I left for that purpose." Then store the actual credentials separately, as above.

## Two-factor traps

- A code that goes to your phone does not help your family if no one can unlock the phone.
- Authenticator apps can be lost with a phone. Save the backup codes.
- Do not use a text code to your own number alone for your email.

## Run a test

Once a year:

- [ ] Ask your contact to confirm they can find the instructions.
- [ ] Try emergency access with a harmless account to check that the process works.
- [ ] Update the sheet after any master password change.
- [ ] Remove anyone who should no longer have access, such as a former spouse.

## If you are the person receiving access

- Ask first whether a legal document gives you authority.
- Do not make payments or delete accounts in a hurry.
- Write a log of what you open and change.
- Watch for automatic renewals and keep needed accounts alive until the estate is sorted out.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your plan should name who may access digital accounts and under what authority.
- A business, domain names or income depend on your logins.
- You want a person other than your executor to hold access.
- A company will not let an executor into an account.
- You are unsure whether instructions contradict your will.
