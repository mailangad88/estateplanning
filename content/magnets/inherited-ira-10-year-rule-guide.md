## How to use this guide

Federal rules decide how fast an inherited IRA must be emptied, and the answer depends on who inherits. Work through the three sorting questions, find your row in the tables, then fill in the calendar. This is general education, not tax advice. Retirement account rules change, so confirm current figures with a tax professional or the IRS before you act. <!-- verify: current SECURE Act and 2024 final regulation details -->

## Step 1: Sort the beneficiary

1. **Is the beneficiary a person, or something else?** A person (or a qualifying see-through trust) is a "designated beneficiary." An estate, a charity or a plain trust that fails the IRS tests is a "non-designated beneficiary."
2. **If a person, are they an "eligible designated beneficiary"?** These are the people the SECURE Act left some flexibility for.
3. **If not eligible, the 10-year rule usually applies.**

| Beneficiary | Category | What usually follows |
|---|---|---|
| Surviving spouse | Eligible designated | Rollover or inherited IRA, many options |
| Minor child of the account owner | Eligible designated | Life-expectancy payments until majority, then 10 years |
| Disabled or chronically ill person (as the tax code defines) | Eligible designated | Life-expectancy payments |
| Someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner | Eligible designated | Life-expectancy payments |
| Adult child, grandchild, friend | Designated, not eligible | 10-year rule |
| Estate, charity, non-qualifying trust | Non-designated | Shorter schedule, often 5 years or the owner's remaining life expectancy |

A grandchild is not a "minor child of the owner," so grandchildren generally fall under the 10-year rule even when they are young. A trust for any of these people has to meet the see-through trust tests to keep the better treatment. <!-- verify: see-through trust requirements -->

## Step 2: Know what "stretch" means now

A stretch IRA spreads withdrawals over the beneficiary's own life expectancy, so the account stays tax-deferred for decades. Before 2020 most beneficiaries could do this. Now only eligible designated beneficiaries can. Everyone else is on the 10-year clock.

## How the 10-year rule works

The whole account must be empty by the end of the tenth year after the year of death. A death in 2026 means a deadline of December 31, 2036.

The twist is yearly withdrawals:

| If the owner died... | Yearly withdrawals in years 1 to 9? |
|---|---|
| Before their required beginning date (generally age 73 for most people now) | Not required. Empty it any time within 10 years |
| On or after their required beginning date | Required each year, then empty by year 10 |

The IRS finalized rules in 2024 that confirmed this. Missing a required yearly withdrawal carries an excise tax. The usual rate is 25 percent of the shortfall, and it can fall to 10 percent if you fix the miss promptly. <!-- verify: current excise tax rates under SECURE 2.0 -->

## Spouse: rollover vs. inherited IRA

A spousal rollover treats the account as yours. A spouse can also keep it as an inherited IRA.

| | Spousal rollover | Stay an inherited IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Whose rules | Your own age and schedule | The deceased's account rules |
| Withdrawal before 59 and a half | 10 percent penalty may apply | No penalty on inherited withdrawals |
| Required withdrawals begin | At your own required age | Based on the deceased's timeline |
| Best when | You will not need the money soon | You are under 59 and a half and need cash |

A younger spouse who needs money before 59 and a half often keeps an inherited IRA first and rolls over later. An older spouse often rolls over. Neither choice fits everyone.

## Taxes on what you take out

- **Traditional IRA and 401(k):** withdrawals are generally taxed as ordinary income to the beneficiary. Inherited money in these accounts has no stepped-up basis.
- **Inherited Roth IRA:** qualified withdrawals are generally income-tax-free if the Roth was open at least five years. A Roth has no required yearly withdrawals for the original owner, but a non-spouse beneficiary still faces the 10-year emptying deadline. Spouses have extra options. <!-- verify: five-year rule for Roth beneficiaries -->
- **Roth conversion of an inherited IRA:** a non-spouse beneficiary cannot convert an inherited traditional IRA to a Roth. A spouse who rolls over can later convert as the owner.

## Naming a successor beneficiary

Many custodians let a beneficiary name their own successor on an inherited IRA. The successor then usually follows the original 10-year clock, or a shorter one. Ask for the custodian's form. If none exists, the account may fall back to the beneficiary's estate.

## Common mistakes

- Taking the whole balance in one year and landing in the top bracket
- Cashing out a 401(k) when a direct transfer to an inherited IRA was possible
- Assuming grandchildren count as eligible
- Missing a yearly withdrawal when the owner was already taking required distributions
- Naming the estate when a person would have gotten more time

## When to talk to an attorney

- A trust is named as beneficiary of the account
- A beneficiary has a disability or receives means-tested benefits
- Several beneficiaries share one account and need to split it
- You are not sure whether the owner reached the required beginning date
- A spouse is choosing between rollover and inherited IRA
