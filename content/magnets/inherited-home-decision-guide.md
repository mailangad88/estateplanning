**How to use this guide.** A house is often the largest asset in an estate and the one with the most feelings attached. Read the first three sections before you decide anything. Then fill in the decision matrix, alone or with your siblings. Nothing here is advice for your situation, and rules on authority, taxes and mortgages vary. Bring your answers to an attorney and a tax professional.

You almost never have to decide in the first few weeks. What you do have to do is keep the house protected while you decide.

## Step 1: Who has authority to act

Until the title is sorted out, you may not be allowed to sell, rent or even change much. Who has authority depends on how the house was owned.

| How the house was owned | Who generally acts | What to confirm |
|---|---|---|
| In the person's name alone, with a will | The executor, once appointed by the court | Court appointment and letters |
| In the person's name alone, no will | An administrator named by the court, with heirs set by state law | Who the court appoints |
| In a revocable trust | The successor trustee | The trust document and a certification of trust |
| Jointly with survivorship | The surviving owner | A recorded death certificate or affidavit |
| With a transfer on death deed | The named beneficiary, after recording paperwork | State recording requirements |

Heirs who have only been told "the house is yours" often cannot sign a listing agreement or a sale contract until the right person has authority. A title company can usually tell you what it will accept. See [Probate vs. Non-Probate Assets](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## Step 2: Protect the house while you decide

These tasks usually need to be done whether you keep, rent or sell.

- [ ] Change or secure the locks, with the executor's agreement.
- [ ] Keep utilities on at a low level to prevent frozen pipes or mold.
- [ ] Forward mail and check for tax or utility notices.
- [ ] Take photos of every room as it stands.
- [ ] Remove cash, medications, firearms, papers and valuables, with a record of who took what.
- [ ] Keep paying the property tax, insurance and any mortgage from estate funds.
- [ ] Do not distribute or give away items until the executor has agreed.

### Insurance on a vacant house

A standard homeowner policy may limit or end coverage when a house is empty for a stretch of time. The length of that stretch, and what happens, depends on the policy. Call the agent soon, tell them the owner has died, and ask whether the policy still applies and whether a vacant-home policy is needed. Do not assume you are covered. A fire or burst pipe in an uninsured house can wipe out much of an inheritance.

## Step 3: What happens with a mortgage

A mortgage does not usually vanish at death. The debt stays with the estate, and the lender still has a lien on the house.

In general terms, federal law restricts lenders from demanding immediate full repayment when certain relatives, such as a spouse or a child who inherits an occupied home, take over the property. That often lets the heir keep the existing loan in place and continue making payments. Details and eligibility are narrower than that sentence suggests, so ask the servicer in writing, and ask an attorney if the answer is unclear.

- Keep paying on time while you sort it out. Missed payments can lead to foreclosure.
- Ask the servicer what documents they need to talk to you.
- Ask if a reverse mortgage is involved. Those have separate deadlines and rules.

## Step 4: A word on tax basis

When someone inherits property, the tax basis of that property is often **stepped up** to its value at the date of death, rather than carried over from what the original owner paid. In general terms, this can reduce the taxable gain if you sell soon after inheriting. The details depend on how the property was owned and titled, and on current law. Keep the date-of-death appraisal, and ask a tax professional before you sell or rent.

## Step 5: Compare keep, rent and sell

### Keep it

- You may live in it, or hold it for a family member to use.
- You take on property tax, insurance, repairs and utilities.
- Co-heirs who want to keep it together need a clear agreement on who pays and who decides.

### Rent it

- Rental income can help cover costs, but rental also brings repairs, tenants, vacancies and landlord duties.
- Landlord insurance usually differs from a homeowner policy.
- Local laws on leases, deposits, and rent rules vary widely.
- See whether you want to manage it yourself or hire a property manager.

### Sell it

- Selling turns the house into cash that can be divided evenly.
- Costs include repairs, a real estate agent's commission, closing costs and transfer taxes where they apply.
- Probate courts in some states need to approve the sale, or a notice period must run first.

## The decision matrix

Score each option from 1 (poor) to 5 (strong) on each factor. Do this separately if heirs disagree, then compare notes.

| Factor | Keep | Rent | Sell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can we afford carrying costs for the next 12 months? | | | |
| Does anyone want to live there? | | | |
| Is the home in a condition we can maintain? | | | |
| Do we have time and temperament to manage a rental? | | | |
| Is the market favorable? | | | |
| Is there a mortgage we need to deal with? | | | |
| Do heirs agree? | | | |
| Do we need the cash? | | | |
| **Total** | | | |

A high total is a starting point for a conversation, not a verdict.

## Step 6: When siblings disagree

This is where inherited houses cause lasting family damage. A few approaches that families often find workable:

- **Separate the money from the memories.** Agree first on a date by which each person may claim items of sentimental value.
- **Get a neutral value.** A date-of-death appraisal gives everyone the same number.
- **Offer a buyout.** One heir who wants to keep the home may buy out the others, usually at an appraised value, with financing if needed.
- **Set a timeline.** "We will decide by March, and if we cannot, we list the house" ends the drift.
- **Use a mediator.** Many communities have low-cost estate or family mediators.
- **Know the legal default.** In some states, a co-owner can ask a court to order the sale of a jointly inherited house. This is expensive and slow, so most families prefer an agreement.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Title is unclear, or there is a dispute about who owns the house.
- The home has a mortgage, reverse mortgage or a lien.
- Someone is living in the house and does not want to leave.
- Heirs cannot agree and one threatens to sue.
- The house is in another state from where you live.
- You are thinking about selling before the estate has been opened.
