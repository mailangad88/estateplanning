**How to use this guide.** Read it slowly, and do one section at a time. Nothing here has to be finished in a day. Rules and deadlines depend on your state and on the documents involved, so the lists below are a starting point to share with a probate attorney or the court clerk.

## First, three things to know

1. **You do not have to do everything this week.** Most deadlines are measured in months, not days. A handful of things in the first two weeks matter, and the rest can be taken in order.
2. **You may not be the legal decision-maker yet.** Being named executor in a will gives you responsibility, but in most states the court must formally appoint you (issuing "letters testamentary") before you can act with banks or sell property. If there is a living trust, a successor trustee may be able to act without court involvement.
3. **Do not pay bills from your own pocket, and do not sign anything promising to pay the person's debts.** Debts belong to the estate. In general, relatives are not personally responsible for them, with exceptions such as joint account holders, co-signers and, in some states, certain medical or spousal obligations. The FTC explains this in its guidance on debts of a deceased relative.

## Days 1 to 3

- [ ] **Get the official pronouncement.** If the death occurred at home under hospice care, call the hospice nurse. If it was unexpected, call 911 or local emergency services. A physician, coroner or medical examiner makes the pronouncement.
- [ ] **Tell immediate family and close friends.** Tell the employer when you are ready.
- [ ] **Secure the home.** Lock doors, collect valuables, arrange for pets and plants, and hold or forward mail. If the house will be empty, tell the insurance company. Many policies limit coverage for a vacant home after a period, so check the policy.
- [ ] **Contact a funeral home or cremation provider.** Under the FTC's Funeral Rule, you can get itemized price lists, choose items individually, and a funeral home cannot require a casket for direct cremation. You do not have to decide the same day.
- [ ] **Look for pre-arranged wishes.** A letter of instruction, a pre-need funeral contract or a veteran's burial request. The VA provides a headstone or marker for eligible veterans at no cost.
- [ ] **Locate the will and any trust documents.** Check a home safe, files, a lawyer's office or a safe deposit box. Do not alter, discard or hide a will, even if you think it is out of date. Many states require whoever holds a will to file it with the court promptly after death.

What can wait: selling property, closing accounts, deciding who gets what, and any large expense.

## Days 3 to 10

- [ ] **Order certified death certificates.** Many families order 10 to 15 at first, and the funeral home often orders for you. Banks, insurers, pension plans, the DMV, brokerage firms and the Social Security Administration each want an original or certified copy. The state vital records office sets the price per copy.
- [ ] **Notify Social Security.** The funeral home often reports the death, but confirm. No Social Security benefit is payable for the month of death. For example, if the person died in July, the payment that arrives in August (which would be for July) must be returned, along with any that arrive later. The payment received during July, which is for June, is generally kept. An eligible surviving spouse or child may receive a one-time $255 lump-sum death payment, which must be applied for within 2 years of the death. Monthly survivors' benefits may also be available. SSA's number is 1-800-772-1213.
- [ ] **Contact the employer** about final pay, accrued leave, employer life insurance, retirement plans and benefits continuation (COBRA for dependents).
- [ ] **Locate life insurance policies.** If you cannot find them, search mail, bank statements (premium payments) and employer benefits. The NAIC Life Insurance Policy Locator helps, and so do state unclaimed property sites (MissingMoney.com is a coalition of state programs).
- [ ] **Write the deadlines that apply on a calendar** (see the table below).
- [ ] **Hold off on closing bank accounts.** Keep them open until you have legal authority. You can stop non-essential automatic payments later, once you have a clear picture.

## Days 10 to 30

### Consider a probate attorney in this window

You do not always need one. Many estates are simple, and many states have streamlined small-estate processes. If there is real estate, a business, a dispute, property in more than one state, tax questions or a trust, or you simply feel uneasy, a first conversation is often worth it, and many attorneys offer it free. Questions to ask:

- **What will the process look like in my state?** ______________________
- **What is the expected cost?** ______________________
- **How long is it likely to take?** ______________________
- **What can I do myself to save fees?** ______________________

### File the will and begin probate if needed

- [ ] **Deliver the original will to the probate court** in the county where the person lived. Some states set a short deadline.
- [ ] **File the petition for appointment** as personal representative or executor. You usually need the death certificate, the will and contact information for all heirs and beneficiaries.
- [ ] **Wait for letters.** Once the court issues letters (a certified document proving your authority), you can open an estate bank account and begin handling assets. Order several certified copies.
- [ ] **Ask about a small-estate shortcut.** Many states let heirs collect assets by affidavit when the probate estate is under a state-set limit. The limits vary widely by state and asset type. A court clerk or attorney can tell you whether it applies.

### If there is a trust

- [ ] **Successor trustee: read the trust first.** Many trusts, and many states' trust codes, require the trustee to give notice to beneficiaries and heirs within a set period after death.
- [ ] **Get an EIN for the trust** from the IRS (free, using the online Form SS-4 application). A trust that was revocable becomes irrevocable at death and needs its own taxpayer ID.
- [ ] **Do not distribute anything** until debts, taxes and expenses are understood.

### Protect against fraud and identity theft

- [ ] **Notify the three credit bureaus** (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) in writing. Ask them to place a "deceased" flag on the file and remove it from marketing lists. Request copies of the credit report to find accounts you did not know about.
- [ ] **Opt out of mail** at DMAchoice, and ask the USPS to forward mail to you as the representative.
- [ ] **Be careful posting the funeral date and address publicly,** since it can tell people the house is empty.

### Start the inventory

- [ ] **Write down every asset as of the date of death:** bank, brokerage, retirement, real estate, vehicles, valuables, business interests and digital accounts. You will need date-of-death values for the estate's inventory and for the tax basis step-up, which generally resets the cost basis of inherited assets to market value at death (IRC section 1014) and can reduce capital gains for heirs.
- [ ] **Take photos of the house and valuable contents** before anything is moved.
- [ ] **Keep a log** of every dollar spent for the estate, every call and every document. An executor may be entitled to reimbursement, and in some states must give a formal accounting.

## Deadlines that commonly apply

Rules vary by state. Ask the court clerk or an attorney licensed in your state for the exact dates.

## What to do with specific things

- **The house.** Keep it insured and maintained. Do not list it for sale until you have legal authority and, if needed, the court's permission. Under the federal Garn-St Germain Act, a lender generally must let heirs keep paying the loan, so do not stop paying.
- **Vehicle.** Keep it insured. After authority is established, transfer title under your state's DMV rules.
- **Utilities, phone and subscriptions.** Keep what the house needs and cancel the rest later, keeping a list.
- **Credit cards.** Do not pay them from your own money, and do not use the card. Notify the issuers, since creditors file claims against the estate.
- **Digital accounts.** Use legacy tools where they were set up. Most services otherwise want a death certificate and proof of authority. Logging in to someone's accounts without authority can violate a service's terms, and the rules vary by state. The RUFADAA framework gives fiduciaries a defined path.
- **Pets.** Arrange care right away and keep receipts.
- **Retirement accounts and life insurance.** Claim through the beneficiary form, not the will. Each beneficiary claims separately. Before moving or withdrawing from an inherited IRA, understand the SECURE Act 10-year rule, because mistakes can be expensive.

## If family tension rises

Estates are where grief and money meet. A few habits reduce conflict. Keep every beneficiary informed in writing on a regular schedule, and monthly is common. Do not give one family member special access to the house or valuables without agreement. Keep records. If a disagreement turns to threats, a contest or a claim that someone is withholding, that is a good time to speak to an attorney.

## A note about being the executor

You can say no. An executor named in a will generally has the right to decline before taking action. After you accept, you owe duties to the beneficiaries, called fiduciary duties. They include keeping estate funds separate from your own, treating beneficiaries impartially and keeping records. Mistakes made out of ignorance are usually correctable if caught early. Most executors can be reimbursed for expenses, and many states allow reasonable compensation.

## When to talk to an attorney

- There is real estate, a business, a trust, or property in more than one state.
- The estate may owe taxes.
- Someone is contesting, or threatening to contest, the will.
- There is no will, or a beneficiary is a minor or has special needs.
- You cannot locate assets or heirs, or creditor claims exceed the assets.
- You would simply like someone to take the process off your plate. Ask about fees up front: hourly, flat or percentage, and what you can do yourself to save cost.
