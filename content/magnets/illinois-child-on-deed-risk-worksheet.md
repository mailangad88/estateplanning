## How to use this worksheet

Many parents add a child to the deed to avoid probate. It can work, and it can also cause problems that show up years later. Fill in the tables, then compare. Illinois answers are given where we could confirm them. Other states differ. For a related worksheet, see the [house in trust deed worksheet](/free/house-in-trust-deed-worksheet).

## What a deed change does

Adding a child is a present transfer. The child becomes an owner now, not at your death.

| Question | Answer when a child is added as a joint owner |
|---|---|
| Does the child own part of the house today? | Yes. They have a legal interest from the date the deed is recorded |
| Can you sell without the child? | Generally no. The child must sign |
| Can the child's creditors reach their share? | Often yes |
| Does it avoid probate? | Often yes for a surviving joint owner, if the deed is set up for survivorship |

## Risk table

Check the ones that apply to your child.

| If this happens | What it can do to your home |
|---|---|
| The child is sued | A judgment may attach to the child's interest in your house |
| The child files bankruptcy | The child's share may be part of the bankruptcy estate |
| The child divorces | The spouse may claim the interest as marital property, depending on how the share was acquired |
| The child dies before you | Their share may pass to their spouse or children, not back to you |
| The child refuses to sell or sign | You may be stuck, or face a partition suit |
| The child moves and stops caring for the house | You have no easy way to remove them |

## Gift tax and basis

A gift of a share of the house is a gift for federal tax purposes. You may need to file a gift tax return if the value is above the annual exclusion, though tax is usually not due until the lifetime exemption is used up. <!-- verify: annual gift exclusion and exemption amounts for 2026 -->

The bigger issue is basis. Basis is the starting number for computing a taxable gain.

| How the child receives the house | Child's usual basis |
|---|---|
| Gift during life | Your original cost (carryover) |
| Inherited at death | Value at your death (often a step-up) |

## Illinois property tax and transfer tax

Illinois does not have a California-style reassessment on transfer, but exemptions such as the homestead exemption are tied to the owner who lives there and filed. After a deed change, check with the county assessor that your exemptions remain. <!-- verify: how a co-owner deed affects Illinois homestead exemptions --> The state real estate transfer tax may not apply to a transfer with no payment. <!-- verify: 35 ILCS 200/31-45 exemption for deeds with no consideration -->

## Medicaid

A transfer for less than full value can create a penalty when applying for long-term care Medicaid. The look-back is 60 months. Giving a child part of your home within that period can delay benefits. Read [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning) and talk to an attorney before any transfer if care is a possibility.

## Compare the options

| Option | Child owns now? | Control kept | Step-up at death | Probate avoided |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deed child on as joint owner | Yes | Shared | Partial | Usually |
| Gift the whole house now | Yes | None | No | Yes |
| Illinois TOD instrument, 755 ILCS 27 | No, only at death | Full | Yes | Yes, if recorded |
| Revocable living trust | No | Full | Yes | Yes |
| Leave by will | No | Full | Yes | No |

The TOD instrument must be signed, notarized and recorded before the owner's death. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 27 execution and recording requirements --> See the [Illinois TOD instrument guide](/free/illinois-tod-instrument-guide).

## Quitclaim or warranty deed

A quitclaim deed passes whatever interest the giver has, with no promise about the title. A warranty deed promises that the title is clear. Family transfers often use a quitclaim deed, since neither side needs the warranty, but a title company may require more. Either deed must be signed, notarized and recorded with the county recorder.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are considering Medicaid or long-term care
- You have more than one child
- The home has grown a lot in value
- You have a mortgage, since the lender may have rights
- The child has creditors or a shaky marriage
