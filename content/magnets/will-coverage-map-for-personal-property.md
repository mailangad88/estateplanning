## How to use this worksheet

A will controls only property that is in your name alone with no beneficiary or co-owner. Everything else follows its title or its beneficiary form. Walk through the map, mark where each item lands, and note the gaps. Rules vary by state, so this is general education.

## Tangible personal property

Tangible personal property means things you can touch and move: furniture, jewelry, art, tools, clothing, collections. Cars, boats and other titled vehicles are tangible too, but they have title papers that matter. Cash, accounts and shares are not tangible, they are intangible property.

## Coverage map

| Item | Does the will control it? | What controls it instead |
|---|---|---|
| Car or boat in your sole name | Yes, usually, through probate | The title. Some states allow a TOD title |
| Car or boat titled jointly | Often no | Survivorship wording on the title |
| Jewelry and personal items | Yes, if in your name alone | Gifts made during life stay with the recipient |
| Storage unit contents | Yes, if you own the contents | The rental agreement controls the unit and key |
| Farm equipment and livestock | Yes, if in your name | An LLC or trust if the farm is held in one |
| Firearms | Yes, but transfer rules apply | Federal and state transfer laws, see the [gun owners checklist](/free/gun-owners-estate-checklist) |
| Safe deposit box contents | Yes, if owned by you | Access rules at the bank |
| Timeshare | Often yes, but contracts matter | The timeshare contract and deed. See the [timeshare exit checklist](/free/timeshare-and-vacation-club-exit-checklist) |
| Property in another state | The will may govern, but that state's court often must be involved | That state's law. See the [real estate in two states guide](/free/real-estate-in-two-states-guide) |
| Items in a revocable trust | No | The trust |
| Anything with a named beneficiary | No | The beneficiary form |

## Listing items: in the will or a memorandum

| Choice | Good when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Name the item in the will | It is valuable, or only one person should get it | You must update by new will or codicil if you sell it |
| Use a separate memorandum | You have many small items that may change | Whether your state honors it. Illinois, for example, has no general rule that a signed list binds the executor <!-- verify: Illinois treatment of personal property memoranda --> |
| Use general language | Items are of little value | Disputes over who gets what |

See the [personal property memorandum template](/free/personal-property-memorandum-template) for a form to start from.

## Leaving a specific item

A clear gift names the item, describes it and names the person. For example:

- "My grandmother's wedding ring, a gold band with a small diamond, to my niece Anna."
- "My upright piano, which is in my living room, to my brother Tom."
- "My collection of watches, to be divided among my children in turns."

Add a backup recipient in case the first one has died. Say who pays for shipping or storage. Say what happens if no one wants an item.

## Items that need extra thought

- **Firearms.** Transfer rules differ by state and by weapon type.
- **Antiques and art.** Get a written value and photos.
- **Jewelry.** Appraise it, label it, and say where it is stored.
- **Vehicles.** Check title status and loans.
- **Storage units.** Tell the executor where the key and contract are. Missed rent can lead to a sale.

## Who gets belongings with no will

If you die without a will, state law says who inherits, usually in the order of spouse, children, parents, then siblings and more distant relatives. Illinois law is in 755 ILCS 5/2-1. <!-- verify: Illinois intestacy order for a spouse with and without descendants --> The family then divides personal belongings, with no instructions about who gets the ring or the piano. See [what happens if you die without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will).

## Property in another state

Real property in another state usually needs to be handled under that state's law, and sometimes a second probate case. Tangible items kept in another state raise the same question. A trust can avoid the second case. See [probate vs non-probate assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## Common mistakes

- Assuming the will controls a jointly titled car
- Listing items you no longer own
- Leaving a gun without checking transfer rules
- Not telling anyone where the storage unit key is
- Forgetting to update the list after a move

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own property in more than one state
- You own firearms, a farm or valuable collections
- You want a list that will bind your executor
- A family member may dispute who gets an item
- Your belongings include titled assets with loans
