Advance care planning means deciding, before a crisis, who will speak for you and what you would want. This course takes five days, one small task a day. It is general education, not legal advice, and rules vary by state.

## Day 1: Who will speak for you if you cannot

If you cannot make medical decisions, someone will make them for you. Without a document, state law picks. In Illinois, for example, the Health Care Surrogate Act lists a guardian, then a spouse, then adult children, and so on. The person the law picks may not be the person you would pick.

A health care power of attorney lets you name your own agent. The agent speaks only when you cannot. Look for someone who is calm under pressure, will follow your wishes even if they disagree, can be reached quickly, and will ask doctors hard questions. A good fit is not always the oldest child or the closest relative.

Name a backup too. People get sick, travel or say no.

A quick test: would you trust this person to say "I know this is what she would want" in a hospital hallway? If so, keep going.

**Today's task:** Write the names of two people you would trust as agent and backup. Next to each, write one sentence about why. Do not ask them yet.

## Day 2: Ask them, and say what you want

Choosing an agent is only half the job. Call or meet the person and ask whether they are willing. Be specific: "If I could not speak for myself, would you speak for me and follow what I tell you?"

Then talk about what matters. You do not need a script. You can start with a story: a friend's hospital stay, a relative's final year. Say what you would and would not want. Ask what worries them. If they say no, that is a useful answer, and it is better to hear it now.

Tell your agent where the documents will be. Give them copies. Tell your family who the agent is, so no one is surprised at the bedside.

**Today's task:** Ask your first choice if they are willing. Write down what you told them about your wishes in three sentences.

## Day 3: Turn values into a living will

A living will states your wishes about treatments such as CPR, breathing machines and feeding tubes if you have a terminal condition or permanent unconsciousness, depending on the form and state. Forms with yes or no boxes can feel hard because real illness is rarely simple.

Begin with values. What does a good day look like? What would you not accept? Then test choices against scenarios: a stroke with a chance of recovery, advanced dementia, a terminal illness. Many people want a "trial period" for certain treatments instead of a flat yes or no. Ask your doctor what each treatment means for someone with your health history.

A living will and a health care power of attorney work together. The agent applies your wishes. Read [living will versus health care power of attorney](/compare/living-will-vs-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

**Today's task:** Write one paragraph, in your own words, about what would matter most if you were seriously ill. This is not a legal document yet. It is the draft for one.

## Day 4: POLST and when a form is a medical order

A living will is a statement of wishes. A POLST (Practitioner Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) is a medical order signed by a practitioner and the patient or the patient's legal representative. The Illinois Department of Public Health says people with a serious, progressive illness, including frailty, who are nearing the end of life should consider one, along with people who do not want CPR. It is voluntary. A facility cannot require one as a condition of treatment or admission.

Most healthy adults do not need a POLST. It is for people whose doctors expect serious decline. If that is you or a parent, ask the doctor whether a POLST fits. In some states the form has a different name, so ask.

Make sure the POLST, the living will and your agent's understanding all match. A conflict between forms causes confusion at the worst time.

**Today's task:** Ask yourself whether a POLST applies to you or someone you care for. If it might, write one question for the next doctor visit.

## Day 5: HIPAA, copies and the family talk

Privacy law can slow a family down. The federal privacy rule lets providers share information with family and friends involved in your care unless you object, but providers can be cautious. A signed HIPAA release naming your agent and others removes doubt. Many estate plans include one.

Then make the paperwork findable. Give copies to your agent, doctor and hospital. Tell your family where the originals are. Keep a note in your wallet or phone about who your agent is and how to reach them.

Hold one family conversation: who your agent is, what your wishes are, and what you ask of everyone else. Siblings who hear it from you are less likely to argue later.

**Today's task:** Sign a HIPAA release if you do not have one, and send copies of your documents to your agent. Then schedule a family conversation.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want your documents to meet your state's signing rules
- You have moved states or own a home in more than one
- Your family disagrees about who should be your agent
- You have a serious diagnosis and decisions are close
- Your existing documents are old or conflict with each other
