## How to use this checklist

A prenuptial agreement (before marriage) or postnuptial agreement (after) can set what each spouse keeps in a divorce and sometimes at death. It does not replace an estate plan. Wills, trusts and beneficiary forms can contradict it, and the form often wins. Use this checklist to line them up. State law controls whether and how these agreements work, so have an attorney in your state review everything.

## Step 1: Know what the agreement says about death

Find your agreement and mark each topic it covers.

- [ ] Which assets are separate property
- [ ] Which assets are marital property
- [ ] Whether either spouse waives the right to claim part of the other's estate
- [ ] Whether either spouse waives rights to a home, pension or retirement account
- [ ] Whether one spouse promises to leave something to the other
- [ ] Support, such as life insurance to cover a spouse or children
- [ ] How the agreement treats children from earlier relationships
- [ ] Whether it ends at divorce, death or both

- **Date signed:** ______________________
- **Attorneys who prepared it:** ______________________
- **Where the original is kept:** ______________________

Many agreements focus on divorce and say little about death. If yours is silent, state law may give a surviving spouse rights you did not expect.

## Step 2: Check the retirement plan trap

Employer retirement plans such as a 401(k) usually give a spouse a legal right to the account. Federal rules generally require the spouse to sign a separate waiver to name someone else, and that waiver typically cannot be signed before the wedding. A prenup alone often does not do it. Ask the plan administrator for its waiver form and whether it needs a witness or notary.

IRAs follow different rules, and community property states can add consent requirements.

- [ ] List each employer plan
- [ ] Ask the plan for a spousal consent form
- [ ] Sign it after the wedding if you intend a different beneficiary
- [ ] Keep the signed copy with the prenup

Common errors to look for:

- [ ] A former partner is still named
- [ ] "Estate" is named by default
- [ ] A promise in the agreement (such as life insurance for a spouse) has no form behind it
- [ ] A minor child is named directly

## Step 4: Review title to property

- [ ] Home: is it in one name, both names or a trust?
- [ ] Does joint title send it to the survivor, whatever the prenup says?
- [ ] Property bought before the marriage: is it still held separately?
- [ ] Accounts that were separate: has money been mixed into joint accounts?
- [ ] Business interests: do any agreements limit who can inherit?

Separate property can lose that status when it is mixed with marital money or when the other spouse is added to title. Ask your attorney how this works in your state.

## Step 5: Update your will and trust

- [ ] Will names an executor and guardian for minor children
- [ ] Will matches promises in the agreement
- [ ] Trust terms match the agreement
- [ ] Any gift to a spouse is stated clearly, including how it is calculated
- [ ] Powers of attorney and health care directives name the right agents
- [ ] Documents say what happens if the marriage ends. Some states treat a spouse as removed after divorce, and others do not for all forms.

## Step 6: Keep fairness and disclosure in view

Courts often examine whether each person understood and agreed. Common safeguards include full written disclosure of assets, separate attorneys, enough time before the wedding and no pressure.

- **Date each side received the draft:** ______________________
- **Date asset lists were exchanged:** ______________________
- **Wedding date:** ______________________
- [ ] Each spouse had their own attorney
- [ ] Asset lists are attached to the agreement

## Step 7: Timeline

| When | Task |
|---|---|
| Before signing | Disclose assets, hire separate counsel, ask how death is handled |
| Before the wedding | Write down new will and beneficiary changes you plan |
| Within weeks after the wedding | Sign spousal waivers, update beneficiary forms |
| Every year | Review forms and statements |
| After any big change (baby, move, sale, inheritance) | Review the agreement and plan |

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your agreement was signed years ago, in another state or without lawyers
- You want a spouse to waive rights to a retirement plan
- Either spouse has children from an earlier relationship
- You own a business or have large separate property
- You live in a community property state or have moved
- You are thinking about a postnuptial agreement
