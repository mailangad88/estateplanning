## How to use this checklist

We are sorry for your loss. A vehicle looks simple, but how it transfers depends on how it was titled, whether there is a loan, and your state's rules. Some states have a short process for vehicles, and some treat them like any other probate asset. This list works in any state. Check each answer with your motor vehicle agency, and use an attorney when the process is unclear.

## Step 1: Keep it safe and legal

- [ ] Park the vehicle somewhere secure
- [ ] Do not drive it until you know that it is insured and that you are allowed to drive it
- [ ] Keep the keys in one place and note who has copies
- [ ] Take photos of the vehicle, inside and out, and of the odometer
- [ ] Check for personal items and documents in the glove box and trunk

## Step 2: Find the paperwork

- [ ] Certificate of title (the original, not a copy)
- [ ] Current registration and insurance card
- [ ] Loan or lease papers
- [ ] Recent service records
- [ ] Death certificate copies
- [ ] Letters of office or other proof of authority, if there is an estate
- [ ] Will, if one exists

- **VIN:** ____________________
- **Odometer reading at death:** ____________________
- **Title held in the name of:** ____________________
- **Lienholder (lender), if any:** ____________________
- **Loan balance:** ____________________

## Step 3: Work out how it transfers

| How it was owned | What usually happens | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| Joint with a surviving owner, with survivorship wording | Surviving owner may retitle with a death certificate and form | Does the title say "or" or "and"? |
| Single owner, with a named beneficiary on the title (offered in some states) | Beneficiary retitles with the death certificate | Does our state allow beneficiaries on a car title? |
| Single owner, no beneficiary, small estate | Some states allow a short affidavit | What is the value limit and the waiting period? |
| Single owner, estate in probate | Executor signs after being appointed | Do I need letters of office, and are certified copies required? |
| Owned by a trust | The trustee transfers it | Does the trust document name the trustee's powers? |
| Leased | The lease company controls the transfer | What does the lease say about death? |

Value limits, waiting periods and forms vary by state. See [probate vs non-probate assets](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets), and for Illinois, the [small estate affidavit guide](/free/illinois-small-estate-affidavit-guide).

## Step 4: Deal with a loan or lease

- [ ] Call the lender and report the death
- [ ] Ask for the payoff amount and the payment status
- [ ] Ask what options the lender allows: keep paying, refinance, sell or surrender the car
- [ ] Do not stop paying without asking, since late payments can lead to repossession
- [ ] Keep paying from the estate account if you have authority

If the loan is more than the car is worth, tell the executor and ask an attorney before you pay anything personally. Most states do not make family members responsible for a dead person's loans, but a co-signer is different.

## Step 5: Fix the insurance

- [ ] Call the insurer and report the death
- [ ] Ask whether coverage continues while the vehicle sits or is retitled
- [ ] Ask what coverage applies if a family member will drive it
- [ ] Remove the deceased driver from the policy and add the new owner
- [ ] Do not cancel until the vehicle is sold or insured by the new owner
- [ ] Ask for a refund of unused premium

A car that is uninsured can leave you liable after an accident. Confirm coverage in writing.

## Step 6: Choose what to do

### If a family member will keep it

- [ ] Complete your state's title transfer forms
- [ ] Pay any title fees and taxes (some states waive tax between certain family members)
- [ ] Register and insure the car in the new owner's name
- [ ] Update the registration address

### If you will sell it

- [ ] Get an idea of its value from a valuation guide, a dealer quote and local listings
- [ ] Confirm who has authority to sign the title
- [ ] Pay off the loan, if any, from the sale price
- [ ] Complete the title and a bill of sale
- [ ] Report the odometer reading as the law requires
- [ ] Deposit the proceeds in the estate account and record them in the [estate ledger](/free/estate-accounting-ledger-template)
- [ ] Notify the motor vehicle agency that the car was sold, and remove the plates if your state requires

### If you will donate it

- [ ] Choose a recognised charity and ask for the paperwork
- [ ] Keep the receipt for the estate's records
- [ ] Ask a tax professional about the estate's deduction

## Step 7: Close out loose ends

- [ ] Cancel toll transponders and subscription services tied to the car, such as connected-car apps
- [ ] Cancel the vehicle registration, if not transferring
- [ ] Return parking permits and any placards for disability access
- [ ] Remove personal data from the car's navigation and phone connections
- [ ] Keep copies of every document for the estate file

## Questions to ask the motor vehicle office

1. What do you have to see for a surviving owner or beneficiary?
2. Is there a short process for a small estate?
3. Which forms need a notary or a certified copy?
4. Are there late fees, and when do they start?
5. What if the title was lost?

## When to talk to an attorney

- The car is owned by several people or its title is unclear
- The loan balance is more than the vehicle's worth
- There is a dispute among heirs about who keeps it
- The vehicle is a collector car or of unusual value
- The title is lost, or the owner lived in a different state
- You are not sure whether you have authority to sign
