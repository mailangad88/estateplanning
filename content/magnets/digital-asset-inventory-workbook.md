**How to use this workbook.** Work through one table at a time. Fill in what exists, where it lives and what you want done with it. Do not write passwords, PINs or recovery phrases in these tables. Write where they are stored instead. Plan on one to two hours for a first pass, then update it once a year.

## Why a written inventory helps

Your executor can only deal with accounts they know exist. Many people hold dozens of logins: email, banks, photo storage, streaming, shopping, work tools. A single missed account can mean lost photos, a subscription that keeps billing or a crypto balance no one can reach.

This workbook goes deeper than the short [Digital Assets Inventory checklist](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory). That list tells you what to think about. These pages give you somewhere to write it down.

## Part 1: What never goes in plain text

Do not put any of these in a document, spreadsheet or note that is not protected:

- Account passwords
- Bank PINs and debit card numbers
- Seed phrases or private keys for crypto wallets
- Answers to security questions
- The master password for your password manager
- Full Social Security numbers

A printed sheet in a drawer can be photographed. A plain document on a shared computer can be copied. A cloud note can be exposed by one weak password.

### Safer alternatives

| Item | Safer place to keep it |
|---|---|
| Everyday passwords | A reputable password manager, with an emergency access feature if it has one |
| Master password and recovery kit | A sealed envelope in a fireproof box or a safe deposit box, with a note of who may open it |
| Crypto seed phrase | Written or stamped on durable material, stored in two separate secure places |
| Device passcodes | Listed in the password manager or in the sealed envelope |
| Two-factor codes | Saved backup codes, stored with the master password envelope |

Tell your executor the location, not the contents. For example: "My password manager emergency kit is in the grey box in the hall closet."

## Part 3: Platform legacy tools

Many large technology companies now let you name a person who may be contacted, or who may request limited access, after you die. Some also let you set an inactive account timer, after which the account is shared or deleted. Names, steps and limits change often and differ by company.

Common patterns include:

- A legacy contact who can download certain data or manage a memorial page
- An inactivity setting that sends data to a trusted person after a set period
- A request process for family that requires a death certificate and proof of authority

Check the settings page of each major account you use. Record in the tables above whether you turned a tool on and who you named. Make sure the person you named matches the person you want, since that choice may be separate from your will.

## Part 4: State law basics (RUFADAA)

Most states have adopted a version of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, often called RUFADAA. It sets rules for when an executor or agent can see digital accounts. Details differ by state, so ask an attorney licensed in yours.

In general terms:

- Your own instructions in an online tool usually come first
- Next come instructions in your will, trust or power of attorney
- If neither exists, the company's terms of service apply
- The content of emails and messages is often treated more strictly than a list of accounts

This is why a written plan matters. Your will or power of attorney can state that your fiduciary may access digital assets. Without it, a company may refuse, even when family asks politely.

## Part 5: Instructions for your digital executor

Your digital executor is the person who carries out your wishes for online accounts. They may be the same person as your executor. Fill in a page for them.

- **Digital executor:** ______________________
- **Backup person:** ______________________
- **Phone and email:** ______________________
- **Where the inventory is kept:** ______________________
- **Where the password manager kit is kept:** ______________________

Tell them in plain words:

- [ ] Which accounts to preserve (photos, documents, email archives)
- [ ] Which accounts to close and in what order
- [ ] Not to delete anything until copies are saved
- [ ] Which email to keep open longest, since it receives reset messages
- [ ] Who should post a notice on social media
- [ ] Who to ask for help with crypto or a web business
- [ ] To keep copies of death certificates for company requests

### Common mistakes

- Closing the email account first, which locks them out of everything else
- Forgetting two-factor codes tied to a phone number that will be disconnected
- Leaving subscriptions running for months
- Naming a digital executor but never telling them

## When to talk to an attorney

- You hold meaningful cryptocurrency or digital business income
- You own a domain or website that earns money
- You want your will, trust or power of attorney to give clear digital access powers
- You live in more than one state or recently moved
- You are unsure whether a company legacy tool conflicts with your will
