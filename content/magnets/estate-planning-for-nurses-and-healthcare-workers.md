## How to use this guide

Health care workers know better than most what happens when no one knows a patient's wishes. You may also work nights, weekends and holidays, which makes the question "who has my children if something happens?" more than theoretical. This guide helps you act on what you have seen. Laws on directives and guardians vary by state, so use it to prepare for an attorney in your state.

## Start with what you have seen

Before you fill in a form, write down what you have witnessed. It is easier to choose well when you are specific.

- **Situations I never want to be in (for example, long-term ventilation with no hope of recovery):** ______________________
- **Situations where I would accept treatment, even difficult treatment:** ______________________
- **What matters most to me at the end of life (comfort, being awake, being at home):** ______________________
- **What I have seen families struggle with most:** ______________________
- **What I have seen go smoothly, and why:** ______________________

## Your health care power of attorney

A health care power of attorney names a person to make medical decisions when you cannot. It is the document families most often wish they had. Our comparison of a [living will and a health care power of attorney](/compare/living-will-vs-healthcare-power-of-attorney) shows how they work together.

Choose an agent who:

- Will honor your wishes, even when they disagree
- Can speak with doctors without freezing
- Can be reached quickly, or has a backup who can
- Knows your preferences because you have talked about them

Medical staff sometimes pick a spouse or parent by default. If you are a nurse, do you want a colleague in that role? That is your call, but your agent should be someone who can handle conflict with family.

- **Agent:** ______________________
- **Backup agent:** ______________________
- **Have we talked through specific scenarios?** ______________________

## Your living will or directive

A living will states treatment preferences. Many health care workers say they prefer specific language about:

- CPR and defibrillation
- Ventilators and breathing tubes
- Feeding tubes
- Dialysis
- Comfort care and pain control
- Organ and tissue donation

Be careful with vague words such as "heroic measures." Say what you mean in terms of outcomes. Some states also offer portable medical orders, often called POLST or similar names, for people who are seriously ill. These are medical orders, not a substitute for a directive. Ask your doctor and your attorney.

- [ ] Directive signed with the formalities your state requires
- [ ] Copies with my agent, my doctor and my employer's health file if I choose
- [ ] A conversation held with my agent and family
- [ ] A wallet card listing my agent and where my documents are

## Planning for children with unpredictable shifts

If you work nights, double shifts or out-of-state contracts, the first hours after an accident matter.

- [ ] Guardian named in a will, with a backup. See [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children)
- [ ] A short-term caregiver who can pick up your kids at once, even if the guardian lives far away
- [ ] A written authorization letting that caregiver consent to medical care in an emergency, if your state allows it
- [ ] Emergency contact lists at school and child care that include that caregiver
- [ ] A plan for a spouse or partner who works the same schedule

Shift-night emergency plan:

- **Who is the first call if I am hurt on shift:** ______________________
- **Who has a key and the code to the house:** ______________________
- **Where the children's documents and medical information are:** ______________________
- **Who tells my employer and unit supervisor:** ______________________

## Financial power of attorney

An accident, illness or injury can take you off the job. A financial power of attorney lets a trusted person pay bills and handle accounts. Consider whether your agent has the time and judgment, and whether they can serve as long as you may be away.

## Benefits worth checking

| Benefit | What to check |
|---|---|
| Employer-paid life insurance | Beneficiary on file, and whether it ends if you leave |
| Supplemental life or accidental death | Beneficiary and coverage amount |
| Retirement plan (401(k), 403(b), pension) | Primary and backup beneficiary |
| Short- and long-term disability | Who is notified and how benefits are claimed |
| Malpractice or occupational coverage | Whether tail coverage affects survivors or former employers |
| Health savings account | Beneficiary |
| Unpaid wages and paid time off | How the employer pays after a death |

Beneficiary forms override your will. See the [beneficiary designations guide](/guides/beneficiary-designations). Name a trust or trustee rather than a young child.

## Temporary and travel contracts

Travel nurses and per diem staff often have several employers, benefit plans and states of license. List each one. Some benefits do not follow you. If you live in one state and work in another, ask an attorney which state's rules govern your documents. Most states accept directives signed elsewhere in some form, but not all treat them the same way.

## A two-page review

- [ ] Health care power of attorney signed
- [ ] Living will signed and discussed
- [ ] HIPAA release naming agent
- [ ] Will with guardian and executor
- [ ] Financial power of attorney
- [ ] Beneficiaries on every account
- [ ] Emergency plan for children
- [ ] Copies stored where agent can reach them without you

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have children and no guardian named
- You live in one state and work or contract in others
- You are divorced or in a blended family and want your wishes followed
- You want treatment preferences that go beyond a form's checkboxes
- You carry student debt, a mortgage or a business and want it addressed
- You have a complex benefit package, such as a pension and a deferred compensation plan
