**How to use this roadmap.** If you are the executor, you may feel like you were handed a job with no manual. This is the manual for the long middle, after the first 30 days. Read the phase table once, then work one phase at a time. Keep the log at the end open on your desk. Rules, deadlines and forms vary by state, so treat the timing here as general and confirm it with the court or an attorney licensed in the right state.

You do not have to do this quickly. Most estates take many months, and some take over a year. Steady and well documented beats fast.

## The roadmap at a glance

| Phase | What happens | Typical tasks | What often slows it down |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Appointment | The court (or the will) gives you legal authority | File the will, petition, take the oath, get letters | Court backlog, a contested will, a missing original will |
| 2. Setup | You open the estate's own finances | Get an EIN, open the estate account, redirect mail | Banks asking for extra documents |
| 3. Inventory | You find and value what the estate owns | List assets, order appraisals, file the inventory if required | Hard-to-value items, accounts you did not know about |
| 4. Creditors | Debts are identified and notice is given | Publish or mail notice, review claims, set a claims deadline | Notice periods that vary by state |
| 5. Bills and taxes | Valid debts and taxes are paid | Pay in the right order, file final returns | Unclear priority rules, missing tax records |
| 6. Accounting | You show where the money went | Prepare a ledger, send it to heirs or the court | Poor record keeping along the way |
| 7. Distribution | Heirs receive what is left | Get approval or releases, distribute, collect receipts | Disputes, real estate that has not sold |
| 8. Closing | The estate ends | Final account, file closing papers, close the account | Waiting on tax clearance or a late claim |

## Phase 1: Appointment and letters

An executor named in a will has no legal power until the court appoints them. The court then issues a document, often called **letters testamentary** (or **letters of administration** if there is no will). Some states use other names. Banks, brokers and title companies ask to see these letters, usually a recent certified copy.

- Order several certified copies. Many institutions want their own.
- Some states offer a simplified process for small estates. Whether yours does, and the dollar limit, varies by state.
- Some states ask for a bond. Many wills waive it, but a court may still require one.

## Phase 2: Set up the estate's finances

The estate is treated as separate from you and from the person who died. Keep it that way.

- Apply for an **EIN** (employer identification number) for the estate. The IRS issues it free online.
- Open an estate checking account in the estate's name and EIN. Deposit every payment to the estate there.
- Pay estate expenses from that account, never from your own pocket if you can avoid it, and never from the account of the person who died after it has been closed.
- Forward mail to yourself and watch for bills, statements and tax forms.

**Common mistake:** mixing estate money with your own. Even a well-meant mix-up can look like a problem to heirs and to a court.

## Phase 3: Inventory and appraisal

The inventory is a list of what the estate owns, with values as of the date of death. Start with the [Asset and Account Inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) if the person who died left no list.

- Real estate usually needs an appraisal. Many people use a licensed appraiser for a date-of-death value.
- Vehicles, jewelry, art, collections and business interests may each need a separate value.
- Note which assets pass outside the estate, by beneficiary form, joint ownership or a trust. These are generally not yours to distribute, though you may still need to report them for tax reasons. See [Probate vs. Non-Probate Assets](/learn/probate/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).
- Some courts require you to file a formal inventory by a set deadline. Ask the clerk.

## Phase 4: Creditor notice

Estates generally give creditors a chance to come forward, and then a deadline passes. How that works varies by state. Some require a published notice in a local paper, some require mailing notice to known creditors, and many require both. The window for claims is often measured in months, and the exact number depends on the state.

- Make a list of every debt you know about: credit cards, medical bills, loans, taxes, utilities.
- Review each claim before paying. You are allowed to question a claim that looks wrong.
- Keep every notice you send and every proof of mailing.

## Phase 5: Paying bills in the right order

Most states set a **priority order** for paying debts. When an estate has enough money, the order does not matter much. When it does not, paying the wrong creditor first can make an executor personally answerable in some states.

In general terms, estate administration costs, funeral expenses and certain taxes tend to rank near the top, with ordinary unsecured debts like credit cards further down. Details differ widely. If the estate looks short of cash, pause and ask an attorney before paying anything beyond the basics.

### Taxes in general terms

- **Final personal income tax return.** The last return covers income from January 1 of the year of death through the date of death. A surviving spouse may file jointly in many cases.
- **Estate income tax return.** If the estate earns more than a small amount of income after death, such as interest, dividends or rent, it may need to file its own return.
- **Estate or inheritance tax.** Most estates owe no federal estate tax, since the exemption is $15 million per person for 2026 <!-- verify -->. Some states have their own estate or inheritance tax with lower thresholds.

A tax professional familiar with estates can tell you which returns apply. Do not distribute everything until you know.

## Phase 6: Accounting

An accounting is a clear record of what came in, what went out and what remains. Some courts require a formal one. Even where it is not required, heirs often ask for it, and handing it over early prevents suspicion.

Your log and your estate bank statements will do most of the work. A simple ledger with date, payee, amount, purpose and a receipt number is usually enough to start.

## Phase 7: Distribution

Distribute only after debts, taxes and expenses are paid or properly reserved, and after any required claims period has ended. Many executors keep a reserve for late bills or tax questions before final distribution.

- Follow the will. If there is no will, state law sets who inherits.
- Have each heir sign a receipt for what they received.
- Some executors make a partial distribution early, when the estate is clearly solvent. Rules on this vary.
- Real estate usually needs a deed or court order to transfer. Ask the title company what they will accept.

## Phase 8: Closing

Close the estate only when the work is done. Depending on the state, closing may mean filing a final account, getting heirs' written approval, asking the court to discharge you, or all three. Keep your records for several years after. A tax professional or attorney can advise on how long.

A tip that many executors find useful: send heirs a short update every month or two, even when nothing has changed. Silence is what usually turns grief into suspicion.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The estate may not have enough money to pay all debts.
- Someone is contesting the will or challenging your actions.
- The estate owns real estate in another state or a business.
- You are unsure whether to pay a claim or in what order.
- You are being asked to distribute before the creditor period has run.
- Tax questions go beyond a simple final return.
