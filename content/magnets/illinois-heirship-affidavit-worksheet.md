## How to use this worksheet

We are sorry for your loss. When someone dies, an Illinois court usually needs to know who the heirs are, meaning the people the law says would inherit if there were no will. Even when there is a will, the court and the representative need to know the heirs so they can receive notice. This worksheet helps you gather that information in one place. It is general information, not advice for your estate. An attorney or the clerk of the court can tell you which forms your county uses.

A note on terms. Some people say "affidavit of heirship" for a sworn statement listing the heirs. Illinois courts also use petitions and orders that name the heirs, such as an Order Declaring Heirship in Cook County. Ask the clerk what your case needs.

## Step 2: Who inherits in Illinois when there is no will

Illinois sets an order of heirs in its descent and distribution statute.

| Family situation | Who inherits |
|---|---|
| Spouse and descendants | One half to the spouse, one half to the descendants |
| Descendants, no spouse | All to the descendants |
| Spouse, no descendants | All to the spouse |
| No spouse or descendants | Parents, brothers, sisters and their descendants share, with surviving parents taking double portions |
| None of the above | Grandparents, then great-grandparents, then nearest relatives |
| No heirs at all | Real estate goes to the county, and personal property to the State Treasurer |

Descendants inherit by branches of the family, which lawyers call per stirpes. If a child died before the parent, that child's children share the child's portion. A family tree makes this easy to see.

## Step 4: Documents list

Courts and attorneys often ask for papers that show who the people are and how they are related.

- [ ] Certified death certificate of the person who died
- [ ] Marriage certificate or divorce decree for each marriage
- [ ] Birth certificates for children, or other proof of the parent-child link
- [ ] Adoption records, if any child was adopted
- [ ] Death certificates of any spouse, child or other heir who died earlier
- [ ] Obituary, family Bible pages or old letters that name relatives
- [ ] The will and any codicils, if one exists
- [ ] A list of full names and addresses for every heir and every person named in the will

Which documents the court will accept depends on the case, so ask your attorney or the clerk before ordering many copies.

## Step 5: Sample statement to share with the attorney

Edit this so it matches what you know.

> I am [name], the [relationship] of [name of person who died]. [He/She/They] died on [date] in [county], Illinois. [He/She/They] was married to [name] until [date or death or divorce]. [He/She/They] had [number] children: [names]. [Names] are deceased, leaving children [names]. [He/She/They] had a will dated [date]. To my knowledge, the heirs are [names].

Do not sign anything under oath until the facts are checked and the attorney has reviewed the form.

## If you cannot find a relative

- [ ] Ask family, friends and the funeral home for contact details
- [ ] Check old address books, holiday card lists and social media
- [ ] Write down every step you took to search, with dates
- [ ] Tell the attorney or the court early, because notice to an unknown or missing heir has special rules

## Common mistakes

- Leaving out a child from an earlier relationship, a half sibling or an adopted child.
- Listing someone who is named in the will but not an heir, or the reverse.
- Guessing at a date of death or a spelling of a name.
- Assuming a spouse always inherits everything.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The family is large, spread out or has several marriages.
- An heir cannot be located, or may be a minor.
- A child's parentage or an adoption is in question.
- There is a will and an heir may object.
- You are asked to sign an affidavit or petition under oath.
