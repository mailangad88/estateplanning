## How to use this guide

When an owner of a small business dies, the business keeps running only if the paperwork already says who is in charge and who owns what. This guide covers Illinois LLCs and S corporations. Read the Illinois rules, check your own documents against the checklist, then take your answers to an attorney and a tax professional. It is educational, not advice for your situation.

## What the Illinois LLC Act does at death

Under the Illinois Limited Liability Company Act, the death of an individual member is an event of dissociation. The member stops being a member. The company does not end, and its contracts, property and debts stay in place.

What the deceased owner leaves behind is a distributional interest, which the Act treats as personal property. A transferee, including an estate or heir, receives the economic rights but not automatic voting or management rights. A transferee can become a member if the operating agreement allows it or if the other members consent. Members generally cannot transfer their interests unless the operating agreement permits it or all other members agree.

Here is what that means in practice.

- The operating agreement is the main document. It can override many default rules.
- Without good terms, heirs may hold an interest but not a say, while partners lose a decision-maker.
- A single-member LLC has no partner to step in, so someone must be named who can act right away.

## Operating agreement terms that decide the outcome

- **Death or incapacity events.** Does either trigger a buyout, a vote or a dissolution?
- **Who can become a member.** Family, a spouse, a trust, only existing members?
- **Buyout obligation.** Must the company or the other members buy the interest, or only have the option?
- **Valuation method.** A formula, an appraisal, a set price updated each year?
- **Payment terms.** Lump sum, note over several years, insurance proceeds?
- **Management after death.** A named successor manager, with authority to sign and bank?

## Passing an LLC interest

An interest can pass in several ways. Each has tradeoffs, and the operating agreement must allow it.

| Method | What it does | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Will | The interest goes through probate | Delay while the estate is opened |
| Assignment to a revocable trust | Trust holds the interest during life and passes it at death | Needs the other members' consent if the agreement requires it |
| Buy-sell agreement | Partners or the company buy the interest at death | Must be funded, valued and kept current |
| Beneficiary designation | Rarely available for an LLC interest | Ask whether your agreement or any statute allows it |

The Illinois transfer on death instrument law is for real property. It does not cover a membership interest, though it may cover real estate the company owns or that you hold individually. Do not assume a form you saw online works for an LLC interest.

## S corporations: who can hold the shares

An S corporation can have only eligible shareholders: individuals, certain trusts and estates. It cannot have partnership, corporation or nonresident alien shareholders. It has a limit of 100 shareholders and one class of stock. A trust that takes shares at death must qualify, for example as a qualified subchapter S trust or an electing small business trust, with the required elections. A trust that does not fit can end the S election and trigger tax costs.

- [ ] Ask your tax professional whether your revocable trust is a grantor trust that qualifies today
- [ ] Ask what happens to the shares after you die, and who would make elections and by when
- [ ] Review any shareholder agreement for transfer limits and buyout rights
- [ ] Confirm that no heir would be an ineligible shareholder

## Buy-sell checklist

Use this with your co-owners and your advisors.

- [ ] Triggering events listed: death, disability, divorce, retirement, bankruptcy, a decision to leave
- [ ] Who has the right or duty to buy: the company, the other owners, or both
- [ ] Price method chosen, with a date for the next review
- [ ] Funding source identified, such as life or disability insurance, a sinking fund or a note
- [ ] Owner and beneficiary of each insurance policy match the agreement
- [ ] Family approvals and spousal consents considered
- [ ] Payment schedule, interest and security for any note
- [ ] Noncompete and confidentiality terms for the seller
- [ ] Lender, landlord and key customer consents identified
- [ ] Attorney and tax professional have both reviewed
- [ ] Signed copies stored where a successor can find them

## Common mistakes

- Never updating an operating agreement after the company grows or an owner marries.
- Funding a buyout with life insurance that is owned by the wrong person.
- Leaving a business interest to a spouse and children without telling the other owners.
- Putting S corporation shares into a trust that does not qualify.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own the business with one or more partners and have no signed buy-sell agreement.
- You are a single-member owner and no one can run the company if you cannot.
- Your estate may owe tax, or most of your wealth is in the business.
- You want to move shares or interests into a trust.
- A family member who does not work in the business will inherit part of it.
