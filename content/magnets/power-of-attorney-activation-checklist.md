**How to use this checklist.** This is for the moment you move from "named agent" to "acting agent." It covers the first month of using a financial power of attorney. A separate handbook explains an agent's role in general. Here the focus is activation: proving your authority, getting institutions to cooperate and starting a clean paper trail. Rules vary by state and by the wording of the document, so read the actual document first.

## 1. Is the power of attorney in effect yet?

There are two common types.

- **Immediate:** authority starts when the document is signed. The agent can act as soon as the principal needs help, though the agent should act only as the principal wants while the principal can still decide.
- **Springing:** authority starts only after a stated event, usually a doctor's written statement that the principal cannot manage affairs.

- [ ] Find the signed original or a certified copy.
- [ ] Read the section on when authority begins.
- [ ] If it springs, read who decides and what proof is needed: one doctor, two doctors, a court?
- [ ] Check your name, any co-agent requirements and the successor agent order.
- [ ] Confirm the document was signed and notarized or witnessed as the law of the state requires.

## 2. Capacity: do not rush past it

If the person who signed has lost the ability to understand the document, they cannot sign a new one. If they still can, a new document may be easier than fighting over an old one. A doctor's current opinion helps.

- [ ] Ask the treating doctor for a written statement describing what the person can and cannot manage.
- [ ] If the person can still understand and decide, consider asking them to confirm the arrangement, update the document or sign a bank's form.
- [ ] If there is doubt, speak to an attorney before acting. See the [guide to powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

## 3. Build an acceptance packet

Institutions do not like surprises. Hand them a packet, not a story.

- [ ] Original or certified copy of the power of attorney
- [ ] Your photo ID
- [ ] The principal's photo ID and account details
- [ ] The doctor's letter, if the power of attorney springs
- [ ] A short cover letter stating what you ask them to do
- [ ] A copy of the institution's own power of attorney form, if it has one
- [ ] A signed agent certification, if your state permits one

Sample cover letter wording:

> I am the agent under a power of attorney signed by [Name] on [date]. A copy is enclosed. I ask that you place my authority on file for the accounts listed below and tell me in writing what else you need. If you decline to accept the document, please give me the reason in writing and the name of the person who made the decision.

- **Institution:** ______________________
- **Person contacted:** ______________________
- **Date packet delivered:** ______________________
- **Response:** ______________________

## 4. If a bank refuses

Refusals happen even when the document is valid. Try these steps in order.

- [ ] Ask for the bank's own form and whether it will accept it.
- [ ] Ask for a manager or the legal or compliance department. Branch staff often do not decide these questions.
- [ ] Ask what exact language or evidence is missing.
- [ ] Ask whether the bank has a deadline for review.
- [ ] Some states have laws about how long an institution has to accept or reject a power of attorney and what reasons are allowed. Ask an attorney whether your state has one.
- [ ] If the institution still refuses, ask an attorney about a letter, a new document signed on the institution's form, or a court order.

A court-appointed guardian or conservator is the fallback, but it costs more and takes longer. See [power of attorney versus guardianship](/compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## 5. Keep your money separate

- [ ] Never deposit the principal's money into your own account.
- [ ] Open or keep accounts titled as the principal's, with you signing as agent.
- [ ] Sign every document as: "[Your name], as agent for [Principal's name] under power of attorney."
- [ ] Keep your own funds and expenses apart.
- [ ] Do not make gifts or pay yourself unless the document clearly allows it. Many powers of attorney limit gifts and compensation.

- [ ] Save every statement, receipt and invoice.
- [ ] Note decisions that involve judgment, such as selling property.
- [ ] Tell other relatives what you are doing, as the document allows. Many disputes start with silence.
- [ ] Reimbursement for your own out-of-pocket costs is usually fine if documented. Ask first if unsure.

## 7. Routine tasks in the first month

- [ ] Redirect or monitor mail
- [ ] List all income: Social Security, pensions, annuities
- [ ] List fixed bills and set up autopay if appropriate
- [ ] Check insurance premiums and policies
- [ ] Notify the Social Security Administration if a representative payee is needed. A power of attorney is not automatically recognized by Social Security, which has its own payee process.
- [ ] Find the most recent tax return and ask who prepares it
- [ ] Review accounts for unusual charges and scams

## 8. Know the limits

An agent acts for the principal, not for themselves. Most powers of attorney do not allow you to change a will, change beneficiary designations or make health care decisions, unless the document says so. Ask first before selling a home, moving funds or paying yourself.

## 9. When authority ends

- [ ] The principal dies. Authority ends at death. Estate administration starts.
- [ ] The principal revokes it while competent.
- [ ] A court ends it, or the document says it ends on a date or event.
- [ ] Divorce may end a spouse's authority in some states.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A bank or insurer refuses to accept the document.
- Capacity is unclear and you are not sure whether a new document can be signed.
- Siblings dispute your decisions or ask for an accounting.
- You are considering gifts, a house sale, or moving assets for Medicaid planning.
- You are named agent and the principal has substantial or complex assets.
