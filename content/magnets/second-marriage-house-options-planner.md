## How to use this planner

The house is where blended family plans most often break. A spouse wants security. Children want what they were told they would get. This planner lays out the common choices, who bears the cost and what can go wrong. Property and marital rights vary by state, so confirm the choice with an attorney where you live.

## Start with what you want

Check the lines that sound like you.

- [ ] My spouse should live in the house for life
- [ ] My spouse should live there only while married to me, or only until they remarry
- [ ] My children should get the house when my spouse dies
- [ ] My spouse should be free to sell and downsize
- [ ] I want a cash share for my children now, with the house going to my spouse
- [ ] I am not sure, and I want a plan that works if things change

## Five ways to hold the house

| Method | Spouse after your death | Children's share | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joint tenancy with spouse | Gets everything automatically | None, unless the spouse chooses | Your children can be left out completely |
| Left to children outright | Has no right to stay, unless the law or will gives one | Own it at once | Spouse may have to move |
| Life estate deed | Spouse lives there for life | Take at spouse's death | Little flexibility, and the spouse cannot sell alone |
| Trust with life interest | Spouse lives there, trustee controls | Take at spouse's death or on a set event | Needs a trustee and funds for upkeep |
| Spouse owns, with a pledge to the children | Full owner | Depends on trust | A promise is not binding |

## Joint ownership, with a warning

Putting a spouse on the deed often gives them the whole house at your death, by operation of law. The will does not matter. If you want children to share, a deed is the wrong tool alone. See /compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust.

## Life estate versus trust

A **life estate** gives your spouse the right to live in the house for life. Your children hold the "remainder" and take over at the spouse's death. It is simple but rigid.

A **trust** can do the same and more. The trustee decides when to sell, whether to buy a smaller house and what happens if your spouse moves out. See /free/house-in-trust-deed-worksheet.

| Feature | Life estate | Trust with a life interest |
|---|---|---|
| Written by | Deed | Trust document |
| Sale by spouse | Usually needs the remainder owners to sign | Trustee can sell if the trust allows |
| Remarriage or moving out | Spouse keeps right unless the deed says otherwise | Trust can end the right on stated events |
| Upkeep | Life tenant usually pays taxes and repairs | Trust pays from a fund, or the spouse pays |
| Flexibility | Low | High |

## Who pays

The person with the right to live there often pays property taxes, insurance and ordinary repairs. Big repairs and a new roof may fall between spouse and children. If nothing is written down, expect a fight.

## What if your spouse needs care

If a spouse moves to a nursing home, the house may become a Medicaid issue. A house that your spouse owns outright may be countable once the other spouse is gone. A trust that holds the house for their lifetime may be treated differently. See /learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning. Rules vary by state.

## Trigger table

| If this happens | The plan should say |
|---|---|
| Spouse remarries | Does the right to live there end? |
| Spouse moves out for 6 months or more | Does the house sell? Who gets proceeds? |
| Spouse needs a nursing home | Can the trustee sell and pay for care? |
| Children and spouse do not get along | Who decides about repairs or sale? |
| A child dies before the spouse | Does the child's share go to their kids? |
| Spouse wants to downsize | Can the proceeds buy a smaller place? |

## House or money?

Some couples avoid the house question. The spouse gets cash and investments, the children get the house, or the other way around. If the house is most of the value, one option is life insurance for the children and the house to the spouse. See /free/blended-family-asset-map-worksheet.

## Before you remarry

Moving in together, or selling and buying again, can change what is yours alone. Many people keep their house in their own name or in a trust and agree in writing, such as in a prenup. Do not sell without asking about the tax. See /free/prenup-and-estate-plan-checklist.

- [ ] List who owns the house today
- [ ] Decide what your spouse needs to feel secure
- [ ] Decide what your children expect
- [ ] Say it out loud to both
- [ ] Get it in writing

## Questions to settle together

- **If one of us dies, where does the survivor live?** ______________________
- **How long?** ______________________
- **Who pays the bills?** ______________________
- **What ends the right?** ______________________
- **Who becomes trustee?** ______________________
- **What do the children get, and when?** ______________________

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want the house to end up with your children after your spouse
- You own the house and your spouse will move in
- A mortgage or tax issue complicates the plan
- Your spouse may need long-term care
- You or your spouse want to sell before or after marrying
