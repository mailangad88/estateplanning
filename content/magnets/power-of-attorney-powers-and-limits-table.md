## How to use this table

A power of attorney is a written grant of authority. The agent can do what the document says, within state law, and not much more. Courts and banks read the wording. Look up your task in the table, then check the document itself. Rules differ by state. It is general education, not legal advice.

## Lookup table

| Task | Typical answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Open or close a bank account | Yes | Core financial power |
| Pay bills and file taxes | Yes | Core financial power |
| Sell or buy a house | Yes, if real estate powers are included | Needs recording; title companies read the document closely |
| Manage investments | Yes | Check any limits |
| Access a safe deposit box | Sometimes | Banks often want specific wording or their own form |
| Withdraw from a 401(k) or IRA | Sometimes | The plan administrator decides what it accepts |
| Run a business | Sometimes | Needs business powers; operating agreements may limit |
| Change a beneficiary designation | Usually only if the document says so | A form, plan or insurer may refuse |
| Create, change or fund a trust | Only if specifically stated | Grants of this power are narrow |
| Make gifts | Only to the extent granted | Often capped and limited to annual exclusion amounts |
| Gift to the agent | Only if clearly allowed | Self-dealing is the top abuse risk |
| Apply for Medicaid | Yes, as a financial task | Eligibility rules still apply |
| Move assets for Medicaid planning | Only with gifting and planning powers | A gift can create a penalty |
| Manage Social Security | Mostly no | The Social Security Administration uses its own representative payee process |
| Deal with the IRS | Only with an IRS form (Form 2848) | The IRS requires its own form |
| Make medical decisions | No | That is a health care power of attorney |
| Vote or sign a ballot | No | Voting is personal |
| Make or change a will | No | A will must be signed by the person who made it |
| Act after death | No | Authority ends at death; the executor takes over |

## Hot powers

Some powers are called hot powers because they can be used to shift property or change a plan. Many states require them to be listed expressly. They include:

- Making gifts
- Creating or changing a trust
- Changing beneficiaries or survivorship rights
- Delegating authority
- Waiving the agent's duties
- Giving the agent compensation or paying the agent for services
- Disclaiming property

Illinois lists specific additional powers in the statutory framework, and a power of attorney that does not include them does not grant them. <!-- verify: Illinois 755 ILCS 45/3-4 and 755 ILCS 45/4-5 on which powers need express language -->

## What an agent cannot do

- Make or revoke the principal's will
- Act after the principal dies
- Make medical decisions, unless the same document is also a health care power of attorney
- Do anything the principal could not do, such as sign for a different person
- Ignore the principal's instructions in the document
- Use the principal's money for the agent's own benefit unless the document allows it
- Override a court order

## After death

The agent's role ends at death. Funeral bills, final taxes and the estate all pass to the executor named in the will, or to a court-appointed person. If an agent pays funeral costs from the principal's account after death, the bank may freeze it. Keep the receipts and talk to the executor.

## Special cases

### Real estate

A deed signed by an agent is valid when the power of attorney authorizes real estate transactions and is attached or recorded. The recorder or title company often wants the original or a certified copy. <!-- verify: Illinois recording rules for powers of attorney that support a deed -->

### Retirement accounts

Plans can restrict outside agents. Some require their own agent forms. Ask the plan before the crisis.

### Trusts

A power of attorney can usually fund a trust only if the document says so. If the principal's trust is already in place and the power of attorney gives the agent authority to fund it, assets can be retitled. Without that, a court may be needed. See the trust funding checklist on this site.

### Medicaid

An agent can complete forms and gather proof. If the document does not authorize gifts, the agent cannot transfer assets to children or a trust to qualify. If it does, the agent must act in the principal's interests, not their own.

## Common mistakes

- [ ] Using a generic internet form that omits needed special powers
- [ ] Assuming a power of attorney lets the agent make medical choices
- [ ] Granting gift powers with no limit
- [ ] Not checking whether banks and plans have their own forms
- [ ] Thinking the power of attorney still works after death

## When to talk to an attorney

- The document needs hot powers or limits
- A bank, plan or title company refuses the document
- A trust or Medicaid plan is involved
- The principal can no longer sign new documents
