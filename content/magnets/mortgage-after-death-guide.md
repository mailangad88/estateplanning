**How to use this guide.** You are dealing with a lot, and the mortgage is one more worry. Take it one step at a time. Nothing here has to be done in a day. Read the first section, make one call, and come back when you are ready. Rules differ by state and by loan, so treat this as a map.

## The short version

- The loan does not usually become due the moment someone dies.
- Federal law limits when a lender can demand full payment from a family member who inherits a home. This is often called Garn-St Germain protection.
- If you inherit the home, you can generally ask the servicer to treat you as a "successor in interest," which gives you the right to information and options.
- Keep payments going if you can, and keep talking to the servicer. Silence is what causes trouble.

## First steps, in order

- [ ] Find the most recent mortgage statement and write down the servicer's name and phone number.
- [ ] Locate the death certificate. Order several certified copies.
- [ ] Call the servicer. Say the owner has died and ask for the "estate" or "successor in interest" department.
- [ ] Ask whether a payment is due and how to make it. Ask to send the first payment even if the paperwork is not complete.
- [ ] Keep notes of every call: date, name, what was said.
- [ ] Confirm that homeowner's insurance and property taxes are being paid.
- [ ] Do not stop paying and do not assume a missed payment will be forgiven.

## Sample first call

> "My [mother] died on [date]. I am her [son and executor / heir]. I would like to know what is needed to be recognized as a successor in interest, what payment is due now, and who can discuss the account with me. I will send the death certificate."

You do not need to sound like a lawyer. A calm, direct request works.

## What federal law generally allows

A federal law (the Garn-St Germain Depository Institutions Act) generally prevents a lender from calling a residential mortgage due solely because of certain transfers. These include a transfer to a relative after the borrower's death, a transfer to a spouse or child, and a transfer to a surviving joint tenant. The details depend on the type of property and the use of the home, usually a home with fewer than five units that the borrower occupied.

A consumer finance rule also requires servicers to have procedures for confirmed successors in interest. A confirmed successor is generally entitled to the same help a borrower can get, such as loss mitigation options, but does not become personally liable for the loan unless they assume it.

Protections vary with the facts, so confirm your situation with the servicer in writing and, if needed, an attorney.

## Who is a successor in interest?

Typically, someone who has received an ownership interest in the home because of the death, such as:

- A surviving spouse or joint owner
- A child or relative who inherited by will or intestacy
- A person who received it from a living trust
- A person named on a transfer-on-death deed, where the state allows one

The servicer may ask for the death certificate, a will or court letters, a deed, and proof of identity.

## Your options

| Option | How it works | Things to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the loan and keep paying | Continue the existing payments as successor | May need confirmation; interest rate stays the same |
| Assume the loan | Take over the loan formally | Some loans permit this without a new credit check for heirs |
| Refinance | Replace the loan with a new one in your name | Needs income and credit; may change the rate |
| Sell the home | Pay off the loan from the sale | Often chosen when no one wants to stay |
| Hand the home back to the lender | A deed in lieu of foreclosure | Reserved for hard cases; talk to an attorney first |
| Reverse mortgage | May become due after death | Different rules: ask the servicer about time limits |

If the home is worth more than the loan, the heirs keep the difference. If it is worth less, heirs are generally not personally responsible beyond the home, unless they signed the loan or agreed to take it on. The estate is responsible for the owner's debts, but most mortgages are secured by the house itself.

## Timeline cautions

Different loans have different time limits. Some reverse mortgages have a short window to act. FHA, VA and other loans can have additional rules. Ask the servicer for any deadlines in writing.

## Keeping the house safe

- [ ] Keep the home insured. A vacant home may need a different policy.
- [ ] Pay property taxes, or confirm they come from escrow.
- [ ] Secure the property and forward mail.
- [ ] Keep utilities on enough to prevent damage.
- [ ] Keep records of every payment you make for the estate.

## What if there is no will?

Ownership may need to go through probate before anyone can sell. The servicer may still allow payments and may discuss the loan with the person handling the estate. See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works). Do not wait for a court to start talking to the servicer.

## Beware of pressure

After a death, calls and letters can arrive from people offering to buy the home quickly, "save" it from foreclosure, or pay off the loan for a fee. Take time. Ask for everything in writing, and talk with an attorney before signing a deed.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The servicer refuses to talk to you or to recognize you.
- You have received a foreclosure notice.
- The home is held in a trust, or in joint names with someone who has died.
- There are several heirs who disagree about keeping or selling.
- The loan is a reverse mortgage.
