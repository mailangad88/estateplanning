**How to use this guide.** Write down the change you want in one sentence. Then work through the three tests below. Most people find the answer in the first two. Keep the change log at the end with your will so the next update starts from facts.

## What a codicil is

A codicil is a short, separate document that changes part of an existing will. It is signed with the same formalities as a will: your signature, and usually two adult witnesses watching you sign. Rules on witnesses, notaries and "self-proving" affidavits vary by state. The codicil does not replace the will. The court reads both together after you die.

A new will replaces the old one entirely. It usually opens with a sentence revoking all earlier wills and codicils.

## Test 1: How big is the change?

A codicil tends to fit when the change is narrow and self-contained.

- [ ] Swapping one executor or alternate executor
- [ ] Changing a single gift, such as a ring or a sum of money
- [ ] Adding or removing one named beneficiary of a small gift
- [ ] Changing a guardian for minor children (some families prefer a new will so the guardian choice is in one clean place)
- [ ] Fixing a name or address

A new will tends to fit when the change touches how the whole plan works.

- [ ] A marriage, divorce or death of a spouse
- [ ] A new child or grandchild you want to provide for
- [ ] Changing who gets the residue (everything left after specific gifts)
- [ ] Moving to a new state
- [ ] Adding a trust, or a pour-over clause into a trust
- [ ] More than two or three separate edits

## Test 2: Will the two documents fit together?

A codicil must refer to the will by its exact date and title, and must say which paragraph it changes. Problems come from codicils that conflict with other language in the will, or that were written years after the will without checking what the will now says.

Ask these questions.

- **Does the will mention the item or person the codicil changes?** ______
- **Do both documents use the same names for the people involved?** ______
- **Have my assets, state or family changed since the will was signed?** ______
- **Is the original will easy to find and in good condition?** ______

Two or more "no" or "not sure" answers point toward a new will.

## Test 3: How many documents will your family have to read?

Each codicil adds a page the executor must locate and the court must accept. Three codicils over fifteen years, one of them lost, can cause a dispute about which version controls. A new will gives one clean set of instructions. As a rule of thumb, once you are on a second codicil, think about restating the whole will.

## Why handwritten changes are risky

It feels logical to cross out a name and write a new one on your signed will. Do not rely on it. Courts in many states treat unsigned or unwitnessed edits as having no effect on the document. In some states, physical marks can also be read as an attempt to revoke part of the will, or the whole will, which can leave you with a different result than you intended. Here are the common failures.

| What people do | What can go wrong |
|---|---|
| Cross out a beneficiary and write in another | The new name may not count, and the old gift may be revoked, so that share goes to someone else |
| Write a note in the margin | A note without proper signing and witnessing is usually not part of the will |
| Staple a new page to the will | A page that was not signed and witnessed with the will is not reliably part of it |
| Tear off a page | Courts may find the whole will was revoked or that the document is incomplete |
| Email instructions to the executor | An email is not a will in most states, though it may help explain your wishes |

If you want your family to know something that does not need to be legally binding, such as who gets your photo albums, a separate [letter of instruction](/free/letter-of-instruction-template) is a safer place than the margins of the will.

## Signing the change

Whichever route you take, sign it properly.

1. Read the final text aloud or slowly on paper. Check names and dates.
2. Sign in front of the witnesses your state requires. Use witnesses who are not beneficiaries.
3. If your state uses a notarized self-proving affidavit, do it again for the codicil or new will.
4. Keep the original in the same safe place as the old will, labeled clearly.
5. If you signed a new will, destroy the older original only after the new one is signed, and keep a note of when and how. Your attorney can advise on whether to keep a marked copy.

## Beyond the will

A will changes only what passes under the will. Retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death accounts and jointly owned property pass by their own paperwork. If your change is about who gets money, check those forms too using the [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).

## When to talk to an attorney

- The change affects more than one or two sentences of your will.
- You have already made handwritten marks on a signed will.
- A family member might challenge the change, for example because it reduces their share.
- You have a trust, a business, or property in more than one state.
- You have had a major life change such as marriage, divorce or a diagnosis.
- You are unsure whether an old codicil or older will is still the one in force.
