## How to use this chart

When an Illinois resident dies without a valid will, the state decides who inherits. The rules are in the Probate Act, section 2-1 (755 ILCS 5/2-1). Find the row that matches your family, then use the family tree to write in names. Intestacy rules differ from state to state, so this chart applies to Illinois law only. It covers property that passes through probate. It is education, not legal advice.

## The chart

| Family situation at death | Who inherits the probate estate |
|---|---|
| Spouse and descendants (children, grandchildren) | One half to the spouse, one half to the descendants, shared by branch (per stirpes) |
| Descendants only | All to the descendants, shared by branch |
| Spouse only, no descendants | All to the spouse |
| No spouse or descendants, but parents or siblings | All to the parents, brothers and sisters in equal parts. If one parent has died, the surviving parent takes a double portion. A deceased sibling's descendants take that sibling's share |
| None of the above, but grandparents or their descendants | Half to the paternal side and half to the maternal side, to the grandparents or the survivor, or if none, to their descendants |
| None of the above, but great-grandparents or their descendants | Split between the two sides in the same way |
| Only more distant relatives | The nearest relatives of equal degree, sharing equally |
| No relatives at all | Real estate goes to the county where it sits. Personal property goes to the county or State under unclaimed property law |

The statute does not treat half-blood relatives differently from whole-blood relatives for these shares.

## "Per stirpes" in plain words

Per stirpes means by branch. If a person had two children and one died, leaving two kids of their own, the living child gets half, and the two grandchildren share the other half. Write your family as branches on the tree below.

## Examples

- **Married, two children, no will:** The spouse gets half. The two children split the other half. Stepchildren are not included.
- **Widowed, one child who died leaving two kids:** The two grandchildren split everything.
- **Single, no children, parents alive:** The two parents split the estate, as do any living siblings. Each takes an equal part.
- **Single, no children, one parent alive, two siblings:** The surviving parent takes a double portion, and each sibling takes one portion.
- **Unmarried partner of 20 years:** The partner is not on the list. Property goes to the relatives above.

## Who the law leaves out

- [ ] Unmarried partners
- [ ] Stepchildren, unless adopted
- [ ] Friends, caregivers and charities
- [ ] In-laws who are not spouses
- [ ] A former spouse after divorce, in most cases

If anyone on this list matters to you, intestacy will not reach them. A will or beneficiary form can. Read [what happens if you die without a will](/guides/what-happens-if-you-die-without-a-will).

## What the chart does not control

Intestacy covers only property in the decedent's own name with no beneficiary. These pass by their own rules:

- Accounts and policies with named beneficiaries
- Joint accounts and joint tenancy property
- Property in a living trust
- A recorded transfer on death instrument for a home
- Payable on death accounts

Check your own list on the [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit). Beneficiary forms are often the most powerful part of a plan.

## What a will changes

A will can leave property to anyone, in any shares. It can name guardians for minor children and an executor you trust. In Illinois, a will usually needs to be signed by the maker and attested by two witnesses, which the [Illinois signing requirements cheat sheet](/free/illinois-signing-requirements-cheat-sheet) explains. If a will is not valid, the estate may fall back to this chart.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have a blended family, or a child from an earlier relationship
- You are unmarried and want a partner to inherit
- You have a child with a disability who receives public benefits
- You own a business, a farm or real estate in more than one state
- You want to leave something to a friend or charity
- A relative of yours has died with no will and you are unsure what happens next
