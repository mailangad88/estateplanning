**How to use this template.** Print a stack of the daily page and keep it in a binder where every caregiver can find it. Fill it in at the same time each day, such as after dinner. Short, factual notes are more useful than long ones. A log helps doctors see patterns, helps siblings share the work, and can help document care and spending if questions come up later with benefits or family. It is a record, not a diagnosis. Call the doctor or emergency services for anything urgent. This is general information, not legal advice.

## The weekly summary

Fill in on the last day of each week.

- **Week of:** ______________________
- **Days with falls or near-falls:** ______________________
- **Changes in appetite or weight:** ______________________
- **Changes in sleep:** ______________________
- **Changes in mood or memory:** ______________________
- **Missed or refused medicines:** ______________________
- **Total hours of care given, and by whom:** ______________________
- **Total spent for the parent, and from which account:** ______________________
- **Call the doctor about:** ______________________

Sample note that helps a doctor: "Tuesday and Wednesday, ate about half of each meal. Thursday, ate almost nothing and slept most of the day. Friday she was confused about the day. No fever." Compare this with "Not eating well, seems off," which tells a doctor little.

## Writing good notes

- **Write what you saw, not what you assume.** "Slept most of the day" beats "depressed."
- **Use numbers where you can.** Hours, cups, steps, minutes.
- **Record the time.** A change at 3 pm and a change at 3 am mean different things.
- **Note what changed from normal.** A baseline helps.
- **Do not edit past entries.** If you made a mistake, add a dated correction.
- **Do not write anything you would not want a doctor, a judge or a sibling to read.**
- **Keep one log, not several.** If an aide keeps their own, collect it weekly.

## Why records matter beyond care

Good records help in several ways, and the details vary by state and program:

- **Doctors and nurses.** A log shows patterns that a visit does not.
- **Siblings.** When everyone sees the same record, there are fewer arguments about who did what.
- **Paid caregivers and agencies.** Notes help them take over smoothly.
- **Medicaid and other benefits.** Medicaid applications for long-term care usually ask for several years of financial records, including payments and transfers. Receipts and a record of what was spent on the parent, and for what, can help show that money went to care rather than to gifts. Federal law has a look-back period for transfers made for less than fair value, and states handle the details differently. Use the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) for a rough date, and talk to an attorney before making large payments.
- **Taxes.** Medical expenses may be deductible in some cases, and a caregiver who is paid may be a household employee. Ask a tax professional.
- **Estate administration.** If your parent has a power of attorney in place, an agent must keep records and account for the parent's money in many states. The log and receipts show proper use.

Keep these along with the log:

- [ ] Receipts for anything bought with the parent's money
- [ ] Bank statements, copied monthly
- [ ] A separate account or card for the parent's spending, never mixed with your own
- [ ] Invoices from paid helpers, with dates and hours
- [ ] Notes from phone calls with insurers and doctors, with date, name and what was agreed

## Handing off

When another person takes over a shift, spend ten minutes on this checklist.

- [ ] Read the last three days of entries together.
- [ ] Point out where the medicines and pill chart are.
- [ ] Review any change in behavior or appetite.
- [ ] Share emergency numbers and the medical information sheet.
- [ ] Agree how to reach each other tonight.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are paying yourself, or another family member, for care.
- Your parent may need Medicaid in the next few years.
- You manage a parent's money and are unsure what records the law expects from an agent.
- A sibling questions how money was spent.
- Your parent has no power of attorney and cannot manage their own affairs.
