**How to use this checklist.** Work through one section per sitting over a few weeks. Tick what is done and write dates beside what is not. Your sixties are when plans written in your forties stop matching your life: children are adults, retirement is close and health decisions become real.

## Plan refresh: does the old plan still fit?

Pull out your will, trust, powers of attorney and health care directive.

- [ ] Are the executor and trustee still alive, willing and able?
- [ ] Are your agents under the powers of attorney still the right people, and is there a backup?
- [ ] Is a guardian still named for a child who is now an adult? That line can come out.
- [ ] Do the shares still match your wishes after marriages, divorces, deaths and grandchildren?
- [ ] Is any gift to a young adult better held in a trust?
- [ ] Were documents signed in another state? Ask whether they still work in your current one.

## Medicare preparation

Medicare's initial enrollment window is seven months: it starts three months before the month you turn 65 and ends three months after. Missing it can mean late penalties for some parts, unless you have qualifying coverage through current work.

- [ ] Mark the first day of your enrollment window on your calendar
- [ ] If still working, ask HR how your employer plan coordinates with Medicare
- [ ] Learn the four parts (A, B, C, D) and what each covers
- [ ] Ask whether Social Security and Medicare enrollment are linked for you
- [ ] Check which doctors and drugs you use, and whether they are covered under each option

## Long-term care: decide before you need it

Medicare does not pay for most long-term custodial care. Families pay from savings, long-term care insurance, hybrid life policies, Medicaid after assets are spent down, or a mix. Premiums are generally lower when you buy in your fifties or early sixties and applicants must pass health screening, so waiting has a cost.

| Option | What it means | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Self-fund | Pay from savings | Is there a number we are comfortable earmarking? |
| Traditional LTC insurance | Premiums buy a daily benefit | Can premiums rise? What is the benefit period? |
| Hybrid life or annuity with LTC rider | Death benefit if care is not used | What does it cost versus separate policies? |
| Medicaid | Needs-based, with look-back rules | Are there transfers we should not make? |

Use the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) before giving away assets. See [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/learn/elder-care/medicaid-planning).

## Retirement accounts and beneficiaries

Retirement accounts are the largest asset for many people at 60, and they pass by beneficiary form.

- [ ] List every 401(k), 403(b), IRA, pension and annuity, including old employers
- [ ] Confirm primary and contingent beneficiaries on each
- [ ] Check spousal consent rules on workplace plans
- [ ] Ask whether a trust should be a beneficiary, and if so, that the trust is written for it
- [ ] Learn when required minimum distributions begin. The starting age depends on your birth year, so check with the IRS rather than assuming
- [ ] Review charitable giving from IRAs if you give regularly

## The legal documents that matter most at this age

- [ ] Durable power of attorney for property, with a backup agent
- [ ] Health care power of attorney and a living will
- [ ] HIPAA release naming the same people
- [ ] A conversation with your agents about what you want
- [ ] Digital access: password manager and who can open it
- [ ] Letter of instruction with where things are

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are planning retirement and own a business or rental property.
- Your estate or your spouse's might exceed a state estate tax threshold.
- You want to help a parent, child or grandchild without hurting benefits.
- Your plan was written before a major change, such as remarriage or a diagnosis.
- You are thinking about gifting assets for Medicaid or tax reasons.
- You are retiring to or from another state.
