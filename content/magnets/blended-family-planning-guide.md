**How to use this guide.** Read the scenarios, pick the one closest to yours, then fill in the worksheet with your spouse. It works best when you do it together and honestly. State law on spousal rights and on stepchildren varies widely, so use this to prepare questions for an attorney licensed in your state.

## Why blended families need extra care

Default rules tend to assume a first marriage with shared children. Without a plan, many states give a surviving spouse a large share, and children from a prior relationship may receive little or nothing. Stepchildren are generally not heirs at all unless named. Joint accounts, beneficiary forms and home ownership can send assets one way no matter what a will says.

The result is rarely what either spouse intended. A common outcome: everything passes to the surviving spouse, who later leaves it to their own children, and the first spouse's children are left out.

## Four common scenarios

### Scenario 1: A second marriage, kids from both sides

Maria and Tom each have two adult children and a house Tom owned before the marriage. Tom's will leaves everything to Maria. When Maria dies, her will leaves everything to her own children. Tom's children get nothing from either estate.

**What families consider:** a trust that gives Maria the use of the house or income for life, then passes what is left to Tom's children. A trustee other than Maria can carry out the second half.

### Scenario 2: Younger spouse, minor children from a prior marriage

Dev, 45, remarries. His children are 12 and 15. His new wife, Lena, is 35 and the kids' stepmother. If Dev dies, who is guardian if his ex is deceased or unavailable? Who controls the money?

**What families consider:** naming a guardian in a will, using a trust to hold funds for the kids, and deciding whether Lena serves as trustee or the role goes to a neutral person.

### Scenario 3: Each spouse keeps their own assets

Anna and Chris keep separate accounts and each leaves their own assets to their own children, while splitting household costs. They want to make sure the survivor has a place to live.

**What families consider:** a written agreement about the home, a right-to-live-there provision, and a clear plan for who pays taxes and upkeep.

### Scenario 4: A partner who is not a spouse

Jordan and Pat live together and are not married. Rules for unmarried partners differ sharply by state, and in many states a partner has no inheritance rights without documents.

**What families consider:** a will or trust naming the partner, beneficiary designations, and healthcare and financial powers of attorney.

## Tools families commonly consider

| Tool | What it can do | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Names guardian and executor, leaves property | Goes through probate. A surviving spouse may have statutory rights in some states. |
| Revocable living trust | Holds assets, can control timing and who gets what | Needs funding, and the survivor may be able to change it unless it becomes irrevocable at the first death |
| Marital trust or "QTIP" style trust | Provides for the spouse for life, then passes to named children | Choice of trustee matters, taxes may apply |
| Beneficiary designations | Move accounts directly to named people | Overrides a will. Check them after marriage. |
| Life insurance | Can provide cash for kids without splitting the house or business | Ownership and beneficiary rules |
| Prenuptial or postnuptial agreement | Records what each spouse waives or keeps | Must meet state formalities and is often best done with separate counsel |
| Joint ownership with survivorship | Passes property to the survivor | Can cut out children entirely |
| Powers of attorney | Allow trusted people to act if someone is incapacitated | Choose someone you trust, think about conflicts |

See [Estate Planning for Blended Families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families) for more.

## Choosing the people

- **Trustee:** a surviving spouse serving as sole trustee over money meant for stepchildren can create tension. Some families name a bank or a trusted friend, or use co-trustees.
- **Executor:** consider whether a child or the spouse is better placed to work with the other side.
- **Guardian:** if minor children from a prior relationship are involved, the other parent's rights come first in most situations.

Read [Choosing a Trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee).

### Worked example

Rafael and Kim each have a house and two kids. They agree that if Rafael dies first, Kim can stay in his house for as long as she likes, with property taxes and upkeep paid from a small fund. When she moves or dies, the house goes to Rafael's children. His retirement account goes 50% to Kim and 50% to the kids. They hold life insurance naming each other's children as beneficiaries to level things out. They write it all in a trust and update the beneficiary forms.

## Common mistakes

- Relying on a will while joint accounts and forms send everything to the spouse.
- Leaving everything "outright" to a spouse and trusting they will "take care of the kids."
- Naming the spouse as sole trustee over stepchildren's inheritance without discussing it.
- Forgetting an old beneficiary form that still names an ex-spouse. Some states revoke these automatically on divorce, but not all, and not for all account types.
- Avoiding the conversation to prevent tension.
- Leaving out a plan if the spouse remarries.

## Talking to your kids

Tell them the roles, not necessarily the numbers. Reassure them that the plan includes them. A family meeting can help. The [Family Meeting Worksheet](/free/family-meeting-worksheet) has an agenda.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You or your spouse have children from a previous relationship, especially minors.
- You want to make sure a spouse is housed but your children inherit later.
- You own a business, rental property or a house you owned before the marriage.
- You are considering a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement.
- You are unmarried and want a partner protected.
- You and your spouse disagree about how to split assets.
