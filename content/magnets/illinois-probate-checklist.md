## How to use this checklist

Probate is the court process for settling an estate, and in Illinois it is governed by the Probate Act of 1975 (755 ILCS 5). We are sorry for your loss. Use this list to see the road ahead and check off each step. Courts and counties differ in small ways, so treat the details as a guide and confirm them with the clerk or an attorney.

## First: is probate needed?

- [ ] Check whether the estate qualifies for the [small estate affidavit](/free/illinois-small-estate-affidavit-guide)
- [ ] Look for a will, and for real estate held in one name
- [ ] Note accounts with beneficiaries, which pass outside probate

## Step 1: File the will

Whoever has the original will must file it with the circuit court clerk. Illinois law says to file it right away after the death. Concealing a will for 30 days after learning of the death can be a crime. Do not wait. <!-- verify: filing deadline wording in 755 ILCS 5/6-1 -->

### Cook County notes

Cook County estates are handled in the Probate Division at the Richard J. Daley Center, 50 West Washington Street, Chicago. The division handles petitions for letters of office, will contests, claims and heirship petitions. The court's website posts probate forms, and the Clerk of the Circuit Court has them as well. Courtroom clerks do not take phone calls, so use the contacts listed on the Probate Division page.

## Step 3: Independent or supervised administration

| | Independent administration | Supervised administration |
|---|---|---|
| Court involvement | Light. The representative acts without asking for approval of each step | Heavier. Many steps need a court order |
| Typical use | Most Illinois estates | Disputes or special concerns |
| Cost and speed | Generally lower and faster | Generally higher and slower |
| Who can object | An interested person can ask the court to end it | The court oversees throughout |

Ask for independent administration in your petition unless there is a reason not to. An interested person can object, which would move the case to supervised administration.

## Step 4: Petition and letters of office

The petition asks the court to admit the will, if there is one, and to name the executor, or an administrator if there is no will. The court issues letters of office. These are the proof that you can act for the estate. You may need a bond, depending on the will and the court.

- [ ] Petition filed
- [ ] Hearing held
- [ ] Bond posted, if required
- [ ] Letters of office issued
- [ ] Certified copies ordered for banks

## Step 5: Notice to heirs

After the will is admitted or the administrator is appointed, the representative sends notice to heirs and legatees. The notice explains their right to ask the court about the will's validity, within a limited time. <!-- verify: deadlines in 755 ILCS 5/6-10, including the 42 day contest period -->

## Step 6: Publish notice and run the claim period

The representative publishes notice to creditors once a week for three successive weeks in a newspaper in the county. The notice sets a claim deadline. Under the statute, that date is not less than six months from first publication, or three months from mailing or delivery to a known creditor, whichever is later. Known creditors get a letter. A separate outer limit bars claims two years after death. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 5/18-12 -->

## Step 7: Inventory and assets

Illinois law asks the representative to prepare an inventory of the estate's assets. The deadline and who gets a copy depend on the case. <!-- verify: inventory timing under 755 ILCS 5/14-1 -->

- [ ] Open an estate bank account with the estate's tax ID number
- [ ] List and value the assets at date of death
- [ ] Protect property, keep insurance current
- [ ] Collect mail and accounts owed to the estate

## Step 8: Pay claims, taxes and expenses

- [ ] Pay valid claims in the order the law sets
- [ ] File the final income tax returns
- [ ] Check whether an [Illinois estate tax return](/free/illinois-estate-tax-guide) applies
- [ ] Keep every receipt

## Step 9: Distribute and close

- [ ] Prepare a final accounting for heirs
- [ ] Get receipts or releases from each heir
- [ ] Distribute what remains
- [ ] File the closing paperwork with the court
- [ ] Ask the court to discharge the representative

## When to talk to an attorney

- The will is unclear or someone may contest it
- There is real estate, a business, or property in another state
- Debts may be larger than the assets
- Heirs cannot be found, or disagree
- The estate may owe Illinois estate tax
