## How to use this guide

Illinois law sets who may see your digital accounts after death or incapacity, and the rules turn on what you wrote down in advance. Read the tiers, use the table to see where your wishes stand, then take the clauses to your attorney. Rules vary by state. This guide is general education.

## The Illinois law in brief

Illinois has adopted the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA), found at 755 ILCS 70. <!-- verify: 755 ILCS 70 citation and effective date --> Most states have adopted a version of the uniform act, but details differ, so check the state where you live.

A fiduciary is a person who acts for you: an executor, an agent under a power of attorney, a trustee or a guardian. The act sets when a fiduciary can reach your online accounts and records.

## Catalogue versus content

| What it is | Examples | Default access for a fiduciary |
|---|---|---|
| Catalogue of communications | Who you emailed, when, the subject lines | More available |
| Content of communications | The text of emails, messages and posts | Needs your consent or a court order |
| Other digital assets | Photos, documents, files, domain names, crypto | Governed by the account terms and your instructions |

The catalogue shows who wrote to whom and when. The content is what the messages say. The law protects content more.

## Three tiers of instruction

1. **An online tool.** Some platforms let you name who may see your data, like Google's Inactive Account Manager or an Apple Legacy Contact. If you use one, the tool generally controls.
2. **Your estate planning documents.** If there is no tool, a will, trust or power of attorney can give instructions.
3. **The terms of service.** If there is neither, the company's own terms apply.

## What a fiduciary can usually do

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Can my executor read my email? | Only if your will or other documents consent to disclosure of content, or a court orders it. The catalogue is easier |
| Does my executor need a court order? | Sometimes. A will with clear consent can avoid it, but companies may still ask for a court order |
| Does my power of attorney cover digital accounts? | Only if it says so. Add a clause |
| Who owns my email? | You hold rights under the account terms. The provider owns the service. Heirs get access through the process above |
| What if the terms forbid transferring accounts? | A fiduciary may be allowed access to manage the account even if the account cannot be transferred |

## Digital executor

A digital executor is a person you name to deal with online accounts. There is no special Illinois title. You can name your regular executor or a separate person in the will. A separate person helps if your executor is not comfortable with technology, but it can also add conflict if roles are unclear.

- **Name my digital executor:** ______________________
- **Backup:** ______________________
- **Why I chose them:** ______________________

## Sample clauses to discuss

These are ideas for conversation, not ready-to-sign language.

- **Consent clause.** "My executor may access the content of my electronic communications and any digital assets, and I consent to disclosure to my executor."
- **Power of attorney clause.** "My agent may access and manage my digital assets and online accounts, including the content of electronic communications."
- **Instruction clause.** "I want my social media accounts memorialized, not deleted," or the reverse.

## Listing digital assets in a will

A will becomes public in probate. Do not list account numbers or passwords in it.

| Put in the will | Keep out of the will |
|---|---|
| Who gets your domain names or digital photos | Passwords and PINs |
| Consent to access | Seed phrases |
| Name of the digital executor | Account numbers |

Put the details in a separate document and tell your executor where it is. See the [digital asset inventory workbook](/free/digital-asset-inventory-workbook).

## Passwords

A password in a will is a bad idea, because the will is a public record, and wills are often read after the accounts are closed. Use a password manager with emergency access, and store the master recovery information in a place your agent can reach.

## Common mistakes

- Naming no one and hoping the family will "figure it out"
- Putting passwords in a will
- Using a platform tool that conflicts with the will
- Forgetting the power of attorney, which matters while you are alive
- Not updating the plan after changing phones or providers

## When to talk to an attorney

- You hold valuable domains, a business or cryptocurrency
- You want content like email kept private after death
- You are drafting a power of attorney and want digital access covered
- A platform has refused your executor
- You live in more than one state
