**How to use this guide.** Find your goal in the table, read that section, and write down the questions it raises. An irrevocable trust generally cannot be changed or canceled once it is signed and funded, except in limited ways that depend on the trust and state law. That is why the first question is always "What am I giving up?" Rules differ by state, and tax rules change, so treat this as a map, not advice.

## Why an irrevocable trust is different

A revocable living trust lets you keep control and change your mind. An irrevocable trust works differently. You transfer property to the trust, and a trustee, who is often not you, holds it for the beneficiaries. In exchange you may get benefits that a revocable trust cannot give: estate tax savings, protection from certain creditors, or eligibility for public benefits.

The price is real.

- You generally cannot take property back.
- You may lose income or access you had.
- The trust may file its own tax return.
- The tax and legal results depend on exact drafting.

## Goal-to-trust table

| If your goal is | Trust often considered | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Keep life insurance out of a taxable estate | ILIT | You give up ownership of the policy |
| Qualify for Medicaid long-term care after the look-back | Medicaid asset protection trust | You cannot use the principal, and a look-back period applies |
| Use a married couple's tax exemption while keeping some access | SLAT | Divorce or the death of the beneficiary spouse can change access |
| Give to charity and receive income | Charitable remainder trust | The charity gets what is left, and the gift is permanent |
| Provide for a person with a disability without losing benefits | Special needs trust | Payments are limited, and a Medicaid payback may apply |

## 1. Irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT)

**What it is.** A trust that owns a life insurance policy. When you die, the proceeds are paid to the trust, not to you, so they usually stay out of your taxable estate.

**How it works.** The trust buys the policy, or you transfer an existing one. You give the trust money each year to pay premiums. Those gifts are often structured to use the annual gift exclusion ($19,000 per recipient in 2026), with notice letters to beneficiaries, called Crummey notices, that give them a short right to withdraw.

**Watch out for.** If you transfer an existing policy and die within three years, the proceeds can be pulled back into your estate under federal tax rules. Missing premium payments or notices can weaken the plan.

**Who it fits.** People whose estate may be close to or above the federal exemption ($15,000,000 per person in 2026), or a state exemption that is lower. Most families are below the federal level, so ask whether you need one.

## 2. Medicaid asset protection trust

**What it is.** An irrevocable trust that holds assets, often a home or savings, so they are not counted for Medicaid long-term care eligibility after the look-back period has passed.

**How it works.** You transfer assets and name a trustee. You generally can receive limited income, but you cannot use the principal. Federal law uses a five-year look-back for transfers before applying for nursing home Medicaid, with state rules shaping the details.

**Watch out for.** Transfers made inside the look-back window can cause a penalty period. Some states treat trusts differently, so the plan must match your state. Use the [Medicaid look-back date tool](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) to see where you stand.

**Who it fits.** Someone who is healthy now and planning years ahead for care costs. It is rarely the right move after a crisis begins.

## 3. Spousal lifetime access trust (SLAT)

**What it is.** One spouse gives assets to a trust for the other spouse and, often, the children. The gift uses part of the giver's lifetime exemption, but the trust can allow the beneficiary spouse to take distributions.

**How it works.** The assets and their future growth can be outside the giver's taxable estate. The family keeps indirect access through the spouse.

**Watch out for.** If the couple divorces or the beneficiary spouse dies early, the giving spouse loses indirect access. Two SLATs for each other can fail if they are too similar. This is where careful drafting matters.

**Who it fits.** Wealthy couples who want to use an exemption before it changes. The 2026 federal exemption is $15,000,000 per person.

## 4. Charitable remainder trust (CRT)

**What it is.** You give property to a trust that pays you, or others, income for a period of years or for life. Whatever remains goes to a charity you choose.

**How it works.** You may receive an income tax deduction for the charity's future share, and the trust can sell appreciated property without paying capital gains tax at the moment of sale. Payments to you are taxed according to the trust's rules.

**Watch out for.** The gift to charity is permanent. The payout rate and term must meet IRS rules. You cannot get the principal back.

**Who it fits.** Charitably minded people with highly appreciated stock or real estate. See the [charitable giving planner](/free/charitable-giving-planner).

## 5. Special needs trust

**What it is.** A trust for someone who has a disability and receives needs-based benefits such as SSI or Medicaid. Money in the trust is not counted the same way as money in the person's own name, when the trust is drafted and used properly.

**How it works.** A trustee pays for extras that benefits do not cover, such as therapies, transportation or equipment, often paying providers directly rather than giving cash. A third-party special needs trust, funded by parents or relatives, usually has no Medicaid payback. A first-party trust, funded with the person's own money, usually must repay Medicaid at death.

**Watch out for.** Payments for food or shelter can reduce SSI. Giving cash to the beneficiary can cause problems. An inheritance left directly to the person can disqualify benefits.

**Who it fits.** Families of a child or adult with a disability. See [special needs trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts) and the [special needs family roadmap](/free/special-needs-family-roadmap).

## When to talk to an attorney

- An advisor or salesperson has recommended an irrevocable trust and you do not yet understand it.
- You are thinking of moving a home or savings for Medicaid planning.
- Your net worth may be near an estate tax threshold.
- A family member with a disability receives benefits.
- You hold life insurance of a size that could affect an estate.
- You are considering a gift larger than the annual exclusion.
