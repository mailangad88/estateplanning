**How to use this guide.** This guide is for a revocable living trust you can still change. If the trust is irrevocable or you are no longer alive, the rules are different, and an attorney should review it. Start with the one-sentence test below, then use the signing checklist.

## The one-sentence test

Finish this sentence: "I want to change ______."

- If the answer is one or two clauses, such as a trustee or a distribution age, an amendment may fit.
- If the answer is "how the trust works" or "most of it," a restatement is usually cleaner.

## What each document does

**Amendment.** A short document that changes named sections of the trust. The original trust stays in place, and the amendment sits on top of it. Everything the amendment does not mention continues as before.

**Restatement.** A new, complete version of the trust. It keeps the trust's name and date of creation, so the trust is the same legal entity, and replaces the old text with a clean document. Deeds and account titles that name the trust remain valid because the trust itself has not changed.

**New trust.** Starting a separate trust requires retitling everything. It is rarely needed for ordinary updates, and a restatement is usually simpler.

## Comparison

| Question | Amendment | Restatement |
|---|---|---|
| Length | A page or a few pages | Full trust document |
| Fits | Narrow changes | Many changes or old, patched trusts |
| Reading experience | Reader must combine two or more documents | One document |
| Risk | Conflicts between old text and the amendment | Dropping something important by accident |
| Cost | Usually lower | Usually higher |
| Accounts and deeds | Stay as they are | Stay as they are if the trust name and date are kept |

## When an amendment fits

- [ ] Replacing a successor trustee
- [ ] Changing the age at which a child receives money
- [ ] Adding or removing one beneficiary
- [ ] Updating one specific gift
- [ ] Fixing an address or a name
- [ ] First change since signing, and the trust is recent

## When a restatement fits

- [ ] You already have two or more amendments
- [ ] You have moved to a new state, since state law affects trust terms
- [ ] You married, divorced, or had a child, and several parts change
- [ ] Family circumstances changed, such as a child with a disability
- [ ] The trust was written many years ago and uses outdated terms
- [ ] You want clearer instructions for your trustee

## What not to do

- **Do not write on the signed trust.** Margin notes and crossed-out lines may not be valid and can invite a dispute.
- **Do not email a "change."** An email is not a trust amendment unless the trust says so and formalities are met.
- **Do not rely on a will to change the trust.** A will does not change a living trust that is already in place.
- **Do not forget to follow the amendment method in the trust.** Some trusts require signing in a particular way, and some require a notary or delivery to the trustee. Follow the trust's method.

## Signing checklist

Rules vary by state, so follow your attorney's directions. Typical steps:

- [ ] Read the final amendment or restatement from top to bottom.
- [ ] Check that it names the trust exactly: trust name and original date.
- [ ] Sign it as the person who created the trust (the grantor or settlor).
- [ ] Use a notary. Most attorneys do this even where it is not required, because banks and title companies ask for it.
- [ ] Use witnesses if the attorney recommends them.
- [ ] If you are also the trustee, sign again in that role.
- [ ] Keep the signed original with the trust, in the same file.
- [ ] Date every page version and mark old versions as superseded.

## After signing

- [ ] Give your trustees and successor trustees a copy, or tell them where to find it.
- [ ] Update a certification of trust, which is a short summary banks often accept instead of the full document.
- [ ] Check whether the pour-over will still matches the trust name and date.
- [ ] Review beneficiary forms for retirement accounts and insurance. See the [beneficiary audit checklist](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).
- [ ] If the change adds new assets, retitle them using the [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist).
- [ ] Add the change to your plan review calendar.

## Common mistakes

1. An amendment that refers to the wrong article or section number of the trust.
2. A restatement that leaves out a protective clause, such as a spendthrift or special needs provision.
3. Three amendments stapled together, none dated.
4. An amendment signed but never kept with the trust.
5. A trustee change that does not match the pour-over will's executor choice.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your trust has been amended more than once.
- You moved to another state after signing the trust.
- You want to change who receives the trust after a divorce.
- A beneficiary has special needs or receives government benefits.
- You are unsure whether your trust is still revocable.
- You are making changes while ill or under pressure from a family member.
