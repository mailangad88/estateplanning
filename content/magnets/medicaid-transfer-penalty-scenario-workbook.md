## How to use this workbook

Medicaid reviews the five years before you apply for nursing home coverage. Gifts and below-market sales in that window can cause a **penalty period**, a stretch of time when Medicaid will not pay for care. Pick the scenario that looks like yours, then fill in the penalty estimate. The look-back is 60 months under federal law as of 2026 <!-- verify -->, and California differs. This is general education, not legal advice.

## The step flow

1. Medicaid looks at the 60 months before the application date.
2. It lists every transfer for less than fair value in that window.
3. It adds the transfers together and divides by the state's average monthly nursing home cost.
4. The result is the number of months of penalty.
5. The penalty clock starts only when you are in care, have applied and would otherwise qualify.

The last point matters most. A gift made in year four does not get you a free ride. The clock does not begin while you still have too many assets.

## What is usually exempt

| Transfer | Exempt? |
|---|---|
| To a spouse | Generally yes |
| Home to a child under 21 | Generally yes |
| Home to a blind or permanently disabled child | Generally yes |
| Any asset to a disabled child, or to a trust solely for that child | Generally yes |
| Home to a child who lived there and gave care for two years | Generally yes if rules are met |
| Home to a sibling with equity who lived there a year | Generally yes if rules are met |
| Small birthday or holiday gifts | Often tolerated, but still reportable |
| Annual gift tax exclusion gifts | Not exempt for Medicaid |

The annual gift exclusion is a tax rule. It does not protect against a Medicaid penalty. This is a common mix-up.

## Scenario table

| If this is your plan | Then this |
|---|---|
| Give your house to a child now | A penalty is likely unless an exemption applies. The child also loses the stepped-up basis. |
| Give the house to the child who cared for you | The caregiver child exemption may apply. It needs proof: two years, care that kept you out of a facility, and living in the home. |
| Sell the house to a child for less than it is worth | The gap is a gift and can count against you. |
| Pay your child back for past help | Repayment without a written agreement made at the time is usually treated as a gift. |
| Pay a child for current care with a written personal care agreement | Can be allowed if the pay is fair and the agreement predates the work. |
| Give to a church | Treated as a transfer for less than fair value. Ask first. |
| Gift three years ago and apply now | You are still inside the window. A penalty may apply to the gift. |
| Gift six years ago | Outside the window. Reporting is still wise. |
| Buy a car, remodel, prepay a funeral | These are spend-down, not gifts, if the value stays with you or is a fair purchase. |

## Caregiver child test

Check each item:

- [ ] The child lived in the parent's home for at least two years right before the parent entered care
- [ ] The care was needed to delay the move to a facility
- [ ] The child can show proof such as medical records, a diary or letters from a doctor
- [ ] The house is the parent's home
- [ ] The transfer is a deed from the parent to that child

Failing any one item can lose the exemption.

## Common mistakes

- [ ] Believing the annual gift limit is also the Medicaid limit
- [ ] Deeding the house first and asking questions later
- [ ] Paying a child for care with no written contract
- [ ] Waiting until after the nursing home admission to think about the look-back
- [ ] Forgetting that some applications count transfers by a spouse

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want to give a house or a large sum
- A penalty has already been assessed
- You or a parent are in care now and savings are shrinking
- A child may qualify as a caregiver child
- You need to reverse a transfer, which sometimes cures a penalty
