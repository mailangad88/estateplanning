## How to use this guide

A special needs trust exists to add to government benefits, not replace them. Trustees get nervous because one wrong payment can cut an SSI check. This guide sorts common purchases by risk and gives you a record sheet. It is general education. Each trust has its own wording, and rules vary by state, so check the trust document first.

## The one rule behind every answer

The trust should pay a **vendor or provider**, not hand money to the beneficiary. Cash to the beneficiary counts as unearned income for SSI. After a small monthly exclusion, SSI usually drops by about the same amount. <!-- verify: current SSI general income exclusion and treatment of cash -->

Think of the trust as a purse the beneficiary does not hold. The trustee buys things for them.

## Step flow before any payment

1. Read the trust. Does it allow this kind of spending? Most say "supplemental needs" and give the trustee discretion.
2. Ask who gets the money: a store, school, doctor or landlord (usually fine) or the beneficiary (usually not).
3. Check whether the item is **shelter** (the one category that can reduce SSI).
4. Ask whether a benefit program, such as Medicaid, already pays. If yes, the trust fills the gap.
5. Write down the payment on the record sheet below.

## In-kind support and maintenance (ISM)

ISM is help with food or shelter that someone else provides. SSA may reduce SSI when it counts. As of the rule change effective September 30, 2024, food no longer reduces SSI. Shelter still can. <!-- verify: ISM food rule change and current shelter items list -->

Shelter items usually include:

- Rent or mortgage payments
- Property taxes and required insurance on the home
- Heating fuel, gas, electricity, water, sewer and garbage service

The reduction is capped at roughly one third of the federal SSI rate. <!-- verify: current ISM cap and how SSA values it -->

## If this, then that

| Purchase | Usually safe? | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Rent or mortgage | Careful | Counts as ISM, so SSI may drop up to the cap. Some families accept the reduction because the housing is worth more than the lost dollars |
| Groceries | Generally yes under current food rule | Pay the store, not the beneficiary. Keep receipts |
| Utilities | Careful | Same ISM shelter rule as rent |
| Medical costs Medicaid will not cover | Yes | Dental extras, therapies, devices, second opinions. Pay the provider |
| Phone, internet, computer | Yes | Pay the carrier or store. Not shelter |
| Car | Often yes | Title in the trust or the beneficiary, per attorney advice. Insurance, repairs and fuel are not shelter |
| Travel and vacations | Yes | Include a companion's costs. Pay airline, hotel or tour directly |
| Pet | Yes | Food, vet care, supplies. Pay vendors |
| College or classes | Yes | Tuition goes straight to the school |
| Caregiver or family member | Careful | Needs a written agreement, fair pay and tax reporting |
| Funeral | Depends | Prepaid funeral contracts are one route. After death, the trust terms control |
| Stocks and funds | Allowed | Investing is a trustee duty, not a benefit event. Follow the trust's investment clause |

## Details on the tricky ones

### Cars

A car the beneficiary uses is usually an excluded resource for SSI, but only one vehicle per household. <!-- verify: SSI vehicle exclusion rule --> Trustees often keep the title in the trust to control sale and insurance. Ask the attorney which approach fits.

### Paid family caregivers

A sibling who helps with bathing, meals and appointments may be paid from the trust. Without a written agreement, that payment looks like a gift to the caregiver and may raise questions. Write down hours, rate and duties. The payment may be taxable income to the caregiver. See /free/personal-care-agreement-guide.

### Funeral and burial

Many families buy an irrevocable prepaid funeral contract for the beneficiary during life. After death, a third-party trust may pay burial costs if the document allows. A first-party trust must repay the state Medicaid agency first, and funeral costs may rank ahead of or behind that claim depending on the state. Ask your attorney.

### Investments

A trust can own stocks, bonds and funds. Growth inside the trust is not income to the beneficiary. Dividends and interest stay in the trust unless distributed. The trustee has a duty to invest prudently. Rules vary by state.

### Education

Tuition, books and fees paid to a school are generally fine. A stipend paid to the beneficiary is not.

## Common mistakes

- [ ] Giving the beneficiary a debit card linked to the trust
- [ ] Reimbursing a relative in cash without a receipt
- [ ] Paying rent without telling the family it may reduce SSI
- [ ] Letting the trustee mix trust money with personal money
- [ ] Forgetting to report trust payments the benefit office asks about
- [ ] Never writing down why a payment helped the beneficiary

## When to talk to an attorney

- The trust does not say what the trustee may buy
- You are thinking about buying a home or vehicle through the trust
- The trust is first-party and the beneficiary is on Medicaid
- You want to pay a family caregiver
- SSA sends a notice questioning a trust payment
