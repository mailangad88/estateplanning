## How to use this planner

We are sorry for your loss. Probate can feel like a long wait with sudden bursts of paperwork. This planner lays out the usual order of events so you know what is coming. Timing varies a great deal by state, county and estate. A simple estate in one place may close in under a year. An estate with real estate to sell, a tax return or a dispute can take much longer. Treat the months below as a rough map, and write your own dates in the tracker.

## Before month one

Do these in the first days, even before you file anything.

- [ ] Order several certified copies of the death certificate
- [ ] Find the original will and keep it safe
- [ ] Secure the home, mail and any vehicles
- [ ] Make a short list of accounts, property and known debts
- [ ] Ask the court clerk or an attorney whether your estate needs full probate or a simplified process

Many states let a very small estate use an affidavit instead of a full case. Rules and dollar limits vary, so ask the clerk. The [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) can also help you think through what a full case may involve.

## The usual timeline

| When | What usually happens | Done? |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1 to 4 | File the will and the petition to open the estate. Ask for a hearing date. | |
| Weeks 4 to 8 | Court appoints the executor and issues letters of office. Open an estate bank account. | |
| Months 2 to 3 | Send notice to heirs and beneficiaries. Publish or mail notice to creditors as your state requires. | |
| Months 2 to 4 | Collect assets, take an inventory, get date-of-death values, notify the IRS you are the fiduciary. | |
| Months 3 to 9 | Creditor claim period runs. Pay valid bills. Keep records of every payment. | |
| Months 6 to 12 | Sell or retitle real estate, file the final income tax returns, and file an estate tax return if the estate is large enough. | |
| Months 9 to 15 | Prepare the accounting, share it with beneficiaries as needed, and make distributions. | |
| Last step | Ask the court to close the estate and get a discharge, if your state uses one. | |

These ranges are common patterns, not promises. Your court may be faster or slower.

## Why each stage takes the time it does

**Opening the case.** Courts schedule hearings on their own calendars. A busy county can take weeks to give you a date. Missing paperwork causes the longest delays here.

**Waiting on creditors.** Most states set a window during which creditors can file claims. You cannot safely close the estate until that window ends. This stage is why even a simple estate rarely finishes in a few weeks.

**Real estate.** A home sale depends on the market, repairs, and the buyer's financing. Start early. See the [selling a house in probate checklist](/free/selling-a-house-in-probate-checklist).

**Taxes.** The final income tax return for the person who died is due on the usual date for the year after death. The estate may also need its own income tax return. Federal estate tax applies only to very large estates. The 2026 exemption is $15,000,000 per person. Some states have a lower threshold.

**Disputes.** A will contest or an argument among heirs can add months or years. See [when heirs disagree](/free/when-heirs-disagree-guide).

## Deadlines to look up for your court

Deadlines are set by state law and local court rules. Do not rely on a general chart. Ask the clerk or an attorney licensed in your state for each of these.

- [ ] How soon the will must be filed after death
- [ ] How long creditors have to make claims, and how they must be notified
- [ ] When the inventory is due
- [ ] How long heirs have to challenge the will after it is admitted
- [ ] Whether the court requires periodic reports or a bond
- [ ] When the final accounting is due

Write each answer with the date and the name of the person who gave it. If you are in Illinois, the Probate Act of 1975 sets many of these. See the [Illinois probate checklist](/free/illinois-probate-checklist).

## If the case feels stuck

Ask these in order.

1. Has every required notice been sent and proof filed?
2. Is the court waiting for a document from you?
3. Is a creditor claim, a tax matter or a property sale holding things up?
4. Is an heir objecting, or has one not responded?
5. Is the next hearing already on the calendar?

Keep a short log of calls and emails: date, person, what they said. Courts often respond faster to a specific question than a general one.

## Habits that keep a case moving

- Keep estate money in the estate account. Never mix it with your own.
- Save every receipt. You will need them for the [estate accounting](/free/estate-accounting-ledger-template).
- Update beneficiaries by short written notes every month or two, even if nothing has changed. Silence breeds suspicion.
- Do not distribute early unless an attorney confirms it is safe. Early distributions can leave you personally responsible if a bill appears later.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The estate owns real estate, a business or property in more than one state
- A beneficiary or creditor is disputing something
- The estate may owe federal or state estate tax
- You are unsure whether to distribute while claims are still open
- The case has been quiet for months and you do not know why
- You are not sure the will is valid or complete
