## How to use this guide

A living will states your wishes. A DNR or POLST is a medical order that tells staff what to do right now. They are different tools, and mixing them up is how wishes get missed. Names and forms vary by state. It is general education, not legal or medical advice. A doctor completes and signs medical orders.

## The orders at a glance

| Term | Stands for | What it says | Who signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| DNR | Do not resuscitate | No CPR if the heart or breathing stops | Doctor, with the patient or surrogate |
| DNI | Do not intubate | No breathing tube or ventilator | Doctor, with the patient or surrogate |
| Code status | Hospital shorthand | "Full code" means all efforts. "DNR" means no CPR. | Recorded in the chart |
| POLST or MOLST | Physician (or Medical) Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment | A portable form with orders on CPR, intensity of treatment and feeding tubes | Doctor or other clinician and the patient or surrogate |
| Out-of-hospital DNR | A DNR for EMTs and home | Tells emergency crews not to start CPR | Doctor, on the state's form |
| Living will | Your written wishes | Says what you want if you are terminally ill or permanently unconscious | You, witnessed |

POLST and MOLST are names for the same idea. The name depends on the state, and some states use other names or do not have a program. Ask your doctor what your state uses.

## What a DNR does and does not do

A DNR covers CPR: chest compressions, shocks and breathing tubes if the heart stops. It does not mean "do not treat."

A person with a DNR can still get:

- Antibiotics and surgery
- Pain relief and comfort care
- Hospital admission and intensive care, unless other orders say no
- Fluids and nutrition, unless other orders say no

A DNI is separate. A person may refuse a ventilator but still accept CPR, or the reverse. It is worth stating each choice on its own.

## POLST compared with a living will

| | Living will or advance directive | POLST |
|---|---|---|
| Is it a medical order | No, it is a statement of wishes | Yes |
| Who completes it | You, with witnesses | You and your doctor |
| Who it is for | Any adult | People with serious illness or frailty |
| Used by EMTs | Often not | Yes |
| When it works | When you cannot speak for yourself and meet the form's conditions | Right away, across settings |

A living will says what you would want in the future. A POLST gives instructions to the nurse or paramedic at the door. Many people with serious illness have both.

## Who should consider a POLST

| If this is you | Then think about |
|---|---|
| Healthy adult | An advance directive and a named agent. A POLST is usually not needed. |
| Living with a serious or advancing illness | Ask your doctor whether a POLST fits your situation. |
| Frail and in a nursing home | A POLST is commonly discussed at admission. |
| Doctor would not be surprised if you died in the next year | A POLST conversation is typical. |
| Facing a planned surgery | Ask about perioperative orders; a DNR is often reviewed before surgery. |

Whether a young, healthy person can get a DNR depends on the doctor and the clinical situation. Most start with a conversation about values.

## Hospital orders versus orders at home

A hospital DNR order lives in the hospital chart. It stops at the hospital door. If you go home, a paramedic who is called usually must start CPR unless they see the state's valid out-of-hospital form.

So the form must be:

- [ ] On the state-approved form, signed by the required people
- [ ] Printed on the color or format the state requires
- [ ] Kept where emergency crews will look, often posted on the refrigerator
- [ ] Copied into the patient's wallet or folder for trips
- [ ] Added to the patient's electronic medical record
- [ ] Known to every caregiver, including home health aides

## Changes and conflicts

- A patient who can speak can change or cancel orders at any time.
- A surrogate can act when the patient cannot, within the state's rules.
- Family members who disagree with the order cannot simply tear it up. Raise concerns with the doctor and, if needed, the hospital ethics committee.
- If the patient's wishes change, make a new form and take the old one down.

## Questions to ask the doctor

- What happens if my heart stops, and what is the realistic outcome?
- Which options are included in this form?
- Does this form cover EMTs, home and nursing facility?
- How do I update it?
- How will you keep it in my record?

## Checklist after signing

- [ ] Original in a visible place at home
- [ ] Copy with your health care agent
- [ ] Copy in your wallet or phone
- [ ] Copy given to your primary care doctor and any hospice
- [ ] Review it after any big change in health

## When to talk to an attorney

- Family members disagree about an order
- You want your living will, power of attorney and POLST to match
- You moved to another state
- A hospital refuses to honor a signed directive
