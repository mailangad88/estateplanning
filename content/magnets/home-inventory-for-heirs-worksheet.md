## How to use this worksheet

Families rarely fight over money as bitterly as over a ring, a quilt or a toolbox. A written list of who should have what can head off that hurt. Do one room per sitting. Take photos as you go, and keep the list with your estate papers. This is general education, and rules about personal property vary by state.

Be honest about two kinds of items: things with real money value, and things with value only to your family. Both matter, in different ways.

## Step 1: Photos and a record

- [ ] Take a photo or short video of each room, including closets and drawers
- [ ] Take close-up photos of valuable or unusual items
- [ ] Note serial numbers, makers' marks and signs of age
- [ ] Save copies somewhere other than your phone
- [ ] Update after any major purchase or move

These records also help with insurance claims after a fire or theft.

## Step 4: Sentimental items

Make a list of the items you think people will care about most. Ask them. You may be surprised.

| Item | Why it matters | Who has asked or would value it | My choice |

| __________ | ________ | __________ | __________ |

Sample entry: "Grandmother's cast iron pan. Used for every Sunday breakfast. Daniel learned to cook with it."

If two people would both like the same item, say so and say what you decided. A short reason can keep the peace.

## Step 5: Fair does not always mean equal

- **Equal value:** Each person receives items of about the same worth.
- **Equal turns:** Heirs take turns choosing items. Draw numbers to decide the order, and reverse the order each round.
- **Targeted gifts:** You decide who gets what. It may feel uneven, so explain why.
- **Sell and divide:** Sell an item and divide the money.

Whichever you pick, write it down. Silence leads to guesses.

- **My method for dividing things:** ______________________
- **Items to sell, and who handles it:** ______________________
- **Items to donate and where:** ______________________
- **Who may decide if people disagree:** ______________________

## Step 6: Make it count

A written list alone may not be legally binding. The law differs by state, but a list that is not tied to your will may be treated as a wish. Many states allow a will to refer to a separate written list of personal items, and some set limits on how it works. A few options:

- Name specific items directly in your will.
- Ask your attorney whether your state allows a signed, dated list that your will refers to.
- Give items away now while you are alive, and enjoy watching them be used.
- Put wishes in a letter of instruction as guidance, understanding it may not bind anyone.

Do not rely on labels stuck on furniture or sticky notes. They can be lost, moved or contested.

## Step 7: Share and review

- [ ] Tell your executor where the inventory is
- [ ] Tell family members about the wishes that affect them
- [ ] Review the list once a year, and after a move or inheritance
- [ ] Update your will if you add or remove important items

## When to talk to an attorney

- You own antiques, art or collections of significant value
- You have a blended family or children who do not get along
- You want your will to refer to a separate list of items
- You own firearms, vehicles or items that need title transfers
- You are giving gifts now and want to understand the tax or Medicaid effects
