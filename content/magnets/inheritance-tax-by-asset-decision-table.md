## How to use this worksheet

Most people who inherit pay no tax on the inheritance itself. The tax questions usually come later, when you sell something, take money from a retirement account or live in a state with its own tax. Find each asset in the table, then fill in the inventory. This is general education and not tax advice. Check figures with a tax preparer.

## Five kinds of tax

| Tax | Who pays | Where the money comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Federal estate tax | The estate | Only very large estates, above a federal exemption of $15 million per person for 2026 <!-- verify: 2026 exemption --> |
| State estate tax | The estate | A group of states set lower thresholds. Illinois has one. See [Illinois estate tax guide](/free/illinois-estate-tax-guide). |
| State inheritance tax | The person who inherits | A few states only. Rates often depend on your relationship to the person who died. |
| Income tax | You | On income the asset earns, or on pre-tax retirement money you withdraw |
| Capital gains tax | You | On profit when you later sell, measured from a stepped-up value |

Illinois has an estate tax but no inheritance tax. States that have an inheritance tax are commonly listed as Iowa (phased out), Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey and Pennsylvania. <!-- verify: current list of inheritance tax states --> Check the state of the person who died, and the state where property sits. Pennsylvania, Kentucky and Maryland often tax children and other heirs at different rates. Spouses are commonly exempt. Use the [state death tax checker](/tools/state-death-tax-checker) to look up a state.

## Decision table by asset

| What you inherited | Income tax when you receive it? | Later tax | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash in a bank account | No | Tax only on interest after death | Report interest, not the principal |
| House or land | No | Capital gain when sold, measured from date-of-death value | Step-up in basis. Get an appraisal. |
| Stocks, funds, brokerage | No | Gain or loss from the stepped-up value | Selling right away usually means little gain |
| Traditional IRA or 401(k) | No, until withdrawn | Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income | Rules for timing apply, see below |
| Roth IRA | Usually no | Usually tax free if rules are met | Still has distribution deadlines |
| Annuity | Depends | The earnings portion is usually taxable | The insurer's form shows it |
| Life insurance (death benefit) | Generally no | Interest paid after death can be taxable | Death benefit usually income tax free |
| US savings bonds | Interest can be taxable | Reported by the estate or the heir | Check whether interest was ever reported |
| Business or farm | No | Basis is stepped up | Ask for a valuation |
| Foreign property or accounts | No US income tax on receipt, usually | Reporting rules may apply | Foreign gift reporting can be required <!-- verify: IRS Form 3520 threshold --> |

## Step-up in basis, in one example

If a parent bought a house for $100,000 and it was worth $400,000 when they died, your basis is generally $400,000. If you sell for $410,000, the gain is $10,000, not $310,000. Inherited property is treated as long-term, no matter how long you hold it. <!-- verify: holding period rule for inherited property -->

For a married couple in a community property state, the rule can be different for both halves. See [step up in basis guide](/free/step-up-in-basis-guide).

## Retirement accounts: the rule to know

Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years of the owner's death. <!-- verify: 10-year rule and annual withdrawal requirements --> Some people are exempt from the 10-year limit, such as a spouse or a minor child of the owner. Taking everything at once can push you into a higher bracket. Ask a tax preparer how to spread it. See [inherited retirement accounts guide](/free/inherited-retirement-accounts-guide).

## Forms you may see

- **Form 1041:** the estate's income tax return, if the estate earned income
- **Schedule K-1:** shows your share of the estate's income
- **Form 1099-R:** retirement distributions
- **Form 1099-INT or 1099-DIV:** interest and dividends
- **Form 706:** federal estate tax return, for large estates

You generally do not report the inheritance itself on your own return. Do report income it produces and gains when you sell.

## When to talk to an attorney

- The estate is near or above a state or federal tax threshold
- You inherited from someone in a state with an inheritance tax
- Foreign assets or a non-citizen is involved
- A large IRA or a business interest is involved
- Basis records are missing
