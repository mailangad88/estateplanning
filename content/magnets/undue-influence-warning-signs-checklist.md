## How to use this checklist

Undue influence means one person overpowers another's free choice, usually someone who is older, ill or dependent. A sudden change in a will, a new power of attorney or a new joint account can be perfectly innocent. It can also be a sign that someone is steering a parent. This checklist helps you tell the difference by gathering facts. It does not prove anything on its own. Rules and definitions vary by state, so use it to prepare for a conversation with an attorney.

## Warning signs in documents

- [ ] A new will, trust or power of attorney signed after a long-standing plan
- [ ] A new agent, trustee or main beneficiary who is a recent arrival in your parent's life
- [ ] A change that cuts out a child or relative without a reason your parent has ever voiced
- [ ] Documents prepared by a lawyer, notary or form service the helper chose, not your parent
- [ ] The helper drove your parent to the signing, stayed in the room or handled the paperwork
- [ ] Your parent cannot explain what the document does or why they signed it
- [ ] Beneficiary forms, deeds or account titles changed with no mention to the family

## Warning signs in money

- [ ] New joint accounts, or one person added to accounts as owner
- [ ] Large gifts, loans or "investments" that do not fit your parent's habits
- [ ] Bills going unpaid even though money is available
- [ ] Credit cards, checks or ATM withdrawals your parent cannot account for
- [ ] A house transferred, listed or refinanced without family knowledge
- [ ] New insurance, annuities or financial products sold through the helper's contacts
- [ ] The helper asking for "reimbursement" or payment for care with no written agreement

## Warning signs in access and isolation

- [ ] Phone calls or visits are screened, cut short or discouraged
- [ ] Your parent is moved to a new home, or away from longtime friends, a faith community or a doctor
- [ ] Mail is redirected, or the helper opens it
- [ ] The helper answers questions your parent is asked, or insists on being present for every talk
- [ ] Your parent seems afraid to disagree with the helper, or repeats phrases that sound rehearsed
- [ ] Family is told "she doesn't want to see you" but only by the helper

## Warning signs in health and behavior

- [ ] Memory problems, confusion or recent hospital stays near the time of a change
- [ ] Sudden mood changes, anxiety or withdrawal around one person
- [ ] Medication that is missed, doubled or managed by the helper alone
- [ ] Your parent says "I owe them" or "they have done so much for me" when asked about gifts

## Risk factors that make influence easier

Influence is more likely to succeed when several of these exist together:

| Risk factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Dependence on the helper for care, rides or meals | The helper controls daily comfort |
| Memory loss or illness | The parent may not catch details |
| Recent loss of a spouse | Grief and loneliness open the door |
| The helper is a caregiver, neighbor or new friend with a role in the finances | Trust and opportunity overlap |
| The helper benefits from the change | Motive is present |
| Secrecy around the signing | Others cannot ask questions |

Family members can be the source too. A child who lives nearby and manages everything may sincerely help, or may quietly steer. The same questions apply to everyone.

## Keep a dated log

Facts carry more weight than feelings. Write them down as you notice them.

- **Date:** ______________________
- **What I saw or heard:** ______________________
- **Who was present:** ______________________
- **Document, account or asset involved:** ______________________
- **How I know (saw it, was told by, bank statement):** ______________________
- **Anything my parent said in their own words:** ______________________

Keep copies of documents, letters, emails and text messages. Do not take originals or access accounts you are not authorized to see.

## Steps families can take

1. **Talk with your parent first, privately and kindly.** Ask open questions: "How are things going with the new arrangement?" Avoid accusing the helper. Accusations tend to push a parent closer to the person you are worried about.
2. **Offer support, not control.** Offer a ride to a visit with their longtime attorney or doctor. A parent who is supported is harder to isolate.
3. **Ask for a private appointment with an attorney.** Many attorneys meet the client alone before any signing, so the client's wishes are heard without the helper present.
4. **Ask whether a doctor's note makes sense.** If memory is a concern, a physician's evaluation near a signing can help later. Read more in the [capacity and signing guide](/free/capacity-and-signing-guide).
5. **Request copies of existing documents.** Your parent can authorize you, or can ask the attorney who drafted them for copies.
6. **Review who can see statements.** Your parent can add you as a trusted contact on accounts, or set up alerts, without giving you control.
7. **Think about bank and mail safeguards.** Some banks flag unusual withdrawals. Ask what protective steps a bank offers.
8. **Know who to call.** If you suspect abuse or exploitation, you can report it, as described below.

## Where to report in Illinois

In Illinois, the Department on Aging runs Adult Protective Services. It takes reports of abuse, neglect and financial exploitation of adults age 60 and older, and of adults ages 18 to 59 with disabilities who live in the community. The statewide hotline is 1-866-800-1409. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. Other states have their own agencies, often found through the state's department on aging or human services. Banks and local police can also take reports of theft or fraud.

## If your parent has already died

Challenges to a will or trust based on undue influence are usually filed in court within strict time limits that vary by state. Courts often look at the relationship between the parties, whether the helper was involved in arranging the document, whether the plan departs sharply from earlier ones and whether the parent had independent advice. If you think a document is suspect, ask an attorney about the deadline soon, because waiting can end your options.

## When to talk to an attorney

- A parent has signed, or is about to sign, a new will, trust, deed or power of attorney that surprises the family
- A caregiver, friend or relative has taken control of accounts, mail or visits
- Money has moved in ways you cannot explain
- Your parent shows signs of memory loss, and a signing is planned
- Your parent has died and you suspect influence over a will or trust
- You want to know whether a guardianship or conservatorship is warranted, which is a serious step with court oversight
