## How to use this guide

Basis is the starting number used to measure taxable profit when you sell. A step-up changes that number at death. It can erase years of gain for heirs. Read the examples, then use the worksheet at the end on your biggest assets.

## Basis in one example

Maria buys stock for $40,000. It grows to $200,000. If she sells it, her gain is $160,000, and she owes capital gains tax on that gain. The $40,000 is her basis.

## Gift versus inheritance

Compare two ways the stock reaches her daughter.

| Path | Daughter's basis | Daughter sells for $200,000 | Taxable gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maria gives it during life | Maria's $40,000 (carryover) | Yes | $160,000 |
| Maria leaves it at death, value $200,000 | $200,000 (stepped up) | Yes | $0 |

In general, property inherited from someone who died receives a basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death. The executor may be able to choose an alternate valuation date six months later, but only when that lowers the estate tax. The heir is also treated as holding the property long term, whatever the real holding period.

A step-down is possible too. If the property fell below the original basis, the basis can drop to the lower value, and the loss is lost.

## Which assets get a step-up

| Asset | Usually stepped up? |
|---|---|
| Home, land, stock, mutual funds | Yes |
| Business interests | Yes |
| Property in a revocable trust | Yes, because it is counted in the estate |
| Property owned jointly with a spouse | Half, in most common-law states |
| IRAs, 401(k)s and annuities | No. Withdrawals are taxed as income |
| Gifts made during life | No. Carryover basis |
| Property in many irrevocable trusts | Often no, depending on how the trust is drafted |

The last row is why a trust drafted to "remove assets from your estate" can cost heirs income tax. Ask whether a trust is built to include assets in the estate.

## Community property states

A handful of states are community property states. When a spouse dies, both halves of community property can receive a step-up, not only the decedent's half. Illinois is not a community property state, so the half-step rule generally applies to a married Illinois couple. Check where you live and where you owned property while married. Moving states can change the answer.

## Planning ideas people raise with attorneys

- **Hold appreciated property until death** rather than giving it away, if the estate is below the estate tax exemption.
- **Give cash or high-basis assets** to the person receiving the lifetime gift, and keep low-basis assets.
- **Review irrevocable trusts** that may be giving up a step-up.
- **Record purchase price and improvements** now. Heirs need the date-of-death value, and the paperwork is far easier to build before death.

Not every idea suits every family. Estate tax, Medicaid rules and family goals can pull the other way.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a child the house to "avoid probate" and sending that child a large capital gains bill.
- Adding a child to a deed during life, which can give the child a carryover basis in their share.
- Assuming an inherited IRA gets a step-up.
- Not getting a date-of-death appraisal after an inheritance.
- Forgetting the cost of improvements when computing basis on a home.

## Worksheet

Complete one block for each major asset.

- **Asset:** ____________
- **Current estimated value:** ____________
- **Your basis (cost plus improvements):** ____________
- **Unrealized gain (value minus basis):** ____________
- **Titled in:** ____________ (your name, joint, trust, child)
- **Planned for:** [ ] sell during life  [ ] give  [ ] hold until death
- **Estimated gain if the heir sold after a gift:** ____________
- **Estimated gain if the heir sold after inheritance:** ____________
- **Questions for the attorney:** ____________

Multiply each gain by your estimated capital gains rate (ask a tax adviser) to compare the two outcomes.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You hold property with large gains, such as a long-owned home, farm or business.
- You are thinking of deeding property to children.
- You have, or are considering, an irrevocable trust.
- You are a surviving spouse unsure of the basis of jointly owned property.
- You moved between community property and other states.
