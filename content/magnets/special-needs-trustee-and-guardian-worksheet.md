## How to use this worksheet

Three jobs matter after parents are gone: someone controls the money, someone makes personal decisions and someone knows the person's daily life. One person can hold more than one job, but it is rarely wise to assume so. Work through the tables. Rules vary by state, so confirm choices with an attorney licensed where the person lives.

## Roles at a glance

| Role | What they do | Who often serves |
|---|---|---|
| Trustee | Holds trust money, pays vendors, keeps records, files taxes | A sibling, a trusted friend, a bank or professional trustee, or a mix |
| Successor trustee | Steps in if the first trustee cannot serve | Named in the trust, in order |
| Trust protector | Can change trustees or fix the trust if the law changes | A lawyer or trusted adviser, not the trustee |
| Guardian | Court-appointed decision-maker, if needed | A sibling, close relative, or a public guardian |
| Agent under a power of attorney | Acts for a person who can sign documents | Chosen by the person themselves, if they have capacity |
| Care coordinator or advocate | Visits, watches services, speaks up | A sibling, friend or nonprofit |

## What a special needs trustee does

A trustee has a legal duty to act for the beneficiary. In plain terms the job includes:

- [ ] Keeping trust money apart from personal money
- [ ] Paying providers and vendors, not handing over cash
- [ ] Keeping receipts and a payment log
- [ ] Reporting to benefit agencies when asked
- [ ] Filing the trust's tax returns
- [ ] Investing prudently
- [ ] Meeting the beneficiary often enough to know what they need
- [ ] Reviewing the trust every year with an attorney

See /free/special-needs-trust-spending-guide for spending rules.

## Who can be trustee

| Candidate | Strengths | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Parent | Knows the person best | May die or become unable; the trust needs a successor |
| Sibling | Knows the family, cost-effective | Can feel burdened; may have conflicts of interest or a divorce |
| Friend | Neutral, loyal | May lack skills or time |
| Bank or trust company | Lasts, professional | Fees, minimum sizes, less personal |
| Nonprofit pooled trust | Experience with benefits | Rules set by the nonprofit |
| The beneficiary | Not recommended | Control of the trust can make assets count for SSI and Medicaid |

A beneficiary generally should not be sole trustee of a trust meant to protect benefits, because control can cause the money to count. <!-- verify: attorney review of this point -->

## Co-trustees and splitting jobs

Many families name two people: a family member for personal knowledge and a professional for paperwork. Another option is a family trustee with a trust protector who can replace the trustee if things go wrong. Name successors in order, not just one.

## Trust protector

A trust protector is an outside person with limited powers, such as removing a trustee, changing the trust to match new law or moving the trust to another state. This role helps because a special needs trust may need to last fifty years. Choose someone who is not the trustee and not a beneficiary.

## Guardian and decision-making

If the person has capacity, supported choices such as a power of attorney, health care proxy and representative payee may avoid guardianship. See /free/guardianship-alternatives-guide. If guardianship is needed, courts consider the person's interests and the proposed guardian's fitness. Naming a guardian in your will can be a statement of preference, not always binding for an adult. <!-- verify: Illinois treatment of will nominations for adult wards -->

Siblings can serve as guardians. The role takes time, travel and sometimes court reports. Ask first.

## Talking with the other children

Do not announce a role. Ask.

1. Share the plan and the person's needs
2. Ask each child what they want to do and not do
3. Explain what money is available and what is not (the trust pays costs, but not for the sibling's time unless written)
4. Offer to pay a fee, if it fits
5. Write the agreement and name a backup

- **Sibling's name:** ______________________
- **Willing to be:** ______________________
- **Conditions:** ______________________
- **Backup:** ______________________

## If you are the sibling

If your parents have passed and you are asked to help, ask for the trust document, the letter of intent and a list of providers. A sibling who steps in with no document often has to ask a court. See /free/letter-of-intent-special-needs-template.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are choosing a trustee and want terms on fees and removal
- You want a trust protector and are unsure of the powers
- A guardianship may be needed
- The family disagrees about the plan
- A sibling is willing but the trust is unclear about pay
