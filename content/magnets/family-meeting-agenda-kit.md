**How to use this kit.** The existing [family meeting worksheet](/free/family-meeting-worksheet) covers what to share and a fill-in sheet of details. This kit covers the meeting itself: who does what, how to keep it from turning into an argument, and what to say when it does. Use Part 1 to plan, Part 2 to run the room, Part 3 for scripts, and Part 4 afterward. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Part 1: Plan the meeting

Decide these before sending any invitation.

- **Purpose in one sentence:** ______________________ (example: "Tell the family who will act for us, and where the papers are.")
- **What we will share, and what stays private:** ______________________
- **Who is invited:** ______________________ (adult children, spouses of children, others)
- **Format:** in person / video / phone for anyone far away
- **Date, length, and place:** ______________________
- **A neutral facilitator, if wanted (attorney, mediator, friend):** ______________________

Choices that help:

- Pick a calm setting, not a holiday dinner or a hospital hallway.
- Allow ninety minutes at most. A second meeting beats an exhausted first one.
- Send a short note ahead so nobody is blindsided. Sample: "We are putting our estate plan in order. We want you to know who will help, and where things are. We are not asking for anything. Can we set aside an hour on [date]?"
- Decide ahead whether spouses of your children attend. There is no single right answer. Some families include them for the practical parts only.

## Part 2: Run the room

### Roles

| Role | Job | Name |
|---|---|---|
| Host | Opens, states purpose, keeps to the agenda | |
| Timekeeper | Calls time on each item, gently | |
| Note-taker | Writes decisions and open questions on the Part 4 sheet | |
| Voice for those not present | Speaks for anyone who could not attend, and reads their questions | |

If a parent hosts, someone else should keep time and notes, so the parent can listen.

### Ground rules

Read these aloud and ask for a nod from each person.

- [ ] One person speaks at a time.
- [ ] We ask questions to understand, not to win.
- [ ] Nothing decided today is changed by one person later without telling the others.
- [ ] What is said here is for the family. We do not discuss it with outsiders.
- [ ] We can pause at any time for five minutes.
- [ ] Questions about fairness are welcome and will be answered, even if the answer is "I will think about it."

### Agenda (about 75 minutes)

| Time | Item | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Welcome and purpose | Host reads the one-sentence purpose |
| 0:05 | Ground rules | Part 2 list |
| 0:10 | Who is named to act | Executor, trustee, agent for money, agent for health care, guardian for any minors |
| 0:25 | Where papers are | Use a one-page locator, and show where it is kept |
| 0:35 | Health care wishes, in general | The values behind choices, not every medical detail |
| 0:50 | What the plan does, in outline | Share as much as you chose in Part 1 |
| 1:05 | Questions and concerns | Round the room once |
| 1:10 | Next steps | Part 4 follow-up list |

First meeting? Do only the roles of agents, where papers are, and next steps. About 30 minutes.

## Part 3: Scripts for hard moments

**Opening.** "This is not about money first. It is about making sure that when something happens, nobody is guessing. I want you to know who I picked to help and why."

**When someone asks "who gets what?"** "I am not ready to share amounts today. What I can tell you is who will act for me and how decisions get made. I will tell you more when the plan is final."

**When siblings compare.** "I hear that you want this to be fair. I have tried to be fair, which is not always the same as equal. Here is my thinking. You can disagree, and I will still listen."

**When someone feels passed over for a role.** "I chose [name] because they live nearby and are organized, not because I love anyone more. If circumstances change, I will revisit it."

**When a spouse of a child speaks up.** "Thank you for caring about this. This conversation is mainly among the children, and we may ask for your thoughts later."

**When it gets heated.** "Let's pause for five minutes. We can come back to this item or put it on the list for next time."

**When someone says they do not want to talk about death.** "I understand. This is not about dying soon. It is the same as putting a spare key somewhere safe. We can keep it short."

**When a parent loses the thread.** The note-taker reads back the last decision and asks, "Did I get that right?" Repeating the point gently beats correcting it.

## Part 4: Follow-up sheet

Fill in during the meeting and send a copy to everyone within two days.

- **Date and who attended:** ______________________
- **Decisions made:** ______________________
- **Who has agreed to serve in what role:** ______________________
- **Where documents are kept:** ______________________
- **Questions we could not answer:** ______________________
- **Who will find the answer, by when:** ______________________
- **Do we need to change any document because of what we learned?** Yes / No. Which: ______________________
- **Next meeting date (if any):** ______________________

Follow-up checklist:

- [ ] Send the written summary, with no more detail than you agreed to share.
- [ ] Update the contacts list and the document locator.
- [ ] Call anyone who was upset, individually, within a week.
- [ ] Ask each person named as an agent to confirm they are willing and have copies of what they need.
- [ ] Book time with your attorney if the meeting showed that something should change.

## Common mistakes

1. **Using the meeting to announce a surprise.** Tell people with major news privately first.
2. **Letting the loudest person set the agenda.** The timekeeper and note-taker prevent this.
3. **Promising more than the documents say.** "You will get the house" is a statement your plan may not support.
4. **Treating silence as agreement.** Ask each person, by name, whether they have questions.
5. **Meeting once and never again.** Plans change. Plan a short check-in each year.

## When to talk to an attorney

- Family members disagree about who should serve, and you want a neutral explanation of what each role involves.
- You plan an unequal division and want to document your reasoning.
- A child has special needs, an addiction, debts, or a troubled marriage.
- You have a blended family, or a business that one child may take over.
- Someone may challenge your plan, or you worry about undue influence.
