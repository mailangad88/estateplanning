## How to use this guide

We are sorry for your loss. Grief and money in the same room often cause conflict, even in close families. Most disagreements are not about greed. They come from hurt, silence or a feeling of unfair treatment. This guide helps you sort the type of dispute, choose a path from calm to formal, and keep records. It is educational. Courts and rules vary by state. If a dispute is already in court, an attorney should advise you.

## Common disputes

| Dispute | What it usually looks like | First thing to try |
|---|---|---|
| Unequal shares | One child got more than another | Read the will or trust itself, then ask why in a calm meeting |
| Who gets the house | One wants to keep it, another wants to sell | A buyout or sale terms. See the [inherited home decision guide](/free/inherited-home-decision-guide) |
| Personal items | Several people want the same item | A choosing rotation. See [cleaning out a parent's home](/free/cleaning-out-a-parents-home-planner) |
| Executor conduct | Heirs suspect delay, secrecy or self-dealing | A written accounting |
| Caregiver payments | A child cared for the parent and wants repayment | Look for a written agreement. See [personal care agreements](/free/personal-care-agreement-guide) |
| Validity of the will | A heir believes the will does not reflect real wishes | See the [contesting a will guide](/free/contesting-a-will-basics-guide) |
| A second spouse versus children | Competing claims to property | Review how the property is titled |

## If you are the executor

You are a fiduciary. You act for the estate, not for your own side of the family. Even if you are also an heir, your job is to follow the will and the law and to treat each beneficiary fairly.

- [ ] Send each beneficiary a copy of the will, once it is filed
- [ ] Give regular written updates, even short ones
- [ ] Keep estate money separate from yours
- [ ] Keep a [ledger of every payment](/free/estate-accounting-ledger-template)
- [ ] Do not favor one heir by giving early gifts or inside information
- [ ] Do not decide a contested question alone. Ask an attorney or the court
- [ ] Be careful about paying yourself. Rules and the will control compensation

Being open is your strongest protection. Heirs who see the numbers rarely suspect them. See [can an executor also be a beneficiary](/blog/can-an-executor-also-be-a-beneficiary).

## If you are a beneficiary

- [ ] Ask for a copy of the will and any trust
- [ ] Ask for an inventory and, later, an accounting
- [ ] Put your concerns in writing, politely and specifically
- [ ] Do not take items from the home without agreement
- [ ] Write down dates and what was said
- [ ] Ask what the next step and expected date are before you assume bad faith
- [ ] Avoid sharing a family dispute on social media

## Step 1: Understand what the documents say

Many fights go away when everyone reads the same pages. Sit down with the will, any trust, and the beneficiary forms for accounts and insurance. Remember that accounts with named beneficiaries pass outside the will, which can make the split look unequal even when no one did anything wrong. See [probate vs non-probate assets](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## Step 2: Hold a family meeting

Set simple ground rules. Meet after the funeral, not at it.

1. Agree on an end time, and a note-taker.
2. Each person speaks without interruption.
3. Start with facts: what the documents say, what has been done, what is next.
4. Then talk about feelings and priorities.
5. End with a written list of agreements and open questions.

Sample opening: "We are all hurting, and we all want this handled fairly. I would like to go through what the will says, what I have done so far, and what happens next. Please save questions for after each part."

## Step 3: Try mediation

A mediator is a neutral person who helps people reach their own agreement. Many families find it faster, cheaper and more private than court. A mediator does not decide. Both sides must be willing, and an agreement can later be made binding, often with a court's approval.

| | Mediation | Court case |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | The family, with a neutral helper | A judge |
| Privacy | Usually private | Usually public record |
| Cost and speed | Often lower and faster | Often higher and slower |
| Relationships | Easier to preserve | Often strained |
| Result | Agreed by all | Imposed |

## Step 4: When court is needed

Some questions only a court can answer. Examples include whether a will is valid, whether an executor should be removed, and what a vague clause means. Courts also order an accounting when an executor refuses to provide one. Deadlines for these steps can be short and vary by state. Do not wait to find out. Ask an attorney about time limits promptly.

Possible court steps include:

- Asking the court to interpret a will or trust
- Asking for an accounting
- Asking to remove or replace the executor or trustee
- Challenging the will
- Seeking court approval of a settlement

## Habits that lower the temperature

- Reply in writing and wait a day before sending anything angry
- Keep one person as the contact point
- Treat sentimental items and money as separate topics
- Give people time to grieve before deciding big matters
- Bring in a neutral helper before positions harden
- Plan ahead for your own family. See the [family meeting worksheet](/free/family-meeting-worksheet)

## When to talk to an attorney

- You think the executor is hiding assets or mismanaging money
- Someone is threatening to challenge the will, or you want to
- The estate includes a business, real estate or large accounts
- An heir is pressuring you to sign something
- You are an executor and a beneficiary and the two roles conflict
- A court deadline may apply
