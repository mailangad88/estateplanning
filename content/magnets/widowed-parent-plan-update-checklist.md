**How to use this.** There is no deadline for most of this page, and nobody needs you to finish it this month. Read it once, pick the one or two things in the first section, and come back later for the rest. Skip anything that does not fit. If you have already begun settling your spouse's estate, our [first 30 days checklist](/checklists/first-30-days-after-a-death) covers that. This page is about your own plan.

## Why your plan changed when theirs ended

Most couples write plans that lean on each other. You were each other's executor, agent and guardian. When one of you dies, the other becomes the single point of the plan. If something happened to you now, the documents may name someone who has died. In that case a court may have to decide, and the person it chooses may not be the person you would have picked.

## The first weeks: only these, if you can

- [ ] Tell someone you trust where your important papers are.
- [ ] Name a person who could care for your children for a few days if you were ill or in hospital. A written note is enough for now.
- [ ] If you hold a health care power of attorney that names your spouse, ask a friend or relative to be listed as a temporary contact with your doctor.
- [ ] Keep paying the basics. Hold off on big financial decisions such as selling the house, moving money or changing investments.

Everything below can wait until you feel steadier. Many people begin around three to six months after the death. Others wait a year. Both are fine.

## Month 1 to 3: Gather and rest

- [ ] Request several certified copies of the death certificate. You will be asked for them.
- [ ] Make a simple list of accounts, policies and who is named. The [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) is a good place to write it down.
- [ ] Notify Social Security if the funeral home has not. Ask about survivor benefits for you and for any children.
- [ ] Look at where your spouse's life insurance and retirement benefits are paying out.

## Month 3 to 6: Your documents

Ask an attorney to review these. Tick each when you have talked it through, not when it is finished.

- [ ] **Will.** Does it still make sense now that your spouse is gone? Many wills leave everything to the spouse first, and name children only as a backup.
- [ ] **Executor.** Is the person you named still willing and able? Is there a backup?
- [ ] **Guardian for minor children.** This is often the most emotional choice. A court will weigh your wishes, so make them clear and give a first and second choice.
- [ ] **Money for the children.** If you die while they are young, who manages what they inherit? A trust is often used instead of leaving money to a child outright.
- [ ] **Financial power of attorney.** Replace any agent who was your spouse.
- [ ] **Health care power of attorney and living will.** Same.
- [ ] **HIPAA release.** Add a person who can speak with your doctors.
- [ ] **Letter of instruction.** Write one, or update it. It is a note to those who will help you that says where things are.

## Month 6 to 12: Titles and beneficiaries

Your late spouse's name may still appear on documents that do not follow a will. Each one needs its own paperwork.

| Item | Typical step | Done |
|---|---|---|
| Home and vehicles | Record a death certificate or file the transfer form your county or state requires | [ ] |
| Joint bank accounts | Ask the bank to retitle in your name | [ ] |
| Retirement accounts you inherited | Ask a tax professional about options before moving money. Rules for spouses differ from other heirs | [ ] |
| Your own life insurance | Replace your spouse as beneficiary. Name a trust or custodian for minor children, not a child directly | [ ] |
| Your own retirement accounts | Name new primary and backup beneficiaries | [ ] |
| Bank and brokerage accounts | Add payable-on-death or transfer-on-death names | [ ] |
| Credit cards, utilities, subscriptions | Close or retitle as needed | [ ] |

## Money and insurance to review, gently

- [ ] **Life insurance.** If you rely on your spouse's income, ask whether you have enough coverage on yourself. A single parent's death leaves no second adult for the children.
- [ ] **Disability insurance.** Check what you have through work.
- [ ] **Budget.** Your household income and expenses have changed. A fee-only planner or your attorney can help you plan. There is no rush.
- [ ] **Taxes.** Your filing status may change in future years. Ask your tax preparer what applies to you.

## What you can say to your children

Children often ask, "What happens to us if something happens to you?" You may not have an answer yet. That is allowed. You can say: "I am working on that, and I have asked someone I trust to look after you for now."

## When to talk to an attorney

- Your documents named your spouse as the only executor, agent or trustee.
- You have children under 18 and no named guardian for them.
- Your late spouse's trust or will still needs to be settled.
- You inherited retirement accounts or real estate and are unsure how to retitle them.
- A child has special needs or receives government benefits.
- You are thinking about remarrying or have a new partner.
