## How to use this kit

Divorce touches more documents than most people expect. A decree divides property, but it does not change a beneficiary form or a power of attorney by itself. Use this kit to build a list, then take it to your attorneys. This is educational and not advice for your case.

One caution comes first. While a divorce case is pending, courts in many places restrict certain changes, such as moving assets, changing insurance or altering beneficiaries. Rules vary by state and by the orders in your case. Ask your divorce attorney before you change anything on this list.

## Part 1: During the case

These are things to gather and ask about, not necessarily to change today.

- [ ] Ask your divorce attorney whether any standing or automatic orders apply to accounts, insurance or beneficiary forms
- [ ] Ask whether you may change a beneficiary on a life insurance policy while the case is pending
- [ ] Ask whether health insurance coverage for your spouse continues, and until when
- [ ] Gather a copy of every current will, trust, power of attorney and health care directive
- [ ] List every retirement account, pension and insurance policy with the current beneficiary
- [ ] Note any policies or accounts the settlement may require you to keep for a spouse or child
- [ ] Do not sign or revoke documents involving your spouse until you have asked

Write down what your attorney tells you:

- **Changes allowed now:** ______________________
- **Changes to wait on until the decree:** ______________________
- **Attorney name and date of this advice:** ______________________

## Part 2: Forms that may not update automatically

Some states have laws that treat a former spouse as if they died first for certain documents once the divorce is final. Those laws vary. They may not cover every account. Federal rules for some employer plans can override state law, so an ex named on an older form may still receive the money.

Two practical points:

- If a settlement requires you to keep a former spouse or a child as beneficiary for a set time, follow that order and write down the end date.
- Ask each plan administrator for a written confirmation of the change. A form you filled in but never submitted does nothing.

## Part 3: Your will and trust

A will signed during the marriage may leave most of your property to your spouse and name your spouse as executor. Depending on your state, a divorce can cancel those gifts and appointments. The details differ. Some states stop there and do not cancel everything, and a trust you created may keep naming your ex as trustee or successor.

After the decree, many people consider:

- [ ] A new will naming a new executor and backup
- [ ] Updating a revocable trust, including the successor trustee and who receives property
- [ ] Reviewing who receives your property if your children are young, and how they receive it (see [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors))
- [ ] Checking that titles to bank accounts and your home match the settlement

## Part 4: Agents for financial and medical decisions

A power of attorney lets a person act for you with money and legal matters. A health care agent speaks for you with doctors. If your spouse holds either role, they usually should be reviewed. Some states automatically end a spouse's authority after divorce. Others require you to revoke it.

- [ ] Sign a new financial power of attorney and revoke the old one in writing
- [ ] Sign a new health care power of attorney or directive and name a new agent
- [ ] Give a copy to the new agent, your doctor and the bank if it wants one
- [ ] Ask banks and brokerages to confirm they received the revocation
- [ ] Update your HIPAA release if your ex is listed
- [ ] Update emergency contacts at work, school, your doctor and your phone

## Part 5: Kids and guardians

If you have minor children, a will is where you name a guardian. After a divorce, the other parent usually keeps legal custody if you die, unless a court decides otherwise. Many parents still name a guardian in case both parents are unable to care for the children.

- [ ] Review who you named as guardian and whether it still fits
- [ ] Ask your attorney how custody orders interact with your guardian choice
- [ ] Decide whether money for the children should be held in a trust rather than paid to a young adult or to the other parent
- [ ] Choose a trustee who is not your ex, if that is your wish
- [ ] Review life insurance amounts so child support obligations are covered if that is part of your settlement

Write down your answers:

- **Guardian if both parents cannot serve:** ______________________
- **Who manages money for our children:** ______________________
- **Life insurance required by decree (amount and end date):** ______________________

See [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children).

## When to talk to an attorney

- You are still married and need to know what you may change while the case is pending
- A settlement or decree requires you to keep insurance or name a beneficiary
- A former spouse is named on an employer plan, an annuity or a trust
- You have minor children or a child with special needs
- You own a business or real estate with your spouse
- You are not sure whether your state cancels gifts to a spouse after divorce
