**How to use this checklist.** Retirement changes how you earn, spend and protect money, and it often changes what your estate plan should say. Tick what is done, mark what is open, and bring the open items to an attorney and your financial or tax advisor. This is educational and not advice for your situation. Rules on retirement accounts, taxes, care and property vary, and some change often, so confirm details with a professional.

## Section 1: Beneficiaries on retirement accounts

Retirement accounts pass by beneficiary form, not by your will. An old form can send money to an ex-spouse, a deceased parent or no one at all.

- [ ] List every IRA, 401(k), 403(b), pension and annuity, with its custodian.
- [ ] Confirm the primary beneficiary on each, by getting a copy of the current form.
- [ ] Confirm the **contingent beneficiary** on each.
- [ ] Check that the form still matches what you want. Marriage, divorce, new grandchildren and deaths are the usual reasons to change.
- [ ] If you are married, ask whether your spouse has rights to a workplace plan. Many employer plans require spousal consent to name someone else.
- [ ] Decide whether to name a person, a trust or a charity, and why.
- [ ] If you name a trust, have an attorney review it first. Not every trust works well as an IRA beneficiary.
- [ ] Avoid naming "my estate" unless an attorney has advised it.
- [ ] Keep copies of the forms with your plan.

See the [Beneficiary Designation Audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit) for a full walk-through.

## Section 2: Required minimum distributions (RMDs)

An RMD is a minimum amount the government generally requires you to withdraw each year from most traditional retirement accounts once you reach a certain age. That age has changed in recent years, and the rules are detailed, so check the current age and the penalty for missing a withdrawal with a tax professional.

- [ ] Find out when your first RMD is due.
- [ ] Ask how the amount is figured and whether it must be taken from each account separately.
- [ ] Decide whether you want to set up automatic withdrawals.
- [ ] Ask your advisor how RMDs fit with your income plan, your taxes and your charitable giving.
- [ ] Ask what rules apply to your beneficiaries after you die. Rules for inherited retirement accounts differ depending on who the heir is and have changed in recent years.
- [ ] Keep track of accounts left at old employers.

## Section 3: Medicare and healthcare directive

- [ ] Confirm when you enroll in Medicare and what you will do about supplemental coverage.
- [ ] Sign a **healthcare power of attorney**, which names someone to make medical decisions if you cannot.
- [ ] Sign a **living will** or advance directive, which states your wishes about life-sustaining treatment.
- [ ] Choose a backup agent.
- [ ] Talk with your agent about what matters most to you. A document does not replace the conversation.
- [ ] Give copies to your agent, your doctor and your hospital, and keep one at home where it can be found.
- [ ] Sign a HIPAA release so your agent can talk to your doctors.
- [ ] Review these documents when your health changes.

See [Healthcare Directives and Living Wills](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) and [Living Will vs. Healthcare Power of Attorney](/compare/living-will-vs-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## Section 4: Power of attorney for finances

A durable financial power of attorney names someone to handle money if you cannot. It can prevent a court-supervised guardianship or conservatorship in many cases, although rules vary by state.

- [ ] Sign a durable financial power of attorney. "Durable" means it continues if you lose capacity.
- [ ] Choose an agent you trust, and a backup.
- [ ] Decide what powers the agent has, such as paying bills, managing investments, handling taxes, making gifts and dealing with real estate.
- [ ] Decide whether the document takes effect right away or only upon incapacity. Each choice has trade-offs.
- [ ] Ask whether your bank or brokerage has its own form and will accept yours.
- [ ] Tell your agent where documents are kept.
- [ ] Set up a way for a second person to see statements, so there is a check on the agent.

See [Powers of Attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).

## Section 5: Downsizing and home title

Moving to a smaller place, a condo or a care community can change what you own and how it is titled.

- [ ] Before you sell, ask how the sale will affect your taxes.
- [ ] Before you re-title a home into your child's name, talk to an attorney. A gift of a home can have tax consequences and expose the home to your child's creditors or divorce.
- [ ] If your home is in a revocable trust, check that any new home is deeded to the trust too.
- [ ] Ask whether a transfer on death deed is available in your state and whether it fits.
- [ ] Update homeowner's or renter's insurance after a move.
- [ ] Update your address with banks, Social Security, Medicare, the IRS and the state.
- [ ] If you move to a new state, have your documents reviewed there. See [Moving to a New State](/life-events/moving-to-a-new-state).
- [ ] Keep a list of what you own and where it is, using the [Asset and Account Inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory).

## Section 6: Gifting basics

Many retirees want to help children or grandchildren while they are alive. Gifts can be generous, but they also affect taxes and long-term care eligibility.

- [ ] Learn the **annual gift tax exclusion**, which is $19,000 per recipient <!-- verify --> (the 2025 figure, indexed over time). Gifts at or below it generally do not require a gift tax return.
- [ ] Remember that larger gifts may count against a lifetime exemption and may need a return, even if no tax is due.
- [ ] Pay tuition or medical bills directly to the school or provider. In general, those payments are treated differently from gifts.
- [ ] Keep records of every gift.
- [ ] Before giving away large amounts, ask how the gifts could affect Medicaid eligibility. Medicaid generally looks back at transfers for 60 months <!-- verify -->, with state variations.
- [ ] Do not give away so much that you cannot cover your own care.

## Section 7: Long-term care thinking

Long-term care is often the largest cost in later life, and one that many people never plan for. These are questions, not answers.

- [ ] Would you want to stay at home, move to assisted living or a care community? What would each cost where you live?
- [ ] Who would help you? Family, paid caregivers, or both?
- [ ] Do you have any long-term care or hybrid insurance? Read the policy to see what it covers and what triggers benefits.
- [ ] Do you understand what Medicare does and does not cover for long-term care?
- [ ] Have you talked with your family about your preferences?
- [ ] Have you looked at Medicaid planning rules, which are detailed and vary by state? See [Medicaid and Long-Term Care Planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning).
- [ ] Have you named who would coordinate your care?

## Section 8: Tidy up the basics

- [ ] Review your will and any trust for changes in assets, family and state of residence.
- [ ] Review the executor and trustee choices. Are they still alive, willing and able?
- [ ] Update your list of accounts, passwords and important contacts.
- [ ] Write a letter of instruction. See the [Letter of Instruction Outline](/checklists/letter-of-instruction-outline).
- [ ] Set a yearly reminder to review all of this.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have not updated your will or trust since before retiring.
- You are retiring to a different state.
- Your beneficiaries include a minor, someone with a disability or a former spouse.
- You are thinking about large gifts, selling your home or moving into care.
- You have a blended family and want to be sure everyone is treated as you intend.
- You are worried about a health event and have no documents in place.
