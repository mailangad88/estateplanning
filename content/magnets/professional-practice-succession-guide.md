**How to use this guide.** Read the first two parts to learn the shape of the problem, then fill in the worksheet. Rules for each profession vary by state and by licensing board, so this guide shows the common patterns and tells you what to ask, not what your state requires.

## The core problem

Most businesses can pass to a spouse or child by will. Many professional practices cannot. States commonly require that the owners of a professional corporation or similar entity be licensed in that profession. An heir who is not licensed may be barred from owning shares, running the practice or even receiving a share of fees for professional services. Illinois, for example, has separate statutes for professional service corporations and medical corporations, and they limit who may hold ownership. Other states have their own versions.

For heirs, this often means the practice has to be sold or redeemed within a set time after the owner dies. Waiting makes it harder. A practice without its professional can lose patients or clients quickly.

## What the licensing limits usually cover

| Question | Typical pattern | Check with |
|---|---|---|
| Who may own shares? | Licensed professionals in the field | State statute and licensing board |
| Can an estate hold shares for a time? | Often for a short period, then must transfer | State statute |
| Who may supervise patients or clients? | Only licensed people | Licensing board |
| Who owns the real estate and equipment? | Often anyone, such as a family LLC | Your attorney |
| Who owns patient or client records? | Special rules apply | Board rules and privacy law |

This split is useful. Equipment, the building and the non-professional value (such as an LLC that owns the real estate) can often pass to family, while the clinical or professional part cannot.

## Option 1: Sale to a colleague or associate

- [ ] A buy-sell agreement exists with a named buyer or a process (see the [Buy-Sell Agreement Checklist](/free/buy-sell-agreement-checklist)).
- [ ] Price is set by a formula or appraisal that reflects goodwill honestly.
- [ ] Insurance funds the purchase.
- [ ] A licensed buyer is ready to take over within the board's time limit.

## Option 2: Associate buy-in over time

An associate buys in over several years. If you die or become disabled, the agreement sets the final price and payment terms.

- [ ] Buy-in terms are written.
- [ ] A non-compete clause is reasonable and enforceable in your state. Some states limit these for health care workers.
- [ ] The associate knows the practice's systems and finances.

## Option 3: Temporary coverage arrangement

Many practices set up a reciprocal arrangement with a colleague who agrees to run the practice, see patients or clients, and handle records for a limited period. This buys time to find a buyer.

## Option 4: Orderly closing

If no buyer is found, the practice must close in the manner the board expects. This usually means notice to patients or clients, a transfer or storage plan for records, and a plan for ongoing treatment or deadlines.

- [ ] Notice letters are drafted.
- [ ] A records custodian is named.
- [ ] Malpractice tail coverage (extended reporting) is understood and priced.
- [ ] Open matters, appointments and filing deadlines have a hand-off plan.

## Records and confidentiality

Patient and client records come with legal duties that do not stop at death. In health care, privacy rules continue to apply, and many states require records be kept for a minimum period. Accountants and attorneys also have confidentiality and retention duties.

## Personal documents that matter more for owners

- [ ] A durable power of attorney that works for financial matters, with a successor agent (see [Powers of Attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney)).
- [ ] A will or trust coordinated with the practice agreement.
- [ ] Disability insurance and key person insurance.
- [ ] Beneficiary forms for retirement plans.
- [ ] Notice requirements from the licensing board or professional liability carrier.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving shares to a spouse who cannot own them.
- Letting the buy-sell price go out of date.
- Forgetting that the practice's lease and loans may carry your personal signature.
- Not telling a colleague about the arrangement.
- Assuming that a will overrides licensing law. It does not.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You want a family member to receive value from the practice.
- You own part of a practice with partners.
- You are close to retirement or have a health concern.
- You are not sure whether your state limits heir ownership.
- A transition could involve selling to a corporation or private equity group.
