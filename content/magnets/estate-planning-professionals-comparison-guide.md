**How to use this guide.** Read the who-does-what table to see which professional fits your question, then use the vetting checklist before you book. Licensing and fee rules vary by state, and Illinois points are noted. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Who does what

| Professional | Main job | Can they draft your will or trust? |
|---|---|---|
| Estate planning attorney | Plans who inherits, who decides if you cannot, and how to reduce probate and tax | Yes |
| Probate attorney | Handles the court process after a death | Often, though the focus is administration |
| Elder law attorney | Planning for aging: long term care, Medicaid, guardianship, benefits | Yes, with a focus on older clients |
| General practice lawyer | A range of legal work | Possibly, depending on experience |
| CPA | Tax returns and tax planning | No, unless also an attorney. They can model tax effects |
| Financial advisor | Investments, retirement income, insurance | No. They coordinate with your attorney |
| Insurance agent | Policies | No, but beneficiary forms are part of your plan |

Titles overlap. One firm may do estate planning, probate and elder law. Ask what share of the work is in each area.

## Estate planning versus the neighbors

| Topic | Estate planning | Probate work | Elder law | Financial planning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Timing | Before incapacity or death | After death | During aging | Ongoing |
| Output | Will, trust, powers of attorney, directives | Court filings, inventory, distribution | Benefits plan, care documents, guardianship cases | Investment and savings plan |
| Question it answers | Who gets what and who decides | How to settle this estate | How to pay for care and protect assets | How much do I have and how should it be invested |

The two fields work together. Your advisor knows the numbers and your attorney makes them legally effective. A beneficiary form in a financial account can override your will, so the two should talk.

## Attorney or financial advisor?

Neither is "better". They answer different questions. If the question is about legal documents or who has authority, you need an attorney. If it is about investments or retirement income, an advisor helps. A CPA is useful when income tax or estate tax is the question.

Many people begin with the attorney, then share the documents with their advisor and CPA.

## Fees: flat or hourly

| Fee type | How it works | Best for | Ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat fee | One price for a defined package | Standard plans | What is in and out of the price? Are updates included? |
| Hourly | Time billed at an hourly rate | Complex or uncertain matters | Estimated hours, billing increments, retainer |
| Hybrid | Flat for documents, hourly for extras | Mixed needs | What triggers extra billing? |
| Subscription | Ongoing fee for reviews and updates | People who want regular check ins | Cancellation and what is covered |

Prices vary by region, firm and complexity. Write down quotes and compare scope, not just price. See [estate plan cost guide](/free/estate-plan-cost-guide). Use the [questions to ask an attorney](/free/questions-to-ask-an-estate-planning-attorney) as a script.

## How to find a lawyer

1. **Ask people you trust.** Friends, a CPA, a financial advisor or a banker may know someone.
2. **Check the bar.** Your state bar or local bar association runs referral services. In Illinois the Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission lists each lawyer's license and discipline record. <!-- verify: ARDC lawyer search availability and name -->
3. **Search for a local firm** that lists estate planning as a main service.
4. **Read the website** for clear explanations, rather than slogans.
5. **Call two or three firms** and compare.

## How to tell whether a lawyer is good

- [ ] Licensed in your state, with no discipline history
- [ ] Estate planning is a regular part of their practice
- [ ] They ask about your family, assets, health and goals before proposing anything
- [ ] They explain choices in plain words and tell you what they do not recommend
- [ ] They explain fees in writing
- [ ] They discuss beneficiary forms and how to title assets, not just documents
- [ ] They tell you how to update and what the firm will do after signing
- [ ] They respond to calls and emails in a reasonable time
- [ ] They do not pressure you to buy a product

Ask about training, but avoid relying on labels. Awards lists and rankings often reflect marketing.

## Out-of-state questions

Law is state law. A lawyer licensed in another state can advise you only about the law of states where they are licensed. In general:

| Situation | Usual approach |
|---|---|
| You live in Illinois and own only Illinois property | An Illinois attorney |
| You live in one state and own a house in another | Attorney in your home state for the plan, local counsel in the other state for the property, often through a deed or trust |
| You moved recently | Have your plan reviewed by a lawyer in your new state. See [moving states estate plan checklist](/free/moving-states-estate-plan-checklist) |
| You live abroad | Ask about both US and local rules |

Real estate follows the law of the state where it sits. A probate case may be needed in each state where you own land. Read the [real estate in two states guide](/free/real-estate-in-two-states-guide).

## Can a lawyer decline, and can a plan be wrong?

A lawyer can decline a case. Common reasons are a conflict of interest, a question about whether you have capacity, an area outside their practice, or a request they believe is unlawful. Ask for a referral.

Yes, a lawyer's plan can be wrong or out of date. Common problems:

- Beneficiary forms never updated to match the documents
- Assets not retitled into a trust
- A move to a new state
- Law changes after signing
- Typos in names or backup choices

Reading the draft, asking questions and following the funding steps matter. A review every few years is a good habit. See [annual estate review planner](/free/annual-estate-review-planner). If you believe a mistake was made, ask the lawyer to correct it, and talk to another attorney about options.

## When to talk to an attorney

- You have a blended family, a business or property in more than one state
- A lawyer declined your request and you do not know why
- You want a second opinion on a draft
- A plan from another state has not been reviewed since you moved
- You are unsure whether a financial professional's advice fits your legal documents
