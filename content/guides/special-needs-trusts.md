A special needs trust holds money for a person with a disability while protecting their eligibility for public benefits. Programs like Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and Medicaid have strict asset limits. Money held correctly in this kind of trust generally does not count toward those limits.

The trust pays for things that improve quality of life, beyond what benefits cover. You may also see it called a supplemental needs trust.

## Why an inheritance can backfire

Imagine a parent leaves $100,000 directly to an adult son who receives SSI and Medicaid. Overnight he is over the asset limit. He may lose benefits until the money is spent down, often on things the programs would have covered anyway.

A special needs trust avoids this. The money belongs to the trust, not to him. A trustee spends it on his behalf.

The same risk applies to life insurance and retirement accounts. If a disabled person is named directly as beneficiary, the payout lands in their name. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

## The main types

| Type | Whose money goes in | Medicaid payback at death? | Common use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Third-party trust | Parents, grandparents, others | Generally no | Inheritance planning by family |
| First-party trust | The disabled person's own money | Generally yes | Settlements, direct inheritances, back pay |
| Pooled trust | Either, depending on the program | Varies | Managed by a nonprofit, smaller amounts |

### Third-party special needs trusts

This is the version most families create in their estate plan. Parents can set it up now or have it created at death through their will or living trust. Whatever is left when the beneficiary dies can go to siblings or others.

### First-party special needs trusts

These hold assets that already belong to the person with a disability. Under federal rules, the beneficiary generally must be under 65 when it is set up. At death, the trust usually must repay the state for Medicaid costs before anything else is paid out.

### Pooled trusts

A nonprofit manages many small trusts together and invests them as a pool. Each person has a separate account. This can work well when there is no family member to serve as trustee.

## What the trust can pay for

A trustee generally has discretion to pay for things that supplement benefits. Common examples:

- Education, tutoring and job training
- Therapies and medical care not covered by Medicaid
- Phones, computers and other equipment
- Furniture, clothing and personal items
- Travel, recreation and hobbies
- A car or transportation costs
- Care management and advocacy

Trustees usually pay providers directly rather than handing cash to the beneficiary. Cash and some payments for food or shelter can reduce SSI. The details are technical and change over time, so many trustees work with an attorney or advisor.

## Special needs trust vs. ABLE account

An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings account for people whose disability began before a set age under federal law. Annual contributions are capped at the amount of the annual gift tax exclusion, which was $19,000 in 2025<!-- verify -->.

ABLE accounts are simple and the beneficiary can often manage the money themselves. Trusts can hold much more and have no annual contribution cap. Many families use both: the trust holds the bulk, and the trustee moves smaller amounts into the ABLE account for everyday spending.

For more detail, see [special needs trust vs. ABLE account](/compare/special-needs-trust-vs-able-account).

## A worked example

Karen and Luis Medina have two children. Their daughter Sofia, 24, has Down syndrome, receives SSI, and is covered by Medicaid. Their son Mateo, 27, is an engineer.

In their estate plan, the Medinas create a third-party special needs trust for Sofia inside their living trust. Mateo is trustee, and a pooled trust is named as backup. They name the special needs trust as beneficiary of part of their life insurance.

They also update grandparents. Sofia's grandmother had planned to leave Sofia $50,000 directly in her will. She changes it to leave the money to Sofia's trust instead.

The Medinas write a letter of intent describing Sofia's routines, doctors, favorite activities and what makes her anxious. They also start an ABLE account so Sofia can pay for small things herself.

When the Medinas die, Sofia keeps her benefits. Mateo uses the trust to pay for her day program, a summer trip with friends, and a new laptop. When Sofia dies, what remains goes to Mateo's children.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming the disabled person directly** on life insurance, retirement accounts or a will.
- **Relying on a sibling informally** instead of using a trust.
- **Forgetting relatives.** Grandparents, aunts and uncles should know where to direct gifts.
- **Using the wrong type.** A first-party trust where a third-party trust would do may cause an unnecessary Medicaid payback.
- **Choosing a trustee without benefits knowledge.** One wrong payment can cause a benefits problem.
- **Overlooking guardianship and decision-making.** At 18, parents lose automatic authority. Options range from supported decision-making to a guardianship. See [power of attorney vs. guardianship](/compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Should the trust be standalone or built into my living trust?
- How should my retirement accounts be left to the trust, given the tax rules?
- Who should serve as trustee, and who as backup?
- What benefits does my child receive now, and which might they need later?
- Does my state have specific rules for these trusts or for pooled trusts?
