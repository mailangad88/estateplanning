When someone dies, the person in charge of their affairs has a long to-do list. That person is the **executor** (or personal representative) under a will, or the **successor trustee** under a trust. This guide walks through the work in the order it usually happens.

The exact steps depend on your state and on how the person set up their plan. Treat this as a map, not a substitute for local advice.

## The first two weeks

The early days are about care, safety and paperwork. A few things often come first:

1. **Get death certificates.** Order several certified copies. Banks, insurers and agencies each want one.
2. **Find the will and any trust.** Check home files, a safe, or the attorney who drafted them.
3. **Secure property.** Lock the home, protect valuables, and keep insurance in force.
4. **Care for pets and dependents.** Make sure no one is left without help.
5. **Forward mail.** Mail shows you accounts, bills and subscriptions you did not know about.

Our [first 30 days after a death](/checklists/first-30-days-after-a-death) checklist covers these in more detail.

## Step 1: Figure out what kind of estate it is

Look at how the person's assets were held. Some pass outside probate by beneficiary designation, joint ownership or a trust. Others are probate assets in the person's name alone.

If everything was in a funded trust, the successor trustee can often settle things without court. If there are probate assets, the executor files with the court. See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works) for that process.

## Step 2: Get legal authority

An executor usually cannot act until the court issues **letters testamentary**. A successor trustee typically shows the trust document and the death certificate instead.

Next, get a tax ID number (EIN) for the estate or trust from the IRS. Then open an estate or trust bank account. All income comes into this account, and all bills go out of it.

## Step 3: Make an inventory

List every asset and its value on the date of death. Typical items:

- Real estate
- Bank, brokerage and retirement accounts
- Life insurance policies
- Vehicles and valuable personal items
- Business interests
- Digital assets, such as online accounts, photos and crypto

The [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) and [digital assets inventory](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory) can help you organize. Date-of-death values matter later for taxes, so get appraisals where needed.

## Step 4: Notify people and institutions

Common notices include:

- Social Security and any pension plan
- Banks, brokerages and insurers
- Credit card companies and the three credit bureaus
- Beneficiaries and heirs, as state law requires
- Creditors, often by published notice in a newspaper

Sending notice to creditors usually starts a deadline. After that window closes, late claims are often barred.

## Step 5: Manage the estate while it is open

You are now responsible for keeping things in good shape. That can mean paying the mortgage, keeping utilities on, maintaining insurance, and managing investments sensibly. Keep receipts for everything.

Avoid mixing estate money with your own. Do not let a relative live in the house rent-free or borrow from the estate without the agreement of everyone affected.

## Step 6: Pay debts and expenses

Pay valid debts and costs from estate funds. These usually include funeral costs, final medical bills, credit cards, loans and the costs of running the estate.

If the estate does not have enough to pay everyone, state law sets an order of priority. Paying the wrong creditor first can make you personally liable. This is a good time to talk with an attorney.

## Step 7: File tax returns

Several returns may be due:

- The person's **final income tax return** for the year of death
- An **estate or trust income tax return** if the estate earns income while open
- A **federal estate tax return** for larger estates, or when a surviving spouse wants to keep the unused exemption
- A **state estate or inheritance tax return** in states that have one

See [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for how those taxes work. A CPA who handles estates is often worth the fee.

## Step 8: Distribute and close

Once debts and taxes are handled, distribute what is left as the will or trust says. Many executors ask each beneficiary to sign a receipt and release.

Prepare a final accounting showing money in, money out and what each person received. Courts often require one. Even when they do not, beneficiaries appreciate it. Then close the estate account and, for probate, ask the court to discharge you.

## A worked example

Kevin O'Brien is named executor and trustee for his mother, Margaret. Her living trust held her house and brokerage account. She also had a small checking account in her own name and an IRA naming Kevin and his sister Fiona.

The IRA goes straight to Kevin and Fiona through the custodian. The trust assets need no court. The checking account holds a modest amount, so Kevin uses his state's small estate process to collect it.

Kevin gets an EIN, opens a trust account and sells the house after six months. He pays Margaret's final bills, files her last income tax return and a trust tax return, and splits the rest with Fiona. He gives Fiona a one-page accounting, and they both sign off. No courtroom was ever needed.

## Common mistakes

- **Paying heirs too early.** If a debt or tax bill surfaces later, you may be liable.
- **Mixing funds.** Paying estate bills from your own account makes records messy and can look improper.
- **Missing deadlines.** Creditor windows, tax deadlines and court filings all have dates.
- **Not communicating.** Silence breeds suspicion. Regular updates to beneficiaries prevent many disputes.
- **Ignoring digital accounts.** Auto-renewing subscriptions and online accounts can drain money for months.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does this estate need probate, or can it use a small estate process?
- What deadlines apply in this state?
- How should I handle a creditor claim I think is wrong?
- What fee am I entitled to, and should I take it?
- When is it safe to make distributions?
