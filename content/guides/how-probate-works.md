Probate is the court process that wraps up a person's affairs after death. The court confirms who is in charge, makes sure debts and taxes are paid, and approves passing what is left to the right people.

People often fear probate more than they need to. In some states it is fairly smooth. In others it is slow and expensive. Knowing how it works helps you decide how much effort to put into avoiding it.

## What goes through probate

Probate only covers **probate assets**. These are things the person owned in their name alone with no beneficiary or co-owner named. Common examples:

- A house titled only in the person's name
- A bank account with no payable-on-death beneficiary
- A car, boat or personal belongings
- A share of property owned as "tenants in common"
- Money owed to the person, such as a final paycheck

Many assets skip probate entirely. These include:

- Life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary
- Accounts with a payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designation
- Property owned jointly "with right of survivorship"
- Assets held in a living trust

For a fuller breakdown, see [probate vs non-probate assets](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## The main steps

The details vary by state, but most probate cases follow the same rough path.

### 1. File the petition

Someone, usually the executor named in the will, files a petition with the local probate court. They file the original will along with it. If there is no will, a close relative usually asks to be appointed **administrator**.

### 2. Notify heirs and beneficiaries

The court requires notice to people named in the will and to legal heirs. This gives anyone with an objection a chance to speak up.

### 3. The court appoints the personal representative

Once the court is satisfied, it issues a document often called **letters testamentary** (with a will) or **letters of administration** (without one). Banks and other institutions ask for this before they will deal with the executor.

### 4. Inventory the assets

The executor finds and lists everything the person owned. Some states require a formal inventory filed with the court. Appraisals may be needed for real estate, businesses or valuable items.

### 5. Notify creditors and pay debts

The executor gives notice to creditors. Many states require a published notice in a local newspaper. Creditors then have a limited time to file claims. Valid debts, final bills and taxes are paid from estate funds.

### 6. File tax returns

The executor files the person's final income tax return. Some estates also need an estate income tax return, and a few need a federal estate tax return. See [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

### 7. Distribute and close

After debts and taxes are settled, the executor distributes what is left according to the will or state law. Many courts require a final accounting. The court then closes the estate and releases the executor.

## A worked example

Harold Becker dies at 82. His will leaves everything equally to his daughters, Ann and Carol, and names Ann as executor. He owns a house in his name, a checking account, and an IRA naming both daughters as beneficiaries.

The IRA passes straight to Ann and Carol by beneficiary designation. No court involved. The house and checking account are probate assets.

Ann files Harold's will with the county court and gets letters testamentary. She publishes notice to creditors, pays Harold's final hospital bill and lists the house for sale. After the creditor window closes and the house sells, she splits the money with Carol and files a final accounting. The whole process takes about ten months.

## What probate costs

Costs vary widely. Common expenses include:

- Court filing fees
- Attorney fees, which some states set by a percentage formula and others leave to hourly billing
- Executor compensation, if the executor takes it
- Appraisal and accounting fees
- Bond premiums, if the court requires the executor to be bonded
- Publication costs for creditor notices

## Why people try to avoid probate

The main reasons people give:

- **Time.** Heirs may wait months or longer for their shares.
- **Cost.** Fees can add up, especially in states with percentage-based fees.
- **Privacy.** Probate files are usually public, including the will and sometimes the inventory.
- **Property in other states.** Real estate in a second state can mean a second probate case there, called **ancillary probate**.

Probate also has upsides. A court supervises the executor, and there is a firm deadline for creditor claims. For some families, that structure is helpful.

## Common ways to avoid probate

- A [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained), properly funded
- [Beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) on retirement accounts and life insurance
- [Transfer-on-death and payable-on-death](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death) designations on bank, brokerage and, in some states, real estate
- Joint ownership with right of survivorship, though this carries its own risks

Each tool has tradeoffs. Joint ownership, for example, can expose the asset to the other owner's creditors. Compare options in [joint ownership vs trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust).

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming a will avoids probate.** It does not.
- **Creating a trust and never funding it.** An empty trust still leaves assets in probate. See [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust).
- **Outdated beneficiary forms.** A form naming a former spouse or a deceased person can send an account into probate or to the wrong person.
- **Forgetting out-of-state property.** A vacation cabin can trigger a second court case.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my state have a simplified process for small estates, and what is the limit?
- How are attorney and executor fees set here?
- Will my estate need a bond?
- Do I own property in another state that could trigger ancillary probate?
- Which of my assets would go through probate today?
