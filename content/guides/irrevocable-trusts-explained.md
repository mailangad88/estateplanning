An irrevocable trust is a trust you generally cannot change or cancel after you sign it. You give up ownership and control of whatever you put inside.

That loss of control is the point. Because the assets are no longer yours, the law may treat them differently for tax, Medicaid or creditor purposes.

This makes an irrevocable trust a tool for specific goals, not a general-purpose plan. Most people's main trust is revocable. See [revocable living trusts explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained) for that version.

## Revocable vs. irrevocable at a glance

| Feature | Revocable trust | Irrevocable trust |
|---|---|---|
| Can you change it? | Yes, any time | Generally no |
| Who controls the assets? | Usually you | A trustee, often not you |
| Counted in your taxable estate? | Yes | Often no, if set up for that |
| Protected from your creditors? | Generally no | Often yes, depending on state law |
| Counted for Medicaid? | Yes | Possibly not, after the look-back period |
| Avoids probate? | Yes, if funded | Yes, if funded |

For a longer side-by-side, see [revocable vs. irrevocable trust](/compare/revocable-vs-irrevocable-trust).

## Why people give up control

Families usually use irrevocable trusts for one of four goals.

### Reducing estate tax

Assets you move out of your estate during life, along with their future growth, may not count toward federal estate tax. As of 2026, the federal estate tax exemption is $15 million per person<!-- verify -->, indexed for inflation after that.

Some states have their own estate or inheritance tax with much lower thresholds. Read [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for more.

### Planning for long-term care

Medicaid generally counts assets you own when deciding if you qualify for nursing home coverage. Assets moved into certain irrevocable trusts may stop counting, but only after a look-back period.

In most states that period is 60 months<!-- verify -->. California's rules differ. Our [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning) guide explains the timing.

### Holding life insurance

An irrevocable life insurance trust, often called an ILIT, owns a life insurance policy. When properly set up, the payout may stay outside your taxable estate. It can also hold the money for children under rules you set.

### Protecting a beneficiary

Some irrevocable trusts protect the person receiving the money rather than the person giving it. A [special needs trust](/guides/special-needs-trusts) can protect a disabled person's public benefits. A spendthrift trust can protect a child's inheritance from their creditors.

## Common types of irrevocable trusts

- **Irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT):** Owns life insurance outside your estate.
- **Medicaid asset protection trust:** Holds assets so they may not count for Medicaid after the look-back period.
- **Third-party supplemental needs trust:** Holds money for a person with a disability without disrupting benefits.
- **Spousal lifetime access trust (SLAT):** One spouse gifts assets to a trust for the other spouse, often for estate tax planning.
- **Charitable remainder trust:** Pays you or others income for a period, then leaves the rest to charity.
- **Dynasty trust:** Holds assets for several generations, where state law allows.
- **Trust that becomes irrevocable at death:** A revocable trust becomes irrevocable when the grantor dies.

Names and rules vary. Not every type is available or useful in every state.

## How an irrevocable trust is set up

1. **Define the goal.** Tax, Medicaid, insurance and protection goals lead to different designs.
2. **Choose a trustee.** Often someone other than you. See [choosing a trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee).
3. **Sign the trust document.** It sets who benefits, what the trustee may do, and what you keep, if anything.
4. **Get a tax ID number** if the trust needs one.
5. **Transfer assets.** Deed real estate, retitle accounts, or have the trust buy an insurance policy.
6. **Report gifts if needed.** Large transfers may require a federal gift tax return, even when no tax is owed.
7. **Follow the rules going forward.** Keep trust money separate and respect the trustee's role.

The annual gift tax exclusion was $19,000 per recipient in 2025<!-- verify -->. Gifts above that to one person in a year generally need a gift tax return. Tax is usually not due until a lifetime limit is used up.

## A worked example

Walter and June Kowalski are in their late sixties. They own a paid-off home worth about $400,000 and some savings. Walter's mother spent her last four years in a nursing home, and the cost used up her savings.

After talking with an elder law attorney, they create a Medicaid asset protection trust. Their daughter Anna is the trustee. They deed their home into the trust and keep the right to live there.

They keep enough savings in their own names to live on. They understand that if either of them needs nursing home care within the look-back period, the transfer may cause a penalty period.

Seven years later, June needs care. Because the transfer happened more than 60 months earlier, the house is generally not counted under their state's rules. When both have died, Anna and her brother inherit the house through the trust.

Now imagine the Kowalskis waited until June was already in declining health. The look-back period would have caught the transfer. Timing was the whole plan.

## What you give up

Being clear about the trade-offs prevents regret.

- **Control.** You usually cannot take the assets back.
- **Flexibility.** Life changes, but the trust may not.
- **Simplicity.** The trust may need its own tax return every year.
- **Step-up in basis, sometimes.** Assets you give away during life may not get the income tax basis adjustment that inherited assets often get. Some trusts are drafted to keep it. Ask about this.

## Common mistakes

- **Funding too late** for Medicaid planning, inside the look-back period.
- **Keeping too much control**, which can undo the tax or Medicaid benefit.
- **Putting in money you may need** to live on.
- **Treating trust assets as your own**, such as paying personal bills from the trust account.
- **Ignoring income tax.** The trust's tax status should be decided at the start.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Which type of irrevocable trust, if any, fits my goal?
- What control or benefit can I keep without undoing that goal?
- How does my state treat this trust for Medicaid and creditors?
- Will the assets get a step-up in basis when I die?
- Can the trust be changed later if laws or my family change?
