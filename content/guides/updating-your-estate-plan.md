An estate plan reflects your life on the day you signed it. When your family, money or address changes, the plan may stop doing what you want. Many people review their plan every three to five years, and right away after a major life event.

Updating is usually faster and cheaper than the first plan. The key is doing it the right way, so the change is legally effective.

## Events that commonly trigger an update

| Event | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Marriage or new partner | Your spouse may have rights under state law, and you may want to name them as executor or agent |
| Divorce or separation | Old documents may still name your ex as beneficiary, agent or executor |
| Birth or adoption | You may want to name a guardian and add a trust for the new child |
| A death in the family | An executor, guardian or beneficiary may no longer be available |
| Moving to another state | Signing rules, spousal rights and probate costs differ |
| Buying property in another state | It may need its own probate unless planned for |
| Big change in wealth | Tax planning or a trust may now make sense |
| A child turns 18 | They may need their own powers of attorney and healthcare forms |
| Starting or selling a business | Succession planning may be needed |
| A family member's health or benefits change | Gifts may need to go through a special needs trust |

## How to change a will

There are two common ways.

1. **A new will.** It usually starts with a statement that it revokes all earlier wills. This is the cleanest option for anything more than a small change.
2. **A codicil.** A short amendment that changes specific parts, like swapping an executor. It must be signed and witnessed with the same formalities as a will.

What doesn't work well: crossing out names, writing in the margin, or stapling a handwritten note to the original. Depending on the state, the court may ignore these changes or question the whole will. See [What Makes a Will Valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid).

## How to change a revocable trust

A revocable living trust can be changed with a **trust amendment** or replaced entirely with a **restatement**. A restatement keeps the original trust's name and date, so assets already titled in the trust stay there. That saves you from retitling everything.

After any trust change, check that new assets have been added to the trust. See [Funding Your Trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) and the [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist).

## The parts people forget

Wills and trusts get the attention, but these items cause many problems.

- **Beneficiary designations** on life insurance, 401(k)s, IRAs and annuities. They override your will. Update them directly with each company.
- **Transfer-on-death and payable-on-death** registrations on bank and brokerage accounts.
- **Powers of attorney and healthcare directives.** An agent may have moved, aged, or fallen out of touch.
- **Joint accounts** opened for convenience that will pass to the co-owner.
- **Digital accounts** and password managers. See the [digital assets inventory](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory).

## A worked example: Mark Feldman

Mark Feldman signed a will and life insurance form in 2014, when he was married to Susan and had no children. Since then he divorced, remarried Jenna, had a daughter, and moved from one state to another.

His old plan had these problems:

- His will named Susan as executor and sole beneficiary. His new state, like many, may cancel provisions for an ex-spouse after divorce, but the will still named no one for Jenna or his daughter.
- His employer life insurance still named Susan. Federal rules for employer plans can mean the named beneficiary gets paid regardless of the divorce.
- His healthcare power of attorney named Susan.
- He had no guardian named for his daughter.

Mark's update included a new will naming Jenna and a trust for his daughter, a new guardian nomination, new powers of attorney under his new state's forms, and new beneficiary forms filed with his employer and IRA custodian. The [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit) helped him find every account.

## Where to keep the updated documents

Once you sign new documents, the old ones can cause confusion. Many attorneys suggest keeping the old originals in a clearly marked folder, or destroying them on purpose, depending on your state's practice and your attorney's advice.

Tell your executor, trustee and agents where the new originals are. Give copies of healthcare forms to your doctors and your healthcare agent. If you have a [letter of instruction](/checklists/letter-of-instruction-outline), update it at the same time so it matches the new plan.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting for a "perfect time."** An imperfect update today may protect your family better than a perfect one never signed.
- **Updating the will but not beneficiary forms.**
- **Making changes by hand** on signed documents.
- **Keeping old originals around** with no clear sign they've been revoked, which can confuse your family.
- **Not telling your executor or agents** about the change.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Is a codicil enough, or should I sign a new will?
- Should I amend my trust or restate it?
- Does my divorce automatically change anything in my state?
- Are my powers of attorney from another state valid here?
- What should I do with my old original documents?
