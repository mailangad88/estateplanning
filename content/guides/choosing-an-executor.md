Your executor is the person who settles your estate after you die. They find and protect your property, pay your final bills and taxes, and hand out what's left according to your will. The job can take several months to more than a year, so the person you pick matters.

Some states use the term "personal representative" instead of executor. The role is the same.

## What an executor actually does

The work is mostly paperwork, deadlines and communication. A typical estate involves these tasks:

1. Finding the original will and filing it with the probate court.
2. Asking the court to be formally appointed.
3. Notifying beneficiaries, creditors, and agencies like Social Security.
4. Getting a tax ID number for the estate and opening an estate bank account.
5. Listing and valuing assets, sometimes with appraisals.
6. Securing property, such as keeping a house insured and maintained.
7. Paying valid debts, funeral costs and expenses.
8. Filing the final personal income tax return and any estate tax returns.
9. Distributing property to beneficiaries and getting receipts.
10. Closing the estate with the court.

For a fuller picture, see [Settling an Estate Step by Step](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step). The [executor workload](/tools/executor-workload) tool estimates how much time the job might take for an estate like yours.

## Qualities that matter most

Love and family position are not the same as fitness for the job. People commonly weigh these traits.

- **Organized.** The executor tracks deadlines, receipts and forms for months.
- **Honest.** They control money that belongs to others.
- **Available.** Someone with time and energy, not stretched to the limit.
- **Calm with family.** They can deliver news fairly and avoid taking sides.
- **Willing to ask for help.** A good executor hires an attorney or accountant when needed.
- **Likely to outlive you.** Many people choose someone in the next generation or around their own age with a younger backup.

Financial skill helps but isn't required. Executors can pay professionals from estate funds for legal and tax work.

## Who can legally serve

Most states require the executor to be an adult of sound mind. Many states bar people with certain felony convictions. Some states place limits on executors who live out of state, such as requiring a bond or allowing only close relatives.

A bank or trust company can also serve. This is more common for large or complicated estates, or when no family member is a good fit. Professional executors charge fees, often based on the estate's value.

## Choosing between family, friend, and professional

| Option | Strengths | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse or partner | Knows your affairs, often the main beneficiary | May be grieving, may be older |
| Adult child | Knows the family, often motivated to finish | Can stir sibling tension |
| Trusted friend | Neutral between relatives | Less access to family information |
| Professional (bank, trust company, attorney) | Experienced, neutral, continuity | Fees, less personal |

Some people name two co-executors. This can feel fair, but it means two signatures on everything and can slow things down if they disagree. Many attorneys suggest one executor with a backup instead.

## A worked example: the Nguyen family

Helen Nguyen is a widow with three adult children. Her oldest, Tom, lives nearby and works long hours. Her middle child, Lisa, lives three states away and is an accountant. Her youngest, Sam, lives with her and helps around the house.

Helen's first thought was to name all three as co-executors to avoid hurt feelings. After talking it through, she chose differently:

- **Lisa as executor.** She is organized, comfortable with tax forms, and has the most flexible schedule. Helen checked that her state allows an out-of-state child to serve.
- **Tom as backup.** He lives near the house and the court.
- **A letter to all three** explaining her reasons, so no one reads the choice as favoritism.

Helen also wrote a [letter of instruction](/checklists/letter-of-instruction-outline) listing her accounts, where to find her passwords, and her funeral wishes. That gives Lisa a head start.

## How to set your executor up for success

- **Ask first.** Confirm they are willing, and tell them where your original will is.
- **Name a backup.** If your first choice dies, declines or can't serve, the backup steps in without a court fight.
- **Consider waiving bond.** Many wills waive the requirement for the executor to buy a bond, which saves the estate money. Some states still require one.
- **Keep good records.** An up-to-date [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) and [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list) save weeks of searching.
- **Think about digital assets.** Tell your executor how to find a list of online accounts. See [Digital Assets in Estate Planning](/guides/digital-assets-estate-planning).

## Common mistakes

- Naming someone out of guilt or birth order rather than fitness.
- Choosing a person much older than you with no backup.
- Naming co-executors who don't get along.
- Never telling the executor they've been chosen.
- Forgetting that a trust needs its own trustee. The same person can do both jobs, but see [Executor vs. Trustee](/compare/executor-vs-trustee) for the difference.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my state restrict out-of-state executors?
- Should my will waive the bond requirement?
- How are executors paid in my state?
- Would naming co-executors cause problems for my family?
- If I have a living trust, should the same person be executor and trustee?
