A beneficiary designation tells a financial company who gets an account when you die. You fill out a form with the bank, insurer or retirement plan. At your death, the company pays the person on the form directly, without probate.

These forms usually override your will. For many families, they control more money than the will does.

## Which assets use beneficiary designations

| Asset | Uses a beneficiary form? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 401(k), 403(b) and similar plans | Yes | Spouse may have rights under federal law |
| IRAs and Roth IRAs | Yes | Tax rules for heirs depend on who is named |
| Life insurance | Yes | Includes policies through work |
| Annuities | Yes | Terms vary by contract |
| Health savings accounts | Yes | Spouse can often treat it as their own |
| Bank accounts | Optional | Called payable on death (POD) |
| Brokerage accounts | Optional | Called transfer on death (TOD) |

For bank and brokerage versions, see [transfer-on-death and payable-on-death](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).

## Why the form beats the will

These accounts are contracts between you and the company. When you sign the form, you tell the company in advance who to pay. Your will only controls assets in your own name with no other instructions attached.

So if your will leaves everything to your three children equally, but your life insurance names only your oldest, your oldest gets the insurance. The will cannot fix it. The comparison [beneficiary designation vs. will](/compare/beneficiary-designation-vs-will) walks through more examples.

## Primary and contingent beneficiaries

Most forms let you name two levels.

- **Primary beneficiary:** Gets the account first.
- **Contingent beneficiary:** Gets it if no primary beneficiary is alive.

You can name more than one person at each level and assign percentages. Make sure the percentages add up to 100.

### Per stirpes and per capita

These Latin terms answer one question: if a beneficiary dies before you, does their share go to their children?

- **Per stirpes** usually means yes. A deceased child's share passes down to that child's children.
- **Per capita** often means the share is divided among the surviving beneficiaries at that level.

Definitions can vary by form and state. Read the company's form carefully or ask.

## Who to name, and who to be careful about

### A spouse

Spouses are a common primary choice, especially for retirement accounts. Federal law gives spouses rights to many employer plans, and a spouse usually must sign a waiver if you name someone else.

### Adult children

Naming adult children directly is simple. It works well when they are responsible and there are no special concerns.

### Minor children

Companies generally will not pay large sums directly to a minor. A court may need to appoint someone to manage the money.

Options include naming a trust or a custodian under your state's transfers to minors law. See [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).

### A person with a disability

A direct payout can disrupt SSI or Medicaid. Many families name a [special needs trust](/guides/special-needs-trusts) instead.

### Your trust

Naming your living trust lets the trust's rules control the money. For retirement accounts, the trust needs language that fits the tax rules for inherited accounts. This is a technical area worth reviewing with an attorney.

## A worked example

Tom Gallagher married Lisa in 2010. He named her on his 401(k) and his work life insurance.

They divorced in 2018. In 2021 he married Rachel, and they had a son, Owen.

Tom updated his will to leave everything to Rachel and Owen. He forgot the beneficiary forms.

He also changed jobs and rolled his 401(k) into an IRA. The rollover form said "spouse" with no name, and the company recorded it as Lisa.

Tom dies in 2026. Depending on his state's laws and the type of account, Lisa may have a strong claim to the IRA and the life insurance. Rachel may end up in a dispute that costs time and money, and the outcome is uncertain.

Ten minutes with each form after the divorce would have prevented the problem.

## Common mistakes

- **Never updating after divorce, marriage, birth or death.**
- **Leaving the contingent line blank.**
- **Naming a minor directly.**
- **Naming your estate,** which can pull the account into probate.
- **Assuming a new job's plan kept your old choices.** It did not.
- **Forgetting old accounts.** A 401(k) from a job 15 years ago still pays whoever is on that form.
- **Unequal results by accident,** like naming only the child who helped open the account.

## How to review your beneficiaries

1. List every account with a beneficiary option. The [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) helps.
2. Log in or call each company and get the current designation in writing.
3. Compare each one against your will or trust.
4. Update anything outdated, and add contingent beneficiaries.
5. Save confirmation letters with your estate planning papers.
6. Repeat after any big life event.

- How should I handle a beneficiary who is a minor or has a disability?
- Do my designations line up with what my will or trust says?
