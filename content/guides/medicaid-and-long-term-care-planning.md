Long-term care is one of the biggest financial risks older adults face. Nursing home costs can use up a lifetime of savings quickly. Medicaid is the main program that pays for this care, but it has strict rules about income and assets.

Medicaid is run jointly by the federal government and each state. Each state sets many of its own limits and rules. Treat this guide as a general overview, and talk with an elder law attorney licensed in your state.

## Medicare and Medicaid are different

People mix them up all the time.

| | Medicare | Medicaid |
|---|---|---|
| Who qualifies | Most people 65 and older | People with low income and assets |
| Long-term nursing home care | Not covered, except short skilled stays | Covered, if you qualify |
| Asset test | None | Strict |
| Run by | Federal government | States, with federal rules |

Many states also offer Medicaid programs for care at home or in assisted living. These are often called waiver programs, and waiting lists are common.

## How Medicaid eligibility works

To qualify for long-term care Medicaid, an applicant usually must meet:

- **A medical test.** They need the level of care the program covers.
- **An income test.** States handle income differently. Some require most income to go toward the cost of care.
- **An asset test.** Countable assets must fall below a low state limit.

### Countable and exempt assets

States split assets into two groups. Rules vary, but this is a common pattern:

- **Often exempt:** the home (within limits, and usually while you or a spouse live there), one car, personal belongings, a small burial fund, and certain prepaid funeral plans
- **Usually countable:** bank and brokerage accounts, CDs, second homes, cash value life insurance above a small amount, and in some states retirement accounts

## The look-back period

When you apply, Medicaid reviews your past financial records. It looks for gifts or transfers for less than fair value.

As of 2026, the federal Medicaid look-back period for nursing home care is 60 months <!-- verify --> in most states. California uses different rules. Gifts made within that window can trigger a **penalty period**. During that time, Medicaid will not pay for care even if you otherwise qualify.

The penalty is usually based on the size of the gift and the average cost of care in your state. Our [Medicaid look-back date](/tools/medicaid-lookback-date) tool can help you see how the window applies to a given date.

## Protections for a spouse at home

Federal law protects the spouse who stays at home, often called the **community spouse**. The rules can let that spouse:

- Keep the home
- Keep a protected share of the couple's assets
- Keep enough income to live on, sometimes including part of the other spouse's income

The protected amounts change each year and vary by state. For married couples, this is often where planning makes the biggest difference.

## Common planning tools

Families often use some mix of the following. Each must be done carefully, because the wrong move can cause a penalty.

1. **A durable power of attorney with gifting powers.** If you lose capacity, your agent may need authority to make transfers. See [powers of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).
2. **A Medicaid asset protection trust.** This is a type of irrevocable trust. Assets moved into it may be protected once the look-back period passes. See [irrevocable trusts explained](/guides/irrevocable-trusts-explained).
3. **Spending down on exempt items.** Paying off a mortgage, making home repairs, or buying a prepaid funeral can turn countable assets into exempt ones.
4. **Transfers allowed by law.** Some transfers carry no penalty. Examples include gifts to a spouse and, in some cases, to a disabled child or a trust for them.
5. **Long-term care insurance.** For people who plan early and qualify medically, insurance can cover care before Medicaid is needed.

If a family member with a disability is involved, a [special needs trust](/guides/special-needs-trusts) may protect their own benefits.

## A worked example

Frank and Dorothy Novak are both 74. They own their house and have savings and investments. Frank has early Parkinson's disease. Their daughter Janet worries about what happens if Frank needs a nursing home.

They meet with an elder law attorney. Frank and Dorothy sign new durable powers of attorney with gifting powers and updated [health care directives](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills). They move part of their savings into an irrevocable trust, keeping the rest for daily living.

Four years later, Frank enters a nursing home. The trust assets are inside the look-back window, so the attorney waits. Meanwhile, Dorothy uses the spousal protections to keep the house and her share of the remaining savings. Once the look-back passes, Frank qualifies, and the trust assets are protected for Dorothy and Janet.

## Estate recovery

After a Medicaid recipient dies, states are required to try to recover certain costs from the estate. Some states only reach probate assets. Others reach more. There are often protections while a spouse or certain children are still living.

Ask an attorney how estate recovery works in your state. It affects choices like whether to keep the house in a trust or in your own name.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting until a crisis.** The look-back period rewards early planning.
- **Giving money to children informally.** This can trigger penalties and expose the money to their creditors.
- **Using an old power of attorney.** Without gifting powers, an agent may be unable to plan.
- **Missing records.** Medicaid often asks for years of bank statements. Unexplained withdrawals can cause delays.
- **Relying on advice from a friend in another state.** The rules differ too much from state to state.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- What are my state's asset and income limits right now?
- Which of my assets are countable here?
- Does my state's estate recovery reach non-probate assets?
- Would an asset protection trust make sense for us?
- How can we protect the spouse who stays home?
- Does my power of attorney allow the planning we might need?
