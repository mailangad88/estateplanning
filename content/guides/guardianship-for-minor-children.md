You name a guardian for your minor children in your will. The guardian is the person who would raise your children if both parents died or could not care for them. A court makes the final appointment, but judges usually follow the parents' written choice unless there's a strong reason not to.

This is often the hardest decision in a family's estate plan. It is also one of the most important reasons young parents make a will at all.

## How guardianship works

When a parent dies, the surviving parent usually keeps custody. A named guardian steps in only when neither parent can care for the child.

At that point, the court holds a hearing. It reviews your will, checks that the named guardian is willing and fit, and appoints them. The guardian then has legal authority to make everyday and major decisions for the child, such as school, medical care and where they live, until the child turns 18.

The rules for how courts weigh a parent's nomination vary by state. A local attorney can tell you how much weight your state gives it.

## Two separate jobs: raising the child and managing money

A guardian of the person raises the child. A separate role covers the child's money.

- **Guardian of the person:** daily care, home, school, health care.
- **Guardian of the estate, conservator, or trustee:** manages any money the child inherits.

Many parents leave money to a trust for the children and name a trustee. This means the guardian doesn't have to go to court to account for every expense, and the money isn't handed to the child outright at 18. See [Leaving Money to Minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) for the options.

Some families give both jobs to one person. Others split them, for example a warm, steady sister raises the kids while a careful, financially minded brother manages the trust.

## What to weigh when choosing

There is no perfect choice. People commonly think through these factors.

- **Values and parenting style.** Would this person raise your children in a way you're comfortable with?
- **Age and health.** Grandparents are loving but may not have the energy to raise a toddler to adulthood.
- **Where they live.** Moving can mean a new school and losing friends, but sometimes it's worth it.
- **Their own family.** Do they have children, and would yours fit in?
- **Relationship with your kids.** Does your child already know and trust them?
- **Willingness.** Ask them directly, and give them room to say no.
- **Stability.** Think about marriage, work and housing.

Money is usually less of a factor than people expect, since your estate, life insurance and a trust can cover the children's costs. The [guardian fund calculator](/tools/guardian-fund-calculator) and [life insurance needs](/tools/life-insurance-needs) tools can help you estimate what that support might cost.

## A worked example: the Delgado-Kim family

Ana Delgado and James Kim have two children, ages 2 and 6. Their first instinct was Ana's parents, who adore the kids. But her parents are in their 70s.

After using a [choosing a guardian worksheet](/checklists/choosing-a-guardian-worksheet), they decided:

- **Guardian:** James's brother, Daniel, who is 40, lives in the same town and has kids of a similar age.
- **Backup guardian:** Ana's close friend, Priya, in case Daniel can't serve.
- **Trustee:** Ana's father, a retired banker, to manage the children's trust. The trust holds the life insurance money and pays Daniel for the children's housing, food, school and activities.
- **A letter to Daniel** describing the children's routines, their pediatrician, and the family traditions Ana and James want kept.

This setup lets Ana's parents stay deeply involved without the daily work of raising young children.

## Special situations

### Divorced or separated parents

If one parent dies, the other parent usually gets custody, even if you'd prefer someone else. A guardianship nomination generally only takes effect if both parents are gone or the surviving parent is found unfit. If you have concerns, talk to an attorney about what your state allows. [Estate Planning for Blended Families](/guides/estate-planning-for-blended-families) covers related issues.

### A child with special needs

If your child will need care past 18, an adult guardianship or other arrangement may be needed later. Money left to them may also affect government benefits. See [Special Needs Trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts).

### Short-term emergencies

Some states let parents sign a separate document naming a short-term or standby guardian. This can cover the gap if you're hospitalized and the court hasn't acted yet.

## Common mistakes

- **Not naming anyone** because the choice feels too hard. A court will then decide without your input.
- **Naming a couple** without saying what happens if they divorce.
- **No backup guardian.**
- **Leaving money outright to the children,** which may require a court-supervised conservatorship.
- **Never revisiting the choice.** The best person when your child was a baby may not be the best person for a teenager.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- How much weight does my state's court give to a parent's nomination?
- Can I name a standby or temporary guardian here?
- Should the guardian and trustee be the same person?
- How should I handle a former spouse or partner who may seek custody?
- At what age should my children's trust pay out?
