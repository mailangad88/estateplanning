A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement you set up during your life to hold your property. You keep full control while you are alive and well. When you die or can no longer manage things, a person you picked takes over and follows your written instructions.

People use them mainly for three reasons: to avoid probate, to plan for incapacity, and to keep the details of their estate more private.

## The three roles in every trust

Every trust has three roles. In a revocable living trust, you usually fill all three at the start.

| Role | What it means | Who usually fills it |
|---|---|---|
| Grantor (also called settlor or trustor) | The person who creates the trust and puts property in | You |
| Trustee | The person who manages the trust property | You, while you are able |
| Beneficiary | The person who benefits from the trust | You during life, then your family |

The document also names a **successor trustee**. That person steps in when you die or become unable to manage your affairs.

Choosing that person well matters a lot. See our guide on [choosing a trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee).

## How it works, step by step

Here is the typical life of a revocable living trust.

1. **You sign the trust document.** It names the trustees, the beneficiaries, and the rules for who gets what and when.
2. **You move assets into it.** This is called funding. You retitle your house, bank accounts and brokerage accounts into the trust's name.
3. **You live your life as usual.** You buy, sell, spend and file taxes as you always have. The trust is mostly invisible.
4. **If you become incapacitated,** your successor trustee manages trust assets for you. This can avoid a court guardianship or conservatorship case for those assets.
5. **When you die,** the trust becomes irrevocable. Your successor trustee pays final bills, then distributes or holds assets as the document says.

"Revocable" means you can change or cancel it. "Living" means you create it while you are alive, as opposed to a trust created by your will after death.

## What a living trust can do

### Avoid probate for trust assets

Probate is the court process for transferring property after death. It can take months or longer, and the filings are often public.

Assets titled in a trust's name usually pass outside probate. Learn more in [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).

This matters most for people who own real estate in more than one state. Without a trust, a family may need a separate probate case in each state.

### Plan for incapacity

A trust lets your successor trustee manage trust assets if you have a stroke, develop dementia, or are badly injured. Your family does not have to ask a judge for authority over those assets.

A trust does not cover everything, though. Retirement accounts and health decisions are handled by other documents, like a [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and a [healthcare directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).

### Control how and when heirs receive money

A will typically hands property over outright. A trust can stagger it.

For example, a trust can hold money for a child until age 30 and pay for school along the way. It can also keep an inheritance separate from a child's marriage.

### Keep things more private

A will filed in probate usually becomes a public record. A trust generally does not get filed with a court. Some states require a short notice or certificate, but the full terms usually stay private.

## What a living trust does not do

People are often surprised by these limits.

- **It does not usually shield assets from your own creditors** while you are alive.
- **It does not reduce estate tax by itself.** Tax planning can be built into a trust, but that is a separate feature.
- **It does not qualify you for Medicaid.** Assets in a revocable trust are generally counted. See [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning).
- **It does not control assets you never put in it.** An empty trust does almost nothing.

That last point causes the most trouble. If you want the difference spelled out, our comparison of [revocable vs. irrevocable trusts](/compare/revocable-vs-irrevocable-trust) walks through it side by side.

## A worked example

Maria and Daniel Okafor are in their fifties. They own a home in Ohio, a small cabin in Michigan, a joint brokerage account, and two retirement accounts. They have two adult children, Grace and Sam.

They sign a joint revocable trust. They are co-trustees. Grace is named successor trustee, with Sam as backup.

They deed the Ohio house and the Michigan cabin into the trust. They retitle the brokerage account. They leave the retirement accounts in their own names, because moving them would trigger tax, but they review the beneficiary forms on each one.

Ten years later, Daniel dies. Maria keeps managing the trust as sole trustee. Nothing has to go through probate in either state.

When Maria later develops memory loss, Grace steps in as trustee using the trust document and a doctor's letter, as the trust requires. After Maria dies, Grace sells the cabin, pays final bills, and splits the rest with Sam. The family avoids two probate cases.

Now imagine the Okafors had signed the trust but never deeded the cabin into it. The cabin would likely need a probate case in Michigan anyway. The paperwork step is what made the plan work.

## Common mistakes

- **Never funding the trust.** Signing the document is only half the job. Our [trust funding checklist](/checklists/trust-funding-checklist) lists what to retitle.
- **Forgetting new assets.** A house bought five years later in your own name is outside the trust.
- **Naming the trust as beneficiary of a retirement account without advice.** This can work, but the trust language needs to fit the tax rules for inherited retirement accounts.
- **Picking a successor trustee without asking them.** The job takes time and organization.
- **Never updating it.** Marriage, divorce, a death in the family, or a move to a new state are common reasons to review.

## What it costs and how long it takes

Costs vary widely by state, by attorney, and by how complex your family and assets are. Many firms charge a flat fee for a trust package that includes the trust, a pour-over will, powers of attorney, and healthcare documents.

The drafting itself often takes a few weeks. Funding can take longer, because banks and county recorders each have their own process.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Is a trust a good fit for my situation, or would a will and beneficiary designations be enough? Our [will vs. trust comparison](/compare/will-vs-trust) can help you think about it first.
- Will you prepare the deeds to move my real estate into the trust?
- How does my state treat trusts for property tax, homestead and title insurance?
- Should my retirement accounts name the trust, my spouse, or my children?
- What happens if I move to another state?
- Does your fee include help with funding?
