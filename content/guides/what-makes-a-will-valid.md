A will is valid when it meets your state's legal requirements at the moment you sign it. In most states that means four things: you are an adult, you have mental capacity, you sign it voluntarily, and you sign it with the formalities your state requires, usually in front of two witnesses. If any of these is missing, a court can refuse to accept the will. If no valid will is found, your property may pass under state law instead. See [What Happens If You Die Without a Will](/guides/what-happens-if-you-die-without-a-will).

The details differ from state to state. This guide covers the common requirements and the problems that lead to challenges.

## The four common requirements

### 1. Age

Most states require you to be at least 18. Some make exceptions for minors who are married or serving in the military.

### 2. Testamentary capacity

Capacity is the legal word for having a clear enough mind to make a will. Courts usually ask whether, when you signed, you understood:

- that you were signing a will,
- roughly what property you own,
- who your close family members are, and
- how the will distributes your property.

The bar is lower than many people think. Someone with early dementia may still have capacity on a good day. What matters is the person's state of mind at the time of signing.

### 3. Acting freely (no undue influence or fraud)

The will must reflect your own wishes. A court can throw out a will, or part of one, if someone pressured you so heavily that the choices were really theirs. Courts look at things like isolation from family, a helper who arranged the attorney, and sudden changes that favor that helper.

### 4. Proper signing formalities

This is the most common reason a do-it-yourself will fails. Typical formalities include:

- The will is in writing.
- You sign it at the end, or someone signs for you in your presence and at your direction.
- Two adult witnesses watch you sign, or hear you acknowledge your signature, and then sign themselves.
- In some states, the witnesses must sign in your presence and in each other's presence.

## Witnesses and the self-proving affidavit

Witnesses confirm that you signed willingly and appeared to be of sound mind. In many states, a witness should be "disinterested," meaning they receive nothing under the will. Some states let an interested witness keep only what they would have received without the will.

A **self-proving affidavit** is a separate sworn statement signed by you and your witnesses in front of a notary. Most states accept it. It lets the probate court accept the will without calling the witnesses to testify, which can save weeks if a witness has moved or died. [How Probate Works](/guides/how-probate-works) explains where this fits in the court process.

## Types of wills and where they're accepted

| Type | How it's made | Acceptance |
|---|---|---|
| Formal (attested) will | Typed, signed, and witnessed | Accepted in every state |
| Holographic will | Written and signed in your own handwriting | Accepted in some states, not others |
| Electronic will | Signed and witnessed electronically under a statute | Allowed in a growing number of states |
| Oral (nuncupative) will | Spoken, usually near death | Very limited, and many states don't allow it |

If your will relies on anything other than the formal type, it is worth checking your state's rules with a local attorney.

## A worked example: the Brennan estate

Walter Brennan, 81, typed a new will at home. It left his house to his neighbor, Karen, who had been helping him with groceries. He signed it at his kitchen table with Karen and her husband as witnesses. He did not use a notary.

After Walter died, his two daughters challenged the will. Three problems came up:

- **Interested witnesses.** Karen was both a witness and the main beneficiary. Under the rules in Walter's state, that weakened her gift.
- **Undue influence questions.** The daughters pointed out that Karen had driven Walter to buy the will form and that the change was sudden.
- **No self-proving affidavit.** Both witnesses had to testify, and both had a stake in the outcome.

The court set aside the new will and admitted Walter's older will instead. If Walter had signed with two unrelated witnesses, used a self-proving affidavit, and perhaps had a short letter or doctor's note about his health, the outcome might have been different.

## What can make a will invalid later

A will that was valid when signed can still be revoked or partly undone.

- **A later will.** A new will usually revokes earlier ones, especially if it says so.
- **Physical destruction.** Tearing up or burning the will with the intent to revoke it.
- **Marriage or divorce.** In many states, divorce automatically cancels gifts to an ex-spouse. In some states, marriage after signing can revoke or change the will.
- **Hand edits.** Crossing out lines or writing changes on a signed will can cause confusion or partial invalidity. See [Updating Your Estate Plan](/guides/updating-your-estate-plan).

## Common mistakes

- Using a beneficiary as a witness.
- Signing without both witnesses in the room.
- Stapling, unstapling and re-stapling pages, which can raise questions about whether pages were swapped.
- Keeping only a photocopy. Courts usually want the signed original.
- Assuming a will downloaded from the internet meets your state's rules. See [Online Will vs. Estate Attorney](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney) for the tradeoffs.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- How many witnesses does my state require, and who may serve?
- Does my state accept handwritten or electronic wills?
- Should I sign a self-proving affidavit?
- I have a family member who may contest my will. What can I do now to make it stronger?
- If I moved here from another state, is my old will still valid here?
