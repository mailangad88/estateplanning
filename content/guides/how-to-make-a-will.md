Making a will comes down to five tasks: list what you own, decide who receives it, choose the people who will carry out your wishes, put it in writing with clear wording, and sign it the way your state requires. The writing is usually the quickest part. The decisions take longer.

This guide walks through each step and points out where people commonly go wrong.

## Step 1: List what you own and how it's titled

Start with a simple inventory. Write down your home and other real estate, bank and investment accounts, retirement accounts, life insurance, vehicles, and valuable personal items.

Next to each item, note two things: whose name it is in and whether it has a beneficiary. This tells you what your will actually controls.

- **Controlled by your will:** assets in your name alone with no beneficiary or transfer-on-death designation.
- **Not controlled by your will:** accounts with a named beneficiary, jointly owned property with survivorship rights, and assets in a trust.

Many people discover that their will controls less than they thought. The [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) checklist can help you sort this out.

## Step 2: Decide who receives what

There are three common kinds of gifts in a will.

1. **Specific gifts.** A named item to a named person, like "my grandmother's ring to my niece, Clara."
2. **Cash gifts.** A set dollar amount, like "$10,000 to the Riverside Food Bank."
3. **The residue.** Everything left after debts, expenses and the specific gifts. This is often the largest part of the estate.

For each gift, think about backups. If your brother is to receive your car but he dies before you, who should get it instead? Wills commonly say "to my children in equal shares, and if a child has died, to that child's children." Without backup language, a gift can fail and fall into the residue or pass under state law.

## Step 3: Choose your people

A will names the people who carry it out. Choose a first choice and at least one backup for each role.

- **Executor** (also called a personal representative): gathers assets, pays debts and distributes property. See [Choosing an Executor](/learn/wills/choosing-an-executor).
- **Guardian** for minor children: the person who would raise your children if both parents are gone. See [Guardianship for Minor Children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children).
- **Trustee** if your will creates a trust: manages money held for children or others over time.

Ask people before you name them. A surprised executor who lives across the country may decline the job.

## Step 4: Put it in writing

You have three broad options for drafting.

| Option | Typical fit | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Online will service | Simple estates, single state, no special family issues | Generic wording that may not fit blended families or special needs |
| Attorney-drafted will | Children from prior relationships, a business, property in several states, a family member on benefits | Higher upfront cost |
| Handwritten (holographic) will | Emergencies, in states that accept them | Not valid in every state, and often unclear |

For a closer comparison, see [Online Will vs. Estate Attorney](/compare/online-will-vs-estate-attorney).

Clear wording matters more than formal wording. "I leave my house at 14 Elm Street to my daughter, Maya Patel" is better than "my real property to my eldest." Name people fully and describe property so a stranger could identify it.

## Step 5: Sign it correctly

Signing is where many do-it-yourself wills fail. Most states require you to sign in front of two adult witnesses, who also sign. Some states have extra rules about who can be a witness.

A common safe practice is to use witnesses who receive nothing under the will. In some states, a witness who is also a beneficiary can lose their gift. Many people also sign a **self-proving affidavit** in front of a notary, which can let the court accept the will without tracking down the witnesses later.

Rules vary by state. [What Makes a Will Valid](/guides/what-makes-a-will-valid) covers the requirements in more detail.

## A worked example: Linda Moreno

Linda Moreno is 58, divorced, with two adult sons, Eric and Paul. She owns a condo, a car, a brokerage account, an IRA and a life insurance policy.

Her inventory shows the IRA and life insurance already name both sons as beneficiaries. Those pass outside the will. Her will needs to cover the condo, the car, the brokerage account and her personal belongings.

Her will says:

- Her late mother's piano goes to Paul, who plays.
- $5,000 goes to her church.
- Everything else goes to Eric and Paul in equal shares. If either son dies before her, his share goes to his children.
- Eric is executor, and her friend Teresa is the backup.

Before signing, Linda also checks her IRA form. It still listed her ex-husband as a contingent beneficiary. She updates it so her plan is consistent.

## Common mistakes

- **No residuary clause.** Property not specifically mentioned may pass as if there were no will.
- **No backup beneficiaries.** Gifts can fail if someone dies first.
- **Leaving assets outright to minors.** Children under 18 can't take legal control of property. See [Leaving Money to Minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors).
- **Changing the will by hand.** Crossing out lines or writing notes in the margin can invalidate parts of the will in some states. Changes are usually made with a new will or a formal amendment called a codicil.
- **Assuming the will controls everything.** Beneficiary forms override the will.
- **Hiding the original.** If no one can find the signed original, the court may treat you as having no will.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my state accept handwritten wills or online-signed wills?
- Who is allowed to serve as a witness in my state?
- Should my will include a trust for my children or grandchildren?
- Do I have property in another state that might need a second probate?
- Is a will enough for me, or would a living trust be worth considering?
