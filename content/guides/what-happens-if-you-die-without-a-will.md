If you die without a will, you die "intestate." Your state's laws then decide who inherits your property, who settles your estate, and who raises your minor children. The law follows a fixed formula based on family relationships, not on what you would have wanted.

Every state has its own intestacy rules. The general patterns below are common, but the details vary, so treat them as a starting point.

## Who inherits under typical intestacy rules

Most states follow a similar order. They look for close relatives first, then move outward.

1. **Spouse and children.** A surviving spouse usually receives all or a large share. Children often share the rest, especially if any are from another relationship.
2. **Children only.** If there's no spouse, children usually share equally. If a child has died, that child's children often take their parent's share.
3. **Parents.** If there's no spouse or descendants, parents often inherit.
4. **Siblings, nieces and nephews.** Next in line in many states.
5. **More distant relatives.** Grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins.
6. **The state.** Only if no qualifying relative can be found.

Who is usually left out: unmarried partners, stepchildren who were not adopted, close friends, and charities. Some states have narrow exceptions, so ask a local attorney.

## Assets that pass outside intestacy

Intestacy rules only control property in your name alone with no beneficiary. These assets usually pass outside them:

- Life insurance and retirement accounts with a named beneficiary.
- Bank and brokerage accounts with payable-on-death or transfer-on-death registration.
- Property owned jointly with right of survivorship.
- Assets held in a living trust.

This is why beneficiary forms matter so much. See [Probate vs. Non-Probate Assets](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets) and [Transfer on Death and Payable on Death](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death).

## Who settles the estate

With no will, no executor has been named. A family member asks the probate court to be appointed as **administrator** (or personal representative). States set a priority list, often starting with the spouse, then adult children, then other relatives.

The administrator may have to post a bond, which is a kind of insurance paid for by the estate. A will often waives this. If relatives disagree about who should serve, the court decides, which adds time and legal fees. [How Probate Works](/guides/how-probate-works) explains the process. The [probate cost estimator](/tools/probate-cost-estimator) can give a rough idea of costs.

## What happens to minor children

If one parent dies, the surviving parent usually keeps custody. If both parents die without naming a guardian, a court chooses one.

Relatives can ask to be appointed, and sometimes more than one does. The judge decides based on the child's best interests, but without any written guidance from the parents.

There's a money issue too. A child's inherited share is usually managed by a court-supervised guardian of the estate or conservator, then handed over at 18. See [Guardianship for Minor Children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children) and [Leaving Money to Minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors).

## A worked example: Kevin Walsh

Kevin Walsh, 45, lived with his partner, Megan, for 12 years. They weren't married. Kevin had a 16-year-old son, Dylan, from an earlier relationship. Kevin owned the house they lived in, in his name alone, plus a savings account and a 401(k).

Kevin died suddenly without a will. Here is how things played out under typical rules:

- **The house and savings account** passed to Dylan as Kevin's only child. Megan, as an unmarried partner, had no right to inherit.
- **The 401(k)** went to Megan, because Kevin had named her as beneficiary years earlier. This was the only asset she received.
- **Dylan's mother** asked to be appointed as administrator. Because Dylan was a minor, a conservator was appointed to manage his inheritance until he turned 18.
- **Megan** had to move out when the house was sold to divide the estate for Dylan's benefit.

With a will, Kevin could have let Megan stay in the house for a time, set up a trust for Dylan until 25, and chosen his own executor.

## What intestacy can cost a family

Dying without a will doesn't only change who inherits. It can also add steps and costs.

- **A bond.** The administrator may need to buy one, paid from the estate.
- **More court involvement.** Some states require more reporting or court approval for an administrator than for a named executor.
- **Selling property to divide it.** When several heirs inherit a house together, it is often sold so the value can be split.
- **Family conflict.** Without written wishes, relatives may disagree about who should serve and who should receive personal items.

## Common misunderstandings

- **"My spouse gets everything."** Often true only if there are no children from other relationships, and not true in every state.
- **"The state takes it all."** Rare, and only when no relatives are found.
- **"We've been together long enough to count as married."** Only a few states recognize common-law marriage, and the rules for proving it are strict.
- **"I don't own enough to matter."** Even small estates may need court action, and the guardianship question has nothing to do with money.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Who would inherit from me under my state's intestacy laws today?
- How can I provide for an unmarried partner or stepchild?
- Do I qualify for my state's small estate procedure?
- Which of my assets already pass outside probate?
- What is the simplest plan that would replace the state's default rules?
