Payable-on-death (POD) and transfer-on-death (TOD) designations let you name who receives an asset when you die. You keep complete control while you are alive. When you die, the asset goes straight to that person without probate.

These designations are one of the simplest probate-avoidance tools available. They also have limits worth understanding before you rely on them for everything.

## The basic terms

- **POD (payable on death)** is used mostly for bank accounts and certificates of deposit.
- **TOD (transfer on death)** is used mostly for brokerage accounts, investment securities, vehicles and, in many states, real estate.

The idea is the same for both. The beneficiary has no rights until your death. You can change or remove them any time.

## Where you can use them

| Asset | Common option | Availability |
|---|---|---|
| Checking, savings, CDs | POD | Widely available |
| Brokerage and investment accounts | TOD | Widely available |
| Savings bonds | Co-owner or beneficiary | Federal rules apply |
| Vehicles | TOD title | Available in some states |
| Real estate | Transfer-on-death deed (sometimes called a beneficiary deed) | Available in many states, not all |
| Retirement accounts and life insurance | Beneficiary designation | Standard, covered in [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) |

Because rules vary by state, especially for cars and real estate, check what your state allows before planning around it.

## How to set one up

### Bank and investment accounts

1. Ask your bank or brokerage for its POD or TOD form, or look in your online account settings.
2. Name one or more beneficiaries and their shares.
3. Add a contingent beneficiary if the form allows.
4. Submit the form and save the confirmation.

### Transfer-on-death deeds for real estate

1. Confirm your state allows them.
2. Prepare the deed with the exact legal description of the property.
3. Sign it with the formalities your state requires, often in front of a notary.
4. Record it with the county before your death. In many states an unrecorded TOD deed has no effect.

The beneficiary does nothing until you die. Then they typically record a death certificate and a short affidavit to take title.

## What happens at death

The beneficiary contacts the bank or brokerage, provides a certified death certificate and ID, and fills out a claim form. The company then pays out or moves the account into their name.

For real estate, the beneficiary records the required documents with the county. They then own the property, subject to any mortgage on it.

No court approval is usually needed. That is why these tools can save time and expense compared with probate. See [how probate works](/guides/how-probate-works).

## TOD and POD vs. a living trust

TOD and POD designations and living trusts both avoid probate. They work differently.

| Feature | TOD / POD | Living trust |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to set up | Usually free or low | Attorney fees |
| Handles incapacity | No | Yes |
| Can delay or stagger gifts | No, beneficiary gets it outright | Yes |
| Good for minor beneficiaries | Usually not | Yes |
| Coordinating many assets | Each one is separate | One set of instructions |

For real estate specifically, see [transfer-on-death deed vs. trust](/compare/transfer-on-death-deed-vs-trust). For a broader look, see [probate vs. non-probate assets](/compare/probate-vs-non-probate-assets).

## A worked example

Helen Park is 72, single, and has two adult daughters, Jenny and Amy. She owns a condo, a savings account, a brokerage account and a car. Her state allows TOD deeds and TOD vehicle titles.

Helen does the following:

- Adds Jenny and Amy as equal POD beneficiaries on her savings account.
- Adds them as equal TOD beneficiaries on her brokerage account.
- Records a TOD deed on her condo naming both daughters.
- Gets a TOD title on her car naming Amy, who will need a car.

She also signs a simple will and powers of attorney. She keeps a small checking account in her own name to pay final bills.

When Helen dies, each asset passes directly. Jenny and Amy handle it in a few weeks, mostly with death certificates. Her will covers personal belongings and the small checking account, which falls under her state's small estate process.

Now imagine Jenny had died a year before Helen, leaving a son. Unless the forms named a backup or used per stirpes, Jenny's share might go to Amy alone or into probate.

It would depend on the account and state law. Helen never meant to cut out her grandson.

## Limits and risks

- **No incapacity planning.** If you cannot manage your accounts, a POD beneficiary has no authority to help. You still need a [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).
- **No control after death.** The beneficiary gets the asset outright, even if they are 19, in debt, or struggling with addiction.
- **Bills may still be owed.** Creditors and final expenses may reach these assets in some states, and the beneficiary may be asked to contribute.
- **Uneven results.** If one account grows and another shrinks, siblings named on different accounts may receive very different amounts.
- **Co-owned property.** A TOD deed usually only works on your share. Joint ownership rules can override it. See [joint ownership vs. trust](/compare/joint-ownership-vs-trust).

## Common mistakes

- **Naming different children on different accounts** and expecting it to come out equal.
- **Naming a minor** as a beneficiary.
- **Forgetting backups** when a beneficiary dies first.
- **Not recording a TOD deed** before death.
- **Leaving nothing for final bills,** so one heir pays for everyone.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my state allow transfer-on-death deeds and vehicle titles?
- Are TOD and POD designations enough for my situation, or would a trust work better?
- How do these designations interact with my will?
- Can creditors or Medicaid reach these assets after my death in my state?
- What should I do if a beneficiary needs protection, such as a minor or a person with a disability?
