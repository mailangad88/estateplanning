Blended families are common. You may be remarried, have children from an earlier marriage, or be raising stepchildren. The love is real, but the default rules of inheritance were not designed for these families.

Without a plan, one side of the family can end up with everything. That is rarely what anyone intended.

## Why simple plans can backfire

Many couples sign "sweetheart wills". Each spouse leaves everything to the other. The survivor then leaves everything to "our children".

In a first marriage, that usually works. In a blended family, it can fail. After the first spouse dies, the survivor owns everything and can change their will at any time. They might remarry, or simply leave it all to their own children. The first spouse's children may get nothing.

### Default state rules

If someone dies without a will, state law decides who inherits. In many states, a surviving spouse gets a large share, and the rest goes to the person's children. Stepchildren usually get nothing unless they were adopted. See [what happens if you die without a will](/guides/what-happens-if-you-die-without-a-will).

## The main planning tools

### A trust for the surviving spouse

Many couples use a trust that does two jobs at once. It supports the surviving spouse during their life. When the spouse dies, what is left goes to the first spouse's own children.

The trust can let the spouse:

- Live in the home for life
- Receive income from trust investments
- Get principal for health, support and similar needs

The spouse cannot change who gets the rest. That protects the children. This setup is often built into a [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained) that becomes irrevocable at death.

### Beneficiary designations

Retirement accounts and life insurance pass by beneficiary form, not by will. These forms often hold a large share of a family's wealth. Common approaches:

- Name your spouse on some accounts and your children on others
- Use life insurance to give children an inheritance right away
- Name a trust as beneficiary when you need control

Federal rules often give a spouse rights in an employer retirement plan, such as a 401(k). Naming someone else usually requires the spouse's written consent. Review all your forms with the [beneficiary designation audit](/checklists/beneficiary-designation-audit).

### Prenuptial and postnuptial agreements

A marital agreement can set what each spouse keeps and what each one gives up. It can waive a spouse's right to claim a share of the estate. Rules for these agreements vary by state, and each spouse usually needs their own lawyer.

### Choosing the right people

Picking a trustee is often the hardest part. Naming your spouse can put them in charge of money your children are waiting for. Naming your child can put your spouse at their mercy.

Options families commonly consider:

- A neutral friend or relative
- A professional or corporate trustee
- Co-trustees, one from each side of the family

For more on this choice, see [choosing a trustee](/guides/choosing-a-trustee).

## A worked example

David Morales and Karen Whitfield marry in their 50s. David has two adult sons. Karen has a teenage daughter, Emma, whom David has helped raise.

David wants Karen to stay in their home if he dies first. He also wants his sons to inherit eventually, and he wants to leave something to Emma.

Their plan:

1. David's trust holds the house. Karen can live there for life. Then it passes to his sons.
2. David names his sons as beneficiaries of a life insurance policy, so they inherit something right away.
3. David names Karen as beneficiary of his 401(k), with her full knowledge.
4. David's trust leaves a set gift to Emma.
5. They name a longtime family friend as trustee, so neither Karen nor the sons have to manage money for the other.

Karen makes a similar plan for Emma. Both sign a postnuptial agreement that confirms the arrangement.

## Talking about the plan

Surprises after a death cause many family fights. Some couples hold a family meeting to explain their plan in broad terms. Others write a letter of instruction. Our [letter of instruction outline](/checklists/letter-of-instruction-outline) can help you start.

You do not have to share dollar amounts. Explaining the why ("I want Karen to stay in our home") often prevents hard feelings.

It also helps to tell your trustee and executor where your documents are kept. They should know who your attorney is and which accounts have beneficiary forms. A short [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list) makes that easy for them.

## Common mistakes

- **Relying on sweetheart wills.** The survivor can change everything later.
- **Forgetting beneficiary forms.** An old form can send a retirement account to an ex-spouse.
- **Assuming stepchildren are included.** They usually are not unless named.
- **Leaving everything to children outright.** This can leave a surviving spouse without a home.
- **Naming rival family members as co-trustees** without a tie-breaker.
- **Never updating after remarriage.** See [updating your estate plan](/guides/updating-your-estate-plan).

## Questions to ask an attorney

- What rights does a surviving spouse have in my state?
- Is my state a community property state, and how does that affect us?
- Should we use a lifetime trust for the surviving spouse?
- Who should serve as trustee?
- Do we need a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement?
- How should we handle stepchildren we want to include?
