A health care directive is a written plan for medical decisions if you cannot make them yourself. It usually has two parts. One part names a person to speak for you. The other part writes down what kind of care you want.

States use different names. You may see "advance directive", "health care proxy", "medical power of attorney" or "patient advocate designation". The ideas are the same even when the labels change.

## The two main pieces

### Health care power of attorney

This document names your **health care agent**. That person talks to your doctors and makes choices for you when you cannot. They can usually:

- Agree to or refuse treatments, surgery and medicines
- Choose doctors, hospitals and care facilities
- See your medical records
- Decide about life support and feeding tubes, within the limits you set
- Make decisions about pain relief and hospice

Your agent is supposed to decide the way you would, not the way they would. That is why talking with them ahead of time matters so much.

### Living will

A living will records your wishes about end-of-life care. It speaks for you when you have a terminal condition or are permanently unconscious. Common topics include:

- CPR and breathing machines
- Tube feeding and IV fluids
- Dialysis
- Comfort care and pain relief, even if it might shorten life
- Organ and tissue donation

Many states combine both pieces into one advance directive form. Others keep them separate. For a closer look at how they differ, see [living will vs health care power of attorney](/compare/living-will-vs-healthcare-power-of-attorney).

## Why most people sign both

A living will cannot predict every situation. Real medical choices are messy. A doctor might ask whether to try a treatment for two weeks and see if it helps.

Your agent fills those gaps. The living will gives them guidance, and the agent applies it to what is actually happening. Without an agent, doctors may follow a state default list. Without a living will, your agent has to guess.

## A worked example

Tom and Linda Park are in their late 60s. Tom signs a directive naming Linda as his agent and his brother James as backup. His living will says he wants comfort care only if he is permanently unconscious with no reasonable chance of recovery.

Years later, Tom has a severe brain injury. After several weeks, his doctors say he is unlikely to wake up. Linda shows the hospital Tom's directive. Tom's adult son wants every treatment continued. Because Tom named Linda and wrote down his wishes, the hospital follows her decision.

Without those papers, Linda and Tom's son would have equal say in some states. That can lead to deadlock, an ethics committee review, or even court.

## How to choose a health care agent

People often pick a spouse or adult child. The best agent is someone who:

- Will follow your wishes even if they disagree
- Can stay calm in a hospital under stress
- Is willing to speak up to doctors and family
- Can be reached quickly, by phone or in person
- Knows you well and has actually talked with you about this

Name at least one backup. Some people name the same person as their financial agent under a [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney). Others split the roles between two people with different strengths.

## Talking about your wishes

The document is only as good as the conversation behind it. Topics people often cover with their agent:

1. What a "good day" looks like to you, and what would make life not worth extending
2. How you feel about being kept alive on machines
3. Whether you would want to try a treatment for a short time
4. Where you would prefer to spend your last days
5. Any religious or cultural practices that matter to you

Some people write a short personal letter to go with the directive. It can explain the "why" behind your choices in your own words.

## HIPAA authorization

Privacy rules can stop doctors from talking to family. A HIPAA authorization lets named people get your medical information. Many directive forms include this language. If yours does not, a separate HIPAA release is common. It can also name people who can get updates but not make decisions.

## Signing rules vary by state

Each state sets its own rules for a valid directive. Some require two witnesses. Some require a notary. Some limit who can be a witness, such as your doctor or your agent.

An attorney licensed in your state can confirm the right form and signing steps. Using the wrong steps can give a hospital a reason to question the document.

## Common mistakes

- **No one can find it.** A directive locked in a safe deposit box helps no one in an emergency. Give copies to your agent, backup and main doctor.
- **Never talking to the agent.** An agent who does not know your wishes may freeze or guess.
- **Vague wording.** "No heroic measures" means different things to different people. Specific choices work better.
- **Not updating it.** A divorce, a new diagnosis or a death in the family are good times to review it. See [updating your estate plan](/learn/basics/when-to-update-your-estate-plan).
- **Confusing it with a POLST.** If you are seriously ill, ask your doctor whether a POLST or similar medical order fits too.

## Where to keep copies

Keep the original somewhere safe but easy to reach at home. Give copies to:

- Your health care agent and backup agent
- Your primary doctor, to place in your chart
- Any hospital or clinic you use often
- A close family member, if that helps avoid conflict

Some states run an online registry for directives. Your patient portal may also let you upload one.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my state use one combined form or separate documents?
- What witness or notary rules apply here?
- Will my directive be honored if I travel or live part of the year in another state?
- Should I add instructions about mental health treatment or dementia?
- How do I revoke an old directive?
