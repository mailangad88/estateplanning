Estate taxes worry a lot of people who will never owe them. The federal tax only reaches very large estates. Still, state taxes and income tax on inherited retirement accounts can catch families by surprise.

This guide explains the three kinds of tax that come up most and the common ways families plan around them. Tax law changes, so confirm current figures with a tax professional.

## Three different taxes

| Tax | Who pays | Where it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Federal estate tax | The estate, before distribution | Nationwide, only above the federal exemption |
| State estate tax | The estate | Only in some states, often with lower thresholds |
| Inheritance tax | The person who inherits | Only in a few states, with rates that depend on the relationship |

A person can live in a state with no estate tax and still own property in a state that has one. That out-of-state property may be taxed there. Ask an attorney licensed in each state where you own real estate.

## The federal estate tax

The federal estate tax applies to the total value of what you own at death, minus debts, expenses and certain deductions. Only the amount above the exemption is taxed.

As of 2026, the federal estate tax exemption is $15 million per person <!-- verify -->, indexed for inflation in later years. An estate under that amount usually owes no federal estate tax at all.

### What counts toward the total

Your taxable estate usually includes:

- Your home and other real estate
- Bank, brokerage and retirement accounts
- Life insurance you own on your own life, even though it pays to a beneficiary
- Business interests
- Assets in a revocable living trust
- Your share of jointly owned property

The life insurance point surprises many people. A large policy can push an estate over the line. Some families use an irrevocable life insurance trust to keep the payout outside the estate. See [irrevocable trusts explained](/guides/irrevocable-trusts-explained).

### Deductions that reduce the total

- **The marital deduction.** Assets left to a surviving spouse who is a U.S. citizen generally pass free of federal estate tax. Different rules apply to non-citizen spouses.
- **The charitable deduction.** Gifts to qualified charities at death are deductible.
- **Debts and expenses.** Mortgages, final bills and estate administration costs reduce the taxable amount.

### Portability for married couples

When the first spouse dies, the unused part of their exemption can often pass to the surviving spouse. This is called **portability**. It usually requires the executor to file a federal estate tax return, even when no tax is due.

Families sometimes skip that return because nothing is owed. Later, they find the survivor's estate has grown and the lost exemption would have helped. Ask a CPA or attorney whether filing makes sense.

## The gift tax and annual exclusion

The gift tax works with the estate tax. Large lifetime gifts use up part of the same exemption you would use at death.

Small gifts are different. As of 2026, the annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient (the 2025 figure) <!-- verify -->. You can give up to that amount to as many people as you like each year, usually with no gift tax return. Payments made directly to a school for tuition or to a medical provider for someone's care are generally not counted as gifts either.

## State estate and inheritance taxes

A minority of states charge their own estate tax. Their thresholds are often far below the federal one. A family that owes nothing federally may still owe state tax.

A few states charge an inheritance tax instead, and one or two have both. Spouses are usually exempt. Children are often exempt or taxed lightly. More distant relatives and friends may pay higher rates.

Because these rules change and differ so much, ask an attorney licensed in your state how they apply to you.

## Income tax on inherited assets

Inheriting money is not usually income. But two income tax rules matter a lot.

### Step-up in basis

When you inherit property like stock or a house, its tax "basis" usually resets to its value on the date of death. If you sell soon after, there may be little or no capital gains tax. This is one reason people sometimes hold appreciated assets until death rather than giving them away during life.

### Inherited retirement accounts

Traditional IRAs and 401(k)s are different. The beneficiary pays income tax on withdrawals. Many non-spouse beneficiaries must empty the account within a set number of years. Planning who inherits these accounts can make a real difference. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

## A worked example

Robert and Elaine Chen are married. Together they own a home, retirement accounts, a brokerage account and a life insurance policy. Their combined estate is well under twice the federal exemption.

Robert dies first and leaves everything to Elaine. The marital deduction means no federal estate tax. Their attorney advises filing a federal estate tax return anyway to claim portability. Elaine now has her own exemption plus Robert's unused amount.

The Chens live in a state with its own estate tax and a much lower threshold. Their plan includes a trust for Elaine that uses Robert's state exemption at his death. That keeps part of the estate out of Elaine's taxable estate when she dies, and lowers the state tax their children may owe.

## Common planning tools

- **Annual exclusion gifts** to children, grandchildren or others
- **Irrevocable life insurance trusts** to keep policy proceeds out of the estate
- **Credit shelter or bypass trusts** for married couples, often used for state tax
- **Charitable gifts or trusts** for people with charitable goals
- **Gifts of appreciating assets** to move future growth out of the estate

Each has tradeoffs, including loss of control and setup costs. Compare options in [revocable vs irrevocable trust](/compare/revocable-vs-irrevocable-trust).

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming the federal number is the only number.** State thresholds can be much lower.
- **Forgetting life insurance counts.** A policy you own is part of your estate.
- **Skipping the portability return.** That can waste a spouse's unused exemption.
- **Giving away low-basis assets without thinking.** Heirs may lose the step-up in basis.
- **Naming the estate as IRA beneficiary.** This can speed up income tax on the account.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my state have an estate or inheritance tax, and what is its threshold?
- Do I own property in another state that could be taxed there?
- Should my plan include a trust for state tax purposes?
- Should my executor file for portability?
- How should I name beneficiaries on my retirement accounts?
