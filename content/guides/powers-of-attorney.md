A power of attorney is a signed document that lets someone you choose handle your money and legal matters. The person you choose is called your **agent** (some states say "attorney-in-fact"). You are the **principal**.

The main reason people sign one is incapacity. Say a stroke, dementia or an accident leaves you unable to manage things. Your agent can then pay bills, file taxes and deal with the bank. Without one, your family may have to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator.

## What an agent can do

An agent steps into your shoes for the tasks the document allows. A broad financial power of attorney usually covers:

- Paying bills and managing bank accounts
- Filing tax returns and dealing with the IRS
- Buying, selling or managing real estate
- Handling retirement accounts and insurance claims
- Talking to government agencies such as Social Security
- Moving assets into a living trust you already created

Some powers are usually off-limits unless the document spells them out. These are often called "hot powers". Examples include making gifts, changing beneficiary designations, and creating or changing trusts. State rules differ, so ask an attorney licensed in your state how your form handles them.

## The main types

| Type | When it starts | When it ends |
|---|---|---|
| Durable | When you sign it | At your death or when you revoke it |
| Springing | When a stated event happens, usually incapacity | At your death or when you revoke it |
| Limited (special) | When you sign it, or a set date | When the task is done or a set date |
| Non-durable (general) | When you sign it | If you become incapacitated, at death, or on revocation |

The word **durable** is the key. A durable power of attorney keeps working if you lose capacity. That is the whole point for most estate plans. Many states now treat a new power of attorney as durable unless it says otherwise, but not every state does.

### Springing versus effective now

A springing power only "springs" into effect once you are incapacitated. That sounds safer, but it can cause delays. Your agent may need letters from one or two doctors before a bank will accept it.

Many people sign a power that is effective right away instead. They trust the agent not to use it until needed. If you do not trust someone with that much power today, it is worth asking whether they are the right agent at all.

### A health care power is separate

A financial power of attorney does not cover medical choices. Those go in a health care power of attorney or advance directive. See [healthcare directives and living wills](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) for how that document works.

## How to choose an agent

Your agent will have real access to your money. Choose for honesty first, then for skill and availability. Questions people commonly ask themselves:

1. Do I trust this person with my bank login and my house?
2. Are they organized enough to keep records and pay bills on time?
3. Do they live close enough, or can they handle things remotely?
4. Will they stand up to pushy relatives or salespeople?
5. Are they willing to do it? Ask them.

Name at least one **successor agent** in case your first choice dies, gets sick or says no. Some people name two co-agents. That adds a check, but it can slow things down if both must sign everything. If you name co-agents, the document should say whether they may act alone or must act together.

## A worked example

Rosa Delgado is 71 and widowed. She has a checking account, an IRA, and a house in her name alone. She names her daughter Marisol as agent and her son Daniel as successor.

Two years later, Rosa has a fall and spends four months in rehab. Marisol uses the durable power of attorney to pay Rosa's property taxes, deal with Medicare billing and move money from savings to checking. She keeps a spreadsheet of every transaction and sends Daniel a copy each month.

Now picture the same fall with no power of attorney. Rosa's bank will not let Marisol touch the account. The family files for conservatorship. That means a court hearing, legal fees, and possibly yearly reports to the judge. A two-page document could have avoided most of that.

## Your agent's duties

An agent is a **fiduciary**. That means they must act in your best interest, not their own. In general, an agent should:

- Keep your money separate from theirs
- Keep receipts and records of every transaction
- Follow your known wishes where possible
- Avoid self-dealing, such as "borrowing" from your account
- Stay within the powers the document grants

An agent who misuses a power of attorney can be sued and, in serious cases, charged with a crime. Clear records protect the agent as much as the principal.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting too long.** You must have mental capacity when you sign. Once dementia sets in, it may be too late, and a court process may be the only option.
- **Using a non-durable form.** A general power that ends at incapacity stops working at exactly the moment it would be used.
- **No successor agent.** If your only agent cannot serve, the document may be useless.
- **Leaving out gifting or trust powers.** If Medicaid planning might come up later, a power without gifting authority can block it. See [Medicaid and long-term care planning](/guides/medicaid-and-long-term-care-planning).
- **Not telling the agent where things are.** An agent who cannot find the accounts cannot help. Our [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) can help.
- **Relying on an old document.** Some banks hesitate with forms that are many years old. Reviewing it every few years helps.

## Power of attorney or guardianship

A power of attorney is private and you choose the agent. A guardianship or conservatorship is a court process, and the judge chooses. Guardianship is often slower, costs more and is public. For a side-by-side view, read [power of attorney vs guardianship](/compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship).

## How it works with a trust

If you have a [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained), your successor trustee manages the trust assets if you become incapacitated. But some things stay outside a trust, such as IRAs, tax filings and a car you forgot to transfer. Your agent covers those. That is why people with trusts still sign a power of attorney.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Is a durable power effective now or springing more common in my state?
- Does my state have a statutory form, and does it fit my needs?
- Should my document include gifting or trust powers?
- How do I make sure my bank and brokerage will accept it?
- What happens to my old power of attorney when I sign a new one?
- Does it need a notary, witnesses, or recording for real estate in my state?
