Children under 18 can't legally own and manage significant property on their own. If you leave money directly to a minor, someone has to be put in charge of it. Without a plan, that's usually a court-appointed conservator or guardian of the estate, and the child may receive everything outright at 18.

You can avoid this by choosing the structure in advance. The two most common tools are custodial accounts and trusts.

## What happens if you leave money directly to a minor

Say a grandmother names her 9-year-old grandson as the beneficiary of a $150,000 life insurance policy. When she dies, the insurer won't send a check to a 9-year-old.

In many states, a court then appoints a guardian of the estate or conservator. That person often has to:

- post a bond,
- file regular accountings with the court,
- ask permission for some spending, and
- turn over all remaining money when the child reaches the age of majority, usually 18.

This costs money, takes time, and ends with an 18-year-old receiving a large sum with no strings attached. Rules vary by state.

## Option 1: Custodial accounts under a transfers to minors act

Every state has a version of the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act (UTMA) or the older Uniform Gifts to Minors Act (UGMA). These laws let a custodian hold and manage property for a child without court supervision.

- You name a custodian in your will, on a beneficiary form, or when you open the account.
- The custodian spends money for the child's benefit, like school or medical costs.
- The child gets full control at an age set by state law, commonly 18 or 21, and up to 25 in some states.

Custodial accounts are simple and cheap. Their main limit is that the end age is fixed by statute. They also hold one child's money each, so they don't work well for pooling funds for several children.

## Option 2: A trust for the child

A trust lets you write your own rules. The trustee holds the money and follows your instructions.

Common choices include:

- **Payout ages.** For example, one-third at 25, one-third at 30, the rest at 35.
- **What the money can be used for.** Health, education, support, a first home, or starting a business.
- **A pot trust.** One shared fund for all your children until the youngest reaches a set age, then split. This works like a family budget, so a child with bigger needs can get more.
- **Separate trusts.** Each child gets their own share right away, held in their own trust.

A trust can be created inside your will (a testamentary trust) or as part of a revocable living trust. A testamentary trust takes effect only after probate. A living trust can avoid probate if it is funded. See [Revocable Living Trust Explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).

## Comparing the options

| | Outright to minor | Custodial account (UTMA/UGMA) | Trust |
|---|---|---|---|
| Court supervision | Often yes | Usually no | Usually no |
| Age child gets control | Usually 18 | Set by state law, often 18 to 21 | Whatever you choose |
| Your control over use | None | Limited | High |
| Setup cost | None | Low | Higher |
| Good for | Small amounts only | Small to moderate amounts | Larger amounts, several children, special needs |

## A worked example: the Hassan family

Omar and Leila Hassan have three children, ages 3, 8 and 12. Omar has a $1 million term life policy through work and a $400,000 401(k).

Their first beneficiary forms named "my children" as contingent beneficiaries after each other. An attorney pointed out that if both parents died while the children were young, a court would likely need to manage the money. Each child might then get their share outright at 18.

They changed their plan:

- They created a revocable living trust with a pot trust for all three children until the youngest turns 23.
- After that, each child's share stays in a separate trust, with half paid out at 25 and the rest at 30.
- They named the trust as the contingent beneficiary on the life insurance and checked with the 401(k) plan about naming the trust. Retirement accounts raise their own tax questions, so they asked their attorney about the payout rules.
- They named Leila's cousin as trustee and Omar's sister as guardian.

## Gifts during your lifetime

Many grandparents give money now rather than later. Gifts up to the annual gift tax exclusion, $19,000 per recipient as the 2025 figure <!-- verify -->, generally don't require a gift tax return. Common vehicles include custodial accounts and 529 education plans. See [Estate and Inheritance Taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes) for the bigger picture.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming minors directly** on life insurance or retirement accounts.
- **Writing "to my children" with no trust** in a will when the kids are young.
- **Choosing a trustee who is a beneficiary's parent in a bitter divorce,** which can lead to conflict.
- **Forgetting special needs.** Money left directly to a child with a disability can affect benefits. See [Special Needs Trusts](/guides/special-needs-trusts).
- **Setting the payout age too young** for a large sum.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- What is the UTMA age in my state, and can I choose a later age?

- How should I name a trust as beneficiary of a retirement account?
- Who would make a good trustee, and should it be the guardian?
- How do I handle a gift to a grandchild whose parents are divorced?
