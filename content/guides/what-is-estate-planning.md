Estate planning is deciding, in writing, who gets your things, who looks after your children, and who makes decisions for you if you can't. It covers two situations: what happens after you die, and what happens if you are alive but unable to manage your own affairs. A good plan answers both.

Most people think of estate planning as "writing a will." The will is one piece. The rest of the plan includes documents for incapacity and the beneficiary settings on your accounts, which often control more money than the will does.

## The core documents in a basic plan

A typical plan for an adult has four or five parts. Each one does a different job.

| Document | What it does | When it takes effect |
|---|---|---|
| Will | Says who receives your property and names an executor and a guardian for minor children | At death |
| Financial power of attorney | Lets a trusted person handle money, bills and property for you | While you are alive, often if you become incapacitated |
| Healthcare power of attorney | Names someone to make medical decisions when you can't | While you are alive and unable to decide |
| Living will or healthcare directive | Records your wishes about life support and end-of-life care | While you are alive and unable to communicate |
| Revocable living trust (optional) | Holds assets during life and passes them at death, usually without probate | During life and at death |

Not every plan needs a trust. Many people do well with a will, the powers of attorney, and careful beneficiary designations. Whether a trust makes sense depends on your assets, your state's probate process, and your family. See [Will vs. Trust](/compare/will-vs-trust) for a side-by-side look.

## How the pieces fit together

Your property passes at death in one of three ways. Knowing which way each asset travels is the heart of estate planning.

1. **By beneficiary designation.** Life insurance, 401(k)s, IRAs and many bank accounts pass to whoever is named on the account form. The will does not control these.
2. **By how the asset is titled.** A house held as joint tenants with right of survivorship goes to the surviving owner. Assets titled in a trust follow the trust's instructions.
3. **By your will.** Anything left over, titled in your name alone with no beneficiary, passes under your will through probate.

Here is why this matters. If your will says "split everything equally between my three children" but your life insurance names only your oldest, the oldest gets all the insurance. The will cannot override the form. For more on this, see [Beneficiary Designation vs. Will](/compare/beneficiary-designation-vs-will).

## A worked example: the Okafor family

Daniel and Grace Okafor are in their late thirties with two children, ages 4 and 7. They own a home together, have two 401(k)s, a joint checking account, and a $500,000 term life policy on Daniel.

Here is what a basic plan for them might look like:

- **Wills** for each of them, leaving everything to the other spouse first, then to the children. Each will names Grace's sister, Ruth, as guardian of the children.
- **A trust for the children** (either inside the wills or as a separate revocable trust), so the kids don't receive a lump sum at 18. The trust might hold money until age 25, with a trustee paying for school, housing and health care.
- **Beneficiary designations** naming each other first, then the children's trust as the backup. Naming the minor children directly could force a court to appoint someone to manage the money.
- **Financial and healthcare powers of attorney** naming each other, with Ruth as the backup.
- **Healthcare directives** stating each person's wishes about life support.

Without this plan, if both parents died in a car accident, a court would choose the guardian. Each child would likely receive their share outright at 18. The Okafors' plan avoids both outcomes.

## Steps to put a plan in place

The process tends to follow the same order for most families.

1. **List what you own.** Write down accounts, real estate, insurance, retirement plans and debts. Note how each is titled and who is named as beneficiary. The [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) can help.
2. **Decide who gets what.** Think about your spouse or partner, children, other relatives, friends and charities.
3. **Choose your people.** Pick an executor, a guardian for minor children, a trustee if you use a trust, and agents for your powers of attorney. Pick backups for each role.
4. **Pick your tools.** Decide between a will-based plan or a trust-based plan, with help from an attorney if your situation is not simple.
5. **Sign the documents properly.** Signing rules for wills and powers of attorney vary by state. Some require witnesses, some a notary, some both.
6. **Update your accounts.** Change beneficiary forms and, if you have a trust, retitle assets into it. This step is often skipped.
7. **Tell the right people.** Let your executor and agents know where the documents are kept.

## Common mistakes

These problems show up often when families settle an estate.

- **Only planning for death.** Without powers of attorney, your family may need a court guardianship or conservatorship to pay your bills if you have a stroke or dementia.
- **Forgetting beneficiary forms.** An ex-spouse may still be named on an old 401(k). In some states divorce revokes that automatically, but federal rules for employer plans can override state law.
- **Creating a trust and never funding it.** A trust only controls what is titled in its name. An empty trust does very little. See [Funding Your Trust](/guides/funding-your-trust).
- **Leaving money directly to minors.** Children can't legally manage inherited money, so a court may step in.
- **Never updating.** A plan written before a divorce, a new child or a move to another state may no longer fit.

## Questions to ask an attorney

If you meet with an estate planning attorney, these questions can help you get more from the meeting.

- Based on my state's probate process, would a trust save my family time or money?
- Are my beneficiary designations consistent with what I want my will to do?
- What signing rules does my state require for wills and powers of attorney?
- How should I plan for a child or relative who receives government benefits?
- Does my plan cover what happens if I become unable to manage my own affairs?
- How often do you recommend reviewing my plan, and what is the cost of updates?
