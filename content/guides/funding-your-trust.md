Funding a trust means moving your assets into it. A trust only controls what it owns. If your house is still in your own name when you die, the trust document has no power over it.

Most of the work is paperwork. You change the owner on titles and accounts from your name to your name as trustee.

## What "titled in the trust" looks like

The new owner is usually written something like this:

> Maria Okafor, Trustee of the Okafor Family Trust dated March 3, 2026

Your attorney will give you the exact wording to use. Banks and county offices are more comfortable when every document uses the same name and date.

## Three ways an asset can connect to your trust

Not every asset gets retitled. There are three common approaches.

| Approach | How it works | Typical assets |
|---|---|---|
| Retitle into the trust | The trust becomes the owner now | Home, other real estate, bank and brokerage accounts |
| Name the trust as beneficiary | You keep ownership, the trust receives it at death | Life insurance, sometimes retirement accounts |
| Leave it out on purpose | It passes another way | Cars, everyday checking, some retirement accounts |

Picking the right approach for each item is the heart of funding.

## Step by step: funding your trust

### 1. Make a full list of what you own

Write down every account and property, plus how it is titled today. Note who is listed as owner and any beneficiary named. The [asset and account inventory](/checklists/asset-and-account-inventory) is built for this.

### 2. Get a certificate of trust

Most states allow a short summary called a certificate or certification of trust. It proves the trust exists and who the trustee is, without showing who inherits. Banks usually ask for this instead of the whole trust.

### 3. Deed your real estate

For each property, a new deed is prepared from you to you as trustee. It is signed, notarized and recorded with the county where the property sits. Some counties require extra transfer forms.

Things to check before recording:

- Whether your mortgage lender needs notice. Federal law generally protects transfers into your own revocable trust, but tell the lender anyway.
- Whether your homestead or property tax exemption carries over. Rules vary by state.
- Whether your title insurance and homeowner's insurance need updating. Ask your insurer to add the trust as an insured.

### 4. Retitle bank and investment accounts

Call or visit each bank and brokerage. Ask for their trust account form.

Some will change the owner on the existing account. Others open a new account in the trust's name and move the money over.

### 5. Assign personal property

Furniture, jewelry, art and collections usually do not have titles. A general assignment document transfers them to the trust in one step. Your attorney often includes one with the trust.

### 6. Handle business interests

If you own part of an LLC or a small corporation, the interest can usually be assigned to the trust. Check the operating agreement or shareholder agreement first.

Some require consent from other owners. Our guide on [business succession planning](/learn/business-owners/business-succession-planning) goes deeper.

### 7. Update beneficiary designations where it fits

For life insurance, many people name the trust as primary or contingent beneficiary. For retirement accounts, the choice has tax effects, so it is worth a careful conversation. See [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

### 8. Keep a funding record

Save a copy of every recorded deed, account letter and form in one folder. Your successor trustee will need it. A simple list showing "asset, how titled, date changed" saves your family hours.

## Assets people usually leave out

Some assets are commonly kept outside the trust on purpose.

- **IRAs, 401(k)s and similar accounts.** Changing the owner can be treated as cashing out. These pass by beneficiary form.
- **Health savings accounts.** These also pass by beneficiary form.
- **Vehicles.** Many states have a simple transfer process for cars after death. Some offer transfer-on-death titles.
- **A small everyday checking account.** Some people keep one in their own name with a payable-on-death beneficiary. Read about [transfer-on-death and payable-on-death](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts).

## A worked example

Linda Reyes, a widow, signs a revocable trust in 2026. Her son Marco is successor trustee. She owns a house, a savings account, a brokerage account, an IRA, a car and a life insurance policy.

Here is how she funds it:

- **House:** New deed to Linda as trustee, recorded with the county.
- **Savings and brokerage:** Retitled in the trust's name at each bank.
- **IRA:** Left in her name. Marco and his sister Elena named as equal beneficiaries.
- **Life insurance:** Trust named as beneficiary, so the trust can hold her granddaughter's share until age 25.
- **Car:** Left out. Her state offers a simple transfer process for vehicles.
- **Household items:** Covered by a general assignment.

Three years later, Linda buys a small condo near her daughter. She takes title in her own name because the closing happened fast. Nobody notices.

When Linda dies, almost everything passes smoothly. The condo does not.

It goes to the trust through her pour-over will, but only after a probate case. One deed would have avoided it.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying property after the trust is signed** and taking title in your own name.
- **Refinancing a house** and letting the lender move it out of the trust. Some lenders ask for this. Ask them to put it back after closing.
- **Opening new accounts** without using the trust as owner or naming a beneficiary.
- **Inconsistent trust names** across documents, which makes banks hesitate.
- **Assuming the attorney did it all.** Some firms fund the trust for you. Others hand you instructions. Ask which one you are paying for.

## How often to check your funding

Many people review funding once a year and after any big event: a home purchase, a refinance, a new account, an inheritance. Our [annual estate plan review](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review) includes a funding check.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Will your office prepare and record the deeds?
- Does my state's homestead or property tax exemption survive the transfer?
- Should my IRA name the trust, or my children directly?
- Does my state offer transfer-on-death titles for cars or real estate?
- What do I need to do when I buy something new?
