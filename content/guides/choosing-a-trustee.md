A trustee manages the property in a trust and follows its instructions. The best choice is usually someone honest, organized, and willing to do the work for as long as it lasts.

That last part matters. Some trusts wrap up in a few months. Others run for decades, such as a trust for young grandchildren or a [special needs trust](/guides/special-needs-trusts).

## What a trustee actually does

The job varies with the trust, but most trustees handle these tasks:

- Take control of trust assets and keep them safe.
- Keep trust money separate from their own.
- Invest sensibly and keep records of every dollar in and out.
- Pay bills, taxes and trust expenses.
- File trust tax returns, often with help from an accountant.
- Communicate with beneficiaries and send reports as the trust or state law requires.
- Make distributions under the trust's rules.
- Make judgment calls, such as whether to pay for a beneficiary's car or tuition.

Trustees are held to a high legal standard called a fiduciary duty. They must act in the beneficiaries' interest, not their own. A trustee who mixes funds or plays favorites can be held personally responsible.

## Qualities that matter most

Being a close relative is not a qualification on its own. People commonly look for these traits:

| Quality | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Honesty | They will control money that is not theirs |
| Organization | Records, deadlines and tax filings pile up fast |
| Good judgment | Many trusts call for discretionary decisions |
| Availability | The job can take hours a week at the start |
| Even temper | They may need to say no to family members |
| Longevity | A long trust needs someone who will be around |

Living far away is less of a problem than it used to be. Most banking and paperwork can be done remotely. Selling a house from across the country is harder, though.

## Family member, friend or professional

There are three main options. Each has trade-offs.

### A family member or friend

This is the most common choice. They know the family and usually serve for little or no fee. The downside is that they may lack experience, and family tension can make their job harder.

### A professional or corporate trustee

Banks, trust companies and some attorneys serve as trustees. They bring experience, neutrality and continuity. They also charge ongoing fees and may feel distant to the family.

### A combination

Some trusts name a family member and a professional as co-trustees. Others name a family member as trustee with a professional handling investments. A third approach names a "trust protector," a person with limited powers to fix problems or replace a trustee.

## How the successor trustee steps in

In a revocable living trust, you are usually your own trustee. Your successor takes over when you die or become incapacitated. The trust document says how incapacity is shown, often by letters from one or two doctors.

For more on how this fits together, see [revocable living trusts explained](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).

## Executor and trustee: same person or not

Your executor handles your will and any probate. Your trustee handles the trust.

Many people name the same person for both, which keeps things simple. The roles are compared in [executor vs. trustee](/compare/executor-vs-trustee).

## A worked example

Ruth and Harold Bennett have three adult children. Their son Alan is an accountant who lives nearby.

Their daughter Claire lives overseas. Their youngest, Tommy, has struggled with gambling.

They name Alan as successor trustee and their friend and neighbor, Priya Shah, as backup. They do not name Claire, because of the distance and time difference. They do not name Tommy, and they direct that his share stay in trust with Alan deciding when to pay bills directly for him.

Because Tommy might pressure his brother, the Bennetts add a trust protector. They choose their longtime financial planner, who can replace Alan with a trust company if Alan ever wants out. Alan knows this in advance and is relieved.

The Bennetts also write a letter explaining their reasons. It is not legally binding. It helps Alan explain decisions to his siblings and keeps the focus on what their parents wanted.

## Common mistakes

- **Not asking first.** A surprised trustee may decline, leaving a gap.
- **Naming no backup.** People die, move or burn out. Many plans name at least two successors in order.
- **Choosing based on birth order.** The oldest child is not always the best fit.
- **Naming co-trustees who do not get along.** Disagreements can freeze the trust.
- **Picking a trustee much older than you.** They may not be able to serve when needed.
- **Leaving no instructions.** Clear standards for distributions make the job easier and fairer.

## Making the job easier for whoever you choose

A few small steps help a lot. Keep a current list of assets and where they are held.

Leave a letter of instruction with passwords, contacts and wishes. Our [letter of instruction outline](/checklists/letter-of-instruction-outline) and [important contacts list](/checklists/important-contacts-list) can help.

Tell your trustee where the original trust document is kept. A trustee who cannot find the document cannot act.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- How does my trust define incapacity, and who decides?
- Should my co-trustees act together or separately?
- Is a trust protector worth adding in my family's situation?
- What can I do to limit conflict between my trustee and other beneficiaries?
- What does a corporate trustee in my area typically charge, and what is the minimum account size?
