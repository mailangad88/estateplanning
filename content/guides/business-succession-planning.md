For many owners, the business is the biggest asset they have. It is also the hardest one to pass on. A house can be sold. A company needs someone to keep it running the day after you are gone.

Succession planning answers two questions. Who will **own** the business next? And who will **run** it? Those may not be the same people.

## The events to plan for

A good plan covers more than death. Owners commonly plan for:

- **Retirement.** You want to step back on your own timeline.
- **Disability.** You cannot work for months or longer.
- **Death.** Your share passes to your estate or heirs.
- **Departure.** A co-owner quits, divorces or files for bankruptcy.

Each one can leave the business without a decision-maker. A plan says who steps in and what happens to the ownership.

## The main tools

### Buy-sell agreement

A buy-sell agreement is a contract among owners. It answers:

- Who can or must buy a departing owner's share
- What events trigger the sale
- How the price is set
- How and when the price is paid

There are two common setups. In a **cross-purchase** agreement, the other owners buy the share. In an **entity redemption** agreement, the company buys it back. Some agreements blend both. Tax results differ, so owners usually review the choice with a CPA.

### Funding the buyout

A buy-sell agreement only works if the buyer has money. Common funding sources:

- Life insurance on each owner
- Disability buyout insurance
- Company savings set aside over time
- An installment note paid over several years

Without funding, the surviving owner may not be able to pay. Your family could end up waiting years, or the deal could fall apart.

### Trusts and your estate plan

Your business interest should fit with the rest of your plan. Many owners move LLC units or shares into a [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained). That can keep the interest out of probate and let a successor trustee act quickly.

Check your operating agreement or bylaws first. Some restrict transfers, even to your own trust.

### Power of attorney

If you are disabled, someone needs authority to vote your shares and sign for you. A durable [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) that covers business interests can do that. Many owners also name an interim manager in company documents.

## Choosing a successor

Owners often face a few paths:

| Path | Works well when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Family member | A child or relative already works in the business | Fairness to other children |
| Key employee | A trusted manager knows the operations | How they will pay for it |
| Co-owner | Partners already share control | Funding the buyout |
| Outside sale | No one inside wants it | Timing and price |

A successor needs time to learn. Many owners start handing over responsibilities years before they plan to leave.

## Valuation

The price in your buy-sell agreement matters a lot. Common methods:

- A fixed value the owners update every year
- A formula, such as a multiple of earnings
- An independent appraisal at the time of the event

A fixed value that nobody updated is one of the most common problems. If the business grew, your family may get far less than it is worth. If it shrank, the other owner may overpay.

Value also matters for estate tax. A large business can push an estate above the federal exemption. See [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes).

## A worked example

Maria Russo and Ben Adeyemi each own half of a commercial landscaping company. They sign a cross-purchase buy-sell agreement. Each buys a life insurance policy on the other. They agree to update the company value every January.

Maria also has two children. Her son Luca works in the business. Her daughter Gina is a teacher. Maria's plan leaves Gina a larger share of her investments and a separate life insurance policy. That keeps things fair without giving Gina a stake in a company she does not work in.

When Maria dies unexpectedly, Ben uses the insurance payout to buy her share at the agreed price. Maria's trust receives the money. Luca stays on as an operations manager and later buys into the company as a partner. Gina receives her share without waiting for a business sale.

## Common mistakes

- **No written agreement.** A handshake understanding often falls apart under grief and money pressure.
- **Unfunded buyouts.** A promise to buy is worth little without the money to do it.
- **Stale valuations.** A number from years ago rarely fits today.
- **Ignoring disability.** Many plans cover death but not a long illness.
- **Equal shares to all children.** This can put an inactive heir in charge of an active one's livelihood.
- **Plan and documents that do not match.** Your will or trust and the buy-sell agreement must say the same thing.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my operating agreement or bylaws allow transfer to my trust?
- Should we use a cross-purchase or entity redemption agreement?
- How should we set and update the value?
- How will the buyout be funded?
- How do I treat children fairly when only one works in the business?
- Could my business push my estate into estate tax?
