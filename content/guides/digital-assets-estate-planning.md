Digital assets are the parts of your life that exist online or on devices. That includes email, photos, social media, online banking, cryptocurrency, domain names, and files in the cloud. Planning for them means making sure the right person can find, access, and then close, keep, or inherit each one.

Without planning, families often hit locked accounts, lost photos, and bills that keep auto-paying for months.

## What counts as a digital asset

People often have more than they realize. Common categories:

| Category | Examples | Typical goal at death |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | Email, messaging apps | Notify contacts, then close |
| Memories | Photos, videos, cloud storage | Download and share with family |
| Social media | Profiles and pages | Memorialize or delete |
| Money | Online banking, payment apps, crypto | Transfer value to heirs |
| Subscriptions | Streaming, software, memberships | Cancel |
| Business | Websites, domains, online stores, ad accounts | Keep running or transfer |
| Loyalty programs | Airline miles, hotel points | Transfer, if program rules allow |
| Devices | Phone, laptop, external drives | Get in and recover files |

Some accounts have real money value. Others have sentimental value. Many have both.

## How the law treats digital access

Most states have adopted a version of a uniform law about fiduciary access to digital assets. It generally creates a priority order:

1. **A platform's own online tool** (like a legacy contact setting) usually controls first.
2. **Your estate planning documents** come next, if you have given clear permission there.
3. **The platform's terms of service** apply if you did neither.

Under these laws, permission to see the *content* of your messages usually requires your express consent. Without it, your executor may only get a list of who you communicated with and when. The details vary by state, so ask an attorney licensed where you live.

## Step by step: planning your digital estate

### 1. Make an inventory

List your accounts, devices and where files are stored. Note what each one is for and what you want done with it.

Our [digital assets inventory](/checklists/digital-assets-inventory) is set up for this. Keep usernames on the list, not passwords.

### 2. Use each platform's legacy tools

Several major services let you name someone who can access or manage your account after death. Others let you choose whether your profile is memorialized or deleted. These settings generally take priority over your will, so check them first.

### 3. Store passwords securely

A password manager is the most common approach. Many offer an emergency access feature for a trusted person.

Others keep a written list in a safe or a safe deposit box. Whatever you choose, tell your executor how to get to it.

### 4. Add digital access language to your documents

Ask your attorney to include express permission for your fiduciaries to access digital assets and the content of electronic communications. This usually goes in your:

- Will, for your executor
- Living trust, for your trustee
- [Durable power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney), for your agent if you become incapacitated

### 5. Plan for cryptocurrency carefully

Crypto is often controlled by private keys or seed phrases. If no one can find them, the value is usually lost.

Many owners leave sealed, secure instructions that explain where the wallet is and how to reach the keys. They avoid writing the keys into any document that could become public.

If you hold crypto through an exchange, check whether it allows a beneficiary or has a death claim process.

### 6. Name the right person

Your executor needs to be comfortable with technology or willing to get help. Some people name a separate "digital executor" for online accounts, where state law allows. Our guide on [choosing an executor](/guides/choosing-an-executor) covers the qualities to look for.

### 7. Keep it current

Accounts and passwords change constantly. Many people refresh their list once a year. The [annual estate plan review](/checklists/annual-estate-plan-review) includes a reminder.

## A worked example

Derek Johnson, 58, runs a small online store selling hand-made furniture. He has personal and business email, plus 20 years of family photos in the cloud. He also has two social media accounts, a password manager, and some cryptocurrency on a hardware wallet.

Derek takes these steps:

- Lists every account in a digital inventory, noting which ones are business and which personal.
- Names his wife, Tanya, as legacy contact on his phone and photo account.
- Sets up emergency access in his password manager for Tanya.
- Has his attorney add digital access language to his will, trust and power of attorney.
- Writes instructions for the hardware wallet and stores the seed phrase in a sealed envelope in a home safe that Tanya can open.
- Names his business partner, Kim, to run the store's accounts if he becomes incapacitated, and gives her the needed admin access now.

When Derek has a sudden heart attack and is in the hospital for weeks, Kim keeps orders shipping. When he later dies, Tanya downloads the family photos, memorializes his social accounts, cancels subscriptions, and moves the crypto to her own wallet.

Without these steps, the store's payment account might have been frozen and the crypto lost for good.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting passwords in the will.**
- **Sharing passwords without legal authority,** which can create problems with platform terms.
- **Forgetting auto-pay subscriptions,** which keep charging after death.
- **Losing access to two-factor codes** sent to a phone no one can open.
- **Not planning for incapacity,** when online banking and business accounts still need managing.
- **Keeping crypto keys only in your head.**

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my state's law on fiduciary access to digital assets apply to me, and how?
- What digital access language should go in my will, trust and power of attorney?
- Can I name a separate digital executor?
- How should I leave instructions for cryptocurrency without risking theft?
- How do I transfer an online business if I die or become incapacitated?
