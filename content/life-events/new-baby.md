## What changes legally when you have a child

A baby is the first person in your life who cannot care for themselves. If something happened to you, a court would decide who raises your child unless you have said so in writing. A will is the usual place to say it. The guide to [guardianship for minor children](/guides/guardianship-for-minor-children) explains how courts treat that choice.

Money changes too. A minor child cannot legally own large amounts of property in their own name. If your child inherits directly, a court may have to appoint someone to manage the money. That usually lasts until your child turns 18 or 21, depending on your state.

Your existing documents may now point the wrong way. An old will, or a beneficiary form that names a sibling or a former partner, does not update itself when a baby arrives.

## What new parents commonly put in place

### A will that names a guardian

Most parents start here. The will names a guardian for your child and a backup in case your first choice cannot serve. It also names an executor to handle your estate.

A court usually gives strong weight to the guardian you name. It still has the final say, so a clear choice and a backup help.

### A way to manage money for your child

Parents commonly set up one of these. The guide to [leaving money to minors](/learn/guardianship/leaving-money-to-minors) compares them in more detail.

- **A trust for your child.** A trustee manages the money and pays for your child's needs. You choose the age at which your child gets control, which can be later than 18.
- **A custodial account under your state's transfers to minors law.** Simpler, but your child usually gets full control at 18 or 21, depending on your state.
- **A revocable living trust.** This can hold most of your assets and include a separate share for each child.

Many parents name a different person as trustee than as guardian. One person raises your child. Another watches the money. That split can work well, but it is a choice, not a rule.

### Updated beneficiary designations

Life insurance and retirement accounts pass by beneficiary form, not by your will. If you name your baby directly, the money may end up in a court-supervised account. Many parents name their trust, or a custodian for the child, instead. See the guide to [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations).

### Powers of attorney and health care directives

These cover what happens if you are alive but unable to make decisions. They matter more once someone depends on your income and your choices.

## Common mistakes

- **Naming a guardian only out loud.** Telling your sister she would raise your child does not bind a court. It needs to be in a signed will.
- **Naming the baby as a direct beneficiary.** This can trigger a court process to manage the money.
- **Choosing a guardian couple without a plan for a split.** If you name "my brother and his wife" and they divorce, it may be unclear who serves.
- **Forgetting the backup.** First choices move, get sick, or change their minds.
- **Not updating after the second child.** Some wills name children individually. A later child may be left out or treated differently by accident.

## A worked example

Priya and Daniel just had their first child, Maya. They have a house, two retirement accounts and a small life insurance policy through Daniel's job.

Daniel's old beneficiary form still names his mother. Priya has no will at all.

They each sign a will naming Priya's sister, Anjali, as guardian, with Daniel's cousin as backup. Their wills leave everything to each other, and then to a trust for Maya if both have died. Daniel's college friend, who is good with money, is named trustee.

They change their life insurance and retirement beneficiaries to each other first, then to the trust. They also buy a larger term life policy. Finally, they write Anjali a two-page letter about schooling, religion and staying close to both sides of the family.

This is one family's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- How does our state handle a guardian named in a will?
- Should we use a trust for our child, or a custodial account?
- At what age do children get control of money in our state without a trust?
- Do we need a separate guardian of the person and guardian of the estate?
- How should we word our beneficiary forms so the money reaches the trust?
- What happens to our plan when we have another child?
