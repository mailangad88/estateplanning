## What changes legally when you start a business

Your business is now an asset. It may also be your family's main source of income. If you die or cannot work, someone needs the legal power to keep it running, sell it or close it.

That power does not appear on its own. Without a plan, your family may need a court to appoint someone. The guide to [business succession planning](/guides/business-succession-planning) covers the options. Meanwhile, bills, payroll and customers keep coming.

If you have co-owners, your death or disability affects them too. Without an agreement, your spouse or children may end up as their new business partners.

## What business owners commonly put in place

### A clear business structure

Owners commonly form an LLC or corporation. This can separate business debts from personal assets, but only if records are kept properly. The operating agreement or bylaws often say what happens to an owner's share at death.

### A financial power of attorney that covers the business

A general [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) may not be enough. Many owners make sure it clearly lets their agent sign contracts, access business accounts and deal with the company.

Some banks and partners also want the business itself to authorize a backup signer.

### A buy-sell agreement

With co-owners, a buy-sell agreement sets out who can buy a departing owner's share, at what price, and how it gets paid. Life insurance often funds the purchase.

### A trust for your ownership

Holding your shares or LLC interest in a [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained) can avoid probate. Your trustee can step in quickly. Check first whether your operating agreement allows the transfer.

### Written instructions

A short operating manual helps. It lists key clients, vendors, passwords, where records live, and who to call. Your family may know nothing about your business. The guide to [digital assets](/guides/digital-assets-estate-planning) covers passwords and online accounts.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming your spouse can step in.** They usually need legal authority to act.
- **Having no buy-sell agreement.** Co-owners and families often end up in conflict.
- **Mixing business and personal money.** This can weaken the protection of an LLC or corporation.
- **Keeping everything in your head.** Passwords and key relationships are hard to rebuild.
- **Forgetting disability.** Owners are more likely to face a period of being unable to work than to die young.

## A worked example

Aisha opened a bakery with her friend Ben. They formed an LLC and each own half. Aisha is married to Omar, who works as a teacher and knows little about the bakery.

At first, they had no agreement about what happens if one of them dies. If Aisha died, Omar would inherit her half. Ben would be in business with someone who does not bake.

They sign a buy-sell agreement. It says the surviving owner buys the other's share at a value set each year. They each buy life insurance on the other to pay for it.

Aisha also signs a financial power of attorney naming Omar, with specific language about the LLC. She moves her LLC interest into her revocable living trust, after checking the operating agreement allows it. She writes a two-page guide for Omar listing the accountant, the bank and the supplier contacts.

This is one owner's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my operating agreement say what happens to my share at my death?
- Should my business interest go into a trust?
- What does a buy-sell agreement need for a business like mine?
- Does my power of attorney cover my business?
- How can I plan for a period when I cannot work?
- Are there estate tax issues as my business grows?
