## What changes legally when a parent dies

Your parent's property does not automatically pass to anyone. Someone must gather it, pay debts and taxes, and distribute what is left.

Who does that depends on your parent's plan. A will names an executor, who usually goes through probate. A trust names a trustee, who can often act without a court. If there is no will, state law decides who inherits and who can serve. The guide on [dying without a will](/learn/wills/dying-without-a-will) explains that process.

Some assets skip all of this. Life insurance, retirement accounts and accounts with a [payable-on-death beneficiary](/learn/beneficiary-designations/payable-on-death-and-transfer-on-death-accounts) go straight to the named person.

## What usually happens first

### The first weeks

Grief comes first. Most legal steps can wait a few days. Some cannot:

- Secure the home and vehicles.
- Find the will or trust.
- Order certified death certificates. Many institutions want an original.
- Notify Social Security. Payments received after death often must be returned.
- Forward mail and keep paying insurance on the home.

The [first 30 days after a death](/checklists/first-30-days-after-a-death) checklist lists these in order.

### Settling the estate

The executor or trustee then:

- Lists all assets and debts.
- Opens a separate bank account for the estate.
- Pays valid debts, final bills and taxes.
- Distributes what is left according to the will, the trust or state law.

If there is probate, a court oversees these steps. The [step-by-step guide to settling an estate](/guides/settling-an-estate-step-by-step) covers each one.

### Your own plan

An inheritance can change your own estate. So can a new role as trustee for a younger sibling, or caring for a surviving parent. Many people update their will, beneficiaries and powers of attorney within a year.

## Common mistakes

- **Distributing assets too early.** Executors can be personally responsible if debts or taxes go unpaid.
- **Mixing estate money with your own.** Use a separate estate account.
- **Assuming you must pay your parent's debts.** Generally, children are not personally liable for a parent's debts. The estate pays them. Some exceptions exist, such as debts you co-signed.
- **Throwing away papers.** Old statements can reveal accounts no one knew about.
- **Ignoring your own plan.** You may now hold more assets, or be named in roles you did not have before.

## A worked example

James's father, Robert, died at 79. James is named executor in Robert's will. Robert owned a house in his name alone and a checking account. He also had an IRA naming James and his sister Kim, and a life insurance policy naming Kim.

The IRA and life insurance pass directly to the named beneficiaries. The house and checking account go through probate.

James opens an estate bank account, pays Robert's final bills, and lists the house for sale. After the court approves, he splits the proceeds with Kim as the will directs. He keeps a spreadsheet of every payment.

When it is done, James updates his own will. He names a trust for his young children to receive his share of the inheritance if he dies.

This is one family's experience. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my parent's estate need probate in their state?
- What are my duties as executor or trustee?
- How long will settling the estate likely take?
- Which debts must the estate pay, and in what order?
- Are any estate or inheritance taxes owed?
- How should I update my own plan after I inherit?
