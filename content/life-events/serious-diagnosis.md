## What changes legally after a diagnosis

The main legal risk is capacity. To sign a will, trust or power of attorney, you must understand what you are signing. Some illnesses and treatments can affect that, sometimes suddenly.

If you lose capacity without the right documents, your family may need to go to court. A judge would appoint a guardian or conservator to make decisions for you. That takes time and money, and the judge may not pick the person you would have chosen.

Time can also matter for the plan itself. Some planning steps, such as certain tax or Medicaid strategies, work better when started early.

## What people commonly put in place

### First: decisions about your care and money

These documents protect you while you are alive. Many people sign them first.

- **A [health care directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills).** It names your health care agent and says what treatment you want or do not want.
- **A HIPAA release.** It lets doctors share your information with the people you name.
- **A [financial power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney).** It lets someone pay your bills, deal with insurance and manage accounts.

Some states also have a medical order form, often called a POLST or MOLST. Your doctor fills it out with you. It tells emergency workers what you want.

### Next: what happens to your property

People commonly update or sign a [will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will) or a revocable living trust. A trust can let a successor trustee take over your finances smoothly if you become unable to manage them.

People also check [beneficiary designations](/guides/beneficiary-designations) on life insurance and retirement accounts. These often matter more than the will.

### Then: your wishes, in writing

A letter of instruction is not a legal document, but it helps a lot. It can cover funeral wishes, who to call, where things are, and messages for loved ones.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting to feel better.** Capacity can change quickly. Signing early keeps options open.
- **Signing documents no one can find.** Give copies to your agents and doctors.
- **Choosing an agent without asking them.** Your health care agent should know your wishes and be willing to follow them.
- **Relying on a spouse to "just handle it."** Spouses often still need a power of attorney for accounts in your name.
- **Forgetting digital accounts.** Email, photos and online bills can be hard for family to reach.

## A worked example

Helen, 58, was diagnosed with an aggressive cancer. She is divorced and has two adult daughters, Grace and Nora. Her will is 15 years old and names her ex-husband as executor.

Within a week, Helen signs a health care directive naming Grace, who lives nearby, as her agent. She signs a HIPAA release naming both daughters. She signs a financial power of attorney naming Nora, who handles money well.

The next month, she signs a revocable living trust and a new will. She names Nora as trustee and executor. She moves her house and brokerage account into the trust. She updates her life insurance to name both daughters equally.

Helen also writes a letter describing the music she wants at her service and what she wants each daughter to have from the house.

This is one person's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- What should I sign first, given my diagnosis?
- How soon can we meet and finish the documents?
- Can you meet me at home or in the hospital if needed?
- Does our state use a POLST or similar form?
- Would a trust help my family if I become unable to manage my affairs?
- Are there tax or Medicaid steps that need to start soon?
