## What changes legally when you retire

Your savings stop growing from a paycheck and start paying for your life. Retirement accounts often become your largest assets. Those accounts pass by [beneficiary designation](/guides/beneficiary-designations), not by your will.

The odds of needing help also rise with age. A period of illness or memory loss can leave you unable to sign checks or make medical choices. Without the right documents, your family may need a court to appoint a guardian or conservator.

Long-term care is a real cost to plan for. Medicare covers little of it. Medicaid may cover it, but only after strict financial rules are met.

## What retirees commonly put in place

### Updated beneficiary forms

Many retirees have accounts from several jobs. Each has its own form. People commonly check every one, including old 401(k)s, IRAs and annuities.

Rules for inherited retirement accounts are complex. Who you name, and whether you name a trust, can affect how fast beneficiaries must withdraw the money and how it is taxed.

### Current powers of attorney and health care directives

A [financial power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) lets someone you trust pay bills and manage accounts if you cannot. A [health care directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) says what treatment you want and who speaks for you.

Older documents may not be accepted easily by banks. Many retirees sign fresh ones.

### A will or trust that reflects your life now

Retirement is a natural time to check that your will or trust still fits. Children have grown. Grandchildren have arrived. Some people have remarried. A [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained) can make it easier for someone to step in if you become unable to manage your affairs.

### A plan for long-term care

People commonly look at long-term care insurance, savings, and how Medicaid works in their state. Medicaid reviews gifts made within a look-back period, which is 60 months in most states as of 2026. <!-- verify --> California's rules differ. An attorney licensed in your state can explain your options.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving old beneficiaries on old accounts.** A former spouse or a deceased parent may still be listed.
- **Giving assets to children to qualify for Medicaid.** Gifts within the look-back period can delay eligibility.
- **Adding a child to bank accounts for convenience.** That child may legally own the account at your death, leaving out siblings.
- **Waiting for a diagnosis.** Signing documents requires mental capacity. Planning early keeps your options open.
- **Keeping too many scattered accounts.** Your executor will have to find and close each one.

## A worked example

Walter and June retired last year. Walter has a pension, an IRA and an old 401(k) from a company that was bought out. June has an IRA and a small brokerage account. Their wills are 20 years old and name a guardian for children who are now in their 40s.

They roll Walter's old 401(k) into his IRA. They update every beneficiary form to name each other, then their three children equally.

They sign a revocable living trust, new pour-over wills, new powers of attorney and new health care directives. Their daughter Claire, a nurse, is their health care agent. Their son Ray, an accountant, is their financial agent and successor trustee.

They meet an elder law attorney to learn how Medicaid works in their state. They decide to buy a long-term care policy for June, who has a family history of dementia.

This is one couple's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Should I name a trust as beneficiary of my retirement accounts?
- Are my powers of attorney current enough for banks to accept?
- How does Medicaid treat my home and savings in our state?
- Would a revocable living trust help if I become unable to manage my affairs?
- How can I treat my children fairly if one helps me more than the others?
- What happens to my pension when I die?
