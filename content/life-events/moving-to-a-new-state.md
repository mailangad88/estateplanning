## What changes legally when you move

Estate planning law is mostly state law. Your old documents were written to fit your old state. They may still be valid, but they may not work as smoothly where you live now.

A will that was valid where you signed it is usually accepted in other states. Powers of attorney and health care directives are less predictable. Banks and hospitals tend to prefer their own state's forms.

Other rules can change too:

- **Marital property.** Moving into or out of a community property state can change how property is treated.
- **Who can serve.** Some states limit out-of-state executors.
- **Taxes.** Some states have their own estate or inheritance tax, with thresholds far below the federal exemption. See the guide to [estate and inheritance taxes](/guides/estate-and-inheritance-taxes).
- **Probate rules.** The process, timing and cost vary by state.

## What people commonly put in place

### A review by an attorney in the new state

Most people do not need to start over. An attorney licensed in your new state can read your documents and say what still works.

### New health care documents

Many people sign a new [health care directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) and HIPAA release on their new state's forms. Doctors and hospitals recognise them more easily.

### A new financial power of attorney

Banks can be slow to accept out-of-state forms. Many people sign a new [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) under their new state's law.

### Trust updates and funding

A revocable living trust usually keeps working after a move. But a new home must be deeded into it. The guide on [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) covers how.

### A plan for property left behind

If you kept a home or land in your old state, your family may face probate there too. Many people put that property in a trust or use a transfer-on-death deed, if that state allows one.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming nothing changed.** Your documents may be valid but awkward to use.
- **Buying a new home outside your trust.** It will not be covered by the trust until deeded in.
- **Forgetting old-state property.** It may need its own probate.
- **Ignoring state taxes.** Your new state may tax estates that owe no federal tax.
- **Keeping an executor who lives far away.** Distance can make the job harder, and some states add requirements.

## A worked example

Frank and Lucy moved from Ohio to Arizona to be near their grandchildren. They have a revocable living trust they signed in Ohio. Their Ohio house is still in the trust. They kept a small cabin in Michigan.

An Arizona attorney reviews their plan. The trust still works. Arizona is a community property state, so the attorney explains how that may affect property they acquire from now on.

They sell the Ohio house. They deed their new Arizona home into the trust. They move the Michigan cabin into the trust too, so their children avoid a separate probate in Michigan.

They sign new Arizona powers of attorney and health care directives. They name their daughter, who lives 20 minutes away, as health care agent instead of their son in Ohio.

This is one couple's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Is my will still valid here, and does it work well under this state's law?
- Should I sign new powers of attorney and health care directives?
- How does this state treat marital property?
- Does this state have an estate or inheritance tax?
- Can my executor live out of state?
- How should I handle property I still own in my old state?
