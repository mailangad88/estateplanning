## What changes legally when you divorce

Your former spouse may still be named in your will, your beneficiary forms and your powers of attorney. Divorce does not always remove them automatically.

Many states revoke gifts to an ex-spouse in a will once a divorce is final. Some also revoke them on certain beneficiary forms. But federal law governs many workplace retirement plans and life insurance policies, and it may follow the form as written. The safest assumption is that a form says what it says until you change it.

The timing matters too. While a divorce is pending, a court order often limits what you can change. You may not be allowed to change beneficiaries or move assets until the case ends. Rules vary by state, so check with your divorce attorney first.

## What people commonly put in place

### During the divorce

Many people revoke a [power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) or [health care directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) that names their spouse. These documents usually are not frozen by the divorce case, but ask your divorce attorney to be sure.

Some people also sign a simple new will during the case. If you die before the divorce is final, you may still be legally married, and your spouse may have rights to your estate.

### After the divorce is final

Once the decree is entered, people commonly:

- Change [beneficiaries](/guides/beneficiary-designations) on retirement accounts, life insurance, and payable-on-death accounts.
- Sign a new will or trust that reflects their new situation.
- Name a new executor, trustee and agent under a power of attorney.
- Retitle property and accounts as the decree requires.

### Planning for children

Your former spouse is often still your children's other parent. If you die, they may become the sole guardian of your children, whatever your will says.

What you can control is the money. Many divorced parents leave assets to a trust for their children. They name someone other than the former spouse as trustee. The guide to [leaving money to minors](/guides/leaving-money-to-minors) explains the options. That way, someone you choose manages what your children inherit.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming the divorce removed your ex everywhere.** Federal rules for workplace plans can override state revocation laws.
- **Changing beneficiaries too early.** A pending divorce often comes with an order not to change them.
- **Forgetting the decree's requirements.** Many decrees require one parent to keep life insurance for the children. Your plan should match.
- **Leaving money to minors outright.** If your children inherit directly, your ex may end up managing the funds as their parent.
- **Not updating the health care directive.** Your ex could still be the person called to make medical decisions.

## A worked example

Sam and Jordan finalized their divorce in August. They share two children, ages 7 and 10. Sam's life insurance, through work, still names Jordan. Sam's will, from five years ago, leaves everything to Jordan.

The decree requires Sam to keep $250,000 of life insurance for the children until the youngest turns 18. While the case was pending, Sam revoked the old power of attorney and named a sister instead.

After the decree, Sam signs a new will and a trust for the children. Sam's sister is trustee. Sam changes the life insurance beneficiary to the trust, which satisfies the decree. Sam also updates a retirement account and a bank account that still named Jordan.

This is one person's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- What can I change while my divorce is pending in our state?
- Does our state automatically revoke gifts to an ex-spouse in a will?
- Do those rules apply to my workplace retirement plan and life insurance?
- How can I make sure my ex does not control money left to our children?
- Does my decree require me to keep certain insurance or beneficiaries?
- Should I use a trust instead of a will now?
