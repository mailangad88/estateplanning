## What changes legally when you buy a home

A home is a large asset with your name on a public record. The deed, not your will, often decides what happens to it first.

How the deed lists you matters. If you own it alone, it usually goes through [probate](/guides/how-probate-works) when you die. If you own it jointly with right of survivorship, it usually passes straight to the surviving owner. Other forms of ownership exist, and they vary by state.

Real estate is also tied to the state where it sits. If you own a home in one state and live in another, your family may face probate in both.

## What homeowners commonly put in place

### A plan for the title

People commonly choose one of these:

- **Joint ownership with right of survivorship.** Simple for couples. The home passes to the survivor. The plan still needs to cover what happens after the second death.
- **A [revocable living trust](/guides/revocable-living-trust-explained).** You transfer the home to your trust and keep full control while alive. At your death, your trustee can pass it on without probate.
- **A [transfer-on-death deed](/guides/transfer-on-death-and-payable-on-death).** Some states let you name a beneficiary for real estate on the deed itself. Not every state allows it.

Some states offer special ownership forms for married couples. An attorney licensed in your state can explain them.

### Funding the trust

If you have a trust, the home has to be deeded into it. Many people sign a trust and never move the house into it. The trust then cannot control the house. The guide to [funding your trust](/guides/funding-your-trust) explains the steps.

### A will that matches

Even with a trust, a will is usually part of the plan. It catches anything left outside the trust.

### Insurance and a power of attorney

Many buyers review life insurance so a spouse or children could keep the home. A financial power of attorney lets someone you trust deal with the house if you cannot, such as refinancing or selling.

## Common mistakes

- **Not knowing how the deed is titled.** Many people are surprised when they finally read it.
- **Adding a child to the deed to avoid probate.** This can expose the home to the child's debts or divorce, and it may create tax problems. It is also hard to undo.
- **Signing a trust but not deeding the home into it.**
- **Forgetting out-of-state property.** A vacation home can mean a second probate.
- **Leaving a home to several children with no plan.** If one wants to live there and the others want to sell, conflict is common.

## A worked example

Rosa bought a small house last year. She is single and has two adult sons, Luis and Mateo. The deed is in her name alone. She has no will.

If Rosa died now, her house would likely go through probate. State law would probably split it between her sons.

Rosa learns that her state allows transfer-on-death deeds. She considers that, but she also has a brokerage account and wants one simple plan. She signs a revocable living trust and deeds the house into it. The trust says the house goes to both sons equally, and that either son can buy the other out at a fair price.

She names Luis as trustee and Mateo as backup. She signs a pour-over will and a financial power of attorney.

This is one person's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- How is my deed titled now, and what does that mean?
- Does my state allow transfer-on-death deeds?
- Would a revocable living trust make sense for my home?
- Will moving my home into a trust affect my mortgage, insurance or property tax?
- How should I plan for a home in another state?
- What happens if my children disagree about the house?
