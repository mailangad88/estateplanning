## What changes legally when you marry

Marriage gives your spouse legal rights in your estate. In most states, a surviving spouse has a right to a share of your property even if your will says otherwise. The size of that share, and how it works, varies a lot by state.

If you [die without a will](/guides/what-happens-if-you-die-without-a-will), state law decides who inherits. A spouse usually gets a large share. In some states, your parents or children from an earlier relationship may share the estate with your spouse.

Some states also treat property differently once you marry. Community property states treat most income earned during the marriage as owned by both spouses. Other states use different rules. An attorney licensed in your state can explain which applies to you.

## What newly married people commonly put in place

### Updated beneficiary forms

This is often the most urgent step. Retirement accounts and life insurance pass to whoever is named on the form, regardless of your will. If your form still names a parent or sibling, that person may get the money.

Some workplace retirement plans give a spouse automatic rights. Changing the beneficiary to someone other than a spouse may require the spouse's written consent.

### A will or trust

A will lets you say who gets what, who serves as executor, and who would raise any children. The guide on [how to make a will](/guides/how-to-make-a-will) covers the basics. Many couples sign "mirror" wills that leave everything to each other, then to children or other family.

Couples with more assets, property in more than one state, or a wish to avoid probate often consider a revocable living trust instead.

### Powers of attorney and health care documents

Being married does not always let your spouse manage your accounts or speak with your doctors. A [financial power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney) and a [health care directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills) make your wishes clear. A HIPAA release lets doctors share information with the people you name.

### Property titles

How you hold title to your home and accounts can decide who gets them. Joint ownership with right of survivorship usually passes the asset to the survivor without probate. Your state may offer other forms of ownership for married couples.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming marriage updates everything.** Beneficiary forms and old wills stay as they are until you change them.
- **Leaving an ex on a form.** Some states revoke an ex-spouse as beneficiary after divorce, but not all. Federal rules for workplace plans may also override state law.
- **Skipping the plan because "it all goes to my spouse anyway."** That depends on your state and your family. It is not always true.
- **Ignoring children from a prior relationship.** Leaving everything to a new spouse can unintentionally cut out your children.
- **Waiting until a baby arrives.** Powers of attorney and health care directives matter from the start.

## A worked example

Marcus and Lena married last spring. Marcus has a 401(k) from his first job and a condo he bought before the wedding. Lena has a ten-year-old son, Theo, from an earlier relationship.

Marcus's 401(k) still names his brother. His condo is in his name only. Lena's will, written years ago, leaves everything to Theo with her mother as guardian.

Marcus changes his 401(k) beneficiary to Lena. Lena updates her will to keep Theo as the main beneficiary of her savings, but leaves a life insurance policy to a trust for Theo. They both sign powers of attorney and health care directives naming each other.

They decide to keep the condo in Marcus's name for now and revisit after they buy a home together. This is one couple's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does our state give a surviving spouse a fixed share of the estate?
- Are we in a community property state, and what does that mean for us?
- Should we hold our home jointly, in a trust, or another way?
- Do we each need a separate plan, or one joint trust?
- How do we provide for a spouse and children from a prior relationship at the same time?
- Is a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement worth discussing for us?
