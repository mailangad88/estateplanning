## What changes legally when you become a caregiver

Being a loving child gives you no legal power over your parent's money or medical care. Banks and doctors need documents your parent signed.

If your parent loses capacity before signing them, your family may need to ask a court for guardianship or conservatorship. That can be slow, costly and public.

Caring for a parent also affects you. You may be spending your own money, taking time off work, or moving a parent into your home. Each of these has legal and financial effects.

## What families commonly put in place

### Your parent's documents

The core set is usually:

- **A [financial power of attorney](/guides/powers-of-attorney)** naming someone to manage money and accounts.
- **A [health care directive](/guides/healthcare-directives-and-living-wills)** naming a health care agent and stating treatment wishes.
- **A HIPAA release** so doctors can speak with family.
- **A will or revocable living trust** that reflects their wishes today.

Your parent must have capacity to sign. If memory is starting to slip, many families act quickly. An attorney meets with the parent alone to confirm the wishes are truly theirs.

### A plan for paying for care

Long-term care can be very expensive. People commonly look at savings, long-term care insurance, veterans benefits and Medicaid.

Medicaid has strict rules on assets and gifts. In most states, it looks back 60 months at gifts and transfers as of 2026. <!-- verify --> California's rules differ. An elder law attorney in your parent's state can explain the options.

### A caregiver agreement

If you are paid for caregiving, a written agreement can help. It shows payments were for services, not gifts. This can matter for Medicaid and for keeping peace with siblings.

### Your own plan

Caregivers often forget themselves. If something happened to you, who would care for your parent? Your own will, powers of attorney and directives should cover that.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting until a crisis.** By then, your parent may not be able to sign.
- **Adding your name to your parent's bank accounts.** This can make you the owner at their death and cut out siblings. It may also expose the account to your debts.
- **Giving away assets to qualify for Medicaid.** Gifts within the look-back period can delay coverage.
- **Not keeping records.** As an agent, you must keep your parent's money separate and track how it is spent.
- **Leaving siblings out of the loop.** Clear communication prevents many disputes.

## A worked example

Carlos visits his mother, Elena, every day. She is 84 and has early memory loss. His sister, Marisol, lives three states away.

Elena has a will from 1998 and nothing else. Carlos has been paying some of her bills from his own account.

Carlos and Marisol find an elder law attorney. The attorney meets with Elena alone and confirms she wants Carlos as her financial agent and health care agent, with Marisol as backup. Elena signs a power of attorney, a health care directive, a HIPAA release and a new will.

Carlos opens a log of every expense and reimburses himself only with records. The attorney explains how Medicaid works in their state if Elena later needs a nursing home. Carlos also updates his own will to name a backup caregiver plan for his mother.

This is one family's set of choices. Yours may look different.

## Questions to ask an attorney

- Does my parent still have capacity to sign documents?
- What do I need to do as an agent under a power of attorney?
- How does Medicaid treat my parent's home in their state?
- Should I be paid for caregiving, and how should we document it?
- How can we keep siblings informed and avoid disputes?
- What happens if my parent cannot sign anything?
